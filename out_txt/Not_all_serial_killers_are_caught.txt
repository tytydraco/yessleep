It’s in the title. Not all serial killers are caught. And I don’t mean
Jack the Ripper, I don’t mean the zodiac killer or the New Orleans axe
man or the any number of truckers butchering sex workers and
hitchhikers.

Those are the people who sleuths online try to identify.

There are some killers out there that don’t even get reported on.
Especially back in the day. Small town murderers, resort town murderers,
they fly under the radar. Local politicians and police will do anything
to preserve the romantic ideas of an idyllic tourist destination on a
lake or in the mountains. They’ll cover up as much as they can and keep
journalists as quiet as church mice for ages.

My mother used to tell me this a lot. There are killers out there hiding
in these towns the locals will do anything and everything to hide away
in the nooks and crannies, the shadows even the most bored and
determined investigators won’t look.

My mother knew this well. She grew up in a small town in Washington
state. Cute resort town on the coast, not too far from the Canadian
border. I’ve been a few times, but mom doesn’t like to visit. Neither
does my uncle.

It was the two of them, plus my aunt Miranda. I never met aunt Miranda.
She died pretty young. 17. And their father, my grandfather, Doctor
James Keyes. He lived there my whole life. Whenever we were in town we
were there to visit him. He was a dentist. One of only two or three in
the town I’d imagine. Made good money. Put my uncle, mother and aunt
through private boarding school with it. They spent the school year in
Seattle and came home for the summers back with my grandfather and my
grandmother. I never met my grandmother either.

Or well I did once but I was young I barely remember it. I never knew
her I should say. Her name was Audrey.

Grandpa had her institutionalized in 1978 when my mother was a child.
Grandma was unstable and manic. I think she had schizophrenia. Or
bipolar disorder. Or both? At least that’s what I’ve been told. She died
in the same hospital grandpa sent her to when I was five I think. Or
six.

No, five, it was 2009.

Grandpa was distraught when he had her institutionalized, my mother
said. And missed her up until her death. But didn’t attend the funeral.
He told my mother he made peace with his wife.

Anyways

My grandpa died too. Only a few weeks ago.

We went for the funeral of course. I remember thinking to myself that I
had wished I spent more time with my grandpa. I never knew him well. My
mother had a complicated relationship with him. My uncle was closer with
him. But I never had that chance to bond with him.

The funeral was nice. Nice enough I guess. The reading of the will went
well too. We got the house. Uncle seemed particularly upset by that. But
he still helped us move in.

Grandpas house was nice. Big. But still cozy. Though it always felt like
it hadn’t left the 90s. After grandpa retired he became a bit of a
recluse and somewhat of a local boogeyman. You could feel that in the
air. It was a warm and welcoming home but with an uncomfortable
undertone, you know? Like a therapists office almost. Or the office of a
nice professor you like. Friendly and inviting but with a certain
sterile and stale vibe.

But it didn’t matter I suppose. we had just gotten a free summer home
after all. Nothing a bit of paint and a trip to ikea couldn’t fix,
y’know? And mom didn’t want to pay for another night in a motel so we
decided just to stay there for a few days before we headed home to pack
our stuff.

Mom took the master bedroom, and my uncle took his childhood bedroom,
which grandpa turned into a guest bedroom. That’s where I stayed usually
on the rare occasion we visited. I would’ve taken my mother’s childhood
bedroom, but grandpa seemed to have gutted it for storage years ago. So
I took my aunt Miranda’s bedroom. Which was kept locked every time we
visited.

And it was

Weird.

Aunt Miranda died young as I mentioned before. Younger than I am now
writing this. She was a teenager. She wasn’t even out of high school
yet. She died in 1988. And her room looked like it hadn’t been changed
since then. Pink wallpaper, a collection of VHS tapes on some shelves,
all the furniture had that weird kinda kitschy vintage style, y’know?
Like it’s blending a glossy, plasticy almost bubbly look with kinda a
weird rustic quilted aesthetic all at once and it’s a little
overwhelming?

There were some very suggestive posters of Heather Locklear and Madonna
and Tom Selleck, Miranda you dog

One poster from the Cosby show, that aged like hell. Might just be the
scariest part of all of this.

Anyways I got over the weird vibes pretty quick. I figured grandpa
probably had his own weird way of dealing with grief. Losing a daughter
must do a lot to someone. Especially when she’s so young. Plus I figured
a lot of this stuff is in pretty good condition. Grandpa must’ve been
cleaning and dusting this room when we weren’t there. I could for sure
pawn some of it off on eBay for a couple hundred easy.

I poked around the room some more and then got comfy in the old bed and
drifted off to sleep, wrapped up in a 40 year old pink and white quilt.

The next day I asked my mother and my uncle about aunt Miranda. I didn’t
know much about her. No one really liked talking about her. So I asked
about her. She was a hemophiliac they said. That’s what got her, I mean,
that’s the reliance of that.

They said she got suspended from school and came home from Seattle
early. In May. I suppose she had a bit of a reputation in school. Mom
said she was always a bit of a bully. On the cheerleading squad and the
social queen. Imagine some ridiculous vintage 80s movie heathers-esque
drama except without the suicide bombing boyfriend.

I don’t get why she was a cheerleader if she was a hemophiliac.
Athletics as a whole seems dangerous with that condition. And even then
it’s not like it was the 1800s, this was the 1980s. They weren’t using
archaic medical knowledge, I’m sure they could’ve done something to
treat or help her. I don’t know, I don’t get it.

Anyways she died after cutting herself on a spade while helping my
grandpa garden. I guess he thought putting her to work around the house
would be punishment enough for getting suspended. According to my
grandfather, he didn’t think a cut like that would be that bad. But she
ended up bleeding out in a couple days I guess. That’s what my mom was
told anyways. And that’s what she told me.

Maybe it’s insensitive but my first thought was how much I hoped that
bedding had been washed since then.

My second thought was that I sure hoped I didn’t have hemophilia too. I
mean it’s genetic, isn’t it? Rare but genetic. A cut from a spade took
her out, what could happen to me?

But my mother reassured me that I didn’t have hemophilia. In fact she
reassured me that none of us did. Except her that is. And that she
didn’t even know her sister had hemophilia until after she was dead. No
one did. Except grandpa I guess.

You know when you get weird information and it kinda sends a chill up
your spine, and then you kinda give a knowing look to everyone else in
the room, not exactly knowing exactly what your knowing look was knowing
about, and no one wants to make eye contact with you? Yeah that
happened. It was weird.

There was a photograph of my grandparents’ wedding day watching over the
kitchen where we were talking. I don’t know why that stuck with me. It
just did. “Mr. and Mrs. James Keyes: 1967” with my grandpas big, goofy
smile. He was distant, but smiled a lot. It was a warm smile. But now
felt weird. Distant but warm no longer. Now just sort of present but
cold. Apathetic. Like he was listening but doing everything in his power
to not hear what we were talking about. It was weird. It’s weird the
emotions we can pick up off decades old photos, isn’t it?

Well anyways

We left to start clearing out some of the rooms I really started to
realize how much of a hoarder grandpa was. I never really noticed it.
Even as a kid when we were visiting his home seemed so clean and
comfortable. But now that we were going room by room, it just started to
feel more cluttered and messy.

But like
And i don’t even know what I mean by this.

But like, organized messy? If that makes sense.

It was junk and it was clutter but reasonable to manage junk and
clutter. In most of the storage rooms, especially my mother’s childhood
room, everything was haphazardly placed where ever but also kept
together. In groups of seven.

Seven spoons at the windowsill joined by seven rubber bands, seven
garden gnomes in varying states of disrepair hiding in the back of the
closet, seven clipboards with the dental files of seven separate
patients, seven newspapers from the same day, February 4th, 1987 kept in
seven separate sheets of plastic in seven separate boxes full of seven
more newspapers from seven other dates. It was weird.

Seven vintage plastic dinosaurs, seven rocks painted like insects, seven
plastic flowers in a bouquet stapled to the wall and above them, eight
garden tools hanging on the wall.

Eight. No seventh.

I brought it up to my uncle but he dismissed it. He said grandpa was
starting to lose it anyways. And all this was just his demented OCD
ridden brain going crazy at the end. He was probably right I guess.

We kept cleaning, and I have to admit there was method to the madness.
It made it kinda easier actually, with everything clumped together in
little “families.”

Mom seemed especially taken by the dental records. The names of the
patients were on them. My mother knew them. So did my uncle. Everyone
did. A string of disappearances when they were growing up. Neighbourhood
girls, usually in their early 20s. Pretty young women. My mother and my
uncle reminisced about them. They knew my grandfather, as a dentist, was
involved with the investigations. Dental records and all that. And there
were some bodies that turned up. But the records didn’t match. And the
mayor was eager to close the case. So it was closed prematurely. And my
mother said my grandpa felt upset with himself for not helping more.

“Dad must’ve kept these after the case was closed” she said. “I know he
felt guilty about them not identifying the girls. Even with the dental
records. Maybe it’s how he coped. But he knew small town serial killers
don’t get caught. He knew the mayor and the chief of police would’ve
closed the case anyways. But he always wallowed in that guilt” my mother
said.

The seven girls that went missing
Vanishing between the seven months of November and May, 1987.

Seven
Seven seven seven
Seven seven seven seven seven

My uncle was right. Grandpa really was losing it.

Guilt really can eat a man alive. Especially if it’s born from wanting
to do more in a situation you can’t help.

Only then to lose a child the next year.

He had a very difficult time processing it I think.

Looking at the dental files made my mother emotional. Uncle too but less
so. Not that they showed it. Distanced apathy has always been my
family’s response to emotion. Except grandpa I guess who just went full
hoarder.

I decided to give them some space. It’s a difficult thing to remember. I
opted to start checking out the attic myself.

And the attic was, as I’m sure you can guess, kinda strange. Dusty. Not
like the rest of the house. And stale. And hot, too. It felt like a
window hadn’t been opened there in decades. It’s a miracle it wasn’t
caked in mold after spring.

But other than that it felt mostly just like an old man’s attic.
Christmas decorations, boxes of old books, and a few dresses and
outfits. All of them had some sort of photo stapled to the hemline of
them. My grandmothers wedding dress had a photograph of my grandparents
wedding day. The wedding dress was, like everything else, vintage and
outdated. With crusty lace and a faint, barely noticeable rusty stain
against the tainted white. Behind that was a vintage prom dress. It was
awfully gaudy, but as was the time I suppose. A photo of a teenage girl
stapled to it. I didn’t recognize her so I assumed it was Miranda.
Admittedly I didn’t really know what she looked like. Framed photos and
photo albums aren’t particularly common in my family.

Behind that was a cheerleading uniform and stapled to it was a picture
of a forest.

Behind that a school uniform from the private school my mother attended,
and a picture of a 7/11.

Behind that again was a Halloween costume. It looked like some kind of
dinosaur or something, and stapled to it was a pamphlet for tourism in
Dallas with the words “HALLOWEEN 1978” in all capital letters messily
scribbled on it.

And beneath the clothes was a box labelled “Dear Audrey Oh Audrey.”

At this point, I said fuck that. I’m not dealing with this shit. I’m not
dealing with my delusional old granddads guilt psychosis.

I told my mother what I found, and she sent my uncle to check it out.
The apple doesn’t fall far from the tree, what can I say?

My uncle brought the box into the kitchen and opened it. It was even
more confusing after opening it. There was a claim for divorce, filed in
1978. On the back was a bizarre poem. If you could even call it that.

“Dear Audrey, oh Audrey, oh what have I done.
Return to me your warm hands and return to me your curled hair,
allow me to run my fingers through it again.
The orderlies have robbed you from me and I have fought for your
memory’s warm embrace.
Dear Audrey, oh Audrey, oh my dear Audrey, the teeth.
Teeth, teeth, teeth, teeth, teeth, teeth, teeth,
The seven records of seven ladies-“

The rest was illegible. Just scribbles.

Beneath that, whatever that was, was a smaller box. Which read “a
gentleman dentists hobbies” and it started to feel even weirder. Like a
Russian nesting doll of family secrets never meant to be told.

Eight little raggedy Anne dolls.

Not seven, eight.

Some were stained with what looked like stale blood, some were stained
with I don’t even know what. Car grease, maybe?

Two of them were weirder than the rest though. Both wearing makeshift
gowns out of toilet paper and tissues. And they were both holding
something.

One was holding a garden trowel. Or I say holding, it was just sort of
hot glued to it. The other was holding a VHS tape with only a frowny
face and the number 8 written on the label.

There was a VHS player in Miranda’s bedroom.

I remember my uncle taking it upstairs and, it wouldn’t have been more
than 2 or 3 minutes, but he screamed. I have never heard that man
scream. Such a guttural, blood curdling scream. And there were more
screams. From the tv I mean. My grandpas voice was screaming calling for
my grandma. Damning the orderlies at the hospital. But then he stopped
screaming about my grandma and more screaming to her. Or referring to
someone by her name.

“Are you happy now, Audrey?! Those girls are dead! I killed them and
it’s your fucking fault you bitch! You made me do this! I love you so
much, Audrey I love you”

At this point of hearing the VHS tape, my mother and I ran upstairs and
sure enough. It was my grandpa recording some poor woman. No, some poor
girl. She was crying, she was screaming. She was wearing my grandmothers
wedding dress. And he was shouting at her, he was kicking her, he was
slashing at her with a spade. Slashing and stabbing her arms and
stomach. She was bound by something, I couldn’t tell what. Her face was
bruised and her nose was bloody. She tried to cry out but she was
missing teeth. A lot of teeth. And blood poured out whenever she opened
her mouth. But I could still make out the face. It was the girl from the
prom dress. That must’ve been Miranda.

My mother threw up and screamed. My uncle dashed to stop the video. It
felt like we were watching it for ages, but must’ve only been a few
minutes. If even that. We turned the tape into the police. I don’t know
what else you could do.

Even today though, it seems nothing happened. Aunt Miranda’s official
cause of death is still complications with hemophilia. And even after
the police searched the house and grounds, the case remains unsolved. I
suppose the wheels of justice don’t turn when tourist dollars are on the
line.

My mother always said small town serial killers don’t get caught.
Especially not in idyllic resort towns nested on a lakeside in the
cascades. Because she watched the women and girls of that town vanish.
And watched nothing ever come of it. But I don’t think she could’ve ever
understood how close to home it was. And I don’t think she could’ve ever
grasped just how unstable her father was.

A respectable dentist, business owner, and self described gentleman who
was seldom any of those things. But who never let it slip. Not even to
his own children.
