​

I got some interest by trying to hlp one of you here. And if you have
spent any time on the darker corners of the internet archiving anomalous
phenomena, you have probably run into my work. Not much under this name,
but numerous others. I am the voice on the grid for someone who cannot
exist on it. For the last eighteen years, I have been the digital hands
of a man who only deals in ink, paper, and cold iron.

But I wasn’t always his apprentice. When I was 21, I was just another
broke girl trying to prove I could make it on my own.

It was 2008. I had just rented the cheapest studio apartment I could
find. The place was a depressing gallery of hideous, generic 1990s
particle-board furniture. There was a mismatched dresser with peeling
veneer, a lumpy sofa that smelled like old dust, and a plastic dining
table that rocked if you breathed too hard.

There was also an old wooden desk left in the corner. Like everything
else in the apartment, it was dull and covered in a thick layer of grey
dust and faint water rings from previous tenants.

But unlike the rest of the cheap junk, I actually liked it. I’ve always
had a massive weakness for old furniture and antiques, and I could tell
it would be really pretty once it was cleaned up. Plus, the way the
landlord had placed it next to the window was just right.

The afternoon light hit the surface perfectly, making it the ideal spot
for me to study or write.

I spent my entire first day cleaning the apartment from top to bottom.
Every single piece of furniture needed a serious scrub to wash away the
years of neglect. Armed with just a basic bucket of soapy water, a cheap
bottle of multi-surface spray, and an old rag, I took my time wiping
down the sticky grease on the dresser drawers, wiping the sofa cushion
seams as good as possible, and clearing out the cobwebs.

Behind the desk, the apartment had a beautiful, exposed brick wall. I
loved the look of the raw red brick, but it was an absolute nightmare to
clean. Decades of gritty crimson dust and crumbling mortar kept flaking
out of the rough joints, turning my rag black every time I tried to wipe
down the masonry.

The old desk got the same treatment. I wiped away the thick layers of
grey dust and buffed the dark wood until the deep grain finally started
to catch the light. By the time I finished the whole room, my hands were
feeling raw, but the place finally looked livable.

As I was unpacking some of my moving boxes and putting my things away in
the freshly cleaned space, I found my old blue notebook at the bottom of
a crate. It was a beautiful journal I had always wanted to use but never
did.

Inspired by how clean the room now looked and how perfect that heavy
desk felt by the window, I decided right then to finally crack it open.
I wanted to use it as a daily journal to log this new chapter of my
life. I carefully placed my desk lamp on the corner, arranged my
favorite fountain pens, and sat down to write. For the first time, I
felt like an actual adult. It was the only thing so far that made that
depressing apartment feel like a home.

The first night was quiet. I slept soundly, waking up excited to finally
organize the deep drawers of the desk.

It was on the afternoon of my second day when the illusion broke.

I pulled the top drawer completely out to wipe down the last bits of
interior dust. Stuck to the very back of the faded velvet drawer liner,
hidden from view unless the drawer was entirely removed, was a piece of
crumpled, yellowed paper. It had six short, clinical rules scrawled on
it in shaky handwriting.

- Rule 1: Never rest both wrists on the wood at the same time.

- Rule 2: If the pen skips a word, leave the blank space. Do not
overwrite it.

- Rule 3: If you hear keyboard typing, freeze until it stops.

- Rule 4: Never close the notebook while the ink is still wet.

- Rule 5: If you drop an item on the desk, slide it off the edge. Do not
pick it up.

- Rule 6: If the shadow of your pen moves independently, close your eyes
and count to ten.

At the very bottom, the paper was violently ripped right through a
single fragmented line:

Ru-

I was young, but I wasn’t stupid. I didn’t think it was a ghost; I
figured it was a creepy prank left by one of the previous tenants to
mess with the new kid. Still, being alone in a creaky, unfamiliar
building makes you cautious.

For the first few days, I was incredibly strict with myself. Every
single time I sat down to write, I was hyper-aware of my body,
constantly shifting my weight to guarantee I never lazily rested both
wrists down on the wood at the same time.

But after a full week of absolutely nothing happening, the paranoia
started to wear off. I wrote in that notebook every night, and the desk
behaved exactly like a normal, inanimate piece of furniture. Day six
turned into day seven, and I began to feel safe. I even started to smile
at how foolish I’d been, letting a torn scrap of paper dictate how I
sat. I grew relaxed. I convinced myself it really was just a stupid
prank.

On the eighth night, right at midnight, I was sitting at the desk, lost
in my thoughts, writing a long journal entry. My posture was loose, and
the tension of the past week was completely gone.

Then, it happened.

I was halfway through a sentence when a sharp, rapid sound filled the
room.

Clack-clack-clack-clack.

It was the unmistakable, loud sound of mechanical keyboard switches
typing right in front of my face. But I was using a fountain pen. There
was no computer in the room.

My heart slammed against my ribs. The sudden shock brought the rules
roaring back into my head. Remembering Rule 3, I froze completely. I
didn’t breathe. I didn’t blink. The invisible typing continued for
thirty agonizing seconds before it abruptly cut out.

The moment the silence returned, adrenaline took over. The rules weren’t
a prank. They were real. And worse: there were more rules on the missing
half of that torn paper that I didn’t know. I was trapped in a blind
game where a single wrong move could finish me.

I started searching frantically, tearing the apartment apart. First, I
pulled every single drawer out of the desk, flipping them over, checking
the bottoms, and searching the empty cavities with my flashlight.
Nothing. I dragged the desk away from the window, scraping the
floorboards, and checked its back panel.

Empty.

My panic grew. I tore the cushions off the lumpy sofa, shoving my hands
deep into the dusty springs until my knuckles bled. I ran to the kitchen
counters, opening every single cabinet, throwing old metal spice jars
and cheap plastic plates onto the floor, checking the undersides of the
shelves. I ripped the clothes out of the particle-board dresser,
throwing them into a pile on the floor, running my fingers under the
peeling veneer. I even checked inside the medicine cabinet in the
bathroom and under the toilet tank lid. Thirty minutes of manic,
desperate hunting, and I had found absolutely nothing.

Exhausted, trembling, and dripping with sweat, I slumped backward
against the exposed brick wall right behind the desk. As my back hit the
masonry, I heard a dull, hollow thud that didn’t sound right.

I spun around, dropped to my knees, and began tapping the red bricks.
Most were solid, but near the floorboards, one block rattled. I dug my
fingernails into the crumbling, sandy mortar and pulled. The heavy brick
slid out of the wall entirely, leaving a dark, rectangular void.

Deep inside the hollow gap, wrapped in a layer of plastic bags and tape
to protect it from the brick dust, was something. The missing scrap of
paper? I tore the tape open with my teeth. Yes! The last rules! The dim
light near the floor was too poor to read the cramped handwriting, so I
scrambled back to my feet, pulled my chair in, and sat down at the desk
to hold the jagged fragment directly under the desk lamp. I began to
read:

- Rule 7: Remove all electronics from the room before 10:00 PM.

- Rule 8: Do not scratch your skin if you feel an itch under your nails
while sitting here.

- Rule 9: If you smell copper, open all windows immediately.

- Rule 10: If you find an entry about a forgotten childhood memory, rip
the page out. Do not read it.

- Rule 11: If a silhouette is sitting at the desk at night, do not look
at it. Cover your head.

- Rule 12: If your reflection in the mirror lags by one second, leave
the building.

- Rule 13: If you break more than one rule the same night, leave the
building immediately, bring no devices, and go to the rectory of
St. Jude’s Church downtown. Ask for Father Thomas.

My eyes widened as I read Rule 7. It was past midnight, and my phone was
sitting right on the bed behind me. That was one broken rule.

Right as that terrifying realization hit, a massive, deafening CRASH
shattered the night. It sounded like someone had thrown a concrete block
through my apartment window right next to me.

The jump-scare completely bypassed my brain. It was a pure, involuntary
physical reflex. I flinched violently, and to brace myself from falling
backward off my chair, my hands instinctively slammed flat down onto the
desk surface.

Both of my wrists pressed firmly against the dark wood.

That was two rules broken.

The temperature in the room dropped instantly. The wood beneath my skin
went ice-cold, like solid dry ice. I gasped and tried to pull my arms
back, but I couldn’t. My flesh felt fused to the surface.

I looked down at my notebook. The ink of the journal entry began to
liquefy. It poured off the paper in dark, viscous streams, pooling onto
the wood. The black liquid rapidly began to form a flat, two-dimensional
silhouette of a person rising from the surface of the desk.

The ink-shadow raised its arms, mimicking my exact panicking posture,
but it was moving a fraction of a second ahead of me.

Panic gave me a sudden burst of hysterical strength. I screamed and
violently wrenched my arms upward. It physically felt like my skin was
being torn raw right off the bone, a horrific sensation of ripping flesh
that made me drop to my knees the second I broke free.

I stayed down for a moment, gasping for air on the cold floor, clutching
my throbbing arms to my chest. Trembling, I forced myself back onto my
feet, gripping the edge of the dresser for support, and staggered
blindly into the bathroom.

I slammed the door shut and turned on the faucet, expecting a river of
blood to coat the sink. But when I held my wrists up under the harsh
bathroom light, there wasn’t a single drop of blood.

Instead, some of the skin where I had touched the wood was completely
ghost-white, waxy, and raised into angry, dead patches. There were no
open wounds but severe, localized ice burns. The desk hadn’t cut me. It
had frozen the warmth right out of my tissue.

I looked up into the medicine cabinet mirror to check my face.

I blinked.

My reflection didn’t.

I stared in absolute horror as my reflection stood completely still,
staring back at me with a blank expression, before it finally, one exact
second late, mimicked my movement and blinked.

Rule 12. Leave the building.

I didn’t grab my shoes. I didn’t grab my phone. And knowing I had broken
multiple rules just leaving the building was not enough: Rule 13 was my
only option left. I grabbed my keys, threw the front door open, and ran
out into the freezing night air in my socks.

I ran six blocks through the empty city streets until I saw the old
stone facade of St. Jude’s Church. I practically threw my weight against
the heavy wooden door of the rectory, hammering on it with my burned
hands.

The door opened.

He didn’t look like a priest from the movies. He was an older, rugged
man wearing a faded black cassock covered in ink stains. He took one
look at the waxy, frozen patches on my wrists, looked out into the empty
street behind me, and pulled me inside, locking the heavy deadbolt.

“You broke the rules,” he said. It wasn’t a question.

He led me into a dimly lit study completely devoid of technology.
Listened to me while we walked there. The walls were lined with
thousands of handwritten ledgers.

He pulled down a massive, leather-bound volume, flipped past pages of
terrifying sketches, and stopped at a page that had nothing but an old
drawing of my desk and some text in a language I did not understand.

“My name is Father Thomas,” he said after he finished reading, his voice
flat and tired. “The desk didn’t just take your ink and memories from
your notebook. It harvested your very being when your wrists hit the
surface after you broke another rule already. Right now, the reflection
you left in that mirror, it then connected to, is practicing how to move
like you. If we don’t act right now, your real muscles will stop
responding to your brain. You will become the stationary object, and it
will walk out of your apartment.”

And I could already feel that happening. Movement started to feel heavy.
Not quite like moving through water but similar. Like a lag in a game.

But Father Thomas being who he is we didn’t just sit in the church
waiting for the paralysis to take me. He grabbed a heavy canvas sack and
forced me back out into the night, lagging limbs and all.

We went straight back to my apartment building. He knew we had to
intercept it before the loop completed.

When we broke down my bathroom door, my reflection was already halfway
out of the glass, its physical form solidifying into cold reality while
my own legs were feeling like they were turning to stone.

Father Thomas didn’t perform an exorcism. He didn’t read Latin. He
shattered the mirror with a heavy iron crowbar, tearing the kinetic link
apart in a shower of silver shards.

Instantly moving was easier again.

We gathered every single piece of that broken glass from the floor. We
brought it down to the church basement, where he locked the shards
inside a heavy, hand-forged iron box. To permanently bind what was left
of the duplicate, he buried the pieces under a thick layer of a very
specific coarse salt, salt he had boiled down from gallons of heavily
salted holy water.

The moment the iron lid slammed shut, the last bits of paralysis left my
body.

I never went back to that apartment. I never turned that phone back on.

The treatment wasn’t a permanent cure; it was a containment. And to have
it safe and under my and Father Tomas’ watch I had to let go of a
permanent home and single identity outside of his church so I could
become the active hands for his network. Me, with my real name,
officially is listed as a nun at his church. But to go out, to find
things, I take on different identities.

I am writing this from a small, isolated terminal room at the far end of
our sanctuary. Father Thomas is in his private, strictly analog study
three rooms over. He refuses to step foot into any room that contains a
device connected to the internet. He is getting older now. Many things
that need on scene intervention fall on me now. But he is not yet
retired.

He handed me a handwritten ledger from 1994 regarding a family that
disappeared after buying a specific set of analog porcelain dolls today.
Tomorrow morning, under a completely different name and background, I am
starting a job at a local antique distribution warehouse where those
exact dolls might have resurfaced.

But as I pack my things, I keep looking at the heavy iron box sitting on
the shelf across from my desk. I am safe for now, but whoever has it has
power over me. As if those shards ever see the light of day again, my
reflection will finish what it started eighteen years ago.

This is how I met Father Thomas.
