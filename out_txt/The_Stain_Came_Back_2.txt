They told me trauma makes the mind fracture. They said when reality
becomes too heavy, the brain simply rewrites it to keep you alive.

It has been eighteen months since I ran barefoot out of that house. Mark
has been missing ever since. When the police searched my car outside my
parents’ driveway that night, the back seat was completely empty. No
body. No blood. Just a damp patch on the upholstery that smelled faintly
of cheap shampoo, and a single plastic button torn from his work shirt.
Without evidence, there was no crime. Only a grieving girlfriend
suffering from severe dissociative amnesia.

I live alone now in a fifth-floor apartment downtown. Concrete walls,
bright LED lights, no carpets, no old wood. Everything I own is white. I
buy bedsheets in bulk and bleach them twice a week. If a single drop of
coffee hits the fabric, I throw the whole set in the trash. I needed a
place where nothing could rot in the dark.

My therapist suggested I install a motion-activated camera in the living
room. I told her it was to prove to myself that nobody was breaking in
at night. The truth was, I wanted to make sure I wasn’t leaving my bed.

Tonight, the air in the apartment felt heavy. Around midnight, the
ventilation system kicked on with a low, steady hum. It vibrated right
on the edge of hearing, building a familiar, suffocating pressure inside
my ears.

My chest started to tingle, like invisible fingers pressing against my
ribs. I went into the bathroom, stripped off my clothes, and stepped
under the hot water. I just stood there, eyes closed, trying to drown
out the hum of the vents. My mind went blank. I don’t know how long I
stayed under the spray, scrubbing my skin until it turned raw, trying to
wash away a smell that wasn’t even there.

When I finally turned off the tap, the bathroom was thick with steam. I
pulled on a clean white cotton t-shirt and wiped the fog from the
mirror. My reflection stared back. My eyes looked hollow, exhausted, yet
my mouth was completely relaxed. Too calm. I lifted the hem of my shirt.
There, on my side near my ribs, was a faint reddish smudge. Sticky. No
cut, no bruise. Just the stain.

A cold sweat broke out on my neck. I walked back into the bedroom to
grab my phone from the nightstand. The screen was lit up with a
notification from my security app. Motion detected in the living room.
Recorded four minutes ago.

With trembling fingers, I opened the clip. In the grainy night-vision
feed, my living room was quiet, lit only by the streetlamps outside.
Then, my bedroom door slowly pushed open. I walked out into the frame. I
was barefoot, wearing the exact white t-shirt I had on right now. In my
right hand, I was holding a single, dust-covered woman’s shoe. The
missing right shoe from the house Mark and I moved into a year and a
half ago.

On the screen, I walked over to the glass coffee table and placed the
shoe neatly in the center. Then I stopped. Slowly, unnaturally, my head
turned toward the camera. Under the infrared light, my skin looked pale,
almost translucent, with thick black veins pulsing beneath my throat. My
lips stretched into a wide, joyless smile, ear to ear. I stepped closer
to the shelf and pressed my palm directly over the lens, smearing it
dark until the video cut out.

I dropped the phone onto the mattress. My heart was hammering against my
ribs as I forced myself to walk into the living room. Sitting in the
center of the glass coffee table was the dusty right shoe. On the shelf
beside it, the white plastic casing of the camera was covered in a
fresh, wet smear of dark blood.

I slowly raised my eyes to the pristine white ceiling above the table.
In the corner where the walls met, a yellow stain was blooming. And it
was moving.
