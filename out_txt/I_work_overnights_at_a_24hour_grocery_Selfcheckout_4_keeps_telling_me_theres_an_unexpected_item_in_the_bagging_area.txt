I should say up front that I’m not a night person. I took the 10 p.m. to
6 a.m. at Hartwell’s because it pays an extra two dollars an hour, and
because there are never more than five customers in the building after
midnight.

My job is mostly the self-checkouts. There are six, and I’m the
attendant. I stand at a little podium with a key card on a lanyard, and
when a machine locks up, I tap the card and clear it. You’ve heard the
voice. It’s flat and pleasant, a woman who has never been in a hurry in
her life.

“Unexpected item in the bagging area.”

It usually means someone put their purse down or bagged something before
scanning it. Nothing is ever in the bagging area. You tap the card, it
says “Please continue,” and it’s over.

Kiosk 4 started doing it at 3:14 a.m., every night, for no one.

No customer. No bag. Just the screen turning red and the voice:
“Unexpected item in the bagging area. Please remove the item and try
again.” The scale readout in the corner of the screen would show a
number, sitting there on an empty platform.

The first night it said 131.2 lbs.

I wrote it down. I write down everything. It’s a habit from my last job,
where nobody believed me about anything unless there was a log.

I tapped the card. The screen went green. “Please continue.”

The next day, Denise didn’t come in.

I didn’t think anything of it at the time. People call out. But the
night after, I asked Marcus, the stock guy, if he’d heard from her, and
he said, “Who?”

I said Denise. Cashier, glasses, always had those little orange candies
in her pocket. He laughed and said I was thinking of somebody at the old
Hartwell’s, because there was no Denise here. I checked the schedule
board. Her name wasn’t on it. I checked the break room. Her locker had a
different name on it that I didn’t recognize, and it had been that way,
as far as anyone could tell, forever.

I could still see her. That’s the part I have trouble with. I could
picture the orange candies.

The second night, 3:14 a.m., Kiosk 4 said 164.0 lbs.

I’m not stupid. I didn’t tap the card right away. I stood there and
listened to it say “Please remove the item” five times, ten times, in
that same flat, pleasant voice, and while I stood there, the lights over
aisles 9 through 12 clicked off one at a time. Then 7 and 8. Then the
freezers hummed down, and something in the building’s walls started to
feel like it was leaning.

I tapped the card.

The lights came back. “Please continue.”

The next morning, the assistant manager, Tyler, wasn’t on the schedule.
Nobody had ever heard of Tyler.

I keep a notebook now, a real paper one, under the podium. I’ve written
down 131.2 and 164.0. Beside them I’ve written Denise and Tyler, in pen,
because the memory is going and I need something in my handwriting that
says they existed.

I weighed Denise’s friend, a woman who works the deli, on the produce
scale one night, as a joke. I told her it was a bet. She was 131 on the
nose.

That’s when I understood what the machine is asking.

It’s not a glitch. It’s an unexpected item. Something the building has
decided shouldn’t be there, and it’s asking me, the only attendant on
duty, to authorize the removal. It needs a person to press the button.
It’s polite about it, but it can’t do it alone.

And I’ve been pressing the button.

I tried not to, the third night. I want that on the record. At 148.6
lbs. I stood at the podium with my hands behind my back and let the
machine ask for six minutes. I counted it out. The lights went off in
sections. The doors at the front, the automatic ones, started opening
and closing by themselves, slowly, like breathing. Somewhere in the
store, a shopping cart rolled by itself down aisle 3 and hit the end
cap.

Then the voice stopped saying “item” and started saying something
different.

“Unexpected attendant in the bagging area.”

I tapped the card.

I haven’t tried it again.

It’s been nineteen nights. I have nineteen numbers in the notebook. I
don’t remember most of the names that go with them. I only know they
were people because I wrote person next to each one, in a column, like a
ledger.

Tonight, at 3:14, Kiosk 4 turned red.

It said 172.0 lbs.

I put the pen down.

I don’t weigh 172. I’ve weighed myself every morning for six years, on a
bathroom scale my dad bought me when I turned eighteen. I weighed 172.0
this morning. I know because I wrote that down too.

“Unexpected item in the bagging area.”

I could press the card and it would go away. I think it would take me,
and I think I’d be the only one who didn’t remember it happened.

But here’s what I figured out about ten minutes ago, standing at this
podium.

The machine can’t remove an item unless an attendant authorizes it.
That’s me. There’s never been anyone else on this shift, and I never
thought about why. Tyler wasn’t an attendant. Denise wasn’t either. They
were just numbers.

There’s one more person who has the power to tap that card. That’s
whoever works the overnight shift after me.

I’m writing this on my phone, standing on Kiosk 4’s scale. I stepped
onto the platform two minutes ago so the machine would stop asking about
an empty space. The screen says 172.0 and the voice has gone quiet, and
the red is fading, and I think it’s waiting.

The front doors just opened.

I can hear keys.

I don’t know who works the overnight shift after me. I’ve never once
seen them. I’ve worked here nineteen nights and not once have I been
relieved at six.

The lights over aisles 9 through 12 just went out.
