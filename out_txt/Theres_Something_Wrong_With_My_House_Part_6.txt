Part Five

By the time I left university, it was properly dark and raining hard
enough that I stood beneath the overhang outside the library for almost
a minute before convincing myself to make the run to the car.

My thesis meeting had gone badly. Not catastrophically, which somehow
would have been easier, but in the slow, humiliating way those meetings
sometimes did, with my supervisor spending forty minutes asking
questions I should have had answers to and circling sentences on a
printed chapter while saying things like, “I’m just not convinced this
follows,” and, worse, “You’ve made this argument more clearly
elsewhere.” By the end, I had become defensive enough to disagree with
comments I knew were reasonable, then spent the walk back to my car
replaying every response and coming up with better ones several minutes
too late.

I was also starving. I had eaten half a muffin at eleven and nothing
since. Hannah was working late and had messaged asking me to pick her
up, followed twenty minutes later by a photograph of takeaway menus and
the message your choice but if you say burgers again I’m breaking up
with you. I had sent back a thumbs-up without choosing anything, then
forgotten about it until I was already driving.

I remembered Juniper at the first set of lights. “Fuck.” Her dinner. I
had been at Hannah’s flat that afternoon to grab my charger, and Hannah
had specifically asked me to feed her before I left because she wouldn’t
be home until late. I had even put the tin on the kitchen bench so I
wouldn’t forget, and then I had forgotten.

I checked the time at the lights and immediately felt worse. Juniper was
not going to starve because her dinner was two hours late, obviously,
but she had kidney disease and Hannah was particular about her feeding
schedule and, more importantly, I had said I would do it. There are
mistakes that become disproportionately irritating when you are already
angry with yourself, and suddenly the entire day seemed to be evidence
that I was incapable of doing anything properly: bad meeting, no lunch,
cat unfed, girlfriend standing somewhere in the rain waiting for me
while I had not even managed to decide what I wanted for dinner.

By the time I reached the road near Hannah’s work, I was driving too
fast. Not dramatically fast. I wasn’t doing a hundred through central
Wellington or anything reckless enough that I would have recognised
myself as reckless; I was simply going faster than the conditions
deserved. I should have slowed down, but I didn’t. I wanted to get
Hannah, get food, get home, feed Juniper and let the day be over. 

There was a message from Hannah waiting at the next light: got thai.
don’t complain i panicked. Then another: outside x. I smiled despite
myself, put my phone face down in the cup holder when the light changed
and accelerated. 

The southerly had strengthened by then, driving the rain almost
horizontally across the road. Pedestrians walked with their heads bowed
and jackets pulled over their mouths, while the shops on either side
dissolved into reflected colour across the wet asphalt: red brake
lights, white headlights, strips of neon breaking apart wherever the
road dipped.

I saw Hannah from perhaps half a block away. She was standing beneath
the awning outside work with a brown paper takeaway bag hugged against
her chest. Her hair was tied back badly and the wind had pulled half of
it loose, sending strands across her face. She saw my car almost
immediately and lifted one hand, and I waved back. 

I remember feeling relieved, which is another detail I hate. There was
no fear, no unease, no sense that anything was about to happen. Hannah
was there. Dinner was sorted. Juniper would be furious with me for
approximately twelve seconds until food appeared. My supervisor could
fuck off until tomorrow.

The windscreen had started fogging from the bottom corners, a pale film
creeping inward and turning the lights ahead soft and fuzzy. The
demister had been losing its fight with the weather since I left
university, so I reached down and twisted the fan higher without taking
my eyes off the road. Nothing happened. I had turned the temperature
instead.

“Cunt.” I glanced down, just for a second. The controls glowed dim
orange beneath the dash. I found the fan dial, turned it all the way up
and heard the vents roar, my foot still on the accelerator.

When I looked up, Hannah was in the road. There was no transition, and
that has always been the part I cannot make behave like memory should. I
do not remember seeing her leave the awning. I do not remember her
stepping from the kerb, checking traffic or beginning to cross. There is
no image of Hannah halfway across the road that I somehow failed to
react to. She was beneath the awning; I looked down; then she was
directly in front of the car.

 The takeaway bag was still held against her chest. Her head snapped
towards me and I watched recognition change her face: her eyes widened,
her mouth opened, one hand came away from the bag and lifted towards the
windscreen, palm out, an instinctive, useless gesture that looked almost
as though she thought she could stop me herself.

I hit the brake and knew before my foot reached it that I was too late.
Her hands struck the bonnet first with a flat double crack, then her
legs. The front of the car caught her above the knees and her body
folded with a violence that looked completely wrong because human bodies
are not supposed to move that quickly. Her hips struck the edge of the
bonnet, twisting her sideways, and the rest of her came down hard enough
that I felt the impact through the steering wheel. 

I screamed her name as her face hit the windscreen. The glass broke
outward from her forehead in a white star, and for a fraction of a
second she was directly in front of me, close enough that I could see
her features between the fractures. Her nose struck next, then her
mouth. One cheek dragged sideways across the glass and left a bright
smear behind it. 

Then she disappeared beneath the bonnet. The steering wheel kicked in my
hands, followed almost immediately by another impact underneath the car,
heavier this time. I felt it through everything at once: the pedals, the
seat, my hands locked around the wheel. The front of the car lifted with
a thick, resistant judder as something struck beneath the chassis, once
and then again farther back, before dropping hard onto the road. 

I stamped on the brake so violently my leg went straight. The tyres
slipped across the wet surface for a moment before catching, and then
the car stopped. I sat gripping the wheel while rain hammered the roof
and the wipers dragged themselves back and forth across the broken
windscreen, one blade passing through the blood and smearing it across
the glass. 

I stared at the place where Hannah had been and knew I had killed her.
There was no question in my mind. I had been driving too fast. I had
looked away. I had been angry about a thesis meeting and thinking about
an unfed cat and takeaway and all the tiny, stupid inconveniences I had
apparently considered more important than watching the fucking road. Now
Hannah was dead because of me.

For a few seconds, everything seemed to pull very far away from me: the
car, the road, the rain, even my own body. I had the strange impression
that I was no longer sitting behind the steering wheel but looking down
at the scene from somewhere above it—a small car stopped in the middle
of a wet Wellington street, brake lights shining red through the rain,
traffic beginning to build behind it, shop windows throwing pale
rectangles across the pavement. Somewhere beneath that car was Hannah,
and somewhere inside it was me. 

Around us, the city continued doing what cities do. Lights changed.
Water rushed along the gutters. Somebody stood beneath an awning looking
at their phone. Farther down the road, a bus sighed to a stop and opened
its doors. There was something obscene about that. I had just divided my
life into before and after, and nobody else had noticed.

I thought about Hannah’s mother before I thought about an ambulance.
Then Liv. Then Juniper waiting beside an empty food bowl, and that was
the thought that broke something in me. I folded forward over the
steering wheel and made an indescribable sound into my hands.

Juniper would keep waiting. She would hear the key in the front door and
come running. She would yell at me for being late, and I would open the
tin of renal food and mash water into it with a fork knowing Hannah
would never stand in that kitchen again, singing terrible songs about
her being an ancient queen. I would have to feed Hannah’s cat after
killing Hannah. 

After that, the rest of the future arrived all at once: police, her
mother flying over from Australia, somebody calling her grandparents, a
tow truck taking the car away. A police officer asking how fast I had
been going and me saying I didn’t know. Asking whether I had been using
my phone. Me explaining that I had looked at it at the previous lights,
not then, as though the distinction could possibly matter.

Every tiny decision I had made that day acquired moral weight. The
half-eaten muffin. The bad thesis meeting. Juniper’s dinner sitting
unopened on the kitchen bench. The fan dial I apparently couldn’t
operate without taking my eyes off the road. I imagined having to
explain that I had been irritated when I got into the car. Hungry.
Distracted. Thinking about takeaway. All those stupid, ordinary things
would become evidence of who I had been immediately before I killed
someone. 

Something tapped against the passenger window three times.

I turned my head.

Hannah was standing outside the car with the takeaway bag tucked against
her chest.

For several seconds, I couldn’t understand what I was looking at. I
recognised every individual part of her without being able to assemble
them into the fact that she was alive: wet black hair stuck to one
cheek, jacket zipped halfway up, one hand raised against the glass.
There was the familiar little crease between her eyebrows that appeared
whenever she thought I was doing something particularly stupid.

She tapped again and said something I couldn’t hear through the rain.

I looked forward. The windscreen was perfect.

Not repaired. Not merely cleaner than I remembered. Perfect. No white
star of broken glass spreading from where Hannah’s forehead had struck
it. No blood dragged sideways by the wipers. No dark strand of hair
caught beneath the blade. The wipers moved smoothly across ordinary
rainwater and left clean glass behind them.

I gripped the steering wheel and looked beyond the bonnet. The road was
empty. The pedestrian crossing was clear, the traffic light above it
still red. A white stop line ran across the road perhaps a metre in
front of me, and my car was sitting entirely behind it.

My foot was pressed hard against the brake. I looked at the speedometer.

Zero.

For a moment, I simply stared at it. I had the absurd thought that
perhaps the needle had fallen back after the crash, as though that
explained anything. Then a horn sounded behind me and Hannah tried the
passenger door. It was locked.

She slapped the window with the flat of her hand. I unlocked the door,
and she pulled it open, bringing a gust of rain with her.

“Jesus, Eve. What are you doing?”

I stared at her face. She looked exactly like Hannah: alive, irritated,
freezing, holding Thai food against her chest while traffic queued
behind me. And I had just watched her die.

I touched her face because apparently some part of me still expected
evidence. Blood. Swelling. A fracture beneath the skin. Instead there
was only Hannah: rain caught in her eyelashes, lipstick worn away at one
corner of her mouth, her fingers warm around my wrist when she told me
to stop poking her.

Then I made her check the car.

Looking back, I don’t know what I thought that would prove. I could
already see the windscreen was intact and the road beneath the bonnet
was empty, but seeing it myself had stopped being enough. I needed
Hannah to look underneath, to check the bonnet, the glass, the tyres. I
needed another person to stand in the rain and confirm that there was no
blood, no dent, no body beneath my car.

She did it because of the way I was looking at her. I understand that
now.

At first she still sounded like herself, making some comment about not
crawling under the car in the middle of traffic. Then I told her I had
hit her, and the humour disappeared.

I explained badly, because there was no good way to explain it. I
remembered her stepping into the road. I remembered her hitting the
bonnet. I remembered the windscreen breaking beneath her face and the
car going over her.

Hannah told me she had watched me pull up to a red light and stop.
Nothing had happened after that.

I had a memory of several seconds she said did not exist.

Traffic had begun moving around us by then, horns sounding behind the
car, so Hannah climbed into the passenger seat and made me move. I
remember insisting she put her seat belt on. I remember the first metre
into the loading bay and almost nothing after it. Somehow I parked.
Somehow the handbrake went on. Hannah took the keys from me before I
could do anything else.

Then I asked her the same questions again. Had she crossed the road?
No. Had the car moved? No. Was she sure? Yes.

I kept asking because every answer contradicted something I remembered
with the same certainty that I remembered Hannah sitting beside me. That
was the part I could not make sense of. The car was undamaged. Hannah
was alive. She had watched me stop at the light and stay there. There
were no skid marks, no broken glass, no blood washing thin in the rain.

I opened the passenger door and vomited into the gutter.

The rest of that night came back to me later in fragments that were
almost embarrassingly domestic. Hannah got us home somehow. I showered
because there was rain in my hair and vomit on one sleeve. I fed
Juniper, then tried to feed her again ten minutes later because I could
no longer remember doing it the first time. Hannah caught me opening the
second tin and took it out of my hand before Juniper could eat herself
into renal failure.

She told me, very gently, that killing the cat through overfeeding would
be a strange response to forgetting her dinner. I laughed because Hannah
wanted me to.

Sometime after midnight, she put me into bed. I tried to sleep. Hannah
lay beside me with one hand against my back, and for a while I kept my
eyes open because I needed to see her breathing. Every time I closed
them, the other Hannah came back: hands striking the bonnet, face
against the windscreen, the weight beneath the car.

The memory did not blur the way a dream should. It did not lose detail
under inspection. If anything, the more I went over it, the more there
seemed to be.

So when Hannah finally fell asleep, I got up.

I spent the next several hours looking for something that would let both
versions of the evening be true. Microsleeps first, then dissociation,
hallucinations, tactile hallucinations, false memory, predictive
processing, REM intrusion—anything involving a brain producing an
experience convincing enough to include movement, sound, impact and
weight. I found papers saying people could hallucinate touch. Case
reports of microsleeps while driving. Explanations of memory as
reconstruction rather than playback, the brain filling gaps with
expectation and prior knowledge.

Each explanation helped for approximately thirty seconds, until I
remembered another detail: the steering wheel kicking in my hands,
Hannah’s palms striking the bonnet before the rest of her, the sharper
sound when her head hit the glass.

I started watching crash-test videos. Not real crashes; I couldn’t make
myself do that. Dummies. Road-safety footage. Slow-motion impacts filmed
from different angles. I watched one clip several times because the
sequence was exactly what I remembered: legs first, the body folding
onto the bonnet, then the head striking the windscreen.

That should have reassured me. It meant my brain could have known what
to invent. I had seen cars hit people in films, advertisements, news
footage. Physics was not secret information. Instead, I found myself
searching whether an impact beneath a car could be felt through the
steering wheel. Of course it could.

By the time Hannah woke, I was sitting on the bathroom floor with my
laptop balanced on my knees, watching a crash-test dummy roll across the
bonnet of a sedan for what was probably the twentieth time. She stood in
the doorway long enough that I didn’t notice her at first, then came
over, closed the laptop and took it out of my hands.

I argued, but not very convincingly. She told me I was going back to
bed, so I did.

I wish I could say I slept, woke the next morning and everything became
ordinary again. That I decided it had been a microsleep, or a
hallucination, or some elaborate failure of memory brought on by stress
and several weeks of bad sleep. Hell, by then I would have taken a
seizure. Anything with a name. Anything that could be put into a search
bar, described to a doctor, reduced to electrical activity or misfiring
neurons or some obscure diagnostic criterion I had never heard of.

By morning, I had almost convinced myself that was what had happened.
Not because I had found an explanation I particularly believed, but
because explanations were still preferable to the alternative. I had
slept for a few hours. Hannah was alive. The car was undamaged. Whatever
had happened belonged to my brain, and brains went wrong in ways that
could be studied.

I lay there while Hannah got ready for work and thought that perhaps,
after coffee and daylight and enough time, the memory would begin to
lose its edges. Maybe by that evening it would feel more like a dream.
Maybe in a week I would be embarrassed by how badly I had frightened
myself.

Then Hannah pulled off her pyjama shorts, and the tattoo on her hip was
gone.
