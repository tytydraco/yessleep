I’m writing this on my phone in my own kitchen with the door pushed most
of the way shut and the oven light on so it looks like I’m doing
something in here. I can hear them through the wall. I need somebody
outside this house to think about this with me, because I’ve been going
around it for an hour and I keep coming back to the same place.

I live in a small town in a valley the pines got to first. Not a
picturesque town, a working one. One gas station, one diner, a church
with a dying congregation and a good roof. The forest here doesn’t stop
politely at the property line. It stands in your yard at night and puts
its hands against your windows, and you get so used to the quiet that
you stop hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own furnace.

Yesterday I drove out to the butcher on Route 6 and bought five cuts of
prime rib. This morning I cleaned the house with the windows open. I
counted the good silver out of the felt roll, five knives, five forks,
five spoons. Five plates from the cream set my grandmother left me, the
ones with the thin green line around the rim. Five wine glasses held up
to the light one at a time to check for spots. My dining table has four
chairs and has never had more than four, so I went down to the basement
and carried up the aluminum folding chair with the frayed webbing and
set it at the end. Four chairs and one folding chair is five chairs.

Four guests, and me. Five.

All of that was done before one o’clock this afternoon. Please hold onto
that. It’s about the only thing in this house I still trust.

At ten minutes to two I went out past the shed to the tree line for
firewood, because the evenings still go cold here and I wanted a fire
lit before anybody arrived. I was bent over a length of split pine when
something hit me.

It was not a punch. I’ve been turning this over all night and precision
is about all I have left, so let me be precise. It was not a blow to a
spot on my body. It was the entire front of me at once, face and chest
and stomach and thighs, one flat enormous pressure, the way a car door
would feel if you happened to be exactly the size of a car door. I went
down on my back hard enough that my lungs emptied and would not refill,
and for a second everything went white. Not black. White, like a page.

I got up with the maul in my hand and turned a full circle. No bear. No
buck. No dog, no man, no prints in the pine needles but mine, going in
one direction and stopping where I fell. There was no wind at all. Not a
little wind. None. The trees stood there the way water stands in a
glass.

And here’s the part I have been carrying around in my mouth all evening
like a chipped tooth. The birds never stopped.

Birds stop. Anything heavy enough to put a grown man on his back sends
every bird within a hundred yards up out of the branches, and then
there’s that long held breath, and then they start again. That is how
the woods work. That is how they have always worked. These never paused.
They were going the whole time, before and during and after, perfectly
ordinary, and whatever hit me did not make one single sound.

I brushed myself off and told myself I’d stood up too fast and gotten a
head rush. My hands were shaking, but hands do that. I carried an
armload of wood back to the house.

I went out at ten minutes to two. The clock over the stove said 3:26
when I came through the door.

I noticed. I remember noticing. And then something in my head very
gently set the thought down and turned me toward the sink, and I let it.

They arrived just after seven, all at once the way they always do, in
that crowd of coats and noise and somebody’s bottle of wine. David,
whose laugh you can hear from the driveway. Elena already complaining
about the state of the road. Sam holding a tin of something he’d baked
and burnt. Mia going straight for my bookshelf like she pays rent on it.
Arthur last, stamping his boots on the mat.

For an hour it was the best night I’ve had in a year, and I want that
written down before the rest of it, because whatever else is true, that
hour was real.

A little while ago I came in here to take the meat out, and while it was
resting on the board I looked back through the doorway into my living
room, just to look at it, just because a full house is a good feeling.

David was laughing with his head tipped back. Elena was pouring. Sam had
my cat upside down in his lap. Mia was reading the spines on my shelf
with her arms folded. Arthur was leaning on the mantelpiece with one
boot crossed over the other.

David, Elena, Sam, Mia, Arthur. And me.

That is six people in a house I prepared for five.

My first reaction was embarrassment. Not fear. I felt this hot little
flush of shame, the kind you get when you realize you’ve left somebody
off a card. I stood there thinking, oh, you absolute idiot. Get another
plate down, cut the big piece in half, and if anyone says anything just
blame the butcher.

So that’s what I started doing. I was fixing my mistake. Everything that
happened after this happened because I was trying to be a good host.

Sixth plate out of the cabinet, no trouble. Sixth glass, no trouble.
Then I looked at the board with five cuts of beef on it sitting in their
own juice and I thought, hang on. Why did I buy five.

The receipt was in the ceramic dish by the front door where receipts
have gone in this house since before I was born. I made an excuse about
checking the porch light and read it in the hall with my back to the
room. Five at rib, bone in. Five.

So yesterday, standing at that counter with my wallet open, I was
certain enough about four guests to pay money on it.

Back through the kitchen. The shopping list is still under the magnet on
the fridge, written Sunday night in my own block capitals, the way I’ve
written since I was nineteen and spent a year filling in forms for a
living. Down at the bottom, in the same hand: ICE, ENOUGH FOR 5 GLASSES.

The wall calendar next to it, today’s square, same capitals. DINNER 7PM.
FIVE FOR MEAT.

I stood there for a while with my thumb on that square.

Here is the thing that has been eating me ever since, and I want to put
it plainly because it took me an hour to see it. Everything in this
house knows there are supposed to be five of us. Nothing in this house
knows which five.

There isn’t a single object in these rooms that can point at a person.
The receipt is a number. The list is a number. The chairs are a number.
All of it just stands there being right about the total and useless
about the rest.

Which means the only place the answer exists is inside my own head, and
my own head is the thing that got hit.

So I started working out which one of them was the extra, and I want to
walk you through what that actually feels like, because I don’t think
you can know until you’re doing it.

David first, because I’ve known him longest. Third grade. We set a
plastic trash can on fire behind the school and let two eighth graders
take the blame, and neither of us ever quite got over it, which is
exactly why it’s still funny thirty years later. He still holds his
breath for half a second before he laughs, like he’s checking whether
it’s allowed. I could hear him doing it through the wall while I typed
that. Not him.

Elena. Five years two desks apart at a firm that hollowed both of us
out, running on shared contempt for a man named Dale. When I had
pneumonia in 2021 she brought soup to my door every day for nine days
and never once knocked, because she knew I’d rather die than be seen
sick. She left it on the step and texted me from the driveway. I know
the exact sound the container made on the concrete. Not her.

Sam. The dog park, both of us pretending to read. His golden retriever
Birdie died last February and the ground was frozen so hard we snapped a
shovel on it, and we stood out there in freezing rain for four hours
getting a hole deep enough. He didn’t cry until it was finished. Then he
did, and I put my hand flat on his back, and we have never said one word
about it since. Not him.

Mia, my old roommate’s little sister, who in 2019 talked me into driving
to the coast on no notice at all, and whose Corolla threw a belt sixty
miles from anything, and who kept me awake in the passenger seat by
making me name every teacher I’d ever had in order from kindergarten up.
I got to eleventh grade before the sun came up. Not her.

And Arthur, who pulled me out of Miller’s Creek when I was fifteen and
the melt had it running fast and brown.

Every one of those holds. Every one has the junk in it that real
memories have, the weather and the smells and the stupid throwaway lines
nobody would invent.

I went into the living room with the bottle and refilled glasses nobody
had asked me to refill, just to be near them, and that’s when Elena
started telling the Dale story, the one about the fire drill, and Mia
finished it for her.

Mia has never met Dale. Mia has never set foot in that building. Mia was
nineteen years old and four hundred miles away the entire time I worked
there.

I stood in the middle of my own living room holding a bottle of wine and
I thought about it properly for the first time. I met David in a
schoolyard in 1994. I met Elena in an office in 2009. I met Mia through
her sister in 2016. I met Sam at a dog park three years ago. Arthur has
been there since I was fifteen. Not one of these people has any business
knowing another one’s middle name.

They all know each other. They know each other’s parents. They know each
other’s cars. Elena and Sam have an ongoing argument about a restaurant.
Mia and Arthur do a whole routine about a wrong turn somewhere near
Boonville that everybody but me finds hilarious, and I laughed along
with it tonight, and I have no idea what happened near Boonville.

I got David alone in the kitchen and asked him, as lightly as I could
manage, how the hell all of us ended up knowing each other.

He said, “You collect people. You’ve always done it. Half of us wouldn’t
know each other if it wasn’t for you.”

That is a nice thing to say and it did not help me at all.

I went down to the basement, which I know how that sounds, but I wanted
to see the chairs. There is one clean rectangle in the dust at the
bottom of the stairs. One. This house contains four dining chairs and
one folding chair and it has never contained a sixth seat, and I carried
that folding chair up myself this morning with the hinge pinching the
web of my thumb.

Coming back up I counted the coats on the hooks by the door. It rained a
little around six and everybody came in damp. Four coats. Four pairs of
boots on the mat.

I told myself somebody had put theirs over the back of a chair. People
do. Somebody in the hall behind me said something about the fire needing
another log and I said yes, absolutely, without turning around, and went
on counting boots like a lunatic.

By this point I had a suspect, and I want to be honest about how badly I
wanted it to be him.

There are eleven framed photographs in my hallway spanning about twenty
five years. David is in six of them. Elena’s in five. Sam’s in three,
Mia’s in four.

Arthur is in none.

Not one. Twenty five years of birthdays and cookouts and one truly
regrettable New Year, and the man who saved my life is not in a single
frame in this house. And on top of that, Arthur has not sat down. Not
once, not all evening. He’s been on that mantelpiece since seven o’clock
with one boot crossed over the other, and when I tried to picture Arthur
sitting down, at any point, in any year, in any room I have ever been in
with him, I couldn’t do it.

So I did it. I got him alone by the fire and I asked him about the
creek.

He didn’t perform. That’s the thing. He got quiet, and he turned his
glass around in his hands a couple of times, and he said, “You’d
stopped.”

I said what.

He said, “By the time I got to you, you’d stopped. You weren’t fighting
it. You’d gone quiet and you looked right at me and you didn’t want to
come up. I’ve never told anybody that. I didn’t think you remembered
that part.”

I have never told anybody that either. Not my grandmother, not a doctor,
not one living soul in twenty five years. I have carried that around
since I was fifteen years old specifically because saying it out loud
would make it a thing that happened.

He put his hand on my shoulder and he said, “You’re looking at me the
way you looked at me in the water.”

And I had to go and stand in the kitchen for a while.

When I came back out I asked the room, casually, whether anyone had
swung by earlier in the day, because I thought I’d heard a car.

Mia said she’d driven past on her way back from town. She said, “You
were out by the trees. Just standing there looking at them. I waved and
you waved back.”

There was nobody on that road. There was nobody anywhere. There were no
prints in the needles but mine, going in one direction, stopping where I
fell.

But she could have. That road runs past the tree line. Somebody could
absolutely have seen me from it. That’s the trouble with all of this.
Every single thing I find is either the end of the world or completely
ordinary depending on which way I tilt my head, and I have been tilting
my head for an hour and I am so tired.

My cat is fourteen years old. I brought her home in a shoebox and she
has spent every day since biting anyone who isn’t me. Tonight she is
asleep in Sam’s lap, on her back, with her feet in the air, purring loud
enough to hear across the room. Sam was scratching under her chin
without even looking down at her, in exactly the rhythm I do it, and
when I opened my mouth to say something my own memory got there first
and handed me the fact that she’s always liked Sam. She’s always liked
Sam. She goes to him at the door.

I don’t know if that’s true. I have no way of finding out if that’s
true. That is what I mean about my head being the thing that got hit.

I caught Mia in the hallway on her way back from the bathroom and asked
her the same question I’d asked David, how we all ended up in each
other’s lives.

She said, “You collect people. You’ve always done it. Half of us
wouldn’t know each other if it wasn’t for you.”

I remember thinking that was a lovely thing for two people to
independently believe about me.

I’ve stopped counting, by the way. Counting doesn’t help. Counting gives
me a number and I already have the number. What I need is a name.

Somewhere in there I heard the tap running in the kitchen while I was
standing in the dining room, and all five of them were in the living
room, and I assumed somebody had gone for water and I did not go and
look, and I’m not going to pretend that was brave.

Because when I finally did come through into the dining room to get
myself together, the table was laid for six.

Six plates. Six glasses. Six sets of my grandmother’s silver laid out
straight, handles square to the edge. Six chairs around a table that
owns four, and the folding chair is one of them, and the other is a dark
wooden thing worn shiny on the arms that has never been in this house in
my life.

I laid that table at half past ten this morning with my own hands. I
have been in the kitchen and the hall all evening.

And God help me, my first feeling was relief. Because six is right. Six
is finally, actually right. Whatever is in my house has stopped lying
about the number. Six of us. Five of them and me, at a table that
finally admits it.

So here is where I am, and I need somebody quicker than me, because
they’re going to call me through any minute now.

David, who I have known since the third grade, who blamed two eighth
graders for a fire we set. Elena, who left soup on my step for nine days
and never knocked. Sam, who I broke a shovel with in the freezing rain
last February. Grace, who drove four hours through the dark the night my
grandmother died and sat with me until it got light without saying a
single word. Mia, who made me name every teacher I ever had. Arthur, who
put me on the bank at Miller’s Creek when I had already given up.

One of those people did not exist at lunchtime today. One of them is a
shape that walked out of my pines at ten minutes to two and put a hand
inside my head and helped itself to thirty years of my life, and it is
sitting in my living room right now being loved.

And I can’t say any of this out loud. If I walk in there and start
talking, five of the people I care about most in this world are going to
look at me like I’ve finally come apart, and the other one is going to
look at me in exactly the same way, and it will be the best performance
anyone has ever given, and I will not be able to tell the difference.

So please. Anybody. Look at what I’ve written and tell me what I missed,
because there has to be a seam somewhere and I can’t find it from in
here. Tell me fast. I’ve been in this kitchen too long already and
people are starting to notice.

Grace just put her head round the door to ask if I need a hand carrying
the plates through. She always does that. She’s the only one who ever
thinks to ask.

I told her I’d be right out.
