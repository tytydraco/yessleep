Four months ago, the doctor sat across from me, clicked his pen, and
handed me a diagnosis that shattered my life: schizophrenia. The sudden
prescriptions, the quiet pity from my family, the constant doubt about
what I was seeing, it changed everything. I took a low stress job as a
graveyard shift security guard at the local mall, figuring the quiet,
empty halls at night would keep my mind grounded.

It was 1:00 AM on a Tuesday when the illusion of safety dissolved.

I was patrolling the upper concourse, my flashlight cutting through the
dim, liminal stillness of the closed stores. That’s when I heard it, a
horrific, wet dragging sound against the polished floor. I rounded the
corner by the food court and froze. One of the young cleaning crew guys
was on his hands and knees, crawling toward me, leaving a thick trail of
blood behind him.

“Hey! What happened?!” I yelled, reaching for my radio.

Before I could press the button, a sharp POP echoed through the empty
hall. A dense, blinding cloud of pink and yellow smoke exploded right
behind him. Out of the haze stepped something that shouldn’t exist.

It was a clown in a lurid, neon patterned jumpsuit, wearing abnormally
large, squeaky shoes, a wild red wig, and a hideous painted smile. In
his gloved hands, he held a massive circus hammer, brightly painted, but
undeniably heavy.

“Hey, stop right there!” I screamed, my voice cracking.

The clown didn’t even look at me. With a fluid, practiced motion, he
raised the heavy mallet above his head and brought it down on the back
of the worker’s head. The sickening crack echoed off the glass
storefronts. Blood splattered across the white tile.

My heart hammered against my ribs like a trapped bird. The clown slowly
turned his head toward me, his oversized shoes squeaking as he took a
step forward.

Panic took over. I unholstered my service pistol, aimed for his chest,
and pulled the trigger. BANG. BANG. BANG.

The rounds slammed into him. He stumbled back and collapsed onto the
floor. I let out a jagged breath, but my relief was cut short. There was
no blood pooling beneath him. Instead, brightly colored paper mouth
coils began bursting out of the bullet holes in his chest, unspooling
like cheap magic tricks.

Slowly, unnaturally, he sat up.

I turned and ran. I sprinted toward the main glass exits at the end of
the concourse, but when I rounded the corner, my jaw dropped. The wide
exit hall was completely blocked by a towering, impossible barricade of
red and white circus tents, wooden carnival booths, and giant stuffed
prizes jammed tightly to the ceiling.

Frantic, I bolted toward the nearest service stairs to get to the lower
level. But as I reached the top of the steps, I looked down. Standing at
the bottom of the staircase, grinning up at me, was the same clown. I
whipped my head around to check behind me, the hallway was empty. There
was only one of him, but somehow he was everywhere at once.

I backtracked in a frenzy, ducking into the nearest clothing store and
diving inside a dark fitting room cabin. I pressed my back against the
wall, holding my breath, my hands trembling violently around my gun.

Then, the music started.

A tinny, warped, mechanical circus tune began playing from somewhere in
the dark store. It got closer and closer, the melody dragging out in
slow, distorted notes. The footsteps stopped right outside my cabin
door.

Driven by pure instinct, I kicked the door open, slammed the heavy metal
butt of my gun directly into his face, and scrambled past as he hit the
ground. I ran straight back toward the blocked exit, it was the only way
out.

I forced my way through the thick velvet curtains of the circus
barricade, pushing into the darkness within. The air smelled of stale
popcorn and copper. My flashlight beam cut through the haze and landed
on a sight that froze the blood in my veins.

Hanging from the makeshift scaffolding, swaying gently like carnival
props, were the bodies of my close coworkers.

The shock hit me like a physical blow. My knees buckled, and I collapsed
onto the cold floor, unable to process the horror. I couldn’t move. I
couldn’t scream.

Then came the sound, an ear splitting, deafening air horn HONK that
vibrated straight through my skull, almost tearing my eardrums apart.
The clown stepped out from behind a striped tent wall, sweeping his arm
wide in a dramatic, theatrical bow, showing off the hanging bodies like
a proud ringmaster.

I laid there, defeated, waiting for the heavy hammer to end my misery.
He stepped right up to me, raised the mallet, and then, burst into a
soundless, manic laugh. He pulled a colorful canister from his pocket,
slammed it onto the floor, and vanished in another blinding cloud of
smoke.

I scrambled up and ran blindly into the night, driving straight to the
police station.

Within twenty minutes, three officers accompanied me back to the mall,
weapons drawn. But when we pushed through the doors, there was nothing.
No circus tents, no colorful smoke, no blood, and no bodies. The floors
were freshly waxed. The air was clear.

The officers looked at my shaky hands, searched my name in their system,
and saw my medical history. Their faces hardened.

“Don’t waste our time again.” one of them snapped, taking my weapon and
driving me home. “Go take your meds.”

The next day, I walked into work numb, expecting to be fired or
arrested. Instead, my dead coworkers were standing in the breakroom,
sipping coffee and laughing about their weekends. I stood in the
doorway, staring at them in utter terror. I had seen one of them get his
head crushed, I had seen the rest hanging like butcher’s meat.

My mind is completely broken, I told myself. The schizophrenia is taking
over.

That night at home, as I looked out my bedroom window into the dark
street, I saw him. The clown was standing beneath the amber glow of a
streetlamp, staring directly up at my room.

I squeezed my eyes shut, rubbed them hard, and recited the grounding
techniques my therapist taught me. “He isn’t real. It’s just a
hallucination. Take your medicine.”

When I opened my eyes and looked back down, the street was empty. A wave
of profound relief washed over me. It was all fake. It was just a
massive, terrifying psychotic break because I had been skipping my
doses.

First thing the next morning, I sat in my psychiatrist’s office, eager
to confess what had happened and get my prescription adjusted so I would
never have to experience that nightmare again.

The doctor clicked his pen, opened a thick yellow folder on his desk,
and paused. His face paled, and he looked up at me with genuine, deep
regret.

“I am so sorry,” the doctor began softly. “There was a severe clerical
error when you were admitted. Four months ago… your lab reports got
mixed up with another patient’s. You don’t have schizophrenia.”
