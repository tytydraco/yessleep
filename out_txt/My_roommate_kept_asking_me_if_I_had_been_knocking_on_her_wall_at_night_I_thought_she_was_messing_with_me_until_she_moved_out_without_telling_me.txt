I lived with a girl named Mara for about six months.

We weren’t best friends or anything. We found each other through a
housing group because rent had gotten ridiculous, and neither of us
could afford a place alone. She took the smaller bedroom because she
worked early shifts and said she didn’t care about space. I took the
bigger one because I had more stuff.

The apartment itself was fine.

Old, but fine.

It was on the second floor above a closed pharmacy. The downstairs
windows were covered with paper from the inside, and the sign outside
still said “OPEN” even though it had clearly been empty for years.
Sometimes at night, when the streetlights came on, the green pharmacy
cross outside would flicker against our living room wall.

That was probably the creepiest thing about the place at first.

Mara’s room shared a wall with the empty pharmacy storage area below
somehow. I know that sounds weird because we were above it, not next to
it, but the building was badly divided. There were strange corners,
blocked-off doors, and one narrow hallway in our apartment that ended at
a wall where a door obviously used to be.

The landlord said the whole building had been renovated badly in the
90s.

That explained most of it.

At least, I thought it did.

The first time Mara mentioned the knocking, we were in the kitchen.

She looked exhausted. Her hair was wet from a shower, and she was
standing there in her socks, waiting for the kettle.

“Did you need something last night?” she asked.

I was half awake and making toast.

“What?”

“Last night. Around three. You knocked on my wall.”

I laughed because I thought she was joking.

“I was asleep.”

She looked at me for a second too long.

“Okay.”

That was it.

I didn’t think about it again until a few nights later, when she asked
me the same thing.

This time, she seemed annoyed.

“Seriously, why do you keep knocking?”

“I don’t.”

“It’s not funny.”

“I’m not doing it.”

She crossed her arms and leaned against the counter. “It’s always three
knocks. Then a pause. Then three again.”

I shrugged and told her it was probably pipes.

She said pipes don’t knock back when you answer.

I remember laughing at that, but she didn’t.

Apparently, the first night she heard it, she thought it was me. She
tapped the wall twice as a joke, and whatever was on the other side
tapped twice back.

I told her sound travels weird in old buildings.

She told me to sleep in her room for one night if I didn’t believe her.

I didn’t.

Mostly because I didn’t want to admit she was starting to freak me out.

A week went by.

The knocking became a normal topic in the apartment, like dishes or
rent. Sometimes she’d mention it in the morning. Sometimes she wouldn’t.
I could tell when it had happened because she would drink two coffees
before leaving for work and barely say anything.

Then one night, I heard it too.

I was in my room, almost asleep.

It was late, but not that late. Maybe midnight. I had my laptop open
beside me and one of those long YouTube videos playing quietly, the kind
you put on when you don’t actually want to watch anything.

Then I heard three knocks.

Not loud.

Just clear.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

I paused the video.

For a second, there was nothing.

Then again.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

It came from inside the apartment.

Or at least, it sounded like it did.

I sat up and listened.

Mara’s door was closed. There was no light under it. I almost called out
to her, but I didn’t want to be dramatic, so I waited.

A few seconds later, I heard her voice from behind her door.

Very quiet.

“Stop.”

I didn’t sleep much after that.

The next morning, neither of us said anything at first.

We moved around each other in the kitchen like people do after an
argument, except we hadn’t had one.

Finally, I said, “I heard it.”

She looked at me immediately.

“You did?”

“Yeah.”

Her eyes filled with tears so fast that it made me uncomfortable.

“I told you,” she said.

That was when it stopped being funny.

We tried to be rational about it.

That’s what people always do before they admit they’re scared.

We made a list of possible explanations. Pipes. Neighbors. The empty
pharmacy. Animals in the walls. Someone outside. Heating system. Old
wood expanding. Literally anything.

The problem was that the knocking didn’t sound like any of those things.

It sounded intentional.

And it always came from the wall behind Mara’s bed.

The next night, I sat in her room with her.

We didn’t make a big deal out of it. We just sat on her bed with the
lamp on, pretending to scroll through our phones. She had pushed her bed
slightly away from the wall, maybe ten centimeters. Not enough to make
the room look different, but enough that she wasn’t touching it anymore
when she slept.

At 2:58, she showed me the time on her phone.

I rolled my eyes, but my stomach dropped.

At 3:03, it started.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

Mara didn’t move.

I stared at the wall.

It was just a normal wall. Off-white paint, a little crack near the
corner, one poster she had taped up badly. Nothing moved. Nothing
changed.

Then the knocks came again.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

I don’t know why I did this. I still feel stupid about it.

I leaned forward and knocked once.

Mara grabbed my wrist so hard it hurt.

“What are you doing?” she whispered.

Before I could answer, the wall knocked once back.

Exactly once.

Not three.

Once.

Like it had understood.

We left her room and slept in the living room that night with the lights
on.

After that, Mara started staying at her boyfriend’s place more often.

I didn’t blame her.

The apartment felt different when she wasn’t there. Not exactly worse.
Just emptier. I could hear the building more. The fridge clicking on and
off. Cars passing outside. The green pharmacy sign flickering faintly
through the curtains.

I also started noticing small things.

The hallway light would be on in the morning even though I always turned
it off.

Mara’s bedroom door would be open, then closed.

One time I came home and found her poster on the floor.

The tape had given out, probably.

But it had landed face down in the middle of the room, much farther from
the wall than it should have.

I sent her a photo of it.

She replied almost immediately.

“Don’t go in there when I’m not home.”

I wrote back, “Why?”

She didn’t answer for almost an hour.

Then she sent:

“Because it only knocks when someone is in the room.”

That message bothered me more than it should have.

I started keeping her door closed.

Not locked. Just closed.

A few days later, I came home from class and found the door open again.

Not wide open.

Just a crack.

Enough to see into the dark room.

I stood in the hallway with my backpack still on, feeling like an idiot.
It was the middle of the day. Sunlight was coming through the kitchen.
Someone outside was unloading crates from a van. Everything was normal.

Still, I didn’t want to walk past that door.

So I said, “Mara?”

No answer.

She was at work. I knew that.

I pushed the door open with my foot.

Her room smelled stale. Like laundry that had been left too long in the
washing machine.

Everything looked normal.

Except the bed.

She had pushed it away from the wall before, but now it was pushed all
the way back against it.

The pillow was pressed into the corner.

Like someone had been lying there, facing the wall.

I texted her again.

“Did you come home today?”

She called me instead of texting back.

Her voice was low and shaky.

“I’m not coming back.”

I thought she meant that night.

“What?”

“I’m staying with Jonas. I’ll come get my stuff when you’re there with
someone else.”

“Mara, what happened?”

She was quiet.

Then she said, “It said my name.”

I didn’t answer.

“What do you mean?”

She started crying then, but quietly. Like she was trying not to make
noise.

“Last night. I went back to get my charger. I know I told you not to go
in there, but I thought if I was quick it would be fine.”

I sat down on the kitchen floor while she talked.

She said she went into her room, turned on the lamp, and grabbed the
charger from beside her bed. As she was leaving, the knocking started.

Not from the wall.

From inside the wardrobe.

Three knocks.

Then her name.

Not loud.

Not whispered either.

Just said clearly.

“Mara.”

She left without her shoes.

I wish that had been enough for me to leave too.

But rent was expensive, and I didn’t have anywhere else to go. Also, I
kept telling myself that she had been tired. That fear can make people
hear things. That old apartments make noise. That maybe her boyfriend
had convinced her the place was creepy and she had built it up in her
head.

I lasted nine more days.

Nothing happened for the first three.

Then I started waking up at 3 a.m.

Not because of knocking.

Because of the feeling that someone had just stopped making noise.

That’s the only way I can explain it.

You know when you walk into a room and people suddenly go quiet, and you
can feel that they were talking about you?

It felt like that.

Every night.

I would wake up and the apartment would be completely silent, but not
naturally silent. Waiting silent.

On the seventh night, I heard my name.

It didn’t come from Mara’s room.

It came from the hallway.

Very soft.

Not enough to be sure.

But enough that I sat up in bed and held my breath until my chest hurt.

The next day, I called the landlord and said I wanted to break the
lease.

He got annoyed, obviously. Asked if something was broken. Asked if there
was mold. Asked if Mara had moved out and left me with the rent.

I said I just didn’t feel safe.

He sighed and said, “Did she hear the knocking too?”

I froze.

I hadn’t told him about that.

“What?”

He went quiet.

Then he said, “Old buildings make sounds.”

I asked him who lived in Mara’s room before us.

He told me he didn’t have time for this.

That night, I packed a bag.

Not everything. Just enough for a few days. Clothes, charger, documents,
laptop. I planned to sleep at my sister’s and figure out the rest later.

While I was packing, I heard three knocks.

From Mara’s room.

I stopped.

Three more.

Then three more.

I kept packing.

I didn’t look at the door.

Then the knocking moved.

That’s the part I still hate thinking about.

It didn’t get louder.

It got closer.

Three knocks from Mara’s wall.

Then three knocks from the hallway wall.

Then three knocks from the wall beside my bedroom door.

Like something was moving through the building, not outside the wall,
but inside it.

I grabbed my bag.

The hallway light was off.

I know I had left it on.

Mara’s door was open.

Just a little.

I had to pass it to reach the front door.

I stood there for maybe a full minute, trying to convince my legs to
move.

Then, from inside her room, someone knocked on the open door.

Not the wall.

The door.

Three times.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

I ran.

I didn’t close my bedroom door. I didn’t turn off the lights. I didn’t
take the trash out or leave the keys neatly on the counter or do any of
the normal things you do when you leave a place.

I just ran out.

The next morning, my landlord texted me.

Not called.

Texted.

“Please return the keys by Friday. Also, do not enter the second bedroom
again.”

I stared at that message for a long time.

Then I replied, “Why?”

He never answered.

Mara and I went back together two weeks later with her boyfriend and my
sister to get our stuff.

Nothing happened.

Of course nothing happened.

That almost made it worse.

The apartment looked completely normal. Dusty, a little messy, but
normal. Mara kept crying while packing her clothes, but she wouldn’t
explain why. She refused to look at the wall behind her bed.

When we were leaving, my sister noticed something.

In the narrow hallway, near the place where an old door had clearly been
sealed off, the paint was cracked.

Not a big crack.

Just three small dents in the wall.

All in a row.

About the height of someone’s knuckles.

I don’t know if they were there before.

I really don’t.

But I know they weren’t the only ones.

Because once I noticed them, I started seeing them everywhere.

Beside Mara’s bed.

Near the kitchen.

Outside my room.

Three small dents.

Always in a row.

Like someone had been knocking from the other side for years.

I don’t live there anymore.

The pharmacy downstairs is still empty. I pass the street sometimes
because it’s near my bus stop, but I don’t look up at the windows.

A few weeks ago, I checked the housing group out of curiosity.

The apartment was listed again.

Same photos as before. Same rent. Same description.

“Quiet apartment in older building. Perfect for two students.”

In one of the pictures, you can see Mara’s old bedroom.

The bed is gone, obviously.

The wall behind it has been repainted.

But if you zoom in, just above where the pillow would have been, the
paint is slightly uneven.

Three little spots.

All in a row.
