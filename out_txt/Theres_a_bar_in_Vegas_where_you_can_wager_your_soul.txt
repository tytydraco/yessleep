I was in a pretty bad slump from a $1,000 bust in Ohio.

Couldn’t ask my family for any more money; they were pretty sick of the
handouts and my fiscal volatility. I had to figure this one out for
myself.

So I took out one last line of credit, bought a one-way ticket out West,
and told myself I was going to win my life back.

The flight to Vegas was six hours of stale air and screaming babies. By
the time I stepped out into Nevada’s dry desert air, it felt like I’d
already lost my sanity.

The address on my phone led me to a strip of decrepit buildings across
the street from some generic local burger joints. No neon, nor the usual
overpromising signs.

I was looking for a home in the wall, one with a painted number on its
side and the sound of a 1980s jukebox leaking through the acacia
exterior.

It was nice and cool inside, maybe a little too cold for my comfort. A
round hardwood counter sat in the center of the room. Pull-tab machines
lined the walls. Green-felt tables occupied the floor. It smelled like
cigarettes and cheap booze, with something a little stronger underneath
it all. Sulfur? Whatever it was, it was pungent. Like a rotten egg.

I sat at one of the many tables. Three other people joined me within a
few seconds. The house dealer arrived last, introducing himself as
Mr. Knock. He was a tall, broad-shouldered man with a brick-shaped head.
I don’t think I saw him blink once that whole night.

He asked everybody to buy in.

They turned and looked at each other, then back at him. One after
another, they said they “agreed to the terms.” A neat stack of poker
chips manifested in front of each of them. Nobody questioned it. When it
got to my turn, I said the words too. The chips appeared right in front
of me like they’d been there from the beginning.

Jimmy sat in the small blind. He was older than the rest of us, a little
pudgy in the middle. He struck up conversation while Knock dealt the
first hand and asked what we planned on doing if we won.

Miss Hughes, the large blind, answered first. Bored face, bored voice,
expensive watches and necklaces. She said something about investing in a
new timeshare with her husband. She looked like the kind of person who
already had three.

To my left was Jon. He said he wanted to use the money to produce his
passion project, a film about magic gunslingers. He pitched it with real
gusto, like he truly believed this was the next big thing… and like we
were all bigwig investors.

Jimmy looked at me next. I felt the pressure to answer.

“I just need to get some debt paid off,” I said.

The other three laughed like it was totally absurd. Maybe it was, if it
meant selling my soul.

Jimmy shook his head and said he planned on using his winnings to pay
off his kid’s college tuition. He wished us all good luck. For a second,
the table almost felt friendly.

I checked my first hand—an eight and a nine. Already a decent basis for
a straight. The flop came ten, eight, and a jack. People started betting
high right away, trying to wipe out the competition. I was forced to put
in half my chips just to stay in. Jimmy folded early.

The turn was another ten. The river, a queen. I raised when the other
two checked. I felt pretty lucky with my hand.

We showed. Miss Hughes had a nine and a king. She took the pot without
ceremony. The precedent for disappointment was set.

Next round I folded early. So did Jon. My stack was already looking
thin.

Jimmy made a brave bet. Hughes didn’t flinch. She raised him into the
ground. Jimmy was left with a single chip. The cards flipped. Hughes
raked in the chips while Jimmy buried massaged his eyeballs, trying to
push them back inside his head.

I took a look at my next hand and felt something crawl up my spine.
Pocket aces. Time to go all in. Hughes and Jimmy hesitated, then folded
almost immediately. Jon was the only one who called, the idiot. He put
his whole stack in with that same determined look I saw in the mirror.
Aimless bravery. Mistaken boldness.

We revealed. I showed the aces first. His confident expression was
neutralized. The cards spilled from his limp fingers—a pair of kings.

“Bust,” Mr. Knock said. “For shame.”

He loosened his tie and unbuttoned the collar of his shirt. His jaw
unhinged. I felt a suction from his widening maw unlike anything I’d
ever felt. Absolute, like the room itself was about to turn inside out.

Jon screamed and fell from his chair, trying to crawl away. A previously
invisible form tore free from his body, ethereal. His soul was ripped
from him with a sound like stretching the cheese. Knock swallowed it
whole and belched into his fist. He dabbed his mouth with a handkerchief
afterwards.

Jon was left on the floor. His hair had turned a bright white. His
fingernails were bloody from clawing the wood. He didn’t appear injured
otherwise, but he was near-catatonic. Bouncers appeared from the shadows
and assisted him just so he would remember how to use the door.

I turned back toward Hughes and Jimmy. Hughes was grinning. Jimmy
wouldn’t make eye contact. I realized I was sweating through my shirt.

“Shall we continue?” Knock asked. He was already reshuffling.

The game went on for another hour. Jimmy and I seemed to decide, without
speaking, to play against Hughes. We bet recklessly high, one hand after
another, getting dumb luck more than once. Her stack progressively
shrank. She chewed the skin on her thumb until it bled. When she finally
called our bluff, I was holding a flush. She shrieked like a parrot
being boiled alive when I flipped my cards.

Knock took her the same way he took Jon. Hughes kicked and cried, and
cursed our names before she went limp. She looked drugged, dazed, like
an animal that had just been declawed. The bouncers escorted her off the
property without incident. She didn’t look back.

Just me and Jimmy left.

He looked at me, silent. I could tell he wanted to apologize for what
was about to happen. I asked him casually to tell me about his kid while
Knock dealt the next hand.

He said his boy had leukemia growing up. Most of the family’s savings
went toward treatment. When it came time for college, they were stumped.

Jimmy looked at his cards. I could see the light leaving his eyes. Bad
hand for sure. He only had a handful of chips left compared to my stack.

I checked my own. A pair of sevens. My lucky number.

We played. I tried to bet high enough that he would just fold and walk
away. I told him there was no shame in taking what he had. Jimmy kept
shaking his head. Said there was no going back for him now. In a way, I
kind of agreed.

The board ran out. I made four sevens. Bags had appeared under Jimmy’s
eyes that weren’t there a moment ago. We revealed. His hand was nothing.

He looked at me and gave a weak smile.

“That’s that, then. Well played, kid.”

Knock took him, just like the other two. The kindness drained from
Jimmy’s face and was replaced by apathy. Emptiness. The bouncers walked
him to the door, like he was a piece of broken furniture.

Knock congratulated me on my winnings. I was taken to the front of the
house to cash out. The chips turned into money, lots of it. They invited
me to come back again as I walked out the door. I never took them up on
the offer.

I don’t ask my family for money anymore. I pay my bills on time. I sleep
quietly and soundly. I wake up every morning without incident.

I’m debt-free now. Living my best life.

But since that night, I don’t think I’ve had a soul to call my own.
