[Part One][Part Two]

After the owl firestorm, the tent stayed quiet for a few days. There was
a part of me that hoped it was over, even though I knew it wasn’t. The
door stayed open, zipper flapping in the breeze, and I kept watching,
waiting for it to close.

Next time it did, I was going to be the one there to get the note. I
wasn’t leaving anything else up to chance.

It had asked for my owl. Whatever it asked for next would almost
certainly be worse, and if the next person refused to do the task, it
would get worse again. I wasn’t going to let someone else make that
choice for all of us. I was going to face it, and if it was something
terrible, at least I would be the one to make the choice. At least I
wouldn’t be waiting on someone else.

“Have you heard anything from Ava?” I asked Maria one afternoon while we
cleaned up the craft room. The kids had really done a number this week.

“She’s doing good. Picked up a job at a pizza place,” Maria gave a half
smile. “I think she regrets leaving like she did. Says she should have
stayed to help.”

I wasn’t sure she would have been much of a help even if she stayed, but
for some reason I appreciated the sentiment.

“She might drop in at the end of the summer for Nico’s birthday,” Maria
said. “She said she wants to see the hope drain out of his eyes when he
officially becomes an adult.”

We laughed at that. That sounded like something Ava would say.

***

I woke up in the middle of the night with the strangest sensation that I
wasn’t alone. I could hear Maria breathing, but that was typical–she was
a loud sleeper. What wasn’t typical was the chattering. A gentle,
nibbling sound came from the window.

I rolled over in bed. The mattress creaked underneath me and I stopped
mid-roll, hoping the sound hadn’t alerted whatever was outside my
window. 

Who was I kidding? I knew exactly what was outside the window. It was
her. She had come back to–what–finish the job? 

A shadow passed over the windowscreen. She was out there. Right outside
on my porch. I could hear her cloak crackle as it snagged on the rough
siding. Sweat pooled under my neck and my heart hammered. 

I had promised myself I would face her the next time. I said I wouldn’t
cower and I wouldn’t be scared, but now that the moment was here, I felt
lodged in place, a rock in a shoe, unable to breathe let alone get out
of bed.

The shadow drifted closer to my window. She was leaning in–coming
closer.

I forced myself to take a deep breath. Then I moved one foot to the edge
of the bed. When nothing happened–when she didn’t jump through the
window and suck out my soul–it gave me the courage I needed to throw my
other foot out from under the covers and stand up. 

I forced myself to take a step. And then another. I crossed the
minefield of loose and disjointed boards and leaned to look out the
window, waiting for her face to spring into view.

There was nothing there. 

Maybe it shouldn’t have surprised me. Phantoms tend to come and go at
will. But it did surprise me. I expected her to be standing on the
porch, and instead the deck was empty. Before really even giving myself
time to think about it, I walked to the door and cracked it open. 

She wasn’t there. 

But something else was wrong.

The tent was zipped.

I swallowed. 

This was it. 

I had told myself I was going to take the next one, but now that I was
actually here, and it was two in the morning, and no one else was
around, and I had no choice, I felt my chest constricting.

I won’t let her scare me.

Under the moonlight, I walked barefoot to the tent.

It loomed ahead. In the moonlight, with the owls spiraling overhead and
trees hanging low over the courtyard, I felt drawn to the tent by a
force I couldn’t explain and couldn’t escape. It was my fate to be
there, and whatever happened that night had the potential to shape the
rest of my life. 

I bent down beside the tent and plucked the note from the zipper.

Bring one of the owls from the fire. Place it in the tent.

My stomach twisted. I had done my best to hide from the memory of that
night, and now I was going to have to relive it. I pictured pulling one
of those charred little bodies out of the ashes and felt sick.

Still, it could have been so much worse. The tent could have asked
anything of me, and I would have been forced to do what it told me. The
request itself was fairly tame, especially after our multitude of
mistakes.  

With shaking hands, I folded the note and got up. I crossed the yard and
walked behind the cabins and made my way to the firepit.

The firepit had remained cold ever since the night with the owls. We
hadn’t lit another fire, half out of fear and half because it just
didn’t sound fun anymore. 

Even from behind the chairs, I could see their little bodies rolled up
in the soot, creating lumps under the surface. I remembered one of them
thrashing and rolling under the cabin. I walked over and stooped down
next to the back porch. The owl was curled up there, and the siding
above it singed by fire. I scooped the owl into my hands, half expecting
it to crumble into ash. Instead, its head flopped over my palm and it
settled there like it was trying to cuddle. It made me want to cry.

I wondered what the tent wanted with it. What had happened was already
so cruel–I hoped there wasn’t a way for the Witch to make it worse. 

At least it hadn’t asked for my live owl this time. At least there was
that.

I went back to the tent and placed the bird on the canvas floor. I
hesitated for a moment, and then I turned and walked back to my cabin,
praying that I hadn’t screwed anything up.

***

The next morning, when I looked out the window at the tent, it was still
mostly unzipped. Normally I avoided the tent unless I had no other
choice, but that day I woke up with a morbid sense of curiosity as to
what had happened to the owl’s corpse. Still in my PJs, I made my way
across the yard and squatted beside the tent. I pulled the flap back.

An owl hunched in the corner, glaring up at me. Last night’s dead owl
was nowhere to be found.

At first I was confused. It seemed like quite a coincidence that a
random owl would appear in the tent after I had left a dead owl the
night before.

But then I looked closer at her tail.

I saw the way the tips of her feathers were singed and melted. 

She shook and soot rained down from her wings.

It was the same bird. 

Only now she was alive.

***

When I gathered the others to tell them, it felt strange. It felt more
like sharing a new gospel than a story about the tent. Those were the
days of miracle and wonder, I suppose. For the first time, we felt awe
instead of dread. Magic instead of terror. The Witch could do good
things if she wanted. The others listened to me like I was whispering
the secrets of the universe.

Maybe I was.

***

Nico and I built a fire a few nights later. He had cleaned all the birds
out of the firepit earlier in the week, which I thought was
exceptionally good of him. I don’t know that I would have had the
stomach for it. 

Ellis and Maria had decided to go play pool in the Great Hall instead of
joining us at the fire tonight. I didn’t totally blame them. Even though
the owls were gone, I could still see the shadow of soot on the siding
where one of the owls had rolled up against the cabin. Sitting by the
fire felt nice, but it also brought back memories.

“We’re extremely lucky,” Nico said.

“How so?” 

“I think we appeased the Witch for the moment. If she’s bringing things
back from the dead for us, we must have done our something right.”

I hoped so. I wasn’t sure what I would do if I had to go through another
round of the Witch’s scarier requests. I’d been thinking about that a
lot over the last few days. Now that Nico and I were alone, it felt like
there were things we needed to talk about.

“I think we should change how we handle the tent,” I said.

Nico looked surprised, which was reasonable. I’d only known about the
tent for a couple of weeks. It was pretty bold of me to think I had the
right to start throwing around new plans. 

Still, his response was receptive. “Oh yeah?” 

“From now on, you and I should alternate who goes to the tent. We should
be the only ones.”

Again, I expected him to balk. Instead, he nodded slowly. “I’ve been
thinking about that. We can’t trust Ellis after the other night.”

“You and I are going to do what we gotta do,” I said.

“Yeah.”

It was decided as simply as that.

The fire was getting colder, sinking into the ash and coals like it was
building a nest for itself. I got up and added another log. I wasn’t
ready to go in yet.

“You know,” Nico said. “You and I have hardly talked since you got here.
I mean, except for stuff related to the tent.”

He was right. When I first showed up, everyone had been so welcoming,
and I had been all shiny and confident, and I had assumed that by the
midpoint of the summer we would all be best friends sneaking off campus
to grab dinner at midnight and watching scary movies on projectors in
our cabins. Instead, we were trauma bonding over magic. We were living
through life and death together and still didn’t know each other’s
birthdays. 

“What do you want to know?”

Overhead I heard a bird or a bat brush up against the leaves. I
shuddered at the memory of our last bonfire and tried to put it out of
my head.

“When you go home, what’s the first thing you’re going to do?”

I thought about it. Then I said, “I’m going to get like ten hours of
sleep, and I’m not going to worry about the Witch waking me up.”

“Come on, Kit. That’s so boring.”

I laughed. “Okay. Well, then maybe I’ll make myself an entire funfetti
cake and eat it while I write my memoir about everything that happened
here.”

“That’s more like it. You’ll have to invite me. We can alternate
chapters.” His words started out lighthearted, but by the time he
reached the end of them, a shadow had fallen across his face. 

Try as we might, it seemed like we couldn’t get our thoughts away from
the tent. 

“How did you learn about the Witch?” I asked. “How did you get pulled
into all of this?”

He looked uncomfortable. I watched his eyes dart off into the distance
as if he thought there might be something out there that would distract
me so he didn’t have to answer. Finally he said, “You weren’t the first
person she called specifically.”

“No?”

“No. She wanted me, too.”

“So you lied to Ava?”

He smiled. “I thought you were probably listening that night. Those
windows are trash.”

I laughed, but then I said, “Why did you lie to Ava?” 

If I was going to split the full responsibility of the tent with Nico, I
wanted to make sure I could trust him.

“I shouldn’t have. It was dumb. But people tend to get scared when you
tell them you’ve been tending to a forest demon since you were twelve
years old. I didn’t want to scare her, especially since things were
going well.”

“I think scaring people is necessary when there’s something to be afraid
of.”

“You’re probably right.”

“So, how did it happen? How did she get you?”

“We were between summers. Back then, one of Sphen’s kids was still at
camp–he was the only one left to watch the tent. One day it told him to
bring me along.”

His tone remained emotionless, but I could see the memories welling up
behind his eyes, threatening to spill into his voice. 

I shook my head. “I can’t believe he brought a twelve-year-old,” I
said. 

“I mean, what choice did he have? We don’t have the luxury of choosing
which orders we want to follow. And it worked out fine. We did exactly
what we were supposed to do for years, and it never asked us for
anything bad. Not until I got lazy. Not until Claudette.”

“But there has to be a line, right? A line we’re not willing to cross?”

“You’ve seen what happens when we don’t listen, and you’ve seen what
happens when we do. As long as we follow the rules, it’s not going to
ask for anything bad.”

He was so sure. So defiant. I actually let myself believe him. 

I decided to let it go for the moment, because he was at least half
right. There was no use dealing in what-ifs. This whole thing was
already insane enough without imagining scenarios that might not even
come to pass.
 
***

The next morning, when I looked out the window, the tent was zipped up
and Nico was already walking down the trail to the main campus with a
note in his hand. 

“I don’t like how active she’s been this summer,” Maria said. “It used
to be closer to once a week. Now it’s almost every day.”

Maybe she’s waiting for us to get it right. I didn’t say that out loud,
because it felt like an unnecessary dig at Ava. 

“I could probably…” Maria’s voice trailed off. 

I looked up from my journal.

“I could probably help with the tent sometimes.”

It was a nice thought, and I understood her guilt and the need to
appease it. Still, it wasn’t a good idea. Me and Nico handling it alone
felt like the first stable decision we’d come to all summer. I felt
confident in Nico, confident in myself, and confident that we could keep
this thing happy. 

“You don’t have to do that,” I said. 

“It just feels wrong to make you two do it all.” 

I didn’t say anything to that. She was right. It wasn’t exactly fair,
but I didn’t want it any other way. I trusted Nico, and I trusted
myself, and I wasn’t sure that I trusted Maria and Ellis in the same
way. I wasn’t sure they had the guts to do what they had to.

“What if we just burn it all down?” Maria asked quietly.

“Wouldn’t that be nice?” I laughed.

***

Around 8:15 that morning, Ellis, Maria, and I started down the hill for
breakfast. We passed Nico on the way up, carrying a stack of pancakes.
We didn’t ask questions, but I felt a huge sense of relief. If it was
asking for pancakes, then the tent was back to normal.

***

A full week passed before the tent asked for something else. It asked me
to paint it a picture of the lake and leave it in the tent. Sphen caught
me in the craft room at 6:00am, covered in paint, and I had to try to
talk my way out of what I was actually doing by explaining my
all-consuming need to express myself through art. My monologue was
worthy of an Oscar, and seemed to have the same effect as explaining
your period to your boomer grandfather. 

Sphen walked away uncomfortable and confused, but the important part was
that he walked away.

***

Nico’s next challenge was to create a circle of 30 pinecones on the tent
floor. He used a dinner plate to make sure the circle was perfect.

***

Two weeks had passed since Nico and I decided to take on the tent on our
own. Ellis didn’t like that he had been sidelined, but I think it was
just because he felt guilty, too. He wanted a chance to prove himself.
Still, he never pushed too hard to be brought back into the circle,
which was good, because I think we might have caved if he had. Things
were back to running smoothly. The tasks had become benign and menial
again, and I had no doubt Ellis could have handled it. All we had to do
was stick to the rules, and we would be fine. No more moral dilemmas. No
more horror. 

***

It was a sunny Thursday the next time I saw the tent zipped. The sun,
persistent to the point of being aggressive, found its way through the
trees and baked the ground below. The damp, loamy earth of late June had
turned to the dust of early August. Even the pine needles crackled in
the wind. 

I was on my way to my cabin to refill my first aid backpack. It had been
a messy afternoon, full of bug bites and bee stings, cut knees and
sprained ankles, and my supplies were depleted. 

The tent had been so quiet and so benign lately that I almost didn’t
even think to look. But I did. I glanced toward the center of the
clearing, and saw that little stainless zipper yanked all the way to the
top of the doorway. 

It struck me as bad timing more than anything–an interruption to an
already busy day. Still, I veered off course immediately and approached
the tent. 

I knelt down next to it and picked the note from where it was tucked in
a fold of the canvas, fully expecting the instruction to be something
simple. It probably wanted food again. It hadn’t asked for food in
several days. Did it actually eat the food? Maybe. The food and plate
and utensils were always completely gone by the next time the tent
called to us. 

I unfolded the note.

There’s a feeling that comes over you when you can’t process the thing
that you’re seeing. You feel cold, but it’s a burning kind of cold. It’s
a little like waking up in a sweat in the winter, getting out of bed and
feeling your skin go icy in the chill morning air. As I looked at the
note, a single thought circulated through my mind. This can’t be right.

I felt like I was going to throw up. This wasn’t supposed to happen. We
had done everything perfectly. The requests had tapered off into things
that Nico and I could do in our sleep. Now this? This wasn’t fair. It
wasn’t right.

And I didn’t know what to do.

I searched the note for any alternative meaning–anything to soften the
blow of what I was seeing. 

But there was nothing. The note was beautifully, perfectly, terrifyingly
succinct.

Bring me a child.
