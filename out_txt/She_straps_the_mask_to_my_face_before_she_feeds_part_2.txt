Previously on this log

I don’t have much time. My left hand is trembling so violently against
my shattered phone screen that I’ve had to retype this opening sentence
four times. I am propped up against the wooden headboard of our bed
right now. Downstairs, I can hear the metallic scrape of the cast-iron
skillet against the wood stove. She’s cooking dinner. It smells like
garlic and melting butter—a sickeningly mundane routine after what just
happened out on Ridge Road.

The comments on my last post saved my life. When I hid in the
crawlspace, I didn’t think anyone would believe me—or frankly give a
shit. But reading your warnings gave me the turbo boost I needed to
move. I managed to escape the cabin, but I didn’t get far. Now I’m
trapped back in our bedroom, and the paralysis from the ventilator is
heavy like silt in my veins. My legs feel like freshly poured concrete.

I need to log exactly what happened after I hit “post” in the
crawlspace, because when she carries that skillet upstairs tonight, it
will be the final record of what is actually living in this cabin, and
what is wearing my betrothed’s skin.

The screen of my phone went dark the exact second her headlights swept
across the foundation vents. I pressed my spine into the damp, frozen
earth beneath the floorboards, breathing through my teeth to keep from
sobbing. Above me, the floorboards groaned under a weight that didn’t
belong to a hundred and twenty pounds of woman. It was a heavy, sliding
thump—the sound of meat and broken bone dragging across hardwood.

“Clay?”

Her voice came through the floor. It was beautiful. It was the exact
cadence she used when we first met—full of that light, effervescent
warmth that makes my chest ache with belonging. But beneath the
syllables, there was a faint, off-kilter hiss. Like air leaking from a
slashed tire.

“Clay, honey, I brought the good tea. The one from the harbor.”

I didn’t dare move. A centipede crawled across the back of my hand, and
I let it. If I breathed, the gray dust in the dirt would hit my throat,
I would cough, and the floorboards would open wide and my own lights
would dim to black.

The footsteps stopped directly above my head. For three long minutes,
there was nothing but the low, rhythmic thrumming of the old generator.
Then came the sound of her knees hitting the wood. She was pressing her
ear to the floor.

“I know you’re down there,” she whispered. The voice didn’t come from
her mouth this time. It seemed to rattle out of the floorboards
themselves. “You didn’t do your chores, my love. The machine is getting
thirsty.”

Five years of waking up to her smile, of holding her hand while the
Atlantic fog beat down against our windows, all while this imposter was
nesting inside her skin. It was hollowing out the woman I loved, using
her like a decaying glove.

The cellar hatch creaked open at the back of the cabin.

I scrambled. The primal animal inside my brain took over. I dragged
myself through the cobwebs and sharp gravel, clawing toward the small
ventilation grate at the front of the foundation. I kicked the metal
screen until it popped out into the dead grass, and I tumbled into the
frosty Cape Breton air.

I ran. I didn’t have my keys—just my flannel shirt and my crumbling
phone clutched in my fist like a weapon. The spruce tendrils scratched
my face as I tore through the woods toward the main ridge road, my lungs
burning with the first real, un-vacuumed air I’d tasted in a week.

I made it to the asphalt. The fog was a solid wall of gray, cutting
visibility down to three feet. I leaned against a rusted guardrail,
gasping, praying for a logging truck. Anything.

Then, I heard the motor.

It wasn’t a car. It was a low, mechanical bellow. Whirl-click.
Whirl-click.

The sound of that cursed ventilator.

Out of the mist, a silhouette I could barely see materialized. It was
Kate. She was wearing her yellow raincoat, the hood down, her rust-red
hair damp from the dew. She looked entirely normal. Except she wasn’t
walking. Her hips were locked, her legs moving in a jerky, synchronized
stride that didn’t match the slope of the road. She moved like a fleshy
marionette stretched over something that had no business existing in
this world.

And trailing behind her on the asphalt, looping like a dead snake, was
the thick plastic hose.

“You forgot your treatment, Clay,” she said, her blue eyes wide, locked
in a thousand-mile stare. She lifted her hand. In her palm, she held the
black rubber mask.

“Get away from me!” I screamed. “What did you do to her? Where is
Kate?!”

She stopped. Her head tilted to the side—a sharp, unnatural
ninety-degree squelch that made the tendons in her neck bulge. The
loving smile on her face didn’t change, The sound came from the hose
trailing behind her, a wet, amplified echo.

“I am Kate,” the hose whispered tenderly. “I’ve always been Kate. I just
needed a bigger chest to hold all the love I have for you.”

She stepped forward, the front of her yellow raincoat parted. The fabric
of her shirt was gone, replaced by a gray, ribbed membrane that pulsed
in time with my own racing heartbeat. The plastic tube didn’t just run
into her back anymore—it was rooted into her chest, feeding directly
into her collarbone, right where the yellow bruises on my own neck
burned like wildfire.

I turned to run back into the woods, but my legs wouldn’t obey. My knees
buckled, sending me crashing hard onto the cold asphalt. My lungs felt
suddenly tight, collapsing inward as if an invisible vacuum had just
engaged in the open air. I couldn’t draw breath. The air in my throat
was being pulled out, traveling across the distance between us, rushing
into the open hose at her feet.

She glided over me, blocking out the lifeless gray sky. She didn’t hurt
me. She just scooped me up, carrying my limp body back to the cabin with
a bone-chilling, tender embrace.

“Shh,” she had cooed, her real mouth finally opening to reveal a dark,
hollow throat that smelled of rot. “Don’t fight anymore, my love. Let me
breathe for both of us.”

That brings us to right now. She dragged me back upstairs, propped me up
against the headboard, and went down to the kitchen. The vacuum effect
from the road is holding me pinned helplessly to the mattress. I can’t
run again.

I can hear her footsteps ascending, the heavy thump-drag of the hose
coming up the steps behind her.

As the bedroom door creaks open, I keep looking back to that moment on
the road when the rubber mask first descended upon my face. Before the
void took me, I had looked past her into the fog. And there, deep inside
the reflection of her bright blue eyes, I didn’t see a monster.

I saw the real Kate. Tiny, trapped, and screaming behind a wall of
glass.

The imposter is grinning ear to ear, as I await my final meal, ready to
join the rest of my bruised and battered life inside her chest, where I
was always meant to be. Forever in love.
