Six years ago, I moved into this cabin. When I look out from my porch
toward the edge of the forest today, I can hardly believe that I
actually built this place with my own two hands. Not entirely on my own,
of course. Every weekend, three good friends joined me on this property.
We dug out the foundation, put up beams, hammered nails in crooked, and
cursed at every piece of timber that refused to sit straight. In the
evenings, we sat exhausted around the campfire, eating greasy pizza
straight from cardboard boxes and treating ourselves to a beer or two as
we proudly admired what was slowly taking shape. In the end, the cabin
wasn’t perfect, but it was mine.

A lot of people ask me why I traded city life for a secluded cabin in
the middle of the woods. To be honest, I used to ask myself the same
question. Today, I know the answer. I had to get away from that place.
My wife died almost ten years ago. We had been married for many years.
We never had children. Not because we didn’t want them. Quite the
opposite. We wanted them more than anything in the world. For years, we
clung to doctor’s appointments, treatments, and hope. But with every
setback, that hope grew a little quieter. And when she was finally
diagnosed with cancer, we stopped talking about children altogether. The
subject settled silently between us, taking up more and more space. I
don’t believe our marriage fell apart because of it. We loved each other
far too much for that. But something inside us broke—especially inside
her. I don’t think she ever stopped mourning the life we could have had
with children. Perhaps she didn’t begin dying on the inside the day her
heart stopped beating. A part of her had probably disappeared years
before.

In any case, after her death, I couldn’t bear to stay in our little
house in the city any longer. It was in a beautiful neighborhood, had a
well-kept garden, and was probably exactly what many people dreamed of
owning. To me, it had become nothing more than a house full of memories.
So I moved into a small apartment and sold the house. With the money, I
bought this property and built the cabin—far enough from the nearest
town that I wouldn’t have to hear passing cars at night anymore. I
wasn’t looking for a fresh start. I simply wanted to find a place where
the silence didn’t hurt so much.

Ever since then, almost every day has begun and ended the same way. I
make myself a cup of coffee or tea, and in the evenings, I sometimes
treat myself to a small glass of whiskey. Then I sit on the porch and
listen to the forest. Most people underestimate just how alive a forest
is at night. Even when you can’t see anything, there’s always something
to hear. The relentless chirping of crickets. Frogs somewhere among the
trees. Small animals rustling through the undergrowth. The occasional
snap of a branch beneath a deer’s hooves. When the wind blows in the
right direction, the forest almost sounds like an enormous orchestra,
with every animal playing its own part. After six years, I know every
single one of those sounds. By now, I probably know the forest better
than I know my own voice. After all, I hardly speak to anyone out here.
And that’s precisely why, a few days ago, I noticed something that
hasn’t left me alone since.

That evening, I was sitting on the porch, as I so often did, with a
steaming cup of tea in my hands. It was one of those mild summer
evenings when even the wind seemed too sluggish to stir the leaves. I
leaned back and let my gaze wander along the tree line until it settled
on one particular spot. Nothing special. No distinctive tree. No
boulder. Not even a clearing. Just a section between two old pine trees
that looked particularly dark at night. It was probably because their
branches intertwined so densely overhead that hardly any moonlight could
reach the forest floor. Even now, I couldn’t tell you why that
particular spot caught my attention. Maybe I was bored. Maybe the
thoughts of a lonely man inevitably begin to wander in strange
directions after a while. Or perhaps it was simply one of those fleeting
ideas that everyone has from time to time. Most people claim they’re
afraid of the dark. But I don’t think that’s true. Darkness itself can’t
hurt us. What we’re really afraid of is what it hides from us. And
perhaps that was exactly why I couldn’t take my eyes off that particular
spot. I stared into the darkness as though hypnotized and asked myself a
question. Just one.

What if, at that very moment, I was staring directly at something? Not
some shadow or a tree. Something else. Standing motionless between the
trunks. Something I couldn’t make out. What if I was staring straight
into its eyes without even knowing it?

I shook off the thought. Of course there was nothing there. The forest
was full of sounds. If something dangerous had been lurking out there,
the animals would have sounded the alarm long ago. But as I continued
staring into the black gap between the trees, I realized that I could
never prove there was nothing standing there. I could only prove that I
couldn’t see anything. And those are two entirely different things.

That fleeting thought disappeared just as quickly as it had come, and I
slept surprisingly well that night. At least, that’s what I still
believed the following morning. I even chuckled to myself over
breakfast. How absurd the whole idea suddenly seemed. I had probably
just been living alone in this cabin for too long. After all, the human
mind is good at recognizing patterns. Especially where there aren’t any.
As far as I was concerned, that was the end of it.

That evening, I made myself a cup of tea as usual and sat down on the
porch. The sun had already disappeared behind the treetops, and the
forest was gradually becoming one enormous black silhouette. I took a
sip. And… looked straight back at that spot. It took me a moment to even
realize what I was doing. My gaze had found the dark space between the
two pine trees before I had consciously thought about it. I frowned.
“Come on,” I muttered quietly into the darkness. “You already made a
fool of yourself yesterday.” Then I forced myself to look somewhere
else.

My gaze wandered up to the moon. It hung as a slender crescent above the
treetops, looking as though someone had painted it onto the night sky
with a delicate brush. How beautiful it was again that evening. I took a
deep breath and let my eyes wander toward the little shed to the left of
the cabin. To the neatly stacked firewood. And finally to the steaming
cup of tea in my hand. But less than ten seconds later, I was looking
between those same two pine trees again. This time, I was startled. Not
because I thought I had seen something there. But because I suddenly
realized that I hadn’t been checking whether something was standing
there. I had only been checking whether there was still nothing there. I
tried to ignore the thought, but… why that particular spot? Why not the
tree right next to it? Why not the edge of the forest on the other side
of the clearing? My brain had suddenly started treating one particular
point differently from the rest of the forest, and I had no idea why.

I shook my head. It was ridiculous. I was sitting there like a child,
frightened by a shadow that I would talk myself out of being afraid of a
moment later. If I really wanted to know whether something was standing
there, the solution was incredibly simple. I got up, went into the
little shed beside the cabin, and retrieved my large LED flashlight.
Normally, I only used it when I needed to fetch firewood at night or
check on a fallen branch. Its beam could easily reach the edge of the
forest.

Back on the porch, I aimed the beam directly between the two pine trees.
The harsh white light cut effortlessly through the darkness. It
illuminated the pale bark of the birch trees farther back, made the pine
needles glisten, and cast sharply defined shadows across the forest
floor. I slowly swept the beam from left to right, pausing at each trunk
for a moment and searching for anything that didn’t belong there. Of
course, I found nothing. I breathed a sigh of relief and switched off
the flashlight. Immediately, the darkness swallowed the space between
the two pine trees again, as though the light had never touched it. I
remained standing there for another moment, staring into the blackness.
What exactly had I just proven? That there was nothing standing there?
Or merely that I hadn’t seen anything at that particular moment? How
could I know that something hadn’t been standing there five seconds
earlier? Or that something wouldn’t be standing there five seconds
later? I switched the flashlight back on and swept the beam over the
same spot a second time. Nothing again. A third time. Still nothing. Why
the hell wasn’t that enough for me? Why was simply knowing that nothing
was there suddenly no longer sufficient?

The next day, I made a firm decision to stop paying that particular spot
any special attention. I wanted to prove to myself that I had become
obsessed with a completely insignificant thought. So I started giving
myself little tasks. That morning, I took a book out onto the porch. An
old detective novel I had already read once before. Whenever I noticed
my gaze drifting back toward the space between the two pine trees, I
forced myself to keep reading.

After half an hour, I closed the book again. Not because it was boring.
But because I had absolutely no idea what had happened in the last
twenty pages. I had been reading, yes. My eyes had moved across every
single line. But my thoughts had repeatedly wandered out into the
forest. Sometimes after ten seconds. Sometimes after twenty. Once, I
managed almost an entire minute. Then my mind wandered again. And every
single time, I thought the same thing: “Just one quick look.” It wasn’t
exactly an urge. More like the need to check a door one last time, even
though you know perfectly well that you’ve already locked it.

Once again, my gaze fell on the edge of the forest, and this time, it
stayed there. I had no idea how long I’d been staring when I suddenly
noticed that I was blinking differently. I deliberately blinked more
slowly. Then unusually quickly. Then with only one eye, followed by the
other. As though I were conducting some ridiculous experiment. Once
again, I shook my head at myself. And yet, I didn’t stop. Because
suddenly, another question occurred to me. What if something out there
moved at the exact moment I closed my eyes? It was absurd. But how could
I possibly know that it really was absurd? How could I know what
happened during those fractions of a second when I couldn’t see
anything?

I had always considered the forest predictable. Every sound had a cause.
The dry snap of a branch—a deer. Rustling in the undergrowth—a hedgehog.
A piercing screech—an owl. It was as simple as that. But at some point,
I noticed that I had stopped listening to the sounds themselves.
Instead, I was paying attention to what I couldn’t hear. Whenever the
crickets fell silent, my head would immediately snap up. How long had it
been quiet? Two seconds? Five? Ten? Had it always been like that? Or had
something moved through the forest? Every time, I told myself that
foxes, wild boars, or deer could easily cause such a reaction. But
hardly had I found a reasonable explanation before my brain was already
asking the next question. What if something else had caused it this
time?

I leaned back and sighed. It was ridiculous, and I was slowly beginning
to get on my own nerves. My thoughts revolved around a dark patch
between two trees, and the more I tried to ignore it, the more
stubbornly it seemed to take root in my mind.

Finally, I stood up. “That’s enough.” If I really wanted to find out
whether there was anything unusual about that spot, I would have to go
there and examine it up close. Not just from my porch.

The walk to the edge of the forest was short. Over the past few years, I
had made that trip countless times. But on this particular occasion, it
felt strangely significant, even though I knew perfectly well how silly
that was. The sun stood high above the treetops, and the sharp scent of
pine resin grew stronger with every step. When I finally stopped between
the two pine trees, I slowly surveyed my surroundings. The place looked
completely ordinary. No dark patch. No threatening atmosphere. Just two
old trees, ferns, moss, and a few branches. I slowly walked around in a
small circle, examining the area from different angles. There was no
noticeable trail leading off to the left. To the right, young fir trees
grew so densely that it was almost impossible to get through. I knelt
down and ran my hand across the forest floor. I searched for anything
that didn’t belong there. Footprints. Broken branches. Animal hair.
Droppings. Scratch marks on the trunks. But the longer I searched, the
clearer it became that there was nothing unusual about the place. I
straightened up again and slowly turned toward the cabin. From this
perspective, it looked surprisingly small. Barely fifty meters separated
me from the porch where I sat every day, staring in this direction. It
was a ridiculously short distance. And yet it always seemed so much
greater once darkness settled between the trees.

For the first time, I was genuinely convinced that I had been making
something out of nothing. On the way back, I even felt relieved. But the
moment I stepped through my front door and closed it behind me, that
same voice in my head spoke up again.

“That’s all well and good. Now you know what that spot looks like during
the day. But you still don’t know what’s there at night.”

I closed my eyes. “No,” I muttered. “We’re not starting this again.”

But I couldn’t shake the thought anymore. I needed something that could
watch the edge of the forest while I slept. Something that never got
tired and never blinked. That’s when I remembered my old trail camera. I
had bought it years ago because wild boars kept tearing up my little
vegetable garden. Eventually, however, the animals had changed their
route, and the camera had ended up in a drawer in the shed along with a
few other things. I retrieved it, wiped the dust from its casing, and
inserted fresh batteries. Fortunately, the old model could also be
switched to time-lapse mode, automatically taking pictures at intervals
of my choosing, even when nothing moved. The memory card had already
been cleared. Perfect. If there really was something out there, I would
see it by the following day at the latest.

I carefully secured the camera to the trunk of one of the two pine
trees, aimed it directly at the spot, and checked its field of view one
last time. A little red light flashed briefly, and then the camera
silently began its work. I should have been satisfied with that. The
camera would be hanging there all night. And yet, I couldn’t shake the
feeling that I needed to take one more look at that spot. Any reasonable
person would have felt the same way, wouldn’t they?

Back inside the cabin, I opened a drawer in the living room cabinet and
took out my old binoculars. I used to occasionally watch deer grazing in
the clearing or follow hawks circling high above the treetops. But I
hadn’t used them in months.

I sat down in my chair on the porch, raised the binoculars to my eyes,
and aimed them between the two pine trees. The world moved closer. I
could make out every single groove in the bark. The fine pine needles
stood out in razor-sharp detail against the steadily darkening sky. Even
the moss between the roots suddenly seemed as though it were only a few
steps away. Only the darkness behind them remained the same. I realized
that binoculars don’t magnify darkness. They merely magnify the
certainty that you still can’t see anything. I slowly lowered the
binoculars and decided to spend the rest of the day on the couch and go
to bed early.

The following morning, however, I woke up earlier than usual. Not
because I was well-rested, because to be honest, I had barely slept a
wink. I had woken up repeatedly, wondering whether the camera had
recorded anything yet. Eventually, I gave up trying to fall asleep
again, pulled on my boots, and walked over to the two pine trees. The
little indicator light was still blinking steadily. It had lasted
throughout the night. I detached the camera from the trunk, removed the
memory card, and returned to the cabin. There, I inserted the card into
my laptop and opened the folder containing the recordings.

The first image appeared. Nothing. Just a few trees. Moss. Ferns. The
second showed exactly the same scene. The third. Still nothing. I
continued clicking through the pictures. Slowly at first, then faster
and faster. Image after image passed before my eyes. Twilight. Darkness.
Pale moonlight. But otherwise… nothing. And I mean absolutely nothing.
No deer. No fox. No rabbit. Not even a damn mouse. I went through the
pictures a second time. Something about them kept bothering me.

Maybe you don’t immediately understand why that disturbed me. To someone
who only occasionally ventures into the woods, there probably wouldn’t
have been anything unusual about it. But to me, there was. For almost
six years, I’ve sat on that porch nearly every evening. I hear animals
moving through the undergrowth everywhere. This forest is practically
always alive, and yet, on that particular night, not a single animal had
appeared anywhere near the two pine trees. Maybe it really was just a
coincidence, and it had been an unusually quiet night. Or perhaps I had
positioned the camera poorly. There was a perfectly reasonable
explanation for every one of those possibilities, and that should have
reassured me. But it didn’t.

I leaned back in my chair and stared at the screen. The camera had
confirmed it—there was nothing there. Or rather… it had confirmed that
during the few hours it had been recording, nothing had passed through
its field of view. Nothing more and nothing less. This attempt had
failed too. The flashlight had only shown me something for a brief
moment. The binoculars had done the same. And now, even the camera had
failed to give me an answer. I closed the laptop. For the first time, I
wondered whether I was even still looking for answers. Or whether what I
was really searching for was a certainty that might never exist.

At that moment, my stomach growled so loudly that I instinctively
flinched. That’s when I realized I hadn’t eaten anything since the
previous day. I opened the refrigerator. Half a jar of mustard. Two
eggs. A piece of butter. Nothing else.

“Just great,” I muttered, reaching for my car keys. Maybe a little
normality would do me some good.

The drive into town took less than twenty minutes. The narrow country
road initially wound through the forest for several miles. Just beyond a
long, sweeping bend, I suddenly took my foot off the accelerator. I
didn’t even know why. There was nothing around the bend. At least…
that’s what I assumed. I had driven this road countless times over the
past six years. And yet the same question crept into my mind. “How do I
actually know there’s nothing around that bend?” I laughed nervously. Of
course I didn’t know. I could only assume. I continued driving, more
slowly than usual, until I reached the supermarket.

The store was almost empty. I wandered through the aisles, placing
bread, coffee, cheese, and a few canned goods into my shopping cart
before finally joining the line at the only open checkout. An elderly
woman was standing in front of me. She was wearing a light blue coat.
Her gray, curly hair reached just above her shoulders and barely moved
as she silently placed her groceries on the conveyor belt. All I could
see was the back of her head. Nothing more. Instinctively, my thoughts
returned to the forest.

“I can’t see her face right now.” Hardly had the thought occurred to me
before another followed with such unsettling ease that a shiver ran down
my spine. “How do I even know she has one?” I closed my eyes for a brief
moment and shook my head. “Get a grip.”

When I opened them again, the woman was still standing with her back to
me at the checkout, calmly placing her groceries on the conveyor belt
and waiting for the cashier to continue. There was nothing unusual about
her. I was almost overwhelmed with relief.

But when I blinked again, I thought I saw her face for a fraction of a
second. Except it wasn’t her face. I knew my eyes had been closed. So it
couldn’t possibly have been a real image. It existed only in that tiny
instant when I couldn’t see anything. And yet that fleeting impression
was so clear and so disturbing that my mind immediately wanted to store
it as reality.

Her eyes were sunken deep into their sockets, so deep that they almost
seemed to disappear into her skull. Where her pupils should have been,
there was nothing but absolute blackness. A blackness that reflected no
light and revealed no depth. It didn’t look as though her eyes were
blind or closed. It looked as though eyes had never been meant to exist
there in the first place.

I snapped my eyes open. All I could see was the back of her head. Gray
hair. Light blue coat. I swallowed and forced myself to breathe in
slowly and deeply. Just my imagination. Of course it was. The cashier
scanned one item after another. Somewhere behind me, someone cleared
their throat impatiently, and the muffled sound of passing vehicles
drifted in through the automatic entrance doors. The world was
functioning perfectly normally. Apparently, only my mind wasn’t. I had
to stay calm and, for God’s sake, not blink again.

But I couldn’t keep it up for long. When my eyelids closed again, the
same face appeared. The black eyes were still there. But this time, it
took me a fraction of a second to notice what was no longer there. Where
her nose and mouth should have been, smooth, pale skin stretched across
her skull. Not injured or mutilated. Rather, it looked as though nothing
else had ever existed there.

I immediately opened my eyes again. The back of her head. Just the back
of her head. I consciously began delaying my blinks. Within seconds, my
eyes were burning, but I forced them to remain open. Tears gathered
along my eyelids, slowly blurring the checkout in front of me, but I
held on. Just a little longer, I told myself. Just until she had paid.
Just…

My eyelids twitched uncontrollably. I lost the battle. When I blinked
again, the face was fully there. The black eyes stared motionlessly at
me. The featureless oval remained. But where there had been smooth skin
just a moment before, an unnaturally wide-open mouth now gaped. I
searched for a tongue, for teeth, or at least for the dark outline of a
throat. It looked less like a mouth than an opening through which you
could disappear into darkness. As though someone had simply carved out
the inside of her head.

I opened my eyes, drew in a sharp breath, and lowered my gaze to the
pale floor tiles. Not again. Please, not again. By now, my eyes were
watering so badly that the outlines of the tiles were blending together.
I knew that sooner or later, I would have to blink again. And suddenly,
I was more afraid of that than anything else.

When my eyelids finally gave in, the face was already waiting for me.
This time, it was smiling. At first, it was only a slight upward
movement of the corners of its mouth. But it didn’t stop. Slowly, they
stretched farther outward. Millimeter by millimeter. Until the smile
almost reached her ears. Tooth after tooth became visible. An endless
number of them, grotesque and exaggerated. So many that my mind
instantly recognized that a human mouth could never look like that. For
one tiny moment, I even had the impression that the teeth were moving.
Not as though they were chewing, but as though they were slowly sliding
against one another, searching for their proper places.

“Excuse me?”

I flinched so violently that I nearly knocked over my shopping cart. I
snapped my eyes open. The woman had turned around.

“You can go ahead of me,” she said. “I left my wallet in the car.”

I stared at her for several seconds. She had a completely ordinary face.
Kind eyes. A few age spots. A tired, polite smile. She looked perfectly
normal. So normal, in fact, that I almost felt ashamed of what my own
mind had turned her into during those brief moments between blinks.

I can barely remember the drive home. I remember starting the engine. I
know I must have eventually left the supermarket parking lot and taken
the country road back to the cabin. But everything in between has
blurred into one continuous gray haze. I couldn’t even tell you whether
another vehicle passed me along the way. Whether I drove past the old
gas station. Or whether I signaled left or right at the intersection.

The only thing I remember clearly is the woman’s faces. Or rather, all
the faces that weren’t hers. Over and over, my mind replayed the same
images, as though it were desperately trying to choose the correct one
from countless possibilities. First, I saw those bottomless black eyes
again, staring at me without truly seeing me. Shortly afterward, that
smooth, featureless oval reappeared, looking disturbingly more human
than anything else. Then came the mouth that opened far too wide, its
darkness seeming deeper than any shadow between the pine trees. The
teeth… And every time, the sequence ended with her real face. With her
kind eyes. With her tired smile. With her gentle voice. I had convinced
myself. I knew she had a perfectly normal face. So why couldn’t my brain
stop showing me all the others?

I’m certain it wasn’t about that woman. She had simply happened to be
there. Anyone else could have been standing in her place. It was the
same question as in the forest. Always the same one. If I couldn’t see
something, how many possibilities would my mind create before I could
finally recognize the truth? And how was I supposed to know that the
image my mind eventually settled on was actually the truth?

When the tires finally rolled onto the familiar gravel driveway leading
to my cabin and came to a stop, I simply sat motionless behind the
steering wheel, staring at the same dark spot between the trees, just as
I had done over the previous days. How much longer was this going to
continue? For days, every single thought had revolved around that one
question. And every attempt to answer it had only led me to another. The
flashlight. The trail camera. The binoculars. All of them had merely
shown me that there was always another doubt. Always another thought
that I couldn’t completely disprove. I felt a profound exhaustion wash
over me. Not the tiredness that follows a long day of work. It was the
exhaustion of a man whose own mind had been fighting against him without
pause for days.

Eventually, I got out of the car. Only later did I remember that the
grocery bags were still sitting on the passenger seat. I had completely
forgotten about them. In the past, something like that would have
annoyed me. This time, I felt nothing. I unlocked the front door and
mechanically walked over to the dining table. The laptop was still
sitting there, and for a moment, I stood motionless in front of it. The
screen had long since gone dark, but I knew exactly what was on it.
Hundreds of pictures. And not a single answer. I tucked the laptop
between my arm and my chest, grabbed a bottle of whiskey and a glass,
and went out onto the porch. By now, the sun had set. The forest
stretched out before me once again. Silent. Dark. Familiar. I poured
myself a generous measure, placed the glass beside the laptop, and
slowly let my gaze wander along the tree line. It no longer had to
search for the way. It found it on its own. Between the two pine trees,
where everything had begun.

I sat there for a long time. I don’t remember how many minutes or hours
passed. All I remember is the feeling that eventually crept over me: I
was waiting for something. Not for movement or a sound. I was waiting
for my mind to finally stop asking me the same questions. But it didn’t.
It asked them again and again and again. Until only one thought
remained…

Maybe I would never be able to sit peacefully on this porch again.
Maybe, with a single question, I had forever lost the only place where I
had found peace after my wife’s death. I closed my eyes. When I opened
them again, the darkness still lay motionless between the two pine
trees. Maybe there really was nothing there. Maybe my mind had turned an
insignificant question into an obsession that had now spread into every
corner of my life. Maybe. But as long as I remained here on my porch,
that “maybe” would never disappear.

So if you ever find yourself sitting on your patio on a mild summer
evening, or looking out of a window into a dark garden, perhaps you’ll
remember my story. Perhaps your gaze will linger for a moment on the
darkest spot. Perhaps you’ll ask yourself the same question I did. Not
whether there’s something out there. But whether you could ever say with
absolute certainty that there isn’t. For your sake, I hope you forget
that thought just as quickly as I originally believed I could forget
mine. Because some thoughts don’t disappear. They simply wait patiently
for you to encounter them a second time. And once they’ve taken root,
they grow a little further with every sleepless night.

As for me, I’m going to stop searching for an answer that can be found
from my porch. I can’t keep sitting here, staring at the space between
two pine trees, hoping that an answer will eventually reveal itself.
Either there’s nothing waiting for me there. Or there’s something I will
never be able to recognize from here. There’s only one way to find out
which. If I ever want to see this place the way I once did, I have to go
between those two pine trees. Not tomorrow. Not next week. Tonight. So
I’m going to get my flashlight now. My father’s old hunting rifle is
still in the gun cabinet. Whether I’ll need it, I don’t know. Probably
not. But after every other attempt has failed, I at least don’t want to
be completely unprepared. Maybe tomorrow I’ll laugh at myself. Maybe
I’ll return with the certainty that all of this was nothing more than
the delusion of a lonely man who had spent too much time alone with his
thoughts. That would actually be the explanation I’d prefer. Because the
other possibility… is the one that keeps drawing me back to this porch.
And I don’t think I can spend another summer evening wondering whether
I’ve been staring into the darkness all this time… or whether the
darkness has been staring back at me.
