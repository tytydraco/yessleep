I inherited my uncle’s remote cabin after he passed away last spring. He
was a recluse, and honestly I barely knew him. The cabin itself
is…rustic, to put it mildly. Think questionable plumbing and a distinct
smell of damp earth. But what really drew my attention was the old mine
shaft about a hundred yards behind the property. It’s been boarded up
for decades, apparently. Uncle Elias was always a bit superstitious
about it, muttering about ‘voices’ and ‘bad luck’ whenever it came up.

Last week, a storm blew through and a large section of the plywood
covering the shaft broke free. Curiosity, a trait my uncle probably
would have despised, got the better of me. Armed with a flashlight and a
healthy dose of unease, I ventured closer. The opening was dark and
smelled like stale, cold air. As I shone my light down, I heard it. A
faint, almost musical humming. It wasn’t wind. It wasn’t the creaks of
the old cabin. It sounded like… singing.

At first I thought it was the storm playing tricks on my ears, but as I
focused, the melody became clearer. It was melancholic, beautiful even,
but laced with a profound sadness. It felt ancient. And then, mixed with
the humming, I heard whispers. Not words I could distinguish, but
distinct, low murmurs that seemed to come from deep within the earth.
They weren’t threatening, more like a sorrowful conversation. I stayed
there for a good half hour, listening, my heart pounding. I’ve never
felt so utterly alone yet so intensely observed.

The next few nights were rough. Sleep offered little escape. I’d wake up
in a cold sweat, the humming and whispering echoing in my mind. I
started seeing things in my peripheral vision, fleeting shadows that
danced at the edge of my sight. The woods around the cabin, which had
felt peaceful, now felt watchful. I tried to convince myself it was just
the isolation and my own overactive imagination, but the sounds
persisted, always strongest when I was near the mine.

Yesterday, I made a mistake. I decided to go back. This time, I brought
a coil of rope and a helmet. I lowered myself about twenty feet down.
The air grew heavier, colder. The humming was much louder now, and the
whispers felt like they were brushing against my skin. It was like being
in a vast, hollow space filled with sorrowful ghosts. Then, as I swung
my flashlight beam around, I saw them. Carved into the damp rock walls,
hundreds, maybe thousands of them. They weren’t words or symbols I
recognized. They looked like distorted faces, crying, screaming, their
mouths agape. And as I looked at them, one of the whispers became clear.
A single word, repeated over and over: “Lost.” I scrambled back up the
rope, my hands raw, my mind reeling. I don’t know what my uncle was
hiding from or what he was trying to protect us from. I just know I
can’t stay here anymore. The mine is calling, and I’m terrified of what
happens if I don’t answer.
