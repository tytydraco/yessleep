Log 1

I’ve had a bit to process my trip, but every conclusion I attempt to
draw falls apart almost immediately when trying to answer the very first
question. Why? Why the hell did my grandpa send me these directions? Why
did I follow them?

The state did briefly try to track my grandfather down right after
Grandma died, but they came up empty-handed, telling us some story about
how often older folks would willingly skip town once their spouse
passes. My grandparents always loved camping, too, taking week-long
trips to national parks at least once a year. The state was more than
happy to use that as further fuel for their fire. The family did take
matters into their own hands and spent a while knocking around, checking
phonebooks, and searching his name online, but we never even found a
trace of him. It got to the point where no one had seen or heard from
him in years, so we dropped it all and kept on with life. He never
talked or showed much interest in family anyway, and as much as it sucks
to say, his occasional silent presence at a Christmas party or
Thanksgiving dinner never really had a lasting impact on anyone.

One previously peaceful, normal day, though, that unmarked mess of paper
appeared in my mailbox. I try not to think about the unsettling,
unconventional logistics of getting that letter delivered, but I know in
the back of my head that there was no mistake. That letter was meant for
me, a grandson who barely remembered what Grandpa looked like, much less
felt any real attachment to him, and it led me right to a battered,
broken, and inhospitable cabin full of what looked like his stuff.
Looking back at the last twenty years only emphasizes how out of
character it would have been for Grandpa to reach out now, alone.

I keep looking at the original letter and wondering, possibly most
confusingly of all, why did I so readily listen to these incoherent
scribbles I’ve been calling directions? Thinking back, I can’t even
remember how I deciphered the list and put it together. I’ve held up the
original letter right next to my translation repeatedly, and I can’t
reproduce the words I originally pulled from the mess no matter how long
I stare. Comparing the two sheets of paper only makes Grandpa’s look
more illegible.

If it’s not already obvious, ever since I’ve gotten home all I can think
about is that trip to the Ozarks; my brain has been running laps around
the windy, repeating roads making up that proto-neighborhood hidden away
in those hills. I was barely there for more than an hour, but I can
perfectly picture every single street and its quirks on command. It even
seems like every time I close my eyes to sleep I’m sent back there,
running in circles, unfamiliar, heavy hooves reverberating off the
pavement just inches behind me. I always try to look back to identify
the source, but that’s when I wake up in a sweat-covered haze with that
god awful growling branded directly into the inner part of my eardrums.
My throat has been sore too, making it painful to speak above a raspy
whisper.

I haven’t tried to go back to the lake, but something’s been gnawing at
me, tempting me to return, and it’s not just the couple thousand dollars
of stuff I lost in that basement. There’s something else back there too,
though. I can’t quite describe the exact feeling. It’s almost like an
entire piece of me was knocked off with my pack, and I haven’t been able
to properly organize my thoughts without that missing piece. I think I’m
worried that whatever I encountered back there– and whatever’s in my
nightmares– will get to the piece before I can will it to return.

I’ve been trying everything I can to forget about that night or even
rationalize it as a startled animal defending its home. I got into
meditation, or rather tried to get into it, but I can’t shake the
tempting whispers creeping their way through the attempts at inner
emptiness. My therapist gave me a handful of grounding techniques for
general work-related stress and anxiety. I couldn’t tell her the real
story without expecting to end up in a psych ward. The grounding
techniques sometimes calm me down enough to fall asleep at night, but
they don’t repel the constant thoughts luring me back to the hills. I
even tried a session of EMDR with a highly rated specialist I was
referred to, but everything felt too tangled and unstable to work
through effectively, at least in a single session. After I saw the bill,
though, I canceled the EMDR pretty quickly.

I wanted and still want to read the journal myself. It’s the only thing
I made it back with, but my brain just doesn’t let me open past the
cover. I’ve tried and tried, but the cover won’t so much as budge. I get
terrified even holding it now. I feel like as soon as I read that
journal I’ll walk through my bathroom door and end up back in the cabin,
face-to-face with that giant bear, lion, deer, or whatever tried to get
to me that night.

The book was sitting on my bookshelf for a day or two as I tried to go
back and make attempts to read, but I just couldn’t. Every time I held
that book I couldn’t shake the sense of dread and anxiety that
overpowered my brain. I felt watched whenever I saw the book. It got to
a point where just leaving the book out in the open was unbearably
unsettling, so it’s been locked away upstairs for about a week now. Even
the thick walls of the gun safe can’t keep it out. Now I don’t need to
see anything to feel it. That book, with its dumb golden embroidery, is
still stuck in my head, trading off with the dark cold dreams of the
chase through the isolated streets tucked into one of thousands of hills
surrounding the lake.

Going out in public ever since I’ve gotten back home has been
significantly more challenging, too. I can’t focus on anything without
that damn journal and my constant, worsening nightmares forcing
themselves into my waking mind. I tried going into work at the treatment
plant last Monday thinking it would help me return to normal, but making
it there and remaining even slightly composed took a herculean mental
effort, nearly pushing me to tears. When I did finally get in, my boss
took one look at me and sent me home, telling me I looked like total
shit and needed to see “a doctor or priest or something” before he would
let me anywhere near the treatment equipment. I was a liability, and the
realization hurt for only a minute before the thought of using my newly
found time off to decipher the journal overpowered my brain.

I caught myself looking over my shoulder more times than I could count
during my brief stint in the city that Monday. Everyone I passed by on
the street or sat next to on the train seemed to stare at me, their eyes
full of concern and fear. No one would ever actually say anything about
it, they just kept staring like they were just waiting for a chance to
ambush me. I swear I even felt the birds and squirrels were staring at
me as I stumbled by. Feeling alone became an increasingly infrequent
privilege I realized I soon may never get again. None of this was going
to stop until I knew more. I needed closure. I needed my piece of myself
back. I needed to know what was in that book.

My whole family believes strongly that blood runs thicker than water,
and they saw what Grandpa did as a full-body blood donation. Ever since
I first asked Mom about the letter she’s become uncharacteristically
reclusive, bitter, and depressed. Our daily check-ins started to spread
out and, with all my recent digging, it seems like I’m on a fast track
to make our relationship worse. The strain felt worth it or even needed
if I was going to get answers.

Chasing those answers, I tried calling Mom a few times one day after a
bit of argument over my unannounced trip down to the Ozarks. She was
worried I’d end up just like her father, alone and untraceable. To her,
taking the chance wasn’t even worth it for someone who never cared about
us to begin with. I’m not so sure he was that callous, though. The
entire trip and surrounding conversation left me with doubts regarding
the reason Grandpa acted the way he did; honestly, it felt like he was
hiding something from everyone in the family. That broken down cabin was
barely standing—it wasn’t a home. It felt like we were all missing a
part of his story he kept buried on purpose.

Each time I called, Mom’s phone rang a couple of times before the
robotic default voicemail message picked up. She was manually declining
my calls. I wasn’t blocked yet, though, and that felt like a little win.
After enough attempts, I settled on sending a few text messages
apologizing again for making the trip alone, asking to get lunch
sometime soon, and attaching a picture of the journal to see if she
recognized it.

I didn’t hear back from Mom, at least not over the next day. She doesn’t
have read receipts on either, so I couldn’t even tell if she saw my
messages. It was depressing, and I wanted to be more concerned, but
frankly, I was obsessed about the potential contents of the journal. I
wanted myself back. It was sad hitting a wall and turning up empty
handed, but I didn’t press my mom further over the phone.

Not even an hour later I got a call, not from Mom, but just as
promising. It felt like either divine or unholy intervention, I’m still
not sure which, when Mom’s sister, aunt Eve, was on the other end of the
line.

Eve was Mom’s much younger sister who always ended up babysitting me as
I grew up, and we were very close as a result. Sure, she needed the
money Mom paid her, but Eve and I just had a ton of fun surfing the
frontiers of the still-emerging web. Our house had one of the only
computers in the whole family at the time, a symptom of Dad’s
photojournalism work. We would spend hours just trying to find new pages
with secrets to explore. She even spearheaded the initial online search
effort for Grandpa back when we were still looking.

Our favorite site lined up perfectly with our little mischievous brains.
The site consisted of a black screen with a picture of a door in the
middle, silent almost all the time. Occasionally it would play a little
sound bite of someone knocking on a door. We would open the site, hide
it as well as possible, and I would spend the evenings just watching Mom
and Dad walk back and forth from the front door, trying to contain my
laughter. They thought we were just getting ding-donged-ditched, and it
was all in good fun. Eventually, though, the cops showed up at the house
to take a complaint, so Eve made us stop that prank.

The call was quick, both of us straight to the point. Eve was flying
into town tomorrow for a work conference. Wanting to keep everything
pretty low-key since she hasn’t flown back in a while and would only be
here for a day, Eve avoided announcing her arrival to everyone, but she
was still “nosy and interested.” She wanted to get dinner with me and
catch up on how everyone in the family has been for the last couple
months. Of course I agreed (as long as she paid,) and, unable to keep
excitement contained, told her I found something that might belong to
Grandpa I wanted to show her. Eagerly, we set up a time for dinner
immediately the following day.

I was so excited after that call. I don’t remember doing anything the
rest of that evening except for staring at my old wall clock,
internalizing each second as it passed. Every soft click brought me
closer to answers, closer to bringing everyone back together. All night,
I sat there, staring at the clock. Staring as the exhaustion set in, my
eyelids got too heavy, and the all too familiar chase from my nightmares
filled my increasingly unoccupied head.

Most of the next day went by pretty quickly. I didn’t wake up until
early-afternoon, grateful for my weekend alarms for the first time in
recent memory. I used to have a bit of a narcolepsy problem, and the
constant alarms were essential until I got medicated and worked on good
habits with my doctor. Those same habits had been thrown out the window
ever since the Ozarks. I wasn’t too concerned, though, I was about to
get back on track right after Eve helped me look through the journal. A
few favorites from X-files filled the silence of my little duplex living
room until it was time for dinner. I opened the safe, took an
uncomfortable minute to shove the journal into a small brown bag, and I
headed out the door.

We were planning on meeting at a local burger joint just up the street
from me at 6:00 that evening. I got there a little early, I think around
5:40-5:45, so I could wait for Eve outside to meet up with and go in
together. If we went in together I wouldn’t have to stumble awkwardly
through a conversation with the host. By 6:15, though, I hadn’t seen Eve
yet and my handful of calls and texts went unanswered, so I toughened up
to go get a table for us before the small diner filled up with folks
hungry for dinner.

I made the long walk across the parking lot and nodded a quick ‘Thank
you’ to the little boy holding the restaurant door open. I slipped past
him and his bright-eyed, proud parents and headed inside. The entryway
was crowded with a large group waiting for a table big enough to seat
them all, and I had to weave through them to get to the front desk. I
took a few deep breaths, ready to try my hardest to speak audibly
through all the idle chatter filling the diner, but, saved as soon as I
reached the desk, I saw Eve waving at me from a table she had already
gotten for us. It was a little weird that I never saw her show up. Eve
was never early to anywhere, but I hadn’t seen her in months and
promised a show on top of that, so maybe she made a special exception.

We exchanged a quick hug before sitting down at the table. A waiter came
over to get our drink orders and we both ordered our food right on the
spot, too. We have been coming to this diner since we were kids. We
didn’t need to see the menu. Eve immediately got real into this rant
about the workplace drama back home. They were hiring a new COO or
something and he was making a bunch of bad changes I guess. Honestly, I
don’t remember her part of the story well. She caught me up on her
Seattle tech-consultant life for a bit, and I shared the same-old news
about family holiday parties and get-togethers we’ve been having since
she was little. There was a little drama to share, one cousin got a
divorce, a great-aunt had passed in her sleep, and there were like ten
new cousins, nieces, and nephews she’ll have to meet sometime soon. She
took it all in especially wide-eyed and focused, I didn’t remember her
caring too much about extended family.

Eve didn’t even pause to transition or take a bite before asking, “So
spill it. What did you find in that crazy old man’s journal?”

I looked at her, confused. “Whoa, did some new X-ray eyes come out or
something up there in Seattle? All I got is a paper bag.”

Her eyes drifted away for a split second as she thought of an answer.
“Oh, I saw what you came in with and made a lucky, educated guess.” She
smirked sheepishly. It was a weird jump to make, but I didn’t press
further. Eve was always the brightest bulb on the long, weathered light
strand making up our family.

I didn’t know how to easily introduce the story, so I just dove in, “I
got a letter the other day. The envelope was so old it looked ready to
rot off, and, get this, there was no return address. It only said ‘To
Eric From Grandpa,’” I paused for a second, waiting for a reaction, but
Eve only nodded, ready to keep listening. “It took me a bit but
eventually I found a page with a bunch of scribbles, and I sent them to
Mom. She wasn’t really interested in it all, actually she’s pretty
pissed I’m doing this, but she did send me a uh, sample, I guess, of
Grandpa’s handwriting. The scribbles I got were illegible, but seeing
that example flipped a switch in my brain or something, and I was able
to piece together a step-by-step set of instructions to a location deep
in the Ozarks.”

She finished for me. “So you followed them and found the journal.” There
was no time for me to confirm, or elaborate on my trip before she asked
to see the book and what I had read. I tried to explain my mental block,
but I couldn’t make sense of it myself. I still can’t. I slid the bag
across the table, settling on a simple “I don’t know. I can’t read it”

Eve took the journal out of the bag and turned it over in her hands a
few times, awestruck. “It’s in perfect condition.” Just like that, she
opened the ten-ton cover I’d been struggling with all this time and let
out a quick chuckle. “I see why you can’t read it. These words are so
slanted and broken I can barely tell it’s English.” She paused for a
moment to think before continuing, “I can take it back home with me and
bring it to a transcriber I know. He works for doctors, so he’s seen
worse than this I’m sure.”

I don’t know why, but I didn’t want to lose the journal or even let it
leave my possession for more than a few minutes. “Actually Mom wanted me
to give her the journal so she could lock it away with the rest of his
stuff that got left behind. She doesn’t want bad memories floating
around. I told her I was going to bring it by after dinner,” I lied.
“Will some pictures work? At least for the first few pages?”

Eve deflated at the idea, but she didn’t fight back, sullenly taking out
her phone to grab the first few pages of the book before sliding it back
across the table to me. Right after she was done, Eve handed me a few
twenty dollar bills for dinner and said she really had to get back to
the hotel and rest up for the conference. She would let me know what the
pages said when she heard back from her friend. I barely had time to
blink before she was past me and out the door, somewhere among the sea
of people waiting for a table to open.

Alone and more than a little upset she couldn’t translate the pages on
the spot, I quietly finished up my burger and left the money on the
table with the bill. Stepping out into the cool night air was a
refreshing feeling after sitting in the alarmingly packed shack of a
diner. I made my way toward my car at the back of the parking lot, well
out of sight and earshot of the businesses surrounding the diner. I
usually liked the walk to get a chance for fresh air. Something was off,
though, I felt it as soon as I went out the diner door. A quick cursory
glance didn’t reveal anything, so I kept walking. I was at my car door
when there was a flicker in the overhead light near my car, filling me
with a deep sense of unease. I barely had half a second to register the
giant blob of flesh before it slammed into my forehead. I hit the cold
pavement and blacked out.

When I came to a bit later, the throbbing pain in my head immediately
brought the memory of the ambush back, and I felt for my phone and keys
after checking my head for blood that wasn’t there. This wasn’t the
first time I’d been mugged, but usually the perpetrator likes to demand
my stuff before resorting right to blows. I felt my left pocket. My
phone was still there, in near-mint condition too. Right pocket next.
The wallet was still there, my leg was a little numb from the cold, but
I’m pretty sure I felt the keys poke into my thigh, and the shadow of my
beaten-up sedan still loomed over me. It felt like some freak accident.
Maybe a bird flew into me or some kids were driving by throwing rocks
out of cars. It’s stupid, but I didn’t know what else to think. Feeling
lucky, I slowly got to my feet and got my bearings, but the luck
vanished almost instantly once I realized what was gone. The journal was
no longer in the bag. In its place was a piece of paper stained dark
red, wrapped around a small object. The streetlight flickered back on,
slowly revealing two sloppy words written on the wet page as I pulled it
from the bag.

Keep Knocking.

My eye caught the glimmer of an engraved ring hugging the finger as I
flinched back, dropping the finger in disgust back into the bag. I knew
that ring. Eve had worn it religiously ever since our great-grandmother
passed it down to her. It was hers.

Flustered and panicking, I looked all around the parking lot, but it was
empty, not even a car on the street in front of the diner. It seemed
darker out too. How long had I been out? Frantically I scanned and ran
around the lot. I looked as long as I could before feeling too exposed.
Defeated and terrified, I got back into my car and sped the few blocks
home. I took the bag and stumbled as I ran up to my garage door and
messed the code up three times before I was able to steady enough to
open the door and rush in, immediately locking it behind me.

I have tried to call Eve over and over, but there was never even a ring
before the increasingly depressing voicemail beep filled the other line.
I’m at a loss. Eve felt like the one person I could talk to about all of
this, and I just shoved one of her fingers into my freezer. Whatever
took my brain took Eve, and it took the journal. Another piece of me is
missing.

I was still leaning against the door desperately trying to think of
anything I could do when my phone vibrated in my pocket. It was a text
from a number I didn’t recognize, and there were four pictures. Two
pictures were of pages in the journal, taken back in the parking lot of
the diner, using my unconscious body as a backdrop. Then, two pictures
of typed text. Transcriptions.

This first one looked like it was on the inside of the cover. I’ve
included it below:

I don’t know what we saw out there or how it found us back home, but it
did, and I’m the only one left.

Carol, desperate “to have the old me back,” and running out of ideas,
gave me this journal today. She said that putting my thoughts to paper
will help her, and everyone else, finally understand what’s been going
on with me. Obviously I agreed, but I’m doing this for more than just
understanding; I don’t even know how to explain what I myself don’t
understand. I’m using this as a warning.

Stay far away from Ozark Falls.

God how I wish that was on the other side of the cover, or the spine, or
literally anywhere else I could see. I should have listened to Mom, but
I don’t think that’s a choice anymore.

The second picture was a transcription of a page opened to somewhere in
the first third of the journal, also included:

September 12th, 1986

I was on the line at the litter factory for the night, surrounded by a
haze of dust that worked to clog both of my nostrils and eyes as densely
as possible. The job offer seemed too good to be true, and I quickly
learned why after hearing about the turnover rate and physical state of
the people that left, or were forced to resign, before me. Turns out
breathing dust twelve hours a day doesn’t bode well for your life
expectancy. I don’t know who the hell thought an open-air litter factory
was a good idea in the first place. They gave us a mask and goggles that
supposedly mitigated the accumulation, but my dark gray-stained
handkerchief argued otherwise. The pay was good, though, and we needed
the money to fend off the foreclosure the bank was fighting for.

Anyway, I was on the line for the night, and Carl, the guy at the boxing
station next to me, came over during the first break. Carl and I got
hired at the same time, went through training together, and we
occasionally made small talk, but that’s usually as far as it goes. He
was an alright guy, if not a little eccentric. I worked on recalling the
evening news in my head, prepared to discuss the awful drought we’ve
been in for a few weeks now. This time wasn’t small talk, though. As the
recently-made only one capable of working in his family, he needed money
as bad as I did, and he was scheming, I could see it in his wide,
shining eyes.

Carl’s composure barely held on his way over to me, and an ocean of
words poured out of his mouth as soon as he was in earshot. He was all
excited and stuff, talking so fast I lost track of how many times I had
to stop him and ask to slow it down. Eventually, I got it out of him.

He had an uncle who worked for a GPS startup, analyzing cutting-edge
satellite imagery to identify new roads, closures, and other changes.
During a routine scan of the Ozarks to check for road erosion, his uncle
had found “something big.” I hadn’t ever been down that way, but I heard
stories about people going down and opening tourist traps for all the
rich people that constantly visit the thousands of lake houses and
condos surrounding the lake, so my interest piqued.

Carl said there was a network of roads, just a few miles out from a town
called Camdenton. The land was owned by some rich old dude that passed
away with no family, so it all went to the government. It’s been years
now, and the foundation the old guy built was still just sitting there,
untouched, ever since the government got its hands on the land.

His uncle gave a couple of grainy pictures to Carl who pulled them out
of his jacket pocket and showed them to me. Even through the poor
quality it was clear that the old man was trying to build a sorta
suburbia-adjacent neighborhood right into the dense forest and rocky
hills, but it looked like the work just stopped after the roads were
outlined and paved. Carl had a hunch and checked through old obituaries
at the library. Apparently the old man that owned the land, Charles
something, caught pneumonia in the middle of his project and croaked one
night alone in his house, leaving his project to be reclaimed by the
lake.

Both Carl and his uncle thought we could get our hands on the land for
super cheap, like manifest destiny cheap, due to its age and a home
construction initiative the government was subsidizing in hopes of
getting more people, and more money, down to the Ozarks. We just needed
to check out the land ourselves to make sure it lined up with the
slightly dated image from the satellite.

Carl’s uncle would work on the paperwork side of things if we were the
boots on the ground. I was skeptical of the price for sure, but anything
sounded better than this factory. Price aside, it all seemed normal at
the time. An old man with no family died in the middle of a project, and
the government was never too keen on building down in the Ozarks
themselves. The spot was only a few hours’ drive, so Carl and I planned
a little day trip on our day off. He had some camping equipment and food
he would bring in the case of an emergency if I would be the one to
drive us down there. I was only a little hesitant at first, so it didn’t
take a lot to get me to cave.

I’m missing the greater connection here, and I’m desperate. I’m
terrified. The two words from that note keep echoing in my head.

Keep Knocking

At this point, for all I know, Eve is in serious danger, and I’ll take
help wherever I can get it. I’m at the end of my rope*.* I guess I want
to cast a sort of wide net by probing the internet for anything similar
that could guide me. I’m also going over to my mom’s hoping she is
willing to help me find Eve, and my journal.
