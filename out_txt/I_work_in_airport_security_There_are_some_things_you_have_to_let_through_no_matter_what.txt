I’ve been working as a Transportation Security Officer in a small local
airport for about five years now.

Or rather, was working as one.

I worked the graveyard shift, when things start to get quiet in the
early hours of the day which you couldn’t consider morning.

The job is uneventful. You spend most of your time sitting in front of a
screen looking at baggage flashing different colors at you.
Occasionally, a suitcase will be flagged for having too big a can of
deodorant, or too big a tub of yogurt. The worst I’ve gotten is a pocket
knife. Other than that, not too much activity.

But there is one exception.

I still remember when I was going through my job training with my
supervisor, going through screening procedures, threat detection and
basic passenger interaction skills. I learned how to operate x-ray
imaging. Orange is for foods, liquids, and clothes. Blue is for dense
metals like steel, iron and brass. Green is for aluminum, glass,
plastic, and electronics. Black or dark red indicated objects x-rays
cannot penetrate.

He watched me as I screened for the first time. I was laser focused,
overanalyzing all the objects on the screen and feeling my anxiety spike
every time he jotted something down into his checklist.

Eventually, I passed and got my certification. I thanked my supervisor
and we were about to part ways, but then he pulled me back.

“I need to tell you this. It’s important. Do you have somewhere you can
write this down?”

I didn’t have any notebook with me so I pulled out my phone.

“Is there something that was missed?” I asked.

“You need to listen to me very carefully, and make sure to write this
down so that you absolutely won’t forget. Every few months, there is a
man who comes by. He’s about six feet tall, and wears a long brown coat
with a suit and tie, a black hat and blue gloves. If you come across
him, you must let him pass no matter what.”

“Why? Is he some sort of VIP?”

“…not exactly. But let him through. Do not talk. Once you see him, stop
talking right away. And absolutely do not call the police. No matter
what.”

He put a strong emphasis on “no matter what”. Resolute, absolute.

He left before I could ask any more questions.

I typed it into my notes. Six feet tall. Brown coat, suit and tie, black
hat, gloves, must let in every few months. Don’t talk, don’t call
police.

I pocketed my phone and drove back home, thinking that this was a weird
piece of training information. I didn’t know what “not exactly a VIP”
was, but I just let it go, thinking it must be someone of relative
importance.

Thankfully, I didn’t have time to forget. Thankfully.

It was around 3am, and the airport was pretty empty. I was talking to
two of my coworkers, Kimberly and Jonathan, when he strolled in. Exactly
as described, with a brown coat, black hat and blue gloves, he took his
time weaving down the line, rolling his luggage behind him. He had his
face covered with a black mask. We immediately shut our mouths and
looked at each other in unison. People from the three other security
lanes had also turned to look.

It’s him.

He put his suitcase on the conveyor belt.

The first thing I noticed was the smell. It reeked of bleach. I wanted
to pinch my nose but I didn’t want to seem rude in front of someone who
might be of high status.

Kimberly waved him through the metal detector. He passed without any
issues.

But on my end, there were issues that were more than minor.

The screen flashed dark blue and black at me, in the distinct shape of 2
axes and 5 firearms, laid out over a block of orange. Stacks of money.

I let it through.

Just what could he be doing to need to fly with these things? And why
are we endorsing this?

The man grabbed his bag and walked towards the gates.

I had a lot of questions, none that could be answered within the three
of us alone. After he was long gone, my coworkers and I huddled to talk
about him but there was not much to say. They only knew as much as I did
and received the same instruction as I had.

Eventually, it became a normal thing over the years. Every few months he
would stop by either my security check or a neighboring one, always some
time after midnight, and we would let him through and go on with our
days. Nobody really talks about it or questions it, so I figured I
shouldn’t be too worried either.

Most of the time, he stores some kind of weapon and money in his
suitcase. On rare occasions, even some unexplainable liquids held in
seal tight cans. Every time he comes around, his suitcase reeks of
bleach, always leaving a lingering smell in the air even after he
leaves. He always wears the same clothes every time too.

But the last time he showed up was different.

Around 2:45am three months ago, he came in like usual. He put his
suitcase on the belt. We let him through security.

I was expecting the usual firearm and money combo.

But this time, the screen blared orange and green back at me.

A dead body.

The worst part was, it didn’t seem like the body was shoved in there
forcefully. Rather, it was the opposite.

It was folded neatly.

Like someone abnormally strong had taken the body, folded it in a zigzag
fashion, breaking through the bones, and then fit it into the suitcase.

Blood drained from my face. I let it pass through. I felt sick to my
stomach. My hands shook.

Jonathan noticed first. After the man left, he asked me what was wrong.
I told him and Kimberly about the body. They seemed just as unnerved as
I was, and shared a look of uneasiness with each other. They reassured
me and told me everything was going to be fine.

I couldn’t sleep that night. Someone had been killed and put into a
suitcase. And I couldn’t do anything about it even though I saw it with
my own eyes.

It took me a month to recover from it with Kimberly’s and Jonathan’s
support. Come to think of it, I probably wouldn’t have pulled through
without them.

However, the relief didn’t last long.

Two months ago, there was a new screening officer, Annabelle, who was
put in a neighboring security lane to mine. We instantly clicked and
became good friends, bonding over screening stories and work
experiences. It helped take my mind off of the man. I didn’t talk about
him. I assumed that she knew and would follow the necessary steps if he
appeared.

That was my biggest mistake.

He showed up again a week ago. At her lane.

Everyone went dead silent, including her. They stared as he approached.

She screened his suitcase and her face went pale. Exactly how I must’ve
looked when I saw that dead body.

She let the luggage pass. He took it and turned to leave.

I breathed a sign of relief. She knew what to do.

I started to turn towards Kimberly when I heard a beeping noise.

Annabelle had reached for her radio.

The man stopped and turned towards her.

She froze.

The two of them looked at each other for a while.

Then, I blinked and he was suddenly next to her in a flash, like he
teleported.

“I get offended easily, you know.”

Poof.

His long brown coat morphed and expanded into a dome, engulfing the two
of them.

Annabelle’s screams pierced through the air, and then the sound of her
gurgling and choking. The sound of her struggling for air.

It seemed to go on forever.

I stared at the dome with a sense of helplessness. My stomach dropped.
The world went quiet. I stood there, in silence, as she was dying.
Frozen, unable to do anything.

After a long while I heard the sound of bones snapping, crackling like
popcorn in a microwave, and then finally, complete silence. I heard
sounds of something wet splatting onto the ground, and then a loud
thump.

With another poof his coat was back to normal.

When it shrunk, it seemed to take Annabelle with it. She had completely
disappeared, and there was no physical evidence to back the horrible
sounds that I had just heard. The surrounding area was completely clean.

The man went to collect his luggage and continued to his gate as if
nothing had happened. 

I sat there for another five minutes to process what I heard. Sounds so
obscene I could only imagine what he had done to her. I felt sick.

My coworkers and I didn’t talk to each other for the rest of our shift.

But this wasn’t what made me quit. It was what happened the day after.

It was unusual for him to show up for two days in a row. After what he
did, the last thing anyone wanted was for him to show up again so soon.

He came to my lane. We did the usual.

His suitcase rolled through my screen.

I felt like throwing up.

There, glowing in orange in the center of an otherwise empty suitcase
was a severed head.

Annabelle stared back at me, her face twisted and mouth open wide, like
someone had frozen her mid-scream.

I stared as the image rolled out of view. I could feel my mind
dissociating from my body. Grief filled every cell in my body.

As the man took his luggage, he looked at me briefly before turning
away. I could swear his brown eyes briefly flashed yellow. They were
squinted. Even though I couldn’t see his full expression, I could tell
he was sneering under that mask.

I resigned as soon as I could.

I still see his eyes in my dreams. Mocking. Teasing. Other times, I
dream about Annabelle’s severed head, screaming at me. It took me a long
time to recover from those sleepless nights.

Annabelle’s family thinks she quit and moved away. I couldn’t bring up
enough courage to tell them the truth. They would’ve thought I was
insane anyways.

Whatever you do, don’t work the graveyard shift for airport security.
You will lose your friends, and you definitely don’t get paid enough for
what you’re up against.

But if you do, don’t pick up your radio. No matter what.
