I was the only person who survived the crash.

There were forty-three people on the train when it came off the tracks.
Forty-two died and I didn’t. I still don’t know why.

People have asked me that enough times that I’ve started wondering what
answer they expect.

I wasn’t awake for the part where somebody apparently decided.

The families asked different questions. In the first week they wanted to
know if I remembered their husband or daughter or mum. Whether they’d
been awake after the impact. Whether they’d said anything. Whether they
were scared.

I could answer almost none of it.

The last thing I remember is a woman across the aisle peeling the label
off a bottle of water. She’d got most of it off in one piece and was
rolling it between her fingers. There was a kid further down playing
something on a Nintendo Switch without headphones, and a man two rows
ahead of me kept clearing his throat every twenty seconds or so. I also
remember a girl in a uni hoodie asked nobody in particular whether
someone could turn the air-conditioning down, because it was freezing.

People had died three metres away from me and I couldn’t tell their
families anything that seemed worth surviving to remember.

Nobody asks me what I remember anymore. The questions I get now are ones
they actually expect me to answer.

I woke up in hospital with two broken ribs, a fractured wrist and so
many messages on my phone it stopped showing me the number. My sister
dealt with most of it while I was in there: reporters, police, the rail
company, people I hadn’t spoken to since school sending enormous
paragraphs that started with I know we haven’t talked in forever but…
She cleared out my voicemail too, because it was full.

The first voicemail came four days after I got home.

I nearly deleted it because the number wasn’t saved, and I’d started
ignoring anything I didn’t recognise after journalists somehow got hold
of my mobile. I only listened because the transcript underneath it said:

can somebody move my arm

The voice belonged to a man. He sounded confused more than frightened.

There was a lot of noise behind him, mostly metal scraping and people
talking somewhere further away. He said hello twice, then asked if
someone could move his arm because it was underneath him and he couldn’t
feel his fingers.

Then he said, “Is that Ben?”

There was a pause.

“Ben, mate, can you hear me?”

The message ended.

I listened again. It sounded like something recorded inside the
wreckage. My phone said it had been left the previous afternoon at 3:44,
which made no sense for a message from the crash, so I decided that must
be when the network had finally delivered it. That seemed reasonable
enough to me. Mobile towers had gone down along the line, emergency
services had talked about communication problems, and my own phone had
been found about twenty metres from me.

I rang the number back. It went to voicemail after five rings, and a
man’s voice, much more cheerful than the one I’d just heard, told me I’d
reached Will and to leave a message. I hung up before the beep.

I searched the number.

It belonged to a passenger named William. Will was thirty-eight, married
and had one sister. His funeral notice was already online, with a link
to a fundraiser his sister Rebecca had set up. She’d replied to every
single comment on it. His wife hadn’t posted anything anywhere.

I stared at Rebecca’s name for probably ten minutes before messaging
her. I almost decided against it, but I’d spent the previous week
telling people I didn’t remember anything useful. This was finally
something I could give somebody.

Rebecca rang immediately.

“Is this him?”

That was the first thing she said.

I told her I thought so.

“Send it to me.” she said instantly.

I hesitated…

“Please” she said, her voice almost cracking.

So I sent it.

She called back about twenty minutes later and asked who Ben was.

“I really don’t know.”

“William didn’t know anyone called Ben.”

“Maybe somebody from the train?”

“No. I mean—”

She stopped.

“When did you get this?”

I told her.

There was a mug on my kitchen bench with a tea bag still sitting in it
from that morning. I’d forgotten to drink it. I remember looking at it
while she spoke because I didn’t really want to concentrate on her
voice.

“They’d already found him,” she said.

I didn’t understand.

“William. They recovered him the night of the crash. He was pronounced
dead at 11:46.”

That still didn’t prove anything. The message could have been delayed.

Then Rebecca said, “They moved him yesterday.”

“Moved him where?”

“To the funeral home.”

She asked me to play the message on the phone to her.

“You’ve got it,” I said.

“I can’t. I keep stopping it when he talks. Just play it and I’ll
listen.”

This time, I listened to the voices behind William. One was too muffled
to understand, but a few seconds later a man said very clearly:

“Ben, take his side.”

Rebecca stopped me.

“Again.”

I played it again.

Ben, take his side.

“I’ll call you back.”

She didn’t call me for three hours.

When she eventually did, she skipped hello again.

“The funeral director has someone called Ben. He helped move William
yesterday afternoon.”

I told her there had to be another explanation.

“Like what?”

I didn’t have one.

The second voicemail was waiting for me when I woke up the next morning.
It was from a woman named Priya.

It was thirty-one seconds long. Most of it was her quietly saying,
“Excuse me,” like she was trying to get somebody’s attention without
being rude. Eventually she raised her voice.

“Excuse me. I think you’ve made a mistake.”

There was movement in the background, then a drawer shutting. Somebody
coughed.

A man said, “Can you put the watch with her effects?”

Priya stopped talking. The message ended a few seconds later.

I sat on my couch with the phone in my hand until the screen went black.

I didn’t contact her family straight away.

I’d told myself sending William’s voicemail to Rebecca was the right
thing because it belonged to her. Sitting there with Priya’s message, I
realised I didn’t actually know if that was true. Maybe hearing the dead
person you loved begging strangers to notice them wasn’t something you
gave somebody just because you happened to have it.

Then I thought about every family member who’d asked me what their
person’s final moments were like.

I contacted Priya’s husband.

His name was Sameer. I told him I’d received something from her number
and thought he should hear it. He asked if he could come over rather
than have me send it.

He was smaller than I expected when he arrived. I don’t know why I
expected him to be tall. He took his shoes off at the door, then
apologised for doing it even though I hadn’t asked him to.

When I played the voicemail, he listened with both hands pressed between
his knees.

Afterwards he said, “The watch.”

I asked what he meant.

“She wore my mum’s watch.”

The police had returned Priya’s belongings that morning. Phone, wallet,
earrings, wedding ring, watch, all of it in a sealed plastic bag with a
property label on it. Her phone had been flat since the crash. He’d
charged it in the car on the way over because he couldn’t stand the idea
of it being flat.

He took it out of his jacket, unlocked it and opened her recent calls.

He didn’t say anything. He just turned the screen around.

The most recent call was to my number. Yesterday, 7:41pm. Thirty-one
seconds.

“It was in the bag,” he said. “It was dead.”

We sat there for a while before he asked, “Did she sound awake to you?”

I knew what he meant and still made him ask twice.

“Did she sound awake?”

“Yes.”

Rebecca told another family. That family told somebody else. Soon people
weren’t contacting me because I was the survivor anymore. They were
contacting me because I was the person who might hear from someone
they’d lost.

A journalist rang twice that week asking whether I’d “received any
recordings.” Somebody’s cousin had talked. I didn’t call her back.

People sent me screenshots of phone numbers so I’d recognise them if
they came through. I saved every one under the person’s name. It seemed
better to know who it was before I pressed play. By the end of the week
I had thirty-four of them.

One came from a woman whose son had been eleven. She’d sent a photo
along with his number, him on a couch holding a Nintendo Switch up to
the camera. I recognised the Switch before I recognised him. His name
was Noah. After that, whenever my phone lit up, the first thing I did
was check it wasn’t him.

One man sent a voice note that was just him saying his wife’s name and
then going quiet for about ten seconds.

I muted my phone.

A few minutes later I turned the sound back on.

For two days nothing happened. Then a message came from a university
student named Chloe.

“It’s really cold,” she said.

A moment later: “Can someone turn it down?”

Something metallic rolled nearby. Somewhere behind it, further off, a
man cleared his throat.

I played it again and counted. About twenty seconds later he did it
again.

I’d sat behind that sound for forty minutes on the train.

I sent the voicemail to Chloe’s mother and spent the rest of the
afternoon feeling sick. I hadn’t thought about the fact that they were
being kept together. I hadn’t thought about whether they could hear each
other.

William called again that evening.

This message was nearly two minutes long.

“I think they think I’m dead,” he said.

He didn’t sound frightened yet. He sounded like someone explaining a
mix-up to people who weren’t listening.

“I can hear them. They talk about me like I’m not here. They keep saying
‘he.’”

For a while there was nothing except a tap running somewhere.

“I can’t move. I haven’t been able to the whole time.”

Then, much quieter: “I think they’ve sewn my mouth shut.”

I stopped the message there.

Rebecca called that night and asked if anything else had come through
from William. I told her no.

I slept better than I had in days.

The next morning I hated myself for that, listened to the rest of the
message and sent it to her. She never replied. I’m not going to write
down the rest of what he said. It was his, and then it was hers.

After that I tried making rules. Nothing after midnight. Nothing
forwarded until I’d heard it twice.

That lasted about a day.

What broke it was a folder I’d forgotten my phone had. When my sister
cleared my voicemail in hospital, she hadn’t really got rid of anything.
The messages had gone into a deleted folder at the bottom of the list,
and they’d sat there ever since, because I’d never scrolled that far.

I only went looking because of the contacts. Most of those old
voicemails had been from numbers I didn’t know, and I’d assumed they
were journalists. But I’d spent a week saving the numbers families sent
me, and now, when I opened the folder, some of them had names.

Nine of them. All left the night of the crash or the day after.

I didn’t play them. I read the transcripts instead, which turned out to
be worse, because the transcription had tried its best.

hello is someone

i can hear you i can hear you can you

its ok its ok its ok its

One was from Priya. Her transcript was two words long.

excuse me

Noah’s number wasn’t there. I checked three times.

They were the answers to what the families had asked me in the first
week. I’ve read the transcripts more times than I can count. I haven’t
played any of them, and I haven’t told anyone they exist. I haven’t
deleted them either.

Leah’s parents contacted me before I’d received anything from her.

She was twenty-six and had been sitting two carriages behind me. Her
dad, Martin, did most of the talking. Her mum mostly sat beside him
during the video call and corrected little things he said. He told me
Leah was impatient, never answered normal phone calls and communicated
almost entirely through voice notes because she said typing took too
long. Her mum rolled her eyes when he said that and told him Leah only
ignored his calls.

It was the first time one of the people whose numbers I’d saved became
someone I could imagine being annoying.

Martin asked me to promise I’d call if anything came through.

I said I would.

Three days later, Leah left a voicemail.

She was crying. “I don’t know where I am,” she said. “Why does everyone
keep touching me?”

Then she said, “Mum?”

That was it.

I called Martin.

He and his wife came to my apartment that night. I played the message
once, and Leah’s mum immediately asked me to play it again.

Martin said no.

She told him to shut up.

Not loudly.

So I played it again.

His wife covered her mouth with both hands. Martin got up and walked
into my kitchen, where he stood looking at the fridge for a while before
asking when I’d received it.

I told him.

“Have the others matched things happening to their bodies?”

“Yes.”

He didn’t need to ring anyone. The funeral home had called him that
morning to say Leah had been prepared, and that the family were welcome
to see her before the service.

Her funeral was in two days. Cremation happened afterwards.

Leah’s mum said, “No.”

Martin turned around.

She said it again. “No.”

“We don’t know what this means.”

“She said people were touching her.”

“I heard it.”

“And now you want to burn her?”

Martin looked at me.

I was angry at him for doing that. Only briefly, but I was.

“What do you think?” he asked.

“I’m not the person to ask.”

“You’re the only person this is happening to.”

“It’s not happening to me.”

He looked down immediately.

His wife was still staring at my phone.

I regretted it before he looked up again.

Martin asked what I would do if it was my sister.

I should have said I didn’t know.

Instead I said, “I wouldn’t cremate her.”

The funeral was postponed.

Leah left two more messages that week. In one, she asked somebody to
turn the light off. The other came about an hour after Martin had been
in to see her.

“I can hear Dad,” she said.

After that he stopped asking whether I thought the messages were real.

The family started fighting. Martin changed his mind every day or two
and rang me whenever he did. The worst call was at about eleven one
night. I could hear a television behind him with the sound down.

“Jen reckons we’re keeping her in a fridge because we’re cowards,” he
said. Jen was Leah’s aunt. “She’s not wrong, is she?”

I didn’t answer that.

“What about burial?”

“I don’t know.”

“You must have thought about it.”

Of course I had. Burial meant leaving her underground, still apparently
aware of whatever happened around her body. Cremation meant doing
something to her that none of us understood. And the fridge couldn’t
last forever, however badly everyone wanted a fourth choice.

I told him that last part.

“That’s not an answer.”

“I know.”

He didn’t say anything for long enough that I checked the call hadn’t
dropped.

“She slept with the hall light on until she was fifteen,” he said
eventually. “Now she wants them to turn it off.”

He hung up before I could think of anything to say to that.

They postponed it again. There were legal and practical limits to how
long it could go on, though, and eventually the cremation was
rescheduled for twelve days after it should originally have happened.

Martin called me that morning.

He didn’t ask me to do anything.

He just said, “Keep your phone near you.”

I did.

I knew what time the service started and roughly how long the rest would
take after that. I spent that part of the afternoon cleaning the oven,
which I had never done before, so I wouldn’t have to think about whether
she could feel it.

Nothing came through. Not during, not afterwards, not that evening.

I started thinking that might be good. Whatever was happening might need
there to be a body. Maybe fire ended it.

It didn’t occur to me until much later that she hadn’t said anything
while it was happening. I’ve thought since about the reasons a person
might not say anything.

I didn’t tell Martin what I was hoping. I didn’t want to give him
something based on my phone being quiet for half a day.

It stayed quiet the next day, and the one after that. On the fourth day
Martin rang to say they’d collected her from the crematorium. He sounded
like he’d slept. I told him I still hadn’t heard anything, and for the
first time since I’d met him, he said thanks and hung up without asking
me another question.

I slept properly that night.

The next morning I woke up at 6:40 and checked my phone.

Nothing.

I lay there for another ten minutes before getting up.

Then the voicemail arrived.

Leah.

I didn’t play it immediately. I went to the bathroom, brushed my teeth
and put the kettle on. I knew that was stupid, but I also knew the
moment I pressed play the morning was going to become about a dead woman
again.

Eventually I sat at the kitchen table and listened.

The message was forty-seven seconds long.

At first Leah wasn’t talking. There was just a faint rustling sound,
then she said:

“I don’t understand.”

A few seconds passed.

“I’m at home.”

Then, quieter:

“No.”

She started crying.

“I’m at Uncle Paul’s.”

“Mum?”

Behind her there was a noise I couldn’t place. A soft, uneven clicking
that stopped and started.

Then Leah said, “How can I—”

The voicemail ended.

I played it twice before calling Martin.

He answered immediately.

“Where are you?”

“At home.”

“Is your wife there?”

“Yes.”

“Where are Leah’s ashes?”

He didn’t answer.

“Martin?”

“Some are here.”

“What do you mean, some?”

His wife had wanted to keep roughly half of them at the house for a
while. Martin had taken the rest to his brother Paul’s the evening
before, because Leah used to stay there when she was younger. The plan
was to scatter them together at the back beach at Sorrento, where she’d
learned to swim, once her mum was ready.

I told him what Leah had said.

He didn’t say anything. He put me on speaker, rang his brother on his
wife’s phone and held the two phones together.

Paul answered after a few rings.

Martin asked where the urn was.

“In the living room. Why?”

“Is anything weird happening?”

“No.”

A second later:

“Well, Max is being a pain in the arse.”

Max was his dog.

“What’s he doing?”

“He won’t come in here.”

Paul sounded embarrassed even saying it.

The dog had spent most of the morning sitting in the hallway, looking
into the living room. If Paul called him, he’d come as far as the
doorway and sit down again.

“He used to do that when Leah stayed,” Paul said. “Sit outside the spare
room till she got up.”

Martin asked him to move the urn.

Paul wanted to know why.

“Just move it.”

There was noise on the other end of the phone. A cupboard opened. Paul
swore about something, then said, “Okay.”

We waited.

I remember hearing Martin’s wife breathing beside him.

A soft clicking came down the line, very faint. It stopped and started,
uneven, getting closer.

I’d played it twice that morning.

“That’s weird,” Paul said.

“What?”

“He’s following me.”

Paul had taken the urn into the kitchen. Max had walked down the hall
after him and sat down outside the kitchen door.

Nobody spoke after that.

That was yesterday.

I haven’t had another message from Leah. I’ve had three from other
passengers. None of them were Noah.

Two families are asking me whether they should cancel cremations.
Another wants to know whether burial would be worse. Rebecca wants to
know if they should have William exhumed. His wife won’t talk about it,
so Rebecca is asking me instead. Sameer asked me what I think happens
when a body decomposes.

My sister rang last night to ask how my ribs were going. They’re nearly
healed. I can feel where they’ve knitted when I breathe in too far.
After she hung up I stood in the kitchen with my hand flat on my side
for a long time. I’m going to die too, eventually. I’m the only person
alive who knows it won’t be the end of anything.

Nobody has mentioned the twelve days. I think about them all the time.
The cold, and the light, and her dad’s voice through the door. I gave
her those because I didn’t want to be the one who said yes, and she went
into the fire anyway.

Martin rang again this morning. Leah’s mum still wants to take her to
Sorrento. He asked me whether they should. He said whatever I told him,
that’s what they’d do.

I haven’t answered any of them.

Until yesterday, I thought whatever was left of these people either
stayed with their bodies or eventually stopped.

Now I don’t think Leah knows where she is.

I think she has more than one answer.
