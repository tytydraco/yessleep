Every person in my husband’s house is lighter than they were a year ago,
and the plant on my nightstand is the only thing in it that has gained
weight.

It has grown over every window. It has grown into the walls. It folds
its leaves when my husband lies to me, and in eleven months I’ve watched
it fold his whole family down to something you could carry in one hand.

They’re asleep right now, standing in the upstairs hall with their hands
folded, swaying. It’s 3:07 a.m. They do this every night.

I’m in the pantry with a towel jammed under the door. It’s the only room
the vine hasn’t taken. I can hear it on the other side, turning pages.

I have seven days. I’ll tell it in order, because the plant taught me to
count.

I managed a plant nursery. It’s a small, honest job. A yellow leaf means
too much water. A leaning stem means not enough light. Plants don’t lie,
and I trusted that about them more than I ever trusted a person.

I was twenty-nine, with eighty-four thousand dollars of horticulture
debt and a sister, Dana, who was my only family. Gideon Ashby came in
last March and asked for an orchid I had to order from Ecuador. He stood
in my greenhouse with his eyes closed and said, “Somebody’s been
overwatering the ferns, and they’re sorry about it.”

I laughed. I hadn’t laughed in a year.

His family ran Ashby Botanicals. You’d know the name if you’ve ever
bought Stillwater, the sedative tincture that costs nine hundred dollars
an ounce and sits in half the hospital pharmacies on the East Coast.
They had the old house on Orchard Row, and money that didn’t announce
itself. When Gideon learned about my loans, he said, “My family doesn’t
believe in young people starting out in debt.”

A month later his mother, Harriet, handed me a folder. The Ashbys had
bought my debt. They would forgive it on our fifth anniversary, “so
you’ll never have a reason to leave out of fear.” I read it twice. I
signed. I cried in the car.

I didn’t read it three times. I’d have found the clause that said if I
left before the fifth year, the whole amount came due at once, to the
family.

The plant came at the wedding.

Gideon’s grandmother, Odalys, is ninety-three and weighs about as much
as a coat. She pressed a blue ceramic pot into my arms at the reception.
A trailing vine, a hand’s length, with feathery gray-green leaves and a
faint smell of lavender.

“A bride’s vine,” she said. “Keep it by the bed. Never prune it. Water
it with the tea in the kitchen jar.” Her hand around my wrist felt like
a bundle of twigs. “And tell it the truth, dear. It likes an honest
house.”

I know plants. I thought it was a sensitive mimosa, the kind whose
leaves close when you touch them, a ripple of folding leaflets like a
shiver. Children love them. They take ten minutes to open again.

This one wasn’t touched.

On the fourth night, Gideon came in late, in his socks, and said, “Hey,
wife.” On the nightstand, six feet from him, every leaf on the vine
snapped shut, tip to pot, quick and silent, like a hand closing.

“It does that,” said Gideon. “She’s shy.”

He was asleep in four minutes. I lay watching the little gray fist of
leaves. It didn’t open until he’d gone to shower in the morning, and
when I walked past it alone, it fanned wide, the way a lung does when
you finally let go of a breath.

It grew.

By the end of the first month the vine had crossed the headboard. It
sent out a tendril the length of my hand every night, fine as thread,
damp at the tip, and by morning the new growth was a little darker and a
little stronger. By month three it had climbed to the ceiling. By month
five it had found the stairs.

I’m a professional. I told Gideon I’d never seen a plant put on two
inches a night in a house without direct sun. I said I wanted to cut it
back.

His face went soft and blank and careful.

“Please don’t,” he said. “It gets upset.”

I watched the family get smaller.

I didn’t see it at first, because it happened the way a candle burns
down, so slowly that you only notice when you look back at the table.
Harriet had been gardening in March. By June she couldn’t kneel. By
August she wore gloves to breakfast, in the heat, and held her teacup
with both hands.

Aunt Pru, in the east wing, stopped coming down for dinner. A tray went
up. A tray came back untouched. Gideon’s cousin Rowan, twenty-six, a big
man who’d carried me across the threshold, needed both arms to lift a
chair. Odalys, who had never been loud, stopped speaking. Then Harriet
stopped. Then the whole table, one by one, in a gentle, polite,
spreading hush, as if a window were closing in each of them.

They smiled. They said they’d never felt better. They said the house was
so restful.

Their eyes had a clear, glossy shine. Like a leaf with the light behind
it.

At 3:07 a.m. every night, the whole house flinched.

It came as a sound first, a soft, rushing, spreading hush, like a crowd
applauding in the next room. It was the leaves. Every leaf in the house,
folding at once.

Then the doors opened.

I’d lie in bed and listen to them come out. Harriet, Pru, Odalys, Rowan,
the cousins, in their nightclothes, barefoot, with their eyes open and
empty. They gathered in the upstairs hall in rows and stood perfectly
still with their hands folded in front of them, swaying a little, as a
plant sways when a hand passes near.

I’d crept out once and counted them. Eleven. They didn’t see me. Their
lips were moving, softly, together.

Not yet. Not yet. Not yet.

At 3:19 a.m. they turned and went back to bed.

In the morning they ate their eggs and said they’d slept like the dead.

That’s when I started the notebook. I’m not proud of how I did it. A
good scientist would have designed a better protocol. I had a
flashlight, and a husband who answered every question in bed with the
same gentle, sleepy honesty. I asked him something, and I watched the
plant.

Do you love me? “Of course.” The leaves stayed open.

Did you know about my loans before you met me? “No.”

The vine folded in a ripple and held shut. A half second later the hush
went through the whole house, nightstand to stairs to walls.

Forty-one entries by July. The plant was never wrong. It never folded
for Harriet’s lies, or Pru’s, or the cook’s. Only Gideon’s. It had
learned one man’s footsteps and the particular lie he told me every
night.

It was the only thing in the house that was afraid of my husband.

The mother plant was in the greenhouse at the back of the property.

Glass and white iron, forty feet long, stone floor. I’d been told never
to go in. The humidity is delicate. On the first Sunday of August, while
the whole household sat at dinner drinking the tea, I took the key from
the kitchen hook.

It was warm in there, and smelled of lavender and cut grass and
something sweet underneath, the smell of a room where someone has been
ill.

A single vine filled the back wall. The trunk was as thick as my thigh,
gray and ridged, rising from a stone planter the size of a bathtub and
branching into the roof. Its leaves were folded shut, hundreds of them,
in a dark, sleeping quilt.

On a brass stand at its foot lay a ledger. I know a growing log when I
see one. Dates. Light. Moisture. Yield.

The first column was brides.

Marta Ellis. Wed. 1987. Distress: 6,100 hrs. Resin yield: 1.1 kg.
“Compliant.”

Odile Voss. Wed. 1994. Distress: 41,000 hrs. Yield: 9.4 kg. “Resisted.”

Seven names. Seven wives. And a note in Harriet’s tidy hand at the
bottom of the page.

The fear is the crop. The calm is what we sell.

I thought I understood. A frightened bride makes a strong resin, and the
resin makes Stillwater. I thought the family was farming me.

Then I turned the page, and the second column was the family.

H. Ashby. Weight: 168 lb (1998). 141. 122. 101.

P. Ashby. 154. 130. 98. 71.

O. Ashby. 140. 120. 88. 64.

Every one of them, every month, for decades. A bathroom scale stands in
the upstairs hall. I’d walked past it a hundred times. The family
stepped on it every Sunday, one at a time, and Harriet wrote the number
in a little book.

At the end of each row, a note, in pencil.

It drinks what it needs. We drink what it leaves.

I stood there in the warm green dark and I understood who owned whom.

The family hadn’t built a business on the plant. The plant had built a
family. For a hundred years it had been feeding on fear and on them,
thinning them, stealing the strength from their hands, until they were
small and calm and rich and docile, and kept a bride in the house whose
terror made the tea that kept them from noticing.

They weren’t farming me. They were farming with me. They were all in the
crop.

I tried to leave that night.

I want you to know I ran. I took my purse and my keys and went down the
stairs two at a time, and I got as far as the front door. It was sealed.
Green had grown over the frame in the hour I’d been gone, leaf over
leaf, a soft living seam. I tore at it with my hands. It bled clear sap,
thick as honey, smelling of lavender.

The whole house sighed.

I screamed. I screamed until my throat tore, and every leaf in the house
folded at the sound, one huge silent hush, and the dark came down like a
lid.

Then I heard them behind me.

The family had come down the stairs. All eleven of them, in a line, in
their nightclothes, with their hands outstretched. They moved slowly,
silently, in a drift. They weren’t angry. Their faces were kind and
blank and wet with tears, and they were reaching for me with hands that
shook.

I fought. I kicked, and I hit Rowan across the face. He didn’t flinch.

They lifted me.

All of them. Eleven people, with their fingertips under my back and legs
and arms. It took no effort. I was heavier than all of them together.
They carried me up the stairs the way you’d carry a sleeping child, and
they laid me in bed. Harriet drew the quilt to my chin. Her hand on my
forehead weighed nothing at all.

“We’re so sorry, dear,” she whispered. “It’s so afraid.”

I looked at her. I looked at the green glaze in her eyes.

“Afraid of what?”

She smiled the sweetest smile.

“Of being cut,” she said.

I’d known what dividing meant. In the trade, it’s what you do when a
mature plant outgrows its pot. You take it out, split the root ball, and
repot each piece in fresh soil.

It was in the ledger. Wren Talley. Wed. April 12. Distress to date:
17,300 hrs. “Promising.” Dividing: March 20.

It was the one thing the plant feared. Every twenty years or so, the
family had to cut it. A mother plant that size chokes without division,
and the resin runs weak. But the cut hurt it. And a hundred years of
feeding on its keepers had made sure that the keepers could no longer
hold the shears.

Look at their hands. Harriet’s. Pru’s. Gideon’s. They shake. They can’t
close.

So the plant had asked for a bride with strong hands.

It grew into the house, and it thinned the family to paper, and it made
certain the only person in that building who could still pick up a pair
of shears was the one it had frightened.

I sat up in bed. The vine had crept across the covers in the night and
laid a single green tendril over my left hand.

It wasn’t tight. It was wet and cool and fine as thread.

It was holding my hand.

The plant had folded for Gideon’s lies to make me trust it. It had drunk
my fear for a year to make me strong. And at 3:07 a.m. every night, when
the house stood in the hall begging, not yet, not yet, it wasn’t begging
for the plant.

It was begging me.

That was five weeks ago.

I haven’t been able to hold anything since. I’ve stopped eating, mostly.
The windows are grown shut, and the house is dim and green. Every
morning there’s a cup of tea on my nightstand, and every morning I pour
it into the pot, and the leaves fold, and it drinks.

I’ve learned its language. It’s the only thing I’ve gained.

It folds for a lie. And it opens, wide and trembling, for the truth. I
asked Gideon last Thursday, as gently as I could, whether he’d let me
leave if I begged. The plant opened as far as it could go. He said,
“No,” and he wasn’t lying.

I asked the plant, in a whisper, if it wanted me to cut it.

It folded.

I asked if it wanted me to stay.

It stayed shut.

I asked, if I cut you, will they be free?

It opened, as slowly as a hand unclosing, and for one moment every leaf
on that vine trembled toward me, and the house went perfectly still.

I’m in the pantry now with the key in my fist. It’s 3:41 a.m. The little
bitter smell of salt and flour is the only thing the vine won’t cross.

My phone lit up a minute ago. Dana. I haven’t spoken to her in a month.

She’s engaged. I introduced her to Gideon’s cousin at my own wedding. I
danced with her at the reception. I put her hand in his.

She sent a photo. A small blue pot on a nightstand. A trailing vine with
feathery gray-green leaves, folded flat, in a ring.

Omg Harriet sent me the cutest engagement gift!! It’s SO shy, it closes
up every time Rowan comes in LOL.

The photo has one leaf open. A single leaf, right at the tip, pointed at
the camera. A brown paper tag is tied to its stem in Harriet’s hand.

Keep close. Tell it everything.

I zoomed in until it came apart into squares.

It only opens when someone is listening.

I have the shears. I took them from the greenhouse an hour ago. They’re
old and heavy, with a dark, narrow blade, and they fit my hand.

It’s 3:44 a.m. The family has come to the pantry door. I can hear them
on the other side, standing in rows, with their hands folded. Eleven
people who weigh less than I do, whispering.

Not yet. Not yet. Not yet.

There’s one more thing. The last page of the ledger, in pencil, in the
margin.

The Cutter keeps the plant calm. The Cutter does not leave the plant.

I looked at the base of the planter again, before I left. Seven rings
tangled in the roots, each on a paper tag, and an eighth tag, hanging
loose, with my name.

The Cutters didn’t die. They joined.

The shears are in my right hand. The key’s in my left.

On the other side of the door, a small, dry voice said, quite clearly:

“Please.”

It’s Gideon. He’s never said it before.

The vine under the door has stopped moving. It’s pointing at me.

If your plant folded every time someone you loved told you a small lie,
and it opened wide when you asked if it wanted to be cut, would you pick
up the shears?

Because the pot on my nightstand is open now. A tiny new tag hangs from
its stem, in my own handwriting, which I didn’t write.

Wren. Dividing: tonight.
