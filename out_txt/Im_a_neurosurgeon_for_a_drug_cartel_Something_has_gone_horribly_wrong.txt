Whenever the cartel called me, it was always a mistake. Not a mistake
that they’d called, but a mistake that someone was dead who shouldn’t be
dead. Javier’s mistake was the first I couldn’t fix for them.

“Dr. F, I need your help,” he said.

“What’s wrong?” I asked.

“Arnaldo isn’t breathing anymore.”

“Did you beat him?”

“I electrocuted him.”

“With what?”

“A car battery.”

I shook my head. Why was I forced to work with such idiots?

Javier gave me the address of a hotel in Tijuana’s Zona Norte. I told
him I was leaving right away. Then I packed my bag with everything I
needed for the operation. A portable extracorporeal membrane oxygenation
system. A self-developed perfusate solution and a nanobot delivery
system. A perfusion chamber. A few scalpels, a package of bandages, and
a bone saw.

The rain was still pouring outside. I put on my hat, ran to my car, and
then put my bag in the trunk and drove toward the border.

As I drove, I thought about Daniel, the investor who’d funded my earlier
research. The medical board had called me a monster for keeping him
alive after his head transplant failed, but how else could I have
learned what had gone wrong? If I’d let him die, I never would have
perfected the procedure.

The border crossing appeared ahead of me. I steadied my nerves, steadied
my hands. Prepared myself the same way I did before performing surgery.

“Where are you headed, Doctor?” the border guard asked.

“A Clínica,” I lied. “I have a meeting with some colleagues early
tomorrow morning.”

He looked at my passport and then waved me through. I drove the rest of
the way to Tijuana and then called Javier.

“I’ll be there in ten minutes,” I told him. “Make sure all of Arnaldo’s
clothes are off. There’s no time to waste.”

Arnaldo and his father, Diego, ran a rival cartel. The man I worked for,
Victor, had been hunting Arnaldo and Diego for years. Now that Victor
finally had Arnaldo, he’d put Javier in charge of learning everything
that Arnaldo knew. But torture is an art, and Javier was a brute. Javier
had killed Arnaldo before he’d learned anything useful. 

I turned onto Coahuila Street. Neon lights glowed above the young women
dancing in front of the bars and strip clubs, wiggling their hips while
they shouted at the American tourists.

I parked in front of the hotel and went up to Javier’s room. He answered
the door shirtless, his body covered in tattoos.

He brought me to the bathroom. Arnaldo lay naked on the floor next to
the bathtub. His skin was covered in bruises and burn marks. On top of
the toilet was a car battery hooked up to a pair of alligator clips.

I got right to work. I laid the shower curtain on the floor, and then I
had Javier help me roll Arnaldo’s body on top of it. I opened my bag and
took out my tools.

Even though Javier and I had worked together for years, he still became
uneasy when he watched me work. Javier the murderer, who’d beaten men to
death, burned them alive, and dismembered them, made the sign of the
cross and said a short prayer as I began disinfecting Arnaldo’s neck.

I flushed Arnaldo’s carotid arteries with an anticoagulant, and then I
picked up the bone saw and cut through his throat, severing the muscle
and tendon while carefully preserving the vocal cords.

The saw vibrated against my palm. When I reached Arnaldo’s spine, the
blade stalled for a moment before finally snapping the bone.

“Are you almost done?” Javier asked.

“Just be quiet and let me work.”

Blood pooled around my shoes, mixing with the antiseptic. The smell used
to make me sick, but I barely noticed it anymore.

I attached the delivery system to the base of Arnaldo’s neck.
Microscopic machines made of graphene and biodegradable polymer began
swimming through Arnaldo’s dying tissue, hunting for the signatures of
cellular distress. Wherever they found damage, they repaired it. Oxygen
deprivation halted. Cell death reversed.

“Is it working?” Javier asked.

“I said be quiet,” I snapped.

The next part was the hardest part. I needed to focus. I carefully
separated the nerves and blood vessels in Arnaldo’s neck and then
connected them to the oxygenation system. The surgery demanded extreme
precision. There was zero room for error.

Another hour, and I’d finished. I placed Arnaldo’s head inside the
perfusion chamber. All that was left was to switch on the oxygenation
system and bring him back to life.

I hesitated for a moment, though, before flipping the switch. I thought
of Daniel again. His body had been consumed by advanced muscular
dystrophy. When he first came to me for help, he couldn’t even shake my
hand. His fingers had curled into permanent claws. His voice was thin
and weak, and for anything longer than a few sentences, he used an
eye-tracking tablet mounted to his wheelchair, staring at one letter at
a time until the words appeared on the screen.

“I’ve followed your research, Dr. F,” he told me. “You’re the only one
who thinks it’s possible.”

“I know it’s possible,” I said.

Daniel was as desperate as I was ambitious. He wanted to be the first
living human to undergo a head transplant. He wanted me to move his
healthy brain into a new, healthy body. I told him we would make history
together.

The hospital refused, of course, and so did the university, but I’d
planned the surgery for so long, I couldn’t imagine anything going
wrong. I did the surgery myself in my basement. I thought the surgery
had been a complete success, but then Daniel woke up in his new body
screaming.

I didn’t want him to suffer, but I needed to understand what I’d done
wrong. I kept him alive, screaming through the morphine I kept giving
him, while I probed the tissues in his neck.

Before the medical board took away my license, they’d called me a
monster. To them, though, there was no difference between a monster and
a god. To me, the difference was everything.

I switched on the oxygenation system. Arnaldo’s eyelids snapped open.
His pupils dilated. He coughed and then spat out a mouthful of blood.

“Can you hear me, Arnaldo?” I asked.

His lips moved, but the only sound that came out was a pained wheeze.

Then his eyes began to focus. He looked around the room. Slowly, he
figured it out. He was a smart man. An Ivy League MBA. He could have had
a good life in the United States if he’d wanted to, but instead he came
home to help run the family business.

“Dr. F,” he said. “The man… I prayed I’d never meet. What have you… done
to me?”

I turned to Javier. “He’s all yours.”

I left the bathroom. Javier began questioning Arnaldo again. A few
minutes later, his phone rang. When he came out of the bathroom, he
looked nervous.

“We need to move him,” he said.

“Where?”

“To Victor’s compound further south.”

“Arnaldo’s in a very delicate state right now. Moving him is risky.
What’s happened?”

“Victor thinks Arnaldo’s father knows where we are.”

Fear gripped my chest, but I didn’t panic. I stayed calm. I picked up
Arnaldo’s head, which floated in glass inside the perfusion chamber, and
then followed Javier down to his van. A few women on the street saw us,
Arnaldo’s head in my arms, encased in glass, and they screamed and ran.

I sat in the back of the van and placed Arnaldo’s head beside me. He
looked up at me through the glass, watching me.

“Is he all right?” Javier asked.

“For now. But we need to move quickly. He won’t last long in the
chamber.”

“Just keep him alive, or Victor won’t be happy.”

Javier got onto the highway and then drove south, toward Victor’s
compound.

The rain fell on the van’s roof. A heavy, droning thud, drowning out the
mariachi music playing on the radio.

“Things are… going wrong…” Arnaldo said. “But I can… help you.”

“How could you help me?” I asked. “Look at yourself.”

“You have talents… We could use… a man like you.”

“I’m sure you could.”

Headlights appeared behind us. I ducked down, so I wouldn’t be seen
through the windows. Javier slowed down and let the other car pass.

“You’re scared?” Arnaldo asked me.

“I’m not scared of anything.”

“Not even… of this?”

“Of becoming a head? Why would I fear that? Nobody else in the world can
do what I do.”

Javier called his mother. Told her he’d be home late. We were running
for our lives, and that was what he was thinking about, making sure his
mother didn’t worry.

Another car appeared behind us. The police this time. They followed us a
few miles and then turned on their lights.

“Shit,” Javier said. “I can’t outrun him in this van.”

“Then just pull over and give him some money,” I said.

“Can you keep Arnaldo quiet?”

“Of course.”

Javier pulled over to the side of the road. I turned the dial
controlling the airflow through Arnaldo’s vocal cords, preventing him
from speaking anymore.

The officer parked behind us and walked up to Javier’s window. Javier
put his right hand on the grip of his gun. I pressed myself against the
wall of the van, hiding in the dark.

“Let me see your license,” the officer said.

Javier gave it to him.

“Where are you headed?”

“I’m going to see my mother in El Rosario.”

The officer peered into the back of the van. “Is anyone else with you?”

“It’s just me.”

Javier tried handing him a few thousand pesos. The officer didn’t take
the money, though. He just stared at Javier’s face.

“Is there a problem?” Javier asked.

“You’re one of Victor’s men, aren’t you?”

Javier tried to raise his gun, but the officer fired first. The bullet
tore through Javier’s skull, splattering blood across the passenger
seat.

Javier slumped over the steering wheel.

The officer walked toward the back of the van. He still hadn’t noticed
me.

I took my gun from my bag. I’d never fired it before. I’d never had to.

I aimed at the van’s back doors. The gun shook in my hands.

As soon as the doors swung open, I began firing, and I kept firing until
the magazine was empty.

The officer fell backward onto the pavement. The rain washed his blood
away.

I went to his body and tore the badge off his jacket. The badge was
real. A real cop, probably working for Diego, but a cop all the same.

My mind began racing. Where would I go? How would I escape? How would I
get back to America?

Victor would know. Victor had always had the answers. But first, I had
to get Arnaldo to him.

I dragged Javier’s body out of the van, wiped his blood off the wheel,
and then set Arnaldo’s head on the passenger seat. He moved his lips
like he wanted to talk to me. I increased the airflow to his vocal
cords.

“My friends… h-here… s-soon…” he said. His voice had become hoarser.
More guttural. “Help me… I’ll h-help you.”

“You can’t help me,” I told him.

I kept driving down the highway, staring at my rearview mirror,
terrified more police lights would appear behind me. 

“Money…” Arnaldo gasped. “I… give you… m-money….”

I ignored him.

The wipers squeaked across the windshield. The mariachi music played on
the radio.

“P-p-please…”

Headlights appeared in my rearview mirror. I gripped the steering wheel
until my knuckles turned white. But then the other car sped up and
pulled past me.

I needed to get off the highway. I wasn’t safe out in the open.

Ahead of me, just off a dirt road, a neon vacancy sign shone in front of
a run-down motel.

I could rest there and call Victor, I thought. He’d help get me out of
this mess.  

I parked behind the motel, out of sight from the highway, and then went
inside. An old woman sat behind the front desk.

“I need a room for the night,” I told her.

“I don’t think I’ve ever seen an American here before,” she said.

“Is it a problem?”

“No. Five hundred pesos for the night.”

I gave her the money. She gave me the room key. As I left her office, I
heard her pick up the phone.

“Sí, un americano.” Yes, an American.

I went into my room, took a sheet off the bed, and then brought the
sheet with me back to the van. I wrapped the sheet around Arnaldo’s head
and then carried him into the room.

I set his head next to the TV and then checked his vital signs. His
blood pressure was dropping. If I didn’t attach his head to a new body
soon, he’d be dead.

“I-i-i-i-t… hur..ur..urts..” he moaned.

I sat on the bed. The room was filthy. Dried blood spattered on the
wallpaper. Cobwebs covering the ceiling.

“Don’t die on me, Arnaldo,” I told him. “Victor needs you alive.”

“Ki…i…i…ll me…e..e…ki…ki..lll..ll”

I turned down the airflow again. I didn’t want to listen to him
anymore. 

I called Victor.

“Where are you?” he asked.

“A motel, just off the highway,” I said. “Javier is dead. Arnaldo won’t
be alive much longer.”

I explained what had happened. Victor said he was sending a few of his
men to the motel.

I put my phone away and then went back to Arnaldo.

Outside, the thunder roared. Lightning lit up the sky. The rain beat
against the window.

I thought of Daniel again, thrashing on the operating table in my
basement while I probed the nerve endings in his neck.

“Kill me,” he’d begged me. “Please, kill me.”

Maybe I should have done it. For days, Daniel had suffered horribly. I
told myself it would all be worth it in the end, though. Soon, I’d have
the power to control life and death.

I looked around the motel room again, and I laughed.

Arnaldo’s mouth opened in a silent scream. Blood leaked from his nose
and the corners of his eyes.

This was bad. This was very, very bad.

But then someone knocked on the door to my room.

I looked through the peephole. It was just one person. I couldn’t make
out his face in the dark.

“Who are you?” I asked.

“Is that you, Dr. F?”

“Victor sent you?”

“Yes. Is Arnaldo okay?”

“He’s in very bad shape. He doesn’t have much time.”

I opened the door and let him into my room. He was enormous. Over six
feet tall, three hundred pounds, with tattoos crawling up his neck. When
he saw Arnaldo’s head beside the TV, though, his face turned pale.

“We need to move quickly,” I told him.

I wrapped Arnaldo’s head in the bedsheet again, and then I followed the
man outside to his Jeep.

I sat in the back seat and set Arnaldo’s head down beside me.

The man drove back onto the highway, glancing at us over his shoulder
every now and then.

“Is it normal for his eyes to be bleeding like that?” he asked.

“No, it’s not,” I said. “His brain tissue is deteriorating. If I don’t
get his head onto a new body soon, he’s going to die.”

We were on the highway for an hour. I started to get nervous.

“You’re taking me to Victor’s compound, aren’t you?” I asked. “Shouldn’t
we be there already?”

The man stayed quiet.

“You aren’t talking now?” I asked. “What’s going on?”

He pulled over to the side of the road. Then he pointed his gun at my
face.

“If Arnaldo dies, so do you,” he told me.

Another car pulled up behind us. Then another man got into the back seat
with me.

He was older. Grey hair. A grey beard.

He placed his hand on the glass in front of Arnaldo’s face.

“My poor son,” he said. “My poor, poor son.”

“Wake up, Dr. F.”

My head throbbed. My vision was blurred.

I tried to rub my temples. It took me a moment to realize that I didn’t
have hands anymore.

I didn’t have a body at all.

Slowly, the room came into focus. Two men sat across from me. One was
the grey-haired man I’d seen in the car. The other was Arnaldo.

“You might as well have killed my son,” Diego said. “You’ve completely
destroyed his brain.”

Arnaldo muttered something, but his words weren’t real words. Drool
dripped down his chin.

His body looked familiar. The hands. The long fingers. They were my
hands. My body.

“It was Victor,” I said. “He forced me to do this. I didn’t have a
choice.”

My eyes darted back and forth. My head was on a wooden table, connected
to some kind of crude machine. It looked nothing at all like the
machines I worked with.

“What have you done to me?” I asked.

“You’re not the only one who can bring back the dead,” Diego said.
“Others have studied your work. They’re still learning, but slowly,
they’re figuring it out.”

“That’s not… possible…”

I recognized my own work in the machine, though. The same techniques I’d
developed through my experiments on Daniel.

Diego turned a dial at the side of the machine. Pressure swelled inside
my skull until it felt like my eyes were going to explode.

I screamed in agony.

Diego laughed.

He turned the dial back. The pressure eased, leaving a dull, throbbing
headache in its place.

One of Diego’s men came into the room and moved my head onto a cart.
Then I was wheeled into a medical lab and connected to a tablet with a
small camera mounted beneath its screen.

“Write down everything about the procedure,” Diego told me. “Everything
you know.”

I’ve been writing for weeks now, explaining each step of the surgery in
as much detail as I can.

Today, though, I noticed the wi-fi had somehow connected.

A miracle, I thought. A chance to get help.

If you’re reading this, please, you have to help me. You have to tell
Victor that Diego has me trapped, bodiless, in his home. I can feel my
brain dulling. My thoughts and memories fading away. I still have so
much more work to do. I still have so much more to understand.
