People ask if my parents loved me. They did, and I would know better
than anyone. My mother used to leave the bathroom light on when she went
to bed because it made a yellow square on my wall. If my dad got up to
pee, he would turn it off without thinking and then turn it on again
before he got back in bed. You could hear the switch twice, with just
enough time between the two clicks for him to get halfway down the hall.

The first night I woke up because there was somebody in the corner where
the yellow square ended. One bare foot hung down from the ceiling. I
could see the dirty part under the toes, and I watched for long enough
that I started to think it was a sock my dad had thrown up there as a
joke. Then the toes spread apart. There was a little space between each
one, the way there was between mine when my mother put a towel under my
feet to cut my nails. I tried to yell and woke up with my mouth full of
the sheet.

Mom found me sitting with my knees pulled up so high that my chin rested
between them. She turned on the bedside lamp and got in next to me
without moving the knees. I always hated being told to lie down right
after a nightmare. She knew I wanted to see the whole room for a while.
She put the side of her face on my pillow and said, “It was only a
dream, Micah. You’re in bed with me now. Can you give me two squeezes so
I know you’re all right?”

She held her hand open on the mattress. I took two of her fingers and
squeezed once, and she waited for the second one. She’d been doing that
since I was small enough to get mad when she moved her hand before I was
finished. I squeezed again, very lightly, because by then I could smell
the lotion she put on her feet at night. It smelled a little like
oranges and a little like the stuff she used to clean the stove.

Dad came in holding Biscuit under the front legs. The cat was long that
way, with his back feet hanging almost to my dad’s knees. Dad used to do
his voice like an old man who’d come back to a restaurant to complain
about his eggs. He made Biscuit say, “I hate to bother anybody at this
hour, but something in this house has been eating my food. I have reason
to believe it’s me.” My mother said, “Russ, set him down before he pees
on you.” Biscuit walked up the blanket and sneezed on my bare ankle, and
I laughed hard enough that the air came out of me in three little jumps.
My dad put the cat back at the foot of the bed before they went out.
Everything was where it belonged when I fell asleep again.

In the daylight there was a tiny wet-looking hump in the paint where I’d
seen the foot. Dad put me up on his shoulders so I could reach it, and
it turned out to be hard enough to pick at with a fingernail. He let me
put a little pencil mark there so I’d know it was the same spot at
night.

That afternoon he made us sandwiches. He was at the counter in a shirt
with a little burn hole from cooking outside, and he put the crusts on a
plate for himself like he always did. He still had a corner of the crust
in his mouth when he turned his head to look at me. His chin kept going
after it reached his shoulder. The skin on the front of his neck went
tight, and I saw the bump in his throat move sideways under it. The
piece of bread dropped out of his mouth and stuck to his bare foot,
butter side down.

I said, “Dad, that looks like it hurts.” I remember the polite little
face I made. My upper lip went up on one side as though I’d watched him
knock his elbow and was waiting to see if I was allowed to laugh. He
took one step toward me with his chest still facing the counter. His
back foot came down on the little plate, which he must have knocked off
when he moved. I heard it break. He said, in the voice he did for
Biscuit, “Oh, there you are. I thought you might go upstairs before I
got a look at you.”

My mother was at the end of the hall. I could see her shirt through the
dining room chairs, so I got down to go to her and hit my hip on the
table. She had a mug halfway up to her chin. There was a wet circle on
the carpet beneath it where the coffee had run down her thumb. I asked
her what was happening, and for the little while I stood there watching,
she didn’t move the mug up or down. Her mouth was open just enough to
show the bottom edge of her front teeth. I touched her wrist and I could
feel a small movement inside it where the blood went through.

I ran up to my room. I’d gone to bed mad before, and on Christmas Eve I
went to bed so excited I could feel my heart in my fingers, but I had
never tried to sleep because I needed the day to stop. I put the blanket
over my head in all that heat. My breath smelled like the two bites I’d
had of my sandwich. I stayed under there until staying under was worse
than looking. When I finally pulled the blanket down, Dad was standing
in the hall rubbing his neck and asking my mother why she’d put her mug
in the basket of clean towels.

He said he’d slipped on the kitchen tile and couldn’t get his head to
turn all the way left. By dinner he had a wide purple place down one
side of his neck. He let me touch it while he sat at the table and tried
to look over his shoulder. His eyes would go a little farther than his
head, and every time they did that I could see the look he’d given me
over the sandwich. I took my finger off him and asked if it hurt where
I’d touched. He said it hurt everywhere except where I’d touched, and he
put my finger back on the bruise.

On the fourth day Mom came into my bedroom just as I was getting
dressed. She was carrying a laundry basket against her hip. She tipped
it forward and let it go, and after the socks hit the floor there was a
little wait before the rest of the washing came down on top of them.
Then she reached up and put one hand over the top of the door. Her arm
straightened. Her feet rose off the carpet without her bending her knees
or pulling herself up in any way I could see. She hung there looking
down at me, as comfortable as if she were standing on the floor.

I thought I could get past under her if I kept close to the hinge. She
lowered herself a few inches, and I stopped. I moved toward the dresser
and she let herself back up. For a little while we did that together.
When I put my foot to the left, the hem of her shirt came down toward my
eyes. When I stepped back, her bare feet rose. She was showing me where
I could stand. She looked pleased when I got it, the way she looked when
I caught a ball in the pocket of my glove instead of letting it smack
the hard part by my thumb.

She brought her face down close to mine. The top of the door creaked,
and the sound in her shoulder came a fraction later, soft and damp. It
hurt all the way into the back teeth on that side. She could still smile
with that half of her mouth. “You can call him if you want,” she said.
My father was sitting on the edge of our bathtub. I could see his knees
past her if I leaned a little. He’d been trying to get a bit of grit out
of the heel of his foot, and he stayed there with his ankle crossed over
his leg and his bottom lip sagging. I called twice. Mom looked at me
with her head a little to the side. She was giving me time to call
louder.

The first three nails came loose when she dropped. There were red half
circles on the top of the door where her fingers had been. She lay on
her side with her damaged hand open and her good one closed around the
sleeve of my shirt. Then Dad stood up in the bathroom so fast he kicked
the wastebasket over. He stepped on the laundry coming through the door
and went down on one knee next to her. I thought he was going to pick
her hand up and put it back where she’d had it.

He said, “Dana, look at me. Can you let go of him for a second?” She
looked at the hand on my sleeve as though it belonged to somebody lying
on the other side of her. Her grip was gone before she got the fingers
open. Then she started making a low sound through her nose because she
couldn’t breathe deeply without moving her shoulder. Dad called Aunt
Bree to come sit with me while he took Mom to get it looked at. He said
on the phone that she’d fallen. I stood where she’d made me stand and
heard him repeat it in our bedroom, in the hall, and once again at the
front door when my aunt arrived.

We brought the trash can in together. Bree asked me whether there was
anything my dad hadn’t told her. I wanted to tell her about the coffee
on the carpet because that was something she could go inside and look
at. I’d seen Dad go over the spot with a brush, so I told her about
Mom’s feet instead.

That Saturday, Dad and I went to the diner with the stuffed fish over
the register. Mom stayed in bed because her shoulder was bad. Dad told
me I could order whatever I wanted, so I asked for a grilled cheese even
though it was early enough that a man behind us was still reading the
breakfast menu.

We took a full bag of cans to the recycling place. They had a strip of
carpet laid over the scale so the cans wouldn’t roll off, and somebody
had left a baby shoe on a post by the driveway. My dad said if we came
back in a year there’d still be somebody looking at that shoe and
thinking maybe its owner would come back. He gave me the money for the
cans. I bought a pack of gum with a little roll of paper around each
piece and picked the color I liked least to give him. When he saw me
doing it, he gave me back half of his piece anyhow.

I told him I’d been having dreams where I got hurt. I said I wanted to
stay up all night with him sometime, and he said sure, but only if I
could make it past the weather on the ten o’clock news. I got mad
because he thought I couldn’t stay up. He made me tell him the last time
I’d actually seen a news broadcast finish. I couldn’t think of one. He
grinned at me for a while and then said, very seriously, that he’d make
the coffee weak the first time. I spent the ride home thinking about
what I’d ask him if the two of us got to sit up talking after Mom went
to bed.

It was dark when we got home. He pulled up beside the house and left the
engine on, with his hand resting loose on top of the gearshift. I waited
for him to turn the key. The red numbers on the dash said it was 8:51,
and I remember wondering how much longer it would take that night for me
to see what the weather man looked like.

Dad said, “I wanted to see if you could still have a good day with me if
I gave you enough time.” He waited until I turned to ask what he meant.
His bruised neck swung around very easily in the light from the dash.
What took me longest to understand was that he’d waited until I had
something left to look forward to. I called him Dad until my voice
sounded strange to me. When I died, I woke up with my fingers pinched
hard around the front of my pajama shirt, trying to pull it loose from a
seat belt that wasn’t there.

It was Saturday morning. There was no gum in my shorts. Dad knocked on
the door with one knuckle and said he thought we might get breakfast,
since Mom was still sleeping. I could see him through the crack in the
door, pushing the hair down over the place where he’d slept on it. I
thought of the baby shoe, and I wanted him to come sit with me so I
could tell him what a nice day I’d had with him. I’d already been
missing him for a long time before he asked.

I went with him. I got eggs and Dad ate the parts I didn’t want. On the
way out I looked at the floor under the register for the fish’s shadow
because I remembered stepping in it the first time. Each time Dad went
past a road that would’ve taken us to the recycling place, it got easier
to talk.

That night I dreamed my mother was kneeling over me on the kitchen
floor. The dishwasher was running, so at first I thought the rough noise
I was hearing came from inside it. Then I realised it came when I tried
to breathe. I’d gotten one hand under the edge of the fridge and
couldn’t get it back. She moved my hand for me very gently, though by
then she’d done enough that it was hard to believe she could see which
part of me still hurt and which part had gone cold. She wiped her thumb
on the front of my shirt. I knew what she was about to do because I
could see the reflection of her hand in the oven door. She said, “Look
at me, honey. You’ll wake up before I finish.” I did wake up, but there
was time between her saying it and my opening my eyes for me to learn
she was willing to keep looking at me.

In the morning Dad played me a recording in my room. He sat at the foot
of my bed where Biscuit usually slept and held the phone close enough to
his own knee that at first I couldn’t hear it clearly. The voice on it
was mine, asking my mother to please move because I couldn’t get my hand
out. My dad moved the phone closer. The boy on the recording said, “If
you look at my face you’ll see which part hurts.” Then there was a
scrape and a long stretch in which I could hear him trying at different
volumes to say the word Mom. It had never occurred to me that I might
sound like a smaller child when I begged. I reckoned a person could
listen to the whole thing without guessing I had already turned nine.

“Why would you keep that?” I asked him. He said, “Because you always
tell us you don’t remember the last part.” He played it again from the
beginning. I went into the bathroom and sat with my feet braced against
the door until I heard him go downstairs. Then the little phone he and
Mom let me use for games lit up on the counter outside. I could see my
name in a message from Dad and the recording under it.

I waited until he was on his own in the garage that evening. He was
sitting on an upside-down bucket cleaning the dried grass off his shoes,
and I stood with the door to the kitchen open behind me. I told him
about the dream in the car. Then I said I’d never told him or Mom that I
didn’t remember the last part. He got his phone out when I asked, and I
saw how long he looked at the message before he pressed on it. I waited
through the whole recording again because I needed to see what happened
to his face at the end.

He put the phone down beside him and said, “Thank God. I thought it was
just me.” There were tears in his eyes when he looked up. It was the
same relief he’d had once when he’d come to pick me up from day camp and
couldn’t find me because I’d gone into the wrong room. I understood him
to mean that he’d wondered if he was the only one in the house who liked
what he was doing. It made me feel stupid for all the times I’d waited
for him to come into a room and fix something. His hand came up off his
knee, and I backed up fast enough that the door caught me in the heel.
He saw me do it and said, “I meant at night. Micah, let me try again.” I
went upstairs before he could.

I sent the recording to my aunt from under my blanket. She called me
right away and asked where I was sitting, and whether I could talk out
loud for another minute. She asked me to tell her what had happened to
my mother’s shoulder again. I said Mom’s feet had come up without her
climbing, and this time I told her what my mother had done to make me
move around the room. She asked if I wanted her to come over that night.
I said I wanted her to come in the morning, when my parents would see
her and have to let me go with her. She said she’d be there at eight and
that I could change my mind about waiting any time.

I was asleep when my father came up later. I know I was asleep because I
woke again with him sitting on the floor between my bed and the wall,
where there was barely room for him to get his knees up. He had taken
his boots off so as not to wake me. When I asked why he was down there
he said, “I thought I’d be here in case you woke up and needed to know
which room you were in.” He had my baseball glove on his lap, opening it
and closing it to soften it up like he’d promised. I turned over to face
the wall until he went away. In the morning I couldn’t get my hand
inside the glove because he’d left a ball in it to hold the shape.

Bree took me to her place for the day and let me eat lunch on the couch.
She told me I could stay as long as I liked. I went home because my
mother sounded so grateful when I called and told her I was having a
good day that I thought she might sit in the bedroom all night if I
stayed away.

On the tenth day I woke up in my own room after midnight. My mouth was
so dry I couldn’t get my lips all the way apart at first. There was
breathing on the other side of the door, low down near the floor. I’d
put the little hook my father had installed for me through its eye, and
the door had enough play in it to move a little when somebody leaned on
it. I could hear it tap once against the frame and then sit still. My
mother said, “What could I say that would make you open it?”

She said it like she was genuinely curious, and she waited a long time
after she asked. Then she tried telling me my dad had gone to get the
car. The next time she said, “Your aunt’s here, honey, and she’s waiting
for you downstairs.” She changed her voice just slightly on honey. That
one was closer. I sat on the floor with my back against the side of the
bed and worked at keeping my breath quiet. If I sucked it in all at once
it made a little whistle in my nose, and I could tell how well she was
listening by how quickly she stopped talking whenever I made the sound.

“I need you to come look at something on my arm,” she said. I had my
knees against my chest, and my right foot slid forward before I could
stop it. I’d helped her dress a couple of times since she hurt her
shoulder. Her shirt would catch on the tape and she’d hear me come in
and ask me to check where it was snagged. Outside the door she said, to
herself, “The one about the arm usually works.” Then she put her cheek
to the carpet. I heard her earrings click against the little brass strip
Dad had put down to keep the carpet from fraying. She must’ve been lying
there looking at my feet for almost a minute before I thought to move
them.

She tried the door once. I saw the tips of her fingers in the gap
underneath, where the carpet flattened. When she drew them out there was
a pink smear on the threshold from the fingers with the bad nails. For a
while she lay breathing into the gap as though she wanted to warm the
feet I’d moved away from her. The next thing I heard was her saying my
name in a voice so weak and embarrassed I nearly opened up. She’d come
to herself on the floor and couldn’t get on her knees without help. I
got back in bed, pulled the covers up, and stayed very still while she
asked if I could go and get my dad.

In the morning Mom made oatmeal and cut me a pear. She stood at the sink
and asked, “When I called you last night, did you think I was still
going to hurt you if you helped me?” I remember the weight she put on
the word *still*. I said I hadn’t heard her call. She put my bowl down
and stayed by my chair for longer than she needed to, holding the dish
towel slack in her good hand. Then she went into the hall and called my
aunt. She said she didn’t think it was a good idea for me to stay with
them until they figured out what was going on.

On the thirteenth day Bree came to get me for more than one afternoon.
It was my job to bring Biscuit. I set his carrier just inside the front
door and went to find him while my parents carried a couple of bags
down. Biscuit was standing at the top of the stairs. His back was
pressed flat to the baseboard and his front paws were curled in against
his chest. He was upright the way Dad held him to do his voice, except
there was nobody holding him. His belly moved in little jerks. One of
his front paws tried to push off the plaster twice before it folded back
in.

Both my parents were on the stairs beneath him. Dad was three steps down
with his side to the wall, and Mom stood at the bottom with her good
hand open on the post. I could see the tips of her fingers through the
gap in the railing. Dad had one foot hanging over the edge of the step
and was leaning so far forward that I thought he had to fall. He stayed
that way for as long as it took me to back away from Biscuit. The cat
watched me until I reached the other end of the landing. Then his neck
stretched up, and I saw his mouth open.

I used to think the shape of a mouth had something to do with what words
it could say. “Biscuit” would have been impossible for him, with no lips
that could close the way mine did and that little cleft going up to his
nose. But he could make the noise of my mother calling me from
downstairs. It came out of him at half her volume and with the long part
of the vowel too high. He drew a breath and did it over, slower. He
could hear himself getting closer. I could tell he liked that.

My dad fell before Biscuit came down. His chin hit the edge of the next
step and stayed there for an instant while the rest of him kept going.
It made a short, thick sound that I could still hear when the rest of
the fall was over. Mom screamed as if somebody had woken her by setting
a hand on her shoulder. The cat hit the floor on his side and started
crying in his own voice. He let me put a towel over him and pick him up.
He was so warm underneath it that I couldn’t bear to leave him on the
ground while I looked down the stairs. Bree was already there with my
father and asking him to stop trying to talk. There was blood all down
the front of his shirt and a place in his cheek that moved when the rest
of his jaw didn’t.

We all left that day. Dad went where they sent him to get his jaw fixed,
and my aunt got Biscuit looked at, too. She called some men to check the
furnace and the stove, and one of them shone his flashlight in the
corner over my bed when I asked. The only thing he found was the pencil
mark I’d made on Dad’s shoulders.

The nights at Bree’s were easy enough that by the third one I was
embarrassed about the things I’d shown her. I cooked a hamburger on the
stove with her and brought the burnt half out to my dad. He had to eat
soup with a little spoon because his jaw was held in place, but he said
he’d be happy to lease my hamburger for the price of one dollar if I
would eat it for him. He wrote the offer out on the back of a grocery
receipt when I couldn’t understand him. Under that he’d written, “I was
scared, too. I didn’t say it right that time in the garage.” I folded it
in half and left it next to his bowl. I could work out more than one
thing he might have meant, and I didn’t want to pick the wrong one
again.

Biscuit started sleeping on my feet. He wouldn’t ride in the crook of
Dad’s arm after the stairs, even when Dad was well enough to hold him.
He’d press his back legs down flat the second they left the ground. I
washed the towel I’d brought him out in and put it on the couch for him.
Eventually we had a whole evening where everybody got to look in the
same direction and laugh at something on television. I watched my
father’s eyes close during the commercials. My mother saw me watching
and handed me the remote so I could mute it and let him sleep.

Nobody watched that house more closely than I did. For five days, the
bathroom light stayed on because no one had been there to switch it off.
In the daytime you could see a little square of it in the hall mirror
from my bedroom. The first rain tapped against the empty cat bowl on the
porch and fell quiet in the grass. When there had been a long enough
break between cars, I could hear the refrigerator turning itself on in
the kitchen and imagine how much it must have sounded like a house with
somebody still in it.

Bree was going to take my parents over to get their work clothes and
pick up the rest of my summer reading books. They had a woman coming to
change the locks at noon the next day. My mother had started to think
somebody might have been coming in and giving them something, and a new
lock was a thing she could get done. I’d promised I’d stay with the cat.
My parents went over first, while Bree changed out of her sandals. I
watched them leave and was all right for maybe a minute. Then I thought
about the two of them going through that door at the same time, with one
good shoulder and half a jaw between them, and I went after them. I
couldn’t get my aunt to understand how fast I needed to go. She was in
the laundry room asking me to give her thirty seconds, and I let the
screen door fall shut before I answered.

It was the nineteenth day. Bree had circled it on her kitchen calendar
because she meant to take me swimming after we got back.

Dad met me in the hall carrying my books. He pulled the front door open
a little wider with his elbow and told me to wait outside. His words
came out thick and close together. There was a sore on his bottom lip
where it kept catching on something in his mouth. I looked past him and
saw that he’d left the light on in my room. When I asked him where Mom
was, he said she was upstairs getting one last thing.

My mother was coming down the stairs barefoot. I could tell how she was
doing by how she leaned, because she’d started lowering herself sideways
to keep the bad shoulder away from the rail. She came down like that for
the first five steps. Then a muscle along the side of her neck jumped
and both her feet went flat on the treads. She got down the remaining
steps with her knees almost straight. I heard a sound from her sling and
saw the little plastic buckle come off and roll into the hall. Her arm
swung down loose against the side of her body.

Dad’s books slid out of his hand. He looked right at me for an instant
before his eyes stopped going where he wanted them to go. I had the
stupid idea that if I didn’t move he could keep me in sight while he was
standing there. Mom said, “You can go to bed after this. You can have
the whole day off tomorrow if you like.” I looked at her and wondered
how many times I’d have to die before I woke up somewhere she didn’t
know to look.

His hand closed around the back of my shirt. I thought he had decided to
start. Instead he got the door all the way open and said, “Go, Micah.
Keep going.” I could see Bree’s headlights swinging around the corner at
the end of our street even though it was still light enough to read
inside. I tried to tell him she was coming. He looked out past me, then
turned and saw Mom halfway down the hall. He shoved me hard enough that
my shin caught against the brick wall beside the porch steps. I thought
I knew why he’d waited to do it until he saw the car. There was a low
place alongside the porch where Bree wouldn’t see me if she looked out
her window.

I remember lying there with my nose almost touching the dirt. I saw a
piece of glass with a leaf stuck to it and a little moving line where
some ants had come up out of a crack in the wall. I kept expecting the
sound of the car door to come from the street. It was quiet enough for
me to hear Dad breathe once up above me. He’d leaned far enough out to
see where I’d landed, and for a second I thought if I looked up he’d
realise there was no time left for us to make up and he’d make up with
me anyway. I got one elbow underneath me. Then his fingers slid under my
arm, and in that same breathless voice he said, “Go, Micah. Keep going.”

He brought me back inside and my mother stood where she’d stopped, with
both shoulders down and her good wrist turned in against her leg. The
door came shut under Dad’s heel. I heard the deadbolt turn while I was
still close enough to see the little lines around his knuckles. On the
stairs I tried to get my foot on the wall to slow him down, and he
waited while I did it. He seemed interested in what my leg would do when
I had to put all my weight on it. When it slipped down he started up
again. I could see over his shoulder by then. My mother stood in the
hall. Her head was angled toward the place on the wall where my heel had
been.

He took me into my bedroom. I’d been working on those summer books on
the floor by the bed because I liked lying on my stomach to read. One of
them was still facedown where I’d left it. Dad set me down across it,
and I felt the pages bow slowly underneath my back. He knelt beside me
with his jaw trembling. His mouth opened enough for me to see the sore
on the inside of his lip again. The sound he made with his mouth could
have passed for crying from across the room. I listened for the place in
it where he’d get control and say my name.

I heard my aunt knock. Three good knocks came from the front door and
carried up the air vent. I opened my mouth to call to her and got the
first part of her name out. Dad jerked and went still, with one hand
stiff around the edge of the book. Mom came through my bedroom door on
her own feet. Her chin was tucked in tight enough to make the skin under
it pucker. I could see the dark line where her sling had rubbed at her
neck for the last two weeks. She put her hand down next to my face, and
one of the bandages dragged across the carpet when she moved a finger.
That little drag was the last clear sound I heard from downstairs or
anywhere else for a while.

She’d asked me so many questions that summer that by then I knew how
long she’d hold still waiting for an answer. In the bedroom she asked if
I could see her. I said yes, and she moved closer. When I turned my head
away, she waited for me to bring it back around. I tried to keep it
turned, but there was a point where the weight of her hand became the
whole room. My ear folded against the book cover when she bore down. The
little shelf over my bed went sideways. I could feel the wet paper
sticking to the back of my arm. I managed to find the pencil mark I’d
made and tried to keep it where I could see it. She asked again, and I
said yes in the tiniest voice I could make, as if it would help her
understand how little I could see.

My dad was still kneeling there. His eyes were directed at the side of
Mom’s head, and there was a wet trail down the skin below one of them.
It had reached the corner of his mouth. I thought if I could tell him
how much I wanted to go back to that diner, he could remember how he’d
felt when we were there. Even then I couldn’t stand the idea of him
thinking I’d stopped loving him. I’d spent days looking for the right
thing to tell him, and I found it when I could barely draw enough air to
say a word. I told him he could have the whole piece of gum.

Mom said, “I didn’t hear you. Can you say that one more time?” She kept
her head over mine. There was a hair caught in the corner of her mouth.
Every time she breathed out, it lifted and settled against her cheek
again. I could have answered her once more. I remember deciding I could,
and deciding I’d rather save it for when I opened my eyes in bed and
someone came in to tell me where I was. Her hair lifted off her cheek

My mother made a noise I’d heard once before, when the door gave way
under her weight. That time it had come out of her through her nose.
This time her mouth was all the way open. Her fingers came off the floor
one by one. She said, louder than she’d ever spoken in our house, “It
was only a dream. It was just a dream. I’ll wake up soon and he’ll be
okay.” My father put one foot down flat on the carpet. There was a quiet
moment when he could finally get up and nothing in my mother’s face told
him what to do.

He went out to get his phone. He’d set it on the bathroom sink when he
came up to get the books. He picked it up and dropped it because his
fingers wouldn’t close all the way at first. When he called for help he
got the street name out and gave the house number twice. The woman on
the other end said she’d heard him and asked him to speak a little
slower because something was making it hard for him to talk. He said, “I
hurt my son.” He leaned against the sink, turned the phone around in his
hand, and said it again.

In my bedroom Mom had put two of her fingers against my palm. She kept
them there after she asked for two squeezes. When I was little and fell
asleep before I gave her the second one, she’d wait anyway. She always
waited until she was sure I was asleep. That night she wanted me to wake
up so badly that she kept offering me things her own mother would’ve
offered her. I could stay home from school. She’d call the office. I
wouldn’t have to explain it to anybody. She started saying what we could
have for breakfast and stopped to get her hurt arm out from under her.

Dad came back to the doorway with the phone in his hand. He asked whose
voice he was hearing. Mom turned toward him and said, “I was here the
whole time, Russ. I couldn’t move my hands.” “He could have the whole
day off tomorrow if he likes,” she said, with the care she used when she
thought he hadn’t heard her the first time. Dad made a little forward
movement with his chin. There was enough light for him to see the two
fingers in my hand. He asked her to show him the rest of her hand, and
she said she couldn’t move yet because I might squeeze. He tried once
more. She leaned down to tell me I could give her just one if that was
all I had in me.

He set the phone down in the doorway and went to her. She said his name
when he grabbed her. She had time to ask him why before he hit her
again. Her hand came all the way out of mine and lay open on the carpet,
with the two fingers still closer together than the others. After she
stopped moving, he stood over her listening to a small sound he thought
she was making. It was the woman on his phone trying to get him to
answer. He picked it up and asked if she was still there. She said yes,
she was. There was nothing right then to stop him going back to my bed.
He put the phone down in the same place, walked all the way to the
bathroom, and shut the door. By the time Bree got upstairs with the
people he’d called, he’d killed himself.

Bree told me later that she’d gone around the back after nobody
answered. There’d been a pair of Russ’s shoes outside the kitchen door,
where he carried them out to brush the grass off. She moved them aside
while she waited for him to let her in. She told me that part at the
hospital in a voice that made me think she’d lie there for a long time
picturing those shoes and wondering how she’d had the time to bother
with them. She got in when the first people came to the door with her.
They came up in heavy shoes and bumped one of the books I’d been
supposed to read over the threshold into the hall. I remember its cover
rising like a little ramp against the baseboard. I remember the first
breath better. My whole chest moved, and I found out how much air it
could take.

The room at the hospital was wider than my room at home, and when I
opened my eyes it took a while to find a corner. There was a little
strip of paint missing near the ceiling over the end of the bed.
Somebody had put a tall chair next to me with a brown sweatshirt folded
across the back. I knew whose it was before anybody came in. Bree kept
it on a hook in her kitchen when her house got cold at night, and there
was a place on the cuff where Biscuit had pulled a thread half an inch
out of the seam.

She told me my father’s call was why help came so quickly. She wanted me
to believe he’d tried to save me. I said I did, and her eye went to the
door even though there was nobody there. I think she’d been expecting
someone else to have to tell me that part. I asked her if she’d brought
Biscuit’s towel in off the couch. It was the first thing I asked for,
and her face moved in a way that let me see how hard it had been for her
to look at me while she waited for me to want something.

He’d been awake since the first breath. I could feel it best when Bree
said his father had called for help. He tried to say something at the
end of every sentence she spoke, as soon as there was room for her to
hear him. I knew what he wanted to ask. He wanted to know if his mother
had got to feel one of the squeezes before his hand went still. I could
have told him the answer. I asked Bree if she’d take me back to her
house when I got out. She said yes. She said she’d moved the dresser
into the spare room where my bed was going to go.

At some point a woman knocked and asked Bree to come out into the hall.
Bree stayed for another minute. She put two fingers in my palm and asked
me to squeeze twice if I wanted her to come right back. He’d been trying
with that hand since she first sat down, and he tried harder when he
felt where she’d put her fingers. I waited until she started to lift
them. Then I gave her one squeeze and took my time over the second, the
way he always had when he wanted his mother to stay a little longer.
Bree bent close enough for her hair to brush my cheek and said, “There
you are.”
