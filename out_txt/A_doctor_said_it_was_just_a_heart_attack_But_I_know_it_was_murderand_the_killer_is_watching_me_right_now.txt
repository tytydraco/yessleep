The room was small and packed. Photos, maps, and scribbled notes covered
the walls. Blue LED strips glowed along the ceiling. In the corner,
three monitors ran lines of green code in the dark.

“Scott! Get up! You’re going to be late!”

I sat straight up. No stretching, no groaning. Within a minute I was
showered, dressed, and downstairs.

My mother was rushing to pack her work bag, stressed the second she saw
me.

“Your sister already left,” she said. “You’d forget your own head if it
wasn’t attached.”

I studied her for a second — the mismatched earrings, a flour smudge on
her sleeve, a burn mark on the wooden spatula by the sink.

“You made blueberry pancakes,” I said.

She froze. “How do you always do that?”

“Wrong flour for bread. A blue stain on your thumb from the berries.
That spatula only comes out for pancakes. And the kitchen smells like
burnt sugar.”

She shook her head, grabbed her keys, told me to watch my sister
tonight, and left.

The bus was loud and packed. I watched the passengers instead of the
street — this was my favorite game. A man across from me had a fresh mud
stain on one shoe, the other spotless. He’d stepped in a puddle getting
out of a cab, the kind that only parks near the west-side construction
site. A lawyer, tapping his briefcase in sets of three. Stressed. A
teenage girl a few seats down had dried tears on her jacket sleeve,
opening and closing a messaging app every few seconds. A fight with her
boyfriend that morning, still waiting on an apology.

I got so lost in it that I nearly missed my stop.

“Stop the bus!”

The driver hit the brakes. I stumbled off the last step and hit the
sidewalk hard, papers scattering everywhere.

Laughter rang out above me. “Look at the freak! Can’t even walk
straight!”

William. A foot taller than me, built like a truck, always looking for a
reason to make my day worse. He stood with his crowd, grinning.

“Did your mom pick your shirt again?” I said, looking past his fists to
the collar. “The iron lines are straight on the back, crooked on the
front. Someone else pressed it on a board, but you put it on in a hurry.
And that safety pin holding your collar shut is there because the top
button’s broken. You don’t know how to fix a button. She does.”

His friends went quiet. His face turned red. He stepped closer — but the
bell cut him off.

“This isn’t over,” he said. “Watch your back.”

The first period was history. Under the desk, I opened my laptop while
Mr. Hugo lectured. The school’s Wi-Fi security was weak, and within
seconds I was inside the phones around me. Digital footprints told me
more than faces ever could.

Maya’s phone up front was crawling — her battery running hot, her screen
lagging. Buried in her storage was a fake file disguised as an exam
guide, quietly spamming her device with adware since she clicked the
wrong download link. Three taps, and it was gone. Her phone sped back
up.

Tyler’s phone showed two chat windows open side by side, sending the
exact same line — you’re the only girl who understands me — to two
different girls in the same minute. His real girlfriend sat two rows
behind him. I logged out. Not a mystery, just noise.

Chloe’s browser had a hidden tab running through a dark web marketplace,
a case of imported beer in her cart, paid for with her mother’s card
under a fake name. A party was coming, and her mother had no idea.

“Scott!” Mr. Hugo’s voice snapped back to me. “What are you doing on
that laptop?”

William stood up before I could answer. “He’s probably watching videos,
sir.”

“I’ve got a live database on the Cold War pulled up,” I said, and before
Mr. Hugo could call the bluff, I hijacked the smart board through its
open Bluetooth, slipped past a university login, and pulled up a live,
detailed map of the Cuban Missile Crisis — troop movements, coded
messages, all of it.

The class gasped. “Excellent work, Scott,” Mr. Hugo said. William’s face
twisted with anger.

At lunch, I hid in the library with an old Sherlock Holmes book. My
father taught me to read Holmes, to notice things nobody bothered with.
He taught me Linux before I turned eight. Then he left for a war zone
overseas and never came home.

On the bus home, the world faded into a black, silent space — the way it
did whenever I sank fully into a deduction. A stranger from Mr. Clark’s
usual route stood frozen in front of me. A red mark circled his wrist
from a dead smartwatch. His right hand clutched his pocket, hiding a
phone flashing a rent-overdue alert. Five lottery tickets sat in his
coat, the silver scratched clean by a coin. He’d spent his last money
chasing a jackpot he wasn’t going to win.

“Hey, kid.” Mr. Clark’s voice broke through, and the bus rushed back. A
small velvet box sat in his coat pocket. His browser history was full of
restaurant searches.

“Definitely, Mr. Clark,” I said, standing. “I think you’ll finally
propose tonight.”

He froze, mouth open, as I stepped off the bus.

Walking home, William was waiting, his friends already circling me.

“I’m still furious about this morning, Bell,” he said.

“I only told the truth,” I said. “You asked out three girls today, by
the way.”

He swung without warning. The punch caught my jaw and sent me stumbling,
blood at the corner of my mouth. He pulled back for another hit — I
ducked and drove a punch into his stomach. It turned into a real fight,
his weight crashing into me, one kick landing hard on my leg.

“Scott!”

Shelly’s voice cut through the street, and William froze like he’d been
unplugged. Everyone but him seemed to know about the crush he had on my
sister.

“Hi, Shelly. Just talking,” he said, forcing a smile.

She walked past him without a glance, grabbed my arm, and pulled me
toward the house. “Your leg’s bleeding. Come on.”

Inside, she cleaned the cut on my lip, scolding me for digging up
secrets and getting hit for it. I noticed a new silver bracelet on her
wrist, scuffed with the same gray marks a cricket bat grip leaves
behind, and white chalk still under her fingernails.

“Did your boyfriend win his match today?” I asked.

She froze, then pressed hard on my bruise out of pure embarrassment.
“How do you always do that?”

“I only observe, Shelly. Most people just look.”

Over pancakes, she mentioned a funeral tomorrow — Maria’s uncle had died
two days before.

“Daniel Rogers?” I asked.

She stared, stunned I already knew his name.

That night, I opened my laptop and pulled every public record I could
find on Daniel — a healthy, thirty-year-old engineer with no history of
heart trouble, dead of sudden cardiac arrest. No poison traces. No signs
of a struggle. Statistically, almost impossible.

Then a stray text from his wife, sent to a friend, stopped me cold: This
wasn’t natural. He said someone was trying to kill him.

I stood up and wrote his name across my wall in black marker, starting
the timeline. Whatever killed Daniel Rogers, his heart hadn’t given out
on its own.

In the cab, my mind sank into a black, silent space. Files, photos, and
CBC company data floated in front of me, sorted by name. Daniel’s
relatives would be too guarded to talk. His female coworkers wouldn’t
be. I locked onto two names: Sarah Jenkins, his project manager, sharp
and always chatting on the company network, and Elena Vance, the quiet
junior developer across from his desk — the one Daniel had stopped
liking her posts three weeks before he died. One of them would crack.

“Ethan’s not answering,” Shelly muttered, glaring at her phone.

“Girls are always like that,” I said, mostly to annoy her.

“Excuse me?”

I pointed out the window instead of answering. “We’re here.”

Inside, I skipped the service. I told Shelly I needed the washroom and
slipped outside instead, scanning the pews on my way past.

“That man there,” Shelly whispered, testing me, pointing at a stranger
in a blue suit. “Tell me about him.”

“Divorced. Left-handed — the scratch on his watch is on the inner side.
His wedding ring’s been off long enough to leave a pale line, and his
collar’s undone. He’s not here for Daniel. He just has nowhere else to
be on a Sunday.”

She stared at me, then pointed to a boy a few rows up. “And him?”

“Trying to text his girlfriend. His thumb won’t stop hovering over the
screen, but his mother clears her throat every time he moves the phone.
He’s stuck.”

She smirked. “You should spy on people for a living.”

I slipped out to the garden after that and found Sarah and Elena on a
bench, talking about nothing useful. So I made myself useful. I rubbed
my eyes red, staggered toward them, and let my body go limp on the grass
in front of them.

They panicked exactly the way I needed them to, hauling me into Sarah’s
car and dabbing water on my face. I opened my eyes slowly, playing
shaken.

“Are you okay, child?” Sarah asked, her voice full of worry.

“Yes, I am okay. I am so sorry,” I whispered, making my voice sound
weak.

“Oh, don’t be sorry, dear,” Elena said, shaking her head. “I think you
just fainted from the shock. You need something to eat. I have a
chocolate bar in my bag.” She quickly opened her purse, pulled out a
small chocolate bar, and handed it to him. “Eat this, child.”

“Thank you,” I said. He opened the wrapper and took a small bite,
pretending to recover.

“Was Daniel your relative?” Sarah asked.

“My uncle,” I said, letting my voice crack. “He was a good man .”

That was the hook. Grief loosens people faster than questions do.

“He wasn’t happy those last few days,” Elena admitted, glancing around
the car like the words might get her in trouble. “On his last day, he
went to show the boss his final project. When he came out of that
office, he looked terrified. That was the last time I saw him.”

I thanked them, gave them my first name, and stepped out of the car with
exactly what I needed. On the walk back, I ran the numbers in my head: a
sudden fear, tied to a final project, tied to a boss. Seventy percent
chance this was murder. The rest depended on what was hiding inside
Daniel’s laptop.

Back home, that laptop fought me harder than anything ever had. Five
separate hacking attempts failed outright. I nearly lost it, screaming
loud enough that Shelly came running.

“What’s wrong with you?” she asked, sitting on my bed.

“What do you do when you’re stuck on something?”

“Try a different angle. If that fails, try the stupidest idea you can
think of. And if that fails too, walk away for a bit.”

We ended up playing chess downstairs. I was losing badly, and when she
moved her Queen right up next to my King instead of attacking from a
distance, it hit me. I didn’t need to hack Daniel’s network remotely. I
needed to be standing right next to it.

That meant getting inside the house. So the next morning I faked being
sick to stay home alone, then took the metro straight to Daniel’s
street. From outside, the lights told me enough — one dark upstairs
room, no one able to easily reach it. That was where he’d died.

I checked Sarah and Elena’s phone locations first to make sure they
weren’t inside, then messed up my hair, put on glasses, and rang the
doorbell playing a lost kid looking for a neighbor.

Charlotte, Daniel’s widow, let me in to make a call. While she went to
find a phone, I memorized the Wi-Fi password off the router in ten
seconds flat, faked a failed call, thanked her, and left.

At the library, I built a fake smart doorbell, registered under
Charlotte’s own name, so the network would trust it without question.
Through it, I slipped into Daniel’s phone. Nothing. Then his laptop,
disguised the same way. Still nothing — someone had wiped it clean, and
they were good.

I gave up for lunch and let my head clear. Sherlock Holmes’ old line
came back to me: when you eliminate the impossible, whatever remains
must be the truth. I’d only been looking at the laptop. I checked
Daniel’s work records instead and found he hadn’t clocked in for two
days before he died. A man like him didn’t just skip work.

Back at the library, I dug through his cloud storage too. Still empty —
until I thought about encryption. Daniel’s desktop wallpaper was a photo
of his own living room, which made no sense for a man like him unless it
meant something. I remembered a quote painted into that wall in the same
color as the room, nearly invisible: something about family, mistakes,
second chances.

I typed the visible words into the password box first. Wrong. Then the
blurred ones Daniel had hidden in the photo, the ones meant to be
missed. That worked.

Inside was one file: The True Details of CBC. Daniel had found proof
that his own company was secretly selling stolen user data to hackers on
the dark web. He’d gone straight to his boss with the evidence instead
of the police first. The boss offered him money, then threatened him
when he refused. Daniel called the FBI, who told him to come in person.
His last written line stayed with me: I don’t know what might happen to
me. I feel like I’m in danger. I’ll try again tomorrow.

He never got the chance.

Digging further, I found a deleted section of code hidden inside his
smart wireless charger — a Trojan virus, built to spike the charger’s
voltage to its limit without warning. I remembered the tiny blister on
his fingertip at the funeral. High voltage meeting wet skin does exactly
that. Daniel had reached for his phone that night, touched the charger,
and the surge went straight through his hand and heart. His pulse
stopped in under a second. No poison. No struggle. Just a doctor calling
it a heart attack, because that’s exactly what it looked like from the
outside.

I traced the virus, chasing its trail toward whoever had planted it. The
progress bar hit ninety percent.

Then the screen went black.

Every light on the tower died at once. Someone on the other end had
noticed me watching, and they’d killed my connection cold. A second
later the screen flickered back on, just long enough to show me one line
of text:

“Welcome to our world, Neophyte Genius.”

Then the whole machine popped, sparked, and died for good.
