My girlfriend Jenna and I were driving down from Portland to attend her
college friend’s wedding outside Bend. We planned to leave before noon,
but my work kept me at my desk until five. By the time I loaded our bags
into the trunk of my Civic, the sun was already down behind the
mountains.

Jenna called her friend to tell her we were running late. Her friend
told us to stick to Interstate 5 down to Eugene and cross over. She
specifically told us to avoid Route 126 through the county forest after
dark, explaining that cell reception cut out completely and people had
reported strangers tampering with stopped cars out there. Jenna told her
we had a full tank and a printed map, so we were taking the forest
cut-off anyway to save forty minutes.

By nine forty-five, the overhead highway lights ended. The road narrowed
down to two cracked lanes of black pavement flanked by dense pine trees.
There were no oncoming cars, no tail lights ahead of us, and no
reflectors on the center line. Jenna’s phone lost its network connection
first, the signal bars dropping to zero and staying there. My phone
dropped its signal two miles later. The dashboard clock read ten
twenty-two when our high beams hit a dark green sedan parked halfway
onto the dirt shoulder.

The driver-side door of the green car was swung wide open into our lane.
A gray work jacket lay in the gravel by the front tire. I slowed the
Civic down to fifteen miles an hour and steered around the open door.
Jenna leaned forward against the dash, staring out into the dark timber
beside the car. She said she thought she saw someone sitting down on the
embankment.

I told her the car was abandoned and we needed to keep moving, but she
insisted we stop to check if someone was hurt in the ditch. I pulled
over thirty feet past the sedan, shifted into park, and left the engine
running with the hazard lights on.

I stepped out onto the shoulder, leaving our headlights pointed down the
empty road. The air was forty-two degrees and smelled of damp pine
needles and wet asphalt. I walked back toward the green car with my
phone light on.

The inside of the car was completely empty. The ignition was intact, but
there were no keys in it. A half-eaten sandwich and an open road atlas
sat on the passenger seat. When I aimed the light across the
floorboards, I saw the interior trunk release lever next to the driver
seat was pulled all the way up.
I turned around to head back to my car. Jenna was watching me through
our back window. Right as I reached my driver door, she cracked her
window two inches and told me to get in right now. She said the trunk of
the green car had just lifted open while my back was turned. I didn’t
stop to look. I yanked the door open, dropped into the driver seat,
slammed it shut, and hit the power locks.

The engine was running fine, but when I dropped the shifter into drive,
the Civic didn’t roll forward. The transmission was engaged, but the
front tires just spun in the loose gravel with a heavy, grinding
shudder, held tight from behind. I threw the shifter into reverse, hit
the gas hard to get two feet of slack, slammed it back into drive, and
cut the steering wheel hard toward the asphalt. The rear frame buckled
with a sharp metal snap, the front tires caught the pavement, and the
car broke free, accelerating down the lane.

Jenna kept her eyes locked on the rear window glass. She told me a man
in a brown coat had stepped out from behind the green car and was
standing in the road right where we had just been parked. He didn’t yell
or run. He just stood upright in the middle of the road with both hands
in his pockets, watching our tail lights until the road bent around a
rock wall and cut him off.

Three miles down the road, an overhead green sign announced a rest area
two miles ahead. The trip odometer showed forty-six miles since our last
turn. I kept the speedometer locked at fifty miles an hour. Five minutes
went by, but the rest area never appeared. The trees on both sides
looked identical to the stretch we had just passed, with the same broken
yellow road lines and the same fallen timber in the ditch. At eleven
zero-four, the headlights picked up another highway sign on the
shoulder. It was the exact same sign, stating the rest area was two
miles ahead.

Jenna told me to slow down because the dashboard display had frozen up.
The map graphic went gray, displaying a system error that said no
position data was available under the canopy. I tapped the screen, but
it stayed locked. Ahead of us, on the right shoulder, our headlights
caught a pair of hazard lights flashing in the distance. As we closed
the gap, I saw the back end of a dark green sedan.
The driver door was standing wide open into the lane. The gray jacket
was still lying on the gravel beside the front wheel.

I didn’t tap the brakes. I crossed the double yellow line into the
oncoming lane to give the sedan plenty of space. As the headlights swept
across the driver side, I looked straight into the car. A man was
sitting in the driver seat now, both hands gripping the wheel, staring
dead ahead through the windshield. He didn’t turn his head toward our
lights at all. Jenna grabbed my forearm and told me not to stop for
anything. I pushed the gas pedal flat against the floor. The speedometer
jumped to sixty-five, then seventy.

The asphalt ran out without any warning signs, switching straight to
hard dirt and loose rocks. The steering wheel shook hard in my grip as
the tires hit washboard ruts. We followed that unpaved path for another
twenty minutes without passing a single house, gate, or marker. At
eleven thirty-eight, the trees cleared out and the dirt tied into a
wide, paved state highway with overhead halogen streetlights and white
mile posts. A yellow sign showed an intersection with State Route 20 one
mile out.
We turned north on Route 20 and pulled into a twenty-four-hour gas
station twenty minutes later. The station had three brightly lit pump
islands and a glass convenience store. I parked under the canopy right
beneath a security camera, shut the motor off, and pocketed the keys.

Jenna stayed locked in the car while I walked inside to talk to the
attendant. An older guy in an orange safety vest was stocking drinks in
the back coolers. I told him we had just driven through the forest
cut-off from Route 126 and passed the same stopped car twice on a loop.
He dropped the plastic bottles and walked behind the counter. He told me
the county road commission had closed that entire cut-off three weeks
ago because of collapsed culverts, but people kept dragging the wood
barricades out of the way into the brush, and nobody had any business
being back there after dark. He picked up the desk phone and called
county dispatch to report a vehicle inside the closed stretch.

Two sheriff deputies arrived at the station fifteen minutes later. They
took our names, ran my license, and checked around the Civic with
flashlights. While the younger deputy took notes on a clipboard, the
older one crouched down by my passenger rear wheel well. He called us
over to the bumper.
Caught tight in the gap between the bumper plastic and the rear quarter
panel was a three-foot length of heavy yellow tow strap with a steel
hook on the end. The hook was looped into our car’s lower frame bracket,
placed there while we were parked on the shoulder. The strap had sheared
clean through under heavy tension, with the torn nylon threads dragging
against the concrete.
