For six weeks now, I’m suffering from a new kind of migraine attack.
They don’t announce themselves through light sensitivity, but through a
strange feeling of fuzziness: It feels as if my skull exists as the only
point in the room shifted by exactly two millimeters to the left.

Being an acoustics engineer, I started to systematically log the
pressure. I used equipment from my lab: A highly sensitive
structure-borne sound sensor, which we normally use for vibration tests
on machine parts, was taped directly to my left temple and wired to an
oscilloscope on my desk.
Yesterday evening, the attack was so heavy that my left eye couldn’t
align the picture with the right one anymore. I switched on the
measuring device.
The oscilloscope instantly spiked.

It wasn’t biological noise. The display showed a constant,
high-frequency sawtooth wave at exactly 14,200 Hertz. But the disturbing
thing was the signal strength: The amplitude wasn’t located inside my
head. The vibration was coming from the outside, focused inward through
my temporal bone.
To damp the oscillation, I took a massive brass tuning stock from my
workbench and pressed the heavy metal firmly against the angle of my
jaw. I wanted to bleed off the sound over the bone structure.

A fatal mistake.

The moment the brass took over the resonance frequency, the tone
shifted. The swelling didn’t rise, it flipped.

The frequency tore open the geometry of my ear canal. A shearing, wet
pain shot like a glowing needle deep into my inner ear. The sudden
pressure shift inside the channel completely ruptured the tissue of the
eardrum and the delicate membranes behind it. Blood mixed with clear
inner ear fluid immediately ran out of my ear and dripped onto my
sweater.

My sense of balance collapsed on the spot. The room didn’t spin—it lost
its horizontals.

I fell off my chair because the floor under my feet suddenly seemed to
stand at a 30-degree angle, even though the floorboards visually stayed
level. My brain tried desperately to process two contradicting spatial
vectors at the same time.

I’m lying on the carpet, unable to straighten myself up. Every time I
try to lift my head, my body jerks to the left, as if an invisible
centrifugal force is pressing me against the wall.

The oscilloscope on the table is still running. The wave on the screen
is flat now. But coming from the speaker of the measuring device, I can
now clearly hear the sound of heavy, irregular breathing.
It breathes in the exact same frequency that was sitting inside my skull
just moments ago.

Then, the breathing from the speaker stopped abruptly.

Instead, the oscilloscope clicked. A very short, mechanical relay click,
like when switching a frequency band.

The piercing pain in my ear gave way to a numb, icy coldness. Blood and
fluid ran in spurts down my neck into my collar. When I blinked, my
brain desperately tried to focus the picture again—but the double vision
remained.

I crawled on all fours up to the desk, pulled myself up by the wood, and
reached for my phone. My hand was shaking so badly that the display
slammed onto the floor three times before I managed to dial emergency
services.

“Emergency dispatcher, what’s your location?”

I wanted to answer. I wanted to tell the dispatcher my address, but only
a wet, gurgling whistle came out of my mouth. The sounds no longer
formed words. My vocal cords vibrated in the exact, unnatural rhythm of
the sawtooth wave.
On the other end of the line, a long silence occurred.

“Hello? Can you hear me? We have a static interference on the line.”

I pressed my hand against my larynx, trying to force the muscles, but my
body obeyed the mechanics of my skull, no longer my will. Through the
receiver, I heard the operator swear under his breath: “What is that
whistling? That hurts in the ears.”
He hung up.

On the desk, the fan of the oscilloscope quietly hummed along. The
baseline on the screen was completely flat again.

The headache is gone. The pressure behind my eye has completely
disappeared. But when I try to speak now, to breathe, or to tap my
fingers on the wood, I no longer produce human noises.

I sit in the dark on the floor of my study. From the hallway outside, I
hear my upstairs neighbor coming down the stairs. I slam my fist against
the door to draw attention to myself.

He stops briefly. Then he curses quietly about the “broken, buzzing
power line” in the hallway and walks on.

They won’t find me. Because to anyone out there, I am no longer a
neighbor crying for help—just an annoying background noise in the wall.
