My family kept a woman locked in our spare bedroom. She gave us
everything we asked for.

Growing up, my family had the ugliest kind of good luck.

It never looked like winning the lottery. It looked like my father
finding four hundred dollars behind the toilet in a gas-station bathroom
the morning our electricity was supposed to be disconnected.

It looked like refund checks from companies we had never paid.

It looked like relatives we had never met dying without children and
leaving us exactly enough money to replace the transmission, fix the
roof, or cover whichever hospital bill had frightened Dad into silence.

Our luck always arrived at the last possible moment, muddy-kneed and
smelling faintly of something rotten.

Dad called it providence.

“Things work out for this family,” he used to say. “They always have.”

Our house had belonged to us for generations. It was a modest brick
place with three bedrooms, two bathrooms, a cramped kitchen, and glass
doors opening onto the backyard. Dad spoke about it as though it were
another member of the family.

Someday, it would belong to either me or my younger brother, Jonathan.

Someday, we would raise our own children there.

Someday, we would understand what it meant to protect the things we had
been given.

One bedroom stood at the far end of the hallway.

We never called it the guest room.

We called it the Room.

Its door stayed locked. Dad carried the key in his wallet. Jonathan and
I weren’t allowed to touch the knob, knock, or stand too close to it.

When I was little, those rules seemed no stranger than being told not to
play with the stove.

Sometimes, late at night, I heard the bed inside squeaking.

Once, I heard someone laugh.

I told myself it was the plumbing.

When I was eight, my mother died in the laundry closet.

She had been packing because we were supposed to move. Dad had found a
better job in another city, and most of our belongings were already
sealed inside cardboard boxes.

That night, Dad took Jonathan and me to a baseball game. Mom stayed home
because she hated crowds.

The game went into the ninth inning tied. Our team won on a walk-off
walk, which Dad declared the funniest possible way for a baseball game
to end. He bought us ice cream on the drive home.

We were still laughing when we opened the front door.

The kitchen faucet was running.

Something burned on the stove.

We found Mom between the washing machine and the wall.

The police called it an accident. She had slipped, struck her head
against the metal shelf, and died before we came home.

We did not move after that.

Dad unpacked every box himself.

For months, he made two cups of coffee each morning. He left one
untouched beside the sink until it went cold. Jonathan and I kept
expecting Mom to walk through the door, embarrassed by the
misunderstanding.

That was the first time I learned that hope can survive long after it
becomes cruel.

Two years later, someone broke into our house while we were visiting our
grandparents.

The burglars took almost everything they could carry: our television, my
game console, the microwave, Dad’s tools, even Jonathan’s jar of
quarters.

The police dusted the windows and wrote down a list of missing property.
They did not sound optimistic.

That night, Jonathan cried while staring at the empty space above the
fireplace.

“Why would they take our stuff?”

Dad pulled him against his side.

“It’ll work out,” he said. “You know it will.”

Then he sent us to bed.

As we walked down the hallway, I heard a low groan from behind the
Room’s door.

Not laughter. Not bedsprings.

Pain.

I stopped.

Jonathan slipped past me without noticing and climbed onto the top bunk
in our bedroom.

I stood beside the locked door, listening.

Another groan came from inside. It was followed by a series of wet,
rubbery squeaks.

I nearly touched the knob.

Then I imagined Dad catching me and hurried away.

Sometime after midnight, the Room opened. I heard Dad whispering,
although I couldn’t make out the words. The door shut again. The lock
clicked.

The following morning, Jonathan woke me by screaming from the backyard.

Every item the burglars had taken was sitting on the grass.

The television rested on folded towels. The microwave had been wrapped
in a bedsheet. Everything else was packed into cardboard boxes, neatly
organized, without so much as a scratch.

A yellow note had been taped to the largest box.

The writing was red and watery.

WE’RE SORRY.

PLEASE DON’T LET HER FIND US AGAIN.

Dad tore the note apart before I finished reading it.

The police assumed the burglars had lost their nerve.

Dad said it was proof.

“Things work out for us,” he reminded me.

For a long time, I believed him.

I was eleven when Dad finally admitted that someone lived in the Room.

It happened during dinner.

Jonathan had spent the afternoon poking me, stealing my homework, and
singing the same three lines of a cartoon theme until I wanted to throw
him through a window.

“I wish I had my own bedroom,” I said.

Dad stopped eating.

“You can’t have the Room.”

“I didn’t say anything about the Room.”

His fork remained suspended over his plate.

Jonathan looked between us. “What’s in there?”

“No more questions.”

“Is it just storage?” I asked.

“Someone lives there.”

Jonathan laughed. “No, they don’t. Where do they use the bathroom?”

Dad’s chair scraped backward.

He grabbed my wrist and hauled me into his bedroom.

The beating he gave me was different from the spankings I had received
before. There was no lecture. No performance of discipline. He struck me
as if he were trying to drive something out of my body.

When he finished, he stared at the wall rather than at me.

“You leave that door alone,” he said. “Some rules keep people alive.”

He beat Jonathan next.

That should have cured our curiosity.

Instead, it made the Room feel alive.

I dreamed about it almost every night.

In one dream, red light spilled from beneath the door. When I opened it,
I found myself standing on a stage before thousands of people. They
pointed and laughed while the walls fell away around me.

In another, I heard Mom calling from inside.

She never asked me to save her.

She only said she was cold.

Jonathan had nightmares too. He began sleeping with his blanket pulled
over his head. Some mornings, his eyes were swollen from crying.

One night, I heard him gasp awake above me.

“What was it?” I whispered.

“A dragon.”

“In the Room?”

He climbed down and sat on the edge of my mattress.

“It breathed fire at me. It said Dad was going to feed me to it.”

“Dad wouldn’t do that.”

Jonathan didn’t answer.

I don’t know whether I said the next part because I believed it or
because I needed him to.

“He’d protect us.”

That night, we made a plan.

Dad had bought us a trampoline because he worried we were spending our
childhood indoors. Whenever he joined us, he emptied his pockets before
climbing onto it.

The key to the Room stayed in his wallet.

The next evening, Jonathan asked him to play catch on the trampoline.
Dad looked exhausted, but the request pleased him. He grabbed a football
and followed us outside.

I pretended I needed the bathroom.

By the time I reached the yard, Dad and Jonathan were already bouncing.
Dad’s wallet, shoes, phone, and keys lay in a pile beside the
trampoline.

I crouched beside them and pretended to untie my sneakers.

My hands shook so badly that I could barely open the wallet.

The key was hidden behind a photograph of Mom.

It was small, dark with rust, and strangely heavy. When I closed my fist
around it, I felt something pulse against my palm.

I almost put it back.

Then the trampoline springs groaned above me.

I shoved the key into my pocket and climbed up to join them.

We waited until midnight.

Dad’s snoring vibrated through his bedroom door. Jonathan followed me
down the hallway with one hand gripping the back of my shirt.

At the Room, he stopped me before I touched the knob.

“It might be hot.”

“What?”

“The dragon.”

He pressed the back of his own hand against the metal.

When nothing happened, he whispered, “It’s okay.”

“Why did you touch it first?”

“So you wouldn’t get burned.”

I remember that more clearly than almost anything else.

My little brother was terrified, but he touched the door first.

The key turned easily.

The Room smelled like damp fabric and old pennies.

Its carpet was yellow-gray, unlike the hardwood in the rest of the
hallway. The walls were cracked plaster. There were no windows, closets,
shelves, or decorations.

There was only a narrow wooden bed.

Someone was lying on it.

Jonathan made a noise like air escaping a balloon.

I found the switch.

A weak bulb flickered overhead.

At first, I thought the woman on the bed was dead. Then I decided she
wasn’t a woman at all.

She looked homemade.

Her body was nearly five feet long and wrapped in a faded green dress.
Her skin had the pale, porous texture of old candle wax. Gray hair hung
from her scalp in uneven clumps. Someone had made her nose by pushing
two holes into the center of her face.

Her eyes were too large for their sockets.

Black pupils floated in pools of milky glass.

Her mouth was a narrow cut.

“Jake,” Jonathan whispered. “Let’s go.”

I should have listened.

Instead, I moved closer.

The woman’s body was covered in seams. Thick black thread crossed her
wrists, throat, elbows, and knees. Some of the stitches had sunk so
deeply that pale flesh bulged around them.

At the base of her neck was a small brass plaque.

JANICE

I reached toward it.

Her head snapped in my direction.

The bed shrieked beneath her.

“Young ones,” she said without opening her mouth. “Which of you came to
be generous?”

Jonathan fled.

I couldn’t move.

Janice sat up. Her limbs unfolded with the dry clicking of sticks
breaking underfoot. Her neck lengthened, carrying her face toward mine.

“Must be you.”

The line of her mouth split open.

There were no teeth inside. Only pink gums and a tongue as narrow and
black as a leech.

She took my hand into her mouth.

Her lips tightened around my wrist.

I screamed and pulled, but her neck stretched farther from the bed,
drawing me deeper into the Room. The bones in my shoulder ground
together.

Then Jonathan came running back.

He struck her across the head with Dad’s baseball bat.

The sound was hollow.

Janice released me.

Jonathan grabbed my shirt, and we tumbled into the hallway together. I
slammed the door and turned the key.

Something hit the other side hard enough to shake dust from the frame.

Dad stood behind us in his underwear.

His face looked empty.

“Oh,” he said. “Oh, boys. What have you done?”

Jonathan ran to him.

“She tried to eat Jake!”

Dad didn’t look at the bruises around my wrist.

He stared at the door.

“I can fix it,” he whispered.

That was the night he told us the truth.

Janice had belonged to our family longer than the house had. Dad didn’t
know who made her. Every generation inherited a different story.

One ancestor claimed she had been stitched together from the clothing of
three women hanged for witchcraft. Another said she was built by a
widower who wanted his wife returned to him. Dad’s father believed
Janice had never been made at all—that she had simply appeared one
winter, lying in a child’s bed.

The details changed.

The agreement did not.

Janice protected the family living in the house. She drew fortune toward
us and misfortune away. She could recover stolen property, heal
sickness, ruin enemies, and provide money when money was desperately
needed.

But she had to be fed.

“Fed what?” I asked.

Dad looked at my arm.

“Time.”

I thought he meant hours. Someone had to sit with her, perhaps. Keep her
company.

Dad explained that Janice did not experience time the way we did. She
could not make any of her own.

She had to take it from us.

A scraped knee might cost an afternoon.

A few hundred dollars might cost a week.

A life saved could cost years.

“What happens to the time?” Jonathan asked.

Dad was quiet for so long that I thought he wouldn’t answer.

“You live it with her.”

Mom’s death returned to me in pieces. The boxes. The running faucet. The
Room waiting at the end of the hallway.

“What happened to Mom?”

“We were leaving,” Dad said.

Jonathan began to cry.

“Janice doesn’t tolerate abandonment.”

“You said Mom slipped.”

“She did.”

“Because of Janice?”

Dad covered his face.

“I knew Janice was angry. I knew something would happen, but I kept
packing anyway. Your mother wanted to leave, and I was too much of a
coward to tell her why we couldn’t.”

“She killed Mom,” I said.

“No.”

“You just said—”

“It was my fault.”

Dad gripped my shoulders.

The desperation in his face frightened me more than Janice had.

“She is good for us, Jake. Do you understand? Look at everything we have
survived. Look at Jonathan. Look at yourself.”

“She tried to swallow my arm.”

“You frightened her.”

“She killed Mom.”

His hand rose.

I flinched.

Dad lowered it again.

“We are a lucky family,” he said. “And lucky families know better than
to be ungrateful.”

Bad things began happening almost immediately.

The car door slammed on Jonathan’s hand when no one was near it. Two
fingers broke.

A windless tree fell across our trampoline.

Dad lost control of his truck on a dry road and missed a concrete
divider by less than an inch.

At night, Janice moaned behind the locked door. The sound carried
through the walls like an injured animal trapped beneath the house.

Dad stopped sleeping.

Then Jonathan fell from the monkey bars at school.

He needed twenty-five stitches across his forehead.

A week later, he became sick.

At first, Dad called it food poisoning. By the third night, Jonathan
could no longer sit up without help. He vomited until there was nothing
left in him. His skin turned gray, and his eyes looked too large for his
face.

Dad took him to the hospital.

He returned alone before dawn.

I sat up in bed.

“Where’s Jon?”

“The doctors don’t know what’s wrong.”

“Is he okay?”

“They think his organs are shutting down.”

The room tilted.

“No.”

“He can recover.”

Dad sat on the lower bunk, facing away from me.

“Janice can fix him.”

I understood before he turned around.

“No.”

“You love your brother.”

“No.”

“This family has carried a responsibility for generations. I was younger
than you when my father brought me into that room.”

“I’m eleven.”

“You’re old enough to understand sacrifice.”

I tried to run past him.

Dad caught me.

He held my wrist so tightly that the bones shifted beneath his fingers.

“This is what men do,” he said. “We protect our families.”

He walked me down the hallway.

I screamed. I begged. I called him every terrible word I knew.

He kept repeating the same promise.

“I won’t let anything happen to you.”

He unlocked the Room and pushed me inside.

Then he shut the door.

I heard the lock turn.

Janice sat at the edge of the bed.

She looked different than before. Fuller. The cracks in her skin had
closed, and her gray hair now reached her shoulders.

“Come here, Jakey.”

I hammered against the door.

“Dad!”

No answer.

Janice began counting backward from ten.

At seven, I promised to do anything else.

At five, I begged her to take me instead of Jonathan, although I didn’t
understand what that meant.

At three, she said my brother’s name.

I went to her.

“Sit,” she said.

I sat beside her.

She took my hand.

The Room disappeared.

Suddenly, I was standing in our kitchen.

Sunlight poured through the windows. Mom stood at the counter with her
back to me, humming as she spread peanut butter across a piece of bread.

For one beautiful second, I thought everything had been a nightmare.

Then Mom turned around.

Her face belonged to Janice.

“Give me a day,” she said.

The kitchen changed.

I was at school, sitting through a lesson I didn’t remember attending.
Then I was home again. Then asleep. Then standing beneath a summer sun.

The days passed faster and faster.

I watched myself grow taller in the reflection of the bathroom mirror. I
watched birthdays vanish. Christmas mornings flickered and died. Seasons
turned like pages beneath someone’s thumb.

Through it all, Janice remained beside me.

Sometimes she wore Mom’s face.

Sometimes Dad’s.

Sometimes my own.

“A year,” she whispered.

I tried to pull away.

Her fingers tightened.

“A year for your brother.”

I lived twelve months in the time it took my body outside the vision to
draw a breath.

I remember every day of that false year, but none of it happened. I
remember attending a school that never existed. I remember friends whose
names vanish when I try to speak them. I remember a little white dog
sleeping at the foot of my bed, although we never owned a dog.

I remember Mom being alive.

That was the cruelest part.

When the Room returned, I was lying on the floor.

Dad did not let me out for almost an hour.

Jonathan began recovering that morning.

The doctors called it miraculous.

Within a week, he came home.

No one asked what the miracle had cost.

After that, Dad took me to Janice every Sunday night.

Sometimes she took only an hour.

Sometimes she took weeks.

I would emerge from the Room to discover that no time had passed
outside, yet I carried months of memories that belonged to lives I had
never lived.

Janice gave me children who called me Dad, then took them away.

She let me grow old beside women I loved and returned me to childhood
without them.

She made me attend Mom’s funeral in a hundred different worlds.

In some, the casket was empty.

In others, Mom sat up during the service and asked why I had allowed
them to bury her.

Every visit left me less certain which life was real.

I began forgetting things that had actually happened.

Jonathan would mention a vacation, and I would remember three different
versions.

Dad would ask me to bring him a tool from the garage, and I would stand
there staring at shelves I had known my whole life.

Once, I called Jonathan by the name of a brother who had never existed.

Our luck improved.

Dad received a promotion.

A pipe burst at school fifteen minutes after Jonathan left the flooded
classroom.

A tumor vanished between my grandmother’s first scan and her second.

Someone mailed us five thousand dollars in cash with no return address.

Dad praised Janice.

Jonathan praised God.

I stopped believing in either of them.

When I was thirteen, Dad lost his job.

He called me into his bedroom.

“I need you to visit her more often,” he said.

I stared at him.

“Just until we get back on our feet.”

That was the moment I understood there would never be enough.

At first, the Room had been necessary to save Jonathan’s life.

Then it became necessary to protect Dad’s job.

Next, it would be the mortgage. A medical bill. A broken appliance. A
desire disguised as an emergency.

Every good thing in our lives would become another reason to give Janice
pieces of me.

“Of course,” I said. “Whatever the family needs.”

Dad smiled with relief.

That night, I played cards with Jonathan on our bedroom floor.

We laughed until our stomachs hurt. After Dad fell asleep, we watched an
old cartoon on VHS and ate cereal directly from the box.

I wanted my final memory of my brother to be a happy one.

When Jonathan fell asleep, I packed a backpack and left.

I walked until sunrise.

Then I hitchhiked.

For three days, I accepted rides from anyone who would take me farther
from home. I slept in the cab of a truck and beneath a playground slide.
I stole food from a convenience store and vomited from guilt afterward.

A police officer found me asleep behind an elementary school.

He asked for my name.

I started crying.

“Please don’t make me go home.”

His expression changed.

He knelt several feet away rather than touching me.

“You’re not in trouble,” he said.

I didn’t believe him.

“My job is to keep you safe.”

I didn’t believe that either.

But I went with him.

Authorities eventually identified me. Dad drove hundreds of miles to
retrieve me, insisting that I had become disturbed after Mom’s death.

When I saw him at the station, I screamed until I couldn’t breathe.

I never told anyone about Janice.

I said only that Dad had locked me in a room and allowed someone to hurt
me.

That was enough to begin an investigation.

It was not enough to convict him of anything.

A judge placed me in foster care. Later, I was adopted by a couple who
lived several states away.

Dad sent letters for years.

Most contained the same phrases.

You don’t understand what you’ve done.

Your brother needs you.

This family cannot survive without sacrifice.

I burned every letter unopened after the first few.

My life afterward was normal in the way a room can look normal after
someone has cleaned up the blood.

I finished school. I found work. I made friends.

But my memories never settled.

I remember graduating from three different colleges, though I attended
only one.

I remember being married for eleven years to a woman named Claire. I can
see the freckle beneath her left eye. I remember the small, tired sound
she made when she laughed.

Claire does not exist.

I searched for her.

No birth certificate. No social media accounts. No marriage license.

Sometimes, I miss her so badly that I have to pull my car over.

I also remember dying.

More than once.

For years, I tried to convince myself that these were dreams Janice had
planted inside me.

Dreams fade, though.

These memories have aged with me.

I gave up looking for Jonathan.

That is the part I cannot forgive.

I knew Dad would eventually need another person to keep Janice
satisfied. I knew there was only one child left in the house.

But I had escaped.

For the first time in my life, I was safe.

I told myself Jonathan might be safe too. Maybe Dad had found another
solution. Maybe Janice preferred the eldest son. Maybe the bargain had
ended when I crossed the state line.

Cowardice can sound almost reasonable when you rehearse it long enough.

Twenty-one years passed.

Then, last Thursday, I received a package.

There was no return address.

Inside was a key.

Small. Rusted. Unnaturally heavy.

I dropped it as soon as I recognized it.

Beneath the key was an envelope containing three things.

The first was a newspaper clipping announcing my father’s death.

The second was the deed to our childhood home.

The house now belongs to me.

The third was a photograph.

Jonathan stood in the Room.

He was thirty-four in the picture, but I recognized him immediately. The
scar from the monkey bars cut a pale line across his forehead.

He looked painfully old.

Not merely tired. Old.

His hair was white. His face sagged around his skull. His hands looked
like our grandfather’s hands had looked shortly before he died.

Behind him, Janice lay on the bed.

She looked almost human.

Her hair was long and golden. Her skin was smooth. Her green dress
looked newly sewn.

Jonathan had written on the back.

Dad is gone.

I don’t know how old I am anymore.

She says the house belongs to you now.

Please come home before she takes the years where I knew you.

I called the police in my hometown.

They searched the house.

It was empty.

There was no Jonathan.

There was no bed in the locked bedroom.

The officer I spoke to sounded irritated. He said the Room contained
nothing but stained carpet and an old green dress.

Yesterday, I received another package.

This one held a photograph of my current house.

It had been taken from across the street.

Last night, I woke to a bed squeaking somewhere inside my walls.

I live alone.

I searched every room twice. I checked the attic, the closets, and the
crawlspace beneath the house.

There was nothing.

This morning, the rusted key was no longer on my kitchen counter.

I found it inside my wallet, tucked behind the only photograph I have of
Jonathan.

A few minutes ago, someone knocked from inside my spare bedroom.

Three slow knocks.

Then my brother said my name.

I know it isn’t him.

I know that.

But he sounds exactly as he did when we were children.

He keeps telling me the knob isn’t hot.

He keeps saying he’ll touch it first.

The door has started opening.

Every time I look away, the gap becomes a little wider.

I can see yellow-gray carpet on the other side.

I can smell old pennies.

Jonathan is crying now.

He says Janice has forgotten what I look like.

He says that means I still have time.

I don’t know whether he means time to run or time to remember him.

But something just reached through the gap.

It is holding a baseball bat.

And the hand wrapped around it is small enough to belong to an
eight-year-old boy.
