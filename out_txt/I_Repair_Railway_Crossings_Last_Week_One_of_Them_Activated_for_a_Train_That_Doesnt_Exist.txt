I’ve spent six years repairing railway signalling equipment, and until
last Thursday I would have told you there was nothing mysterious about a
level crossing activating by itself.

Annoying, yes. Potentially dangerous. But not mysterious.

Water gets into junction boxes. Insulated joints fail. Track circuits
drop out. Rodents chew things they shouldn’t. Lightning can leave
equipment behaving strangely for days afterwards.

There is always a reason.

That was what I kept telling myself at 2:13 in the morning while I stood
beside an empty stretch of track, watching both crossing gates lower in
front of absolutely nothing.

The red lights began flashing.

The bell started ringing.

Then, through the soles of my boots, I felt something moving along the
rails towards me.

There was no train on the network.

There wasn’t even supposed to be one within thirty kilometres.

And according to the recordings I’ve listened to since, I made one
mistake that night.

I opened the crossing.

I’m posting this because the fault has appeared somewhere else.

I work for a contractor that handles signalling maintenance for several
regional lines. I’m deliberately keeping the location vague because I
still have my job, technically, and because there is now an internal
investigation involving people much higher up the food chain than me.

The crossing was nothing special.

Single track.

Two lane road.

Boom gates, flashing lights, audible warning bells. Bush on one side, a
few scattered houses on the other. The nearest town was maybe ten
minutes away.

At 1:36 a.m., our control room received a notification that the crossing
had entered an active state without an associated train movement.

That sounds dramatic if you don’t work around signalling equipment.

It isn’t.

A crossing doesn’t need to “see” the train itself. Depending on the
setup, it detects occupancy or approaching rail vehicles through track
circuits or axle counting equipment and begins the warning sequence
before the train reaches the road.

Faulty detection can make the system think something is there when it
isn’t.

The safe failure state is generally inconvenient on purpose.

If the equipment becomes uncertain, you want the gates down rather than
up.

I was the closest technician.

My supervisor, Mark, called while I was driving.

“Probably moisture,” he said.

“It hasn’t rained.”

“Condensation, then.”

“Thirty degrees yesterday.”

“Then a possum pissed in it. I don’t know, mate. Go look.”

The control centre had reset the crossing remotely once. It returned to

No train movements.

No maintenance vehicles.

Nothing authorised on the track.

When I arrived, the gates were up.

The road was empty.

The crossing looked completely normal.

I parked inside the maintenance area, put on my high visibility clothing
and contacted control before entering the rail corridor.

For anyone unfamiliar with railway work, you don’t simply wander onto
tracks because someone told you there aren’t any trains around.

Possession authorities, track protection and network rules exist because
“there shouldn’t be a train” is a very efficient way to get killed.

Control confirmed that the section was clear.

I started with the equipment cabinet.

No obvious water ingress.

No burnt components.

No animal damage.

The voltages looked reasonable.

I checked the event logger.

The crossing had activated at 1:21.

Reset at 1:22.

Activated again at 1:36.

Then returned to standby at 1:43 without anyone resetting it.

That last part bothered me.

Faults clear themselves all the time, but the sequence looked clean.

Too clean.

The system wasn’t recording a generic failure.

It was recording an approach.

Something was entering the detection section, progressing towards the
crossing, passing through it and leaving on the opposite side.

Exactly like a train.

I called Mark.

“You sure nothing ran through here?”

“I’m looking at the graph now.”

“Because the logger thinks something did.”

“Track machine, maybe?”

“At one thirty?”

“Could’ve been an unlisted movement.”

“Can control check the axle count?”

“Already asked.”

“And?”

“Nothing.”

I looked down the railway line.

The track disappeared into darkness in both directions.

There are different kinds of quiet when you work nights.

You become conscious of them.

A suburban line is never actually silent. Electrical equipment hums.
Roads hiss in the distance. Houses have air conditioning units and dogs.

Out there, between towns, you hear insects, wind in dry grass and

That night, there was almost no wind.

I remember that because a plastic inspection tag hanging from the

“Do another physical inspection,” Mark said. “Call me if you find

Connections.

Battery condition.

Relay states.

Not tyre marks.

Not footprints.

I was tired enough that ordinary things were beginning to feel

Then the next.

Nothing was touching it.

The invisible pressure passed over the first rail side sensor.

The crossing bell stopped.

Not gradually.

It cut off in the middle of a ring.

The lights kept flashing.

The vibration became violent.

And then something passed through the crossing.

I can’t describe what I saw because I didn’t see anything.

The gate nearest me shuddered violently, as though caught in the

Loose gravel jumped between the rails.

My shirt pulled towards the track.

Crunch.

Crunch.

The gates had activated again.

“So open it.”

Nothing yet.

Right.

“Get back in your car,” I said.

This equipment doesn’t get to invent trains.

Sideways.

No car.
