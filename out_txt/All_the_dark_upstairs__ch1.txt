The old infirmary is one long front of red brick with bands of pale
stone. Just before seven on an October evening only its east end was
lit. A short row of windows glowed along the ground floor there. Above
them and beside them, the whole length of the front, dark ones went up
floor after floor to the roof.

I stood across the road with the park at my back, its trees under the
street lamps. I fell for the building the way you fall for a face across
a room. My count of the dark windows went wrong somewhere along the
second floor, poor work for a nurse who counts patients for a living.
The lit row is the Wren, the one ward still open, a sixteen-bed hospice
in the east wing. The rest of the building has been closed since 1994.

I am a staff nurse, four years qualified, on my first night on the Wren.
Still glad of the job, I am typing this at the nurses’ station after
counting the residents in bed at nine.

I walked along the front, past the old main doors, to the car park at
the east end. My last ward on nights had a car park I came to know far
too well, at six pounds a night. The Wren’s is free, its lines last
painted around the moon landing.

The side entrance is the staff’s own door. Its handle was cold brass,
worn smooth and pale where a century of hands had gone round it coming
on shift. I stood a moment wondering whose. A framed fire plan from 1962
hangs beside the door, the whole hospital with its rooms lettered in
tiny capitals, WARDS, OPERATING THEATRE, MORTUARY, CHAPEL. Guess it is
the only map of the dark part anyone gets to see.

A rules board faces the staff-room door, the first thing you see as you
come in. It is varnished oak four feet tall, lettered in gold like a
church’s list of who paid for the roof. Its heading reads NIGHT
DIRECTIONS. Some of its gold letters are dull with age and some bright
with fresh gilt.

Handover was under the rules board. Here the nurse in charge is called
the sister. The day sister gave us fourteen residents in ten minutes, in
her coat already. Room 12 has been stripped since its resident died last
week, and is out of use until it is redecorated. Last week’s new
resident is in one of the two double rooms at the east end, on the
courtyard side. Noor, the night sister, eased the keys and the handover
sheet out of the day sister’s hands, like a cup from somebody who has
held it too long.

Mia, the care assistant, is younger than me but has been here three
years, and taught me the Wren’s habits at speed. ‘Biscuits in the blue
tin are for the residents,’ she said. ‘Biscuits in the red tin are also
for the residents. Kettle takes four minutes, five if you watch it.’

Mr Garrow, the building’s caretaker, passed the door with a wire cage of
laundry that two men would lift together. He carried it alone, in a
pressed three-piece suit, a tie and polished shoes, and set it down to
meet me. ‘We’re glad to have you, Nurse Marsh,’ he said, ‘and if
anything needs fixing, we’ll have it fixed.’ Courteous and kind and in
no hurry, he was somehow a size too much for the doorway. I felt
underdressed for the laundry in my running shoes with the fraying lace.

Five desks stand in the staff room for the four names on the roster.
Noor’s and Mia’s are lived in, with photographs and hand cream. Mine and
Mr Garrow’s are bare.

The fifth is somebody’s. A lavender wool cardigan hangs over the back of
its chair. A mug with roses and a gold rim stands on the desk, clean and
dry, beside a large-print library book with the town library’s label on
its spine and a bookmark standing up in it. Mia steered the tea trolley
round that chair with her eyes on the cups.

I have typed the board out whole because a good document has always had
me itching to copy it.

NIGHT DIRECTIONS

ONE. Light the lamps before the last of the daylight goes. Leave until
morning any lamp the dark finds unlit.

TWO. Count the residents at the turn of every hour. Count aloud. If the
count is wrong, count again. It will be right the second time.

THREE. A door that is open was opened. Leave it as you found it and
write it in the book. Doors to the old hospital stay shut.

FOUR. Answer the residents’ bells at any hour. Nothing else in this
building is calling for a nurse.

FIVE. The food lift is long out of service. If its bell rings, let it
ring.

SIX. Residents may ask you to sit with them. Sit. The house has never
minded a nurse sitting with the dying.

[a gap here. two lines of nothing, and the varnish shinier in it]

SEVEN. Residents ask who else is in the room. Answer them with what you
can see, and leave it at that.

EIGHT. Leave by the door you came in by. Decline any door the building
offers you.

What a board, bless it. Somebody had it lettered a century ago and
somebody keeps it up. As to the gap between SIX and SEVEN, I have filed
it as a decorator’s slip. The slip offended me on behalf of whoever did
the gilding, so I went down the rest of the board for a second mistake
and found it careful to the last full stop.

SEVEN is the one I keep turning over. In four years of questions at
three in the morning, the board is the first to tell me to answer with
what I can see and leave it at that. EIGHT made me smile outright. I
have worked in four hospitals, and not one of them ever offered me a
door, only stairs and an ID badge.

We are funded for fourteen, Noor says, the way you would say the boiler
is old. The money comes per resident. When a bed goes empty, I gather,
the people Noor calls ‘upstairs’ get out the calculator and start
counting the staff next.

At nine, before the count, Mia asked Noor whether Room 12 was still
stripped to the mattress. ‘We’ll count it with the rest, love,’ Noor
said.

Noor took a torch off its charger, testing the beam on her palm. Here we
say the count aloud door by door, the two of us walking the corridor
from 1 to the doubles like a roll call at school. At each door she kept
the beam low, off the pillow, on the shape of a resident under the
covers. Then she said the number from the doorway, low and clear. I had
started under my breath out of habit because on my other wards the night
count went in silence and the sleepers slept on.

‘Go on,’ Noor said, ‘count aloud, love, it settles the house.’ So I said
my numbers out loud too.

By the far end we were saying it together, ‘eleven, and twelve’s empty,
and three in the doubles’, with twelve’s door standing ajar and its bare
mattress showing in the corridor light. The residents lay still as our
numbers passed their doors. In the park-side double the second bed stood
made up with its covers folded back, as it is every evening.

Fourteen. I wrote it in the shift log and walked back up the corridor
behind Noor, over the bands of grey and white marble laid across our
path. It is the handsomest floor I have ever worked on.

The board on the staff-room wall promises a strange night or two. My
last phone lost a year’s diary in one go, so I am keeping a copy of the
strange nights on this sub, where strangers do not know my face. To keep
it that way, I have changed the names, even the building’s, which felt
disloyal.

The ten o’clock round is the best of the night, because everyone is
still awake enough to have opinions. The lady in 2 had her knitting out
and kept it in her lap, needles and all. Over in 3 the gentleman wanted
the racing results. The gentleman in 4 and his bad hip asked for nothing
and needed his pillows done twice.

Mrs Pemberton in 6 is a retired schoolmistress of eighty-eight, small,
sharp and courteous, and dying of a failing heart in her own good time.
Pemberton is the name I gave her. She would mark the substitution in the
margin, I think, and take a point off me for it. Her hands lay folded on
a book face down on the blanket, thin hands with the rings gone loose on
them.

Her water jug stood full at the near edge of her bedside locker, where
she keeps it herself, in her reach. She watched me over her glasses to
see what I would do about it.

‘The day girl puts it on the windowsill,’ she said, ‘where it can enjoy
the view.’ I lifted the jug by its handle rubbed dull where a great many
other thumbs had gone on a great many other nights. It was heavier than
it looked, cold through from the tap. I poured it down to half at the
basin, light enough for her hands, and set it back at the near edge
exactly where it had been.

Mrs Pemberton looked at the jug, and then at me, for a long moment. ‘My
Tom kept it half full,’ she said. ‘On my side of the bed, for fifty-one
years, and he knew better than to move it.’ So the jug stayed in her
reach and stayed hers, and I understood what the test had been. She
wanted her wits seen on my first night, and her say over her own water,
so I turned the handle to her hand.

She asked where I had trained, whether I read and whether I could spell
‘necessary’. The last one I answered slowly. Her eyebrows went up while
I dithered between one ‘c’ and two, and came down again at the double
‘s’.

When I left she said goodnight to me by my name, which she had not asked
for and must have read off my badge. I folded her glasses onto the
locker and said hers back.

The round ran on to the doubles after her. On the courtyard side, last
week’s new resident was asleep under a crocheted blanket from home while
her neighbour sat up writing to her daughter. On the park side a
gentleman slept with his hearing aid out on the locker, the spare bed
beside him. The midnight round went by without a murmur from anyone.

I have kept a diary since I was sixteen, since the night a coin made the
first choice I ever trusted to chance. That was the year my mother moved
to another city and left the choice to me, so the coin decided I would
stay with my grandmother. The coin is still in the back of my phone
case.

I have let chance have its say ever since, which explains a good deal
about my haircut. These days it has its say through a single red dice in
my tunic pocket.

Near two I went in to Mrs Pemberton again because her breathing had a
catch in it at the one o’clock count. I stood by the bed with my hand on
the rail to listen. She opened her eyes and looked past my shoulder at
the corner by the window, her face composed for a visitor. ‘Nurse,’ she
said, ‘he is by the window again, is he not.’

I wanted to turn round and check the room, years of habit pulling at my
neck. Instead I held my head still, my eyes on her jug and my hand flat
on the bed rail. ‘What I can see is your jug,’ I said, ‘and your glasses
on the locker, and the clock, and you.’

Then I left it at that. She closed her eyes and went back to sleep.

When her breathing evened out I came away. (I did not look at the window
on my way out.) Back at the desk I rolled my neck, still stiff from the
hardest thing I had done since handover. Above me the call panel shows a
dark lamp for each room. It is past two by the station clock, the
corridor quiet both ways each time I look.

Mia went on her break at half past two. Noor went into Room 4 with the
gentleman’s pain relief shortly before three. The tablets take about an
hour to work, so until then he settles only with her hand holding his
hip steady. Six counts since nine had each given me fourteen. At three I
walked the hourly count on my own, the staff nurse’s job, saying the
numbers aloud at each door as Noor had shown me.

The count came out fifteen.

I walked it again from 1, slower and louder. Twelve’s door stood ajar as
it had at nine, when I had seen a bare mattress in the corridor light.
In my torch beam I saw the bed made up in fresh linen with its covers
folded back, and a cup of tea on the locker with steam standing off it.
A room out of use stays stripped, so twelve’s bed should still have been
bare. There was nobody in the room. I kept my feet on the threshold,
said ‘twelve’s empty’ at the door and went on with my numbers to the
doubles. The second count came out fourteen.

I wrote fourteen in the shift log. It will be right the second time, the
board says.

From the doubles I saw somebody at the far end of the corridor, past the
last lamp, standing quite still by the west doors with their
grey-painted glass. I raised my torch that way, as you would to anyone
on a ward at night, but the beam fell short and the shape stayed dark,
too far off for a face. I went to see who it was, looking in at sleeping
residents. At the station, halfway along, I looked up again and found
the far end empty. I walked on and found the west doors to the old
hospital shut, as they probably should be.

Mia stepped out of the staff room a moment after I reached the desk. I
asked her whether she had made twelve’s bed, meaning to ask about the
doors next. ‘No,’ she said and went into the kitchen.

Before I sat down I warmed my hands on the radiator, as I do before
touching a resident. Then I replayed the first count, the one that came
out fifteen, door by door in my head. I listened for my own ‘twelve’s
empty’. It ought to be easy. But I walked that count half asleep and
remember it dimly.

No matter how hard I listen, the first count gives me nothing back. I
can hear myself saying ‘twelve’ at its door. ‘Empty’ should come next,
but my memory skips to my own voice at the doubles. I found my hands
cold around the pen, a minute after warming them on the radiator.

The extra one could be the park-side spare bed, made up every evening.
In my torch beam I could have mistaken its folded-back covers for a
fourth sleeper in the doubles.

The bed and the cup in twelve could be Mia’s. Night staff rest out of
sight on breaks, as I have done myself. Her break began at half past
two, time to make the bed, drink her tea and be back in the staff room
by three. She would rather deny it than be caught resting.

That is what I would tell any nurse who came to the desk with a count
like mine. I accept it and cap the pen.

Anyone standing so still by the west doors at that hour was most likely
one of us. Or I was tired enough to see a person in the grey paint on
the glass.

While I’m new I won’t trouble Noor, so I walk the counts alone. She is
just down the corridor, her hand on the gentleman’s hip while his pain
settles.

Just before four o’clock I looked up from the shift log. A lamp was lit
on the call panel above the desk, amber behind its numbered glass. It
was twelve’s.

Any call lamp stays lit until a nurse cancels it at the bedside. I may
have missed the two soft notes the panel sounds for a call. I had sat
there daydreaming, reading the roster to the bottom and starting again.

Twelve is where a resident died last week. I am the first to laugh at
that sort of thing. Even so, a small chill went over my skin.

None of my residents could reach twelve’s bell. Either the old call
system had rung by itself, as old systems do now and then, or it was
some silly joke on the new nurse. I heard Mia filling the kettle in the
kitchen.

Filled for the whole ward, it takes longer than four minutes. I sit
under the lamp and write the choice out in my diary, as I have done
since I was sixteen.

When the kettle boils, Mia will bring me the teas for anyone awake, see
the lamp and ask why I have left it lit. I grip the dice in my tunic
pocket.

The call comes from an empty bed, so it can go unanswered. I could do
the teas round with that lamp burning at my back all the way down the
ward, then have to tell Mia why I walked past a call. I would never find
out what lit it.

Going in to cancel it is my job, and I will be asked about this call. I
would go alone into a room that had a death in it last week, made up now
with the cup on its locker. With that chill still on my skin, I would
rather stay here.

Noor runs the ward, so twelve’s call is hers to decide. If I fetch Noor,
the gentleman’s pain comes straight back. On my first night I would be
the new nurse who pulled the sister away from a man in pain for a lamp
in an empty room.

Twelve’s lamp is lit above me. The shift log says fourteen.

One or two, I leave the call lit and take the teas round past twelve’s
door. Three or four, I go into twelve and cancel the call at the
bedside. Five or six, I fetch Noor out of the gentleman’s room and show
her the call. I’m throwing the dice before the kettle boils.
