He came up to me on the street one day. I was walking not far from that
outdoor, high-end mall to a sandwich spot where I had placed a pickup
order. I didn’t think twice about him when I first saw him. Seeing
homeless people was a daily occurrence in the city, and the only reason
I gave him a second glance was because of how much he stuck out among
the wealthier shoppers around him. I never did learn if he actually was
homeless. God only knows where he came from.

He was a short, thin, dirty-looking, middle-aged man in ratty clothing
that was too big for him. The top half of his face was covered with a
sun-faded baseball cap, his chin and cheeks covered in unkept grey
stubble. He was standing by the button for the crosswalk, looking around
as if he was lost. I soon noticed that he was staring deeply into the
faces of people walking around him, one after another, as if he was
trying to make eye-contact with whoever would take him up on it. No one
spared him a second glance, of course they didn’t, but I kept an eye on
him for safety. 

I continued walking towards the small crowd that was waiting to cross
the street so that I could stand among them. Before I got to them, the
Man turned his head and his eyes landed right on my own. I got my first
good look at his eyes: big, blue eyes that stared out from behind
rectangular, wire-frame glasses. His eyes had an almost innocent quality
to them, they held no heaviness. I thought to myself that he must be
crazy. I stared back, as was my habit to show people walking on the
street that I was not someone to be messed with. Regardless, he strode
toward me as if someone had fired a starting pistol. He stopped directly
in front of me, forcing me to come to a halt as I stared at him in
confusion and frustration. 

The first thing that hit me was his stench: a mix of dried piss and
rotten food. I opened my mouth to tell this creep to screw off, when he
interrupted me.

“We’re going to the grocery store,” he said, then turned and began to
amble along.

Suddenly, I was walking in step behind him. 

It took me a moment to realize I was following him. I tried to stop, to
turn around, but I couldn’t. I had lost control of my own legs. I tried
to turn my head around, to reach out and grab a telephone pole or
something to stop myself, but it was as if my brain no longer controlled
my body. We kept walking, weaving through pedestrians as we made our way
down the sidewalk. I tried to open my mouth to yell, to say anything at
all, but it would not listen to me. I could feel that my face was frozen
in a look of ambivalence. 

I was starting to panic. I could feel my eyes darting in every
direction, pleading for strangers to look at me and free me from
whatever this was. What had happened? Had this man drugged me somehow?
Was I dreaming? Was I going insane? Was it witchcraft? All I knew was
that I was following him, and I could not stop. 

We made it to the grocery store and walked inside. He walked me to the
produce section and stopped in front of the apples. He didn’t look up at
me as I unwillingly stopped next to him. He pointed at a section of the
apples with a dirty fingernail and spoke.

“Straighten all the Granny Smith apples, turn every other one upside
down. One at a time.”

To my shock, my hands reached out and began to straighten the green
apples in front of me. I continued for what felt like hours, unable to
even turn my head away from the task. I could feel everything. It was
still my body. My hands cramped early on, and I never stopped to stretch
them. He watched the entire time, offering miniscule critiques of my
performance which my hands would respond to, correcting my work. He
never looked away, never got bored. You would have thought we were doing
surgery by the level of care that he and my body gave to our task.
Finally, I finished. I was amazed that no grocery store worker had
stopped us. Customers had walked and reached around us many times, but
no one seemed overly concerned by our work. Would I have said anything
had I seen this? I would have hoped so, but I wasn’t sure.

“Move on to the tomatoes. Turn them all upside down.”

Finally, after several sections of produce, he walked me to the front
door and out onto the street. 
“Walk home. I will see you tomorrow.”

So I walked home. Unable to disobey.

When I crossed the threshold of my apartment’s front door, my body
snapped out of it. I froze where I stood. I began to shake, then slowly
angled my head down to look at myself. I appeared ok. I patted myself in
several places. I was fine. I took a few steps in each direction to be
sure that I was in control of my own movement. When I was confident that
I was, I sprinted back out the door.

It took several minutes to run to the nearest police station, but I ran
the whole way. It took almost an hour to get seated in front of an
officer, but I waited. I explained what had happened. I demanded some
sort of drug test, which they eventually gave me. I asked how it was
possible. The officer stared at me with a confused look on his face
after hearing me out. He brought in his supervisor and he listened to my
story as well. 

“Did he threaten you or something?” The supervisor asked. “I’m sorry,
I’m just not sure I get what you’re saying.”

“Well… no, but-”

“Did he indicate in any way that if you didn’t do it, there was going to
be any consequence?”

“No, but-”

“So a stranger told you to do something, and you just did it? You didn’t
walk away?”

“I couldn’t!”

It went in circles for several more minutes. In the end they told me to
just walk away next time. I couldn’t seem to make them understand that I
very literally had no control of my own body.

Later on, the drug test came back. I was negative for everything. 

I didn’t sleep at all that night, terrified that the Man would make good
on his promise of seeing me the next day. I called my office and said I
would work from home that day. 

Around eleven in the morning, there was a knock at the door. I froze,
careful not to move a single muscle. There was no way he knew where I
lived. I was pretty sure he hadn’t followed me.

Then a voice called from behind the door: “Come outside.”

My legs stood me up and walked me to the door. I fought my body with
every ounce of my will, but it didn’t make a difference. My hand shot
out and turned the doorknob, then pulled the door open. 

He stood there, in the same dirty clothes as the day before, his blue
eyes magnified by his thick glasses. He stunk just as bad as he had. 

“Let’s go.”

That day he brought me to a gas station and had me taking very
deliberate steps to make a square, all while facing the same direction.
It was ninety-eight degrees out and we were out there for four hours.

As soon as I was free, I got in my car and drove for hours. I ended up
in the desert in a little town in the middle of nowhere. I didn’t know
where I was going, but I knew I had to get as far away as possible. I
would figure it out from there. I pulled into a gas station as the sun
came up, having driven all night. I filled up the tank, then went to get
back in my car.

He was already sitting in the passenger seat.

We drove back and went to the mall. He had me count all of the
lightbulbs in the building. I didn’t finish by the time the mall closed,
but he let me go home, assuring me that we would finish tomorrow. I
tried tying myself to the bed that night, plugging my ears with
noise-cancelling headphones, and blindfolding myself.

I got up first thing in the morning, not having heard or seen a thing,
the blindfold still tight across my eyes. I easily pulled my right arm
free of the shoddy bond I had tied and freed myself completely. I felt
myself stand and leave the apartment. I could smell him as soon as the
door opened. We walked for several minutes, leaving my apartment
building, walking on the street, and entering another building. I could
hear people beside us as we went. I heard some quiet comments about my
blindfold, and each time it lit a small flame of hope in me that they
would stop to ask what was going on. No one did. Eventually, I stood
still and took off the blindfold, unwillingly, and saw the Tyrant
smiling at me. We finished the lightbulbs in the mall that day.
This happened day after day. Every time it was something different, but
it was always strange. One day, he brought me to the public library and
we went through the stacks, taking books out one at a time, rotating
them in midair, then putting them back just how we found them. It always
went on for hours, and each second was agony.

I never understood why he made me do any of this. He was dedicated to
watching every task he made me do, but none of them made any sense nor
did they benefit him in any way. It didn’t matter how useless the
motion, he was passionate about it. He would encourage me as we did
whatever we did, never losing patience or enthusiasm. He never
introduced himself, or spoke of anything that didn’t have to do with the
immediate task or to tell me that he would see me the next day. I didn’t
know if he was homeless, if he had a name, or if he was even human. It
seemed that he had no sort of identity outside the completion of these
useless ordeals. 

I took to calling him “the Tyrant.”

My boss was getting concerned about me. I had been working from home for
days, and as far as she could tell, I had not been getting anything
done. I forced myself to go into work one morning, extra careful to make
sure that the Tyrant was not following me. I convinced myself I could
shake him, but a part of me knew otherwise. I parked a mile away then
called a ride-share that took me the rest of the way, so that I couldn’t
be tracked from or by my car. It worked for the first half of the day. I
went about my work day the same as I always did before the Tyrant
appeared.

Then, in the middle of a meeting, he showed up. 

He had walked all the way to the conference room door. Somehow he had
gotten past the locked front door, reception, and up to this floor
unobstructed despite looking like a security risk. No one had stopped
him. The whole room turned when he walked into the conference room. 

“I’m sorry, are you lost?” My boss asked.

The Tyrant did not spare her a glance. His eyes found mine as I
trembled. 

“We are taking the bus,” he said, before turning back around and walking
out the door.

I stood up and walked to the door. Out of the corner of my eye, I could
see my coworkers staring at me as if I was crazy.

My boss called after me as I walked out, unable to turn around and offer
an apology or even an apologetic look. She followed me to the door, but
stopped at the threshold and watched me follow the Tyrant continue along
the sidewalk. 

Just like he promised, we rode the bus that day. That was it. We would
sit on one for God knows how long, then he would randomly have us switch
to another. I quickly recognized that there was no logic to what we were
doing. We were not headed in a consistent direction. He would often have
us double back in the same direction using a different bus line, or we
would ride in a big square. I tried so hard to get someone to recognize
that something was wrong. I stared like a madman at anyone and everyone.
No one met my glance. There was an old woman who sat across from me who
I stared at for several minutes, my eyes wide as saucers as I tried to
get her attention. I tried to force my jaw open to make a noise, but it
would not happen. I watched the woman step off the bus after several
stops. The Tyrant spoke to me every so often, assuring me that we were
“almost there” and relating the plans for the nonsensical route as if it
mattered. 

Before the buses stopped for the night, we got off right in front of my
car and he walked off.
When I got home, I sobbed for hours. I sobbed until no more tears came.
I sobbed until my voice went out so I was just lying on the ground
shaking as my face continued the sobbing motions. I felt hopeless. There
was no way to escape this. There was no moving away. There was no asking
for help. Who would believe me? 

No. The Tyrant had me. 

Then the idea came to me. 

It took a few days, but when I got what I needed and set it up, I was
sure it would work. Before I went to sleep, I tied myself to the bed as
best I could, spreadeagled. This time, I bought handcuffs, and cuffed
myself to the bedframe, then tossed the key as far as I could. I tried
my best to get out, but I couldn’t. Finally, this would work. After
almost an hour of uncomfortable fidgeting, I finally fell asleep.

It was morning when the pain woke me. I felt my arms straining against
the restraints, the metal of the handcuff digging into the skin of my
wrists and the rope on the other hand burning as it rubbed against me.
My hips shot up and down from the bed, trying to wrest the remainder of
my body from its prison.

Nice try. 

I had to wait for several minutes as this continued. The pain in my
wrists was growing, blood was starting to trickle down my arms. I
thought I would dislocate a limb. Then the door unlocked: he forced me
to give him a key after the last time I tried to lock him out. It swung
open and I caught a glimpse of his impassive face. As always, he looked
the same, but then…

PING.

I wish I could have grinned at him as he stood up a little straighter,
alarm showing everywhere but his soulless face. For the first time, he
looked startled. He looked to the source of the small noise, and saw the
grenade whose pin he had just pulled by yanking the door open.

BANG!

My eyes burned and my ears rang. I turned my head away from the scene
and shut my eyes tight. That was when I noticed that I wasn’t struggling
anymore, that I had moved my own head. I looked down at myself and
attempted to flex my hands. They moved when I wanted them to.
I turned to look at the door and my breath caught in my throat. The
remains of the Tyrant were slumped against the wall, his entire left arm
and the left half of his chest were gone, only bloody red meat remained.
I could see his brain on the left side of his head. His eyes were glassy
and lifeless.

Jesus, how am I going to explain this to the cops?

I took a few deep breaths, steadying myself, and when I was ready, I
took a deep breath in, preparing to yell at the top of my lungs for
help. Someone would come hear me and untie me, I would yell all day if I
had to, but someone would come. I was free.

“HEL-”

My voice caught in my throat. My arms went rigid.

No.

Then, searing pain as my body tore my hand out of the handcuff,
shredding layers of skin and breaking bones. My now free, mostly ruined
hand, now fumbled at my other restraint until my other arm was freed.

Thankfully, he did not make me look at him as I untied my legs, but I
heard him. He sounded as if nothing was wrong.

“Put me back together. Every last piece.”
