They said that, from the moment women began carrying their first child
in their wombs, they came to know the purest and most beautiful love in
existence. A love capable of healing hearts and destroying worlds.

The French philosopher Elisabeth Badinter believed that this love was,
in truth, a great myth. To her, unconditional maternal love was a social
construct that varied across cultures, eras, peoples, and societies.
According to this view, many women, during pregnancy and even after
giving birth, experienced feelings of inadequacy, fear, insecurity,
depression, and sadness. Of course, they also experienced happiness,
perhaps the most absolute and beautiful happiness of all. But they lived
with nightmares, pain, and suffering, too.

To me, however, a mother’s love was the most beautiful thing in the
world.

It was my mother’s greatest quality, and the thing I envied most about
her. It wasn’t her academic degree. Nor her successful career as a
lawyer. Not even the beautiful apartment in one of the city’s wealthiest
neighborhoods. Much less her hair, which somehow remained flawless even
after a full night’s sleep. What I envied was the way she could show me,
in every gesture and every word, a love that was sincere and true. Even
when, throughout my childhood, I went out of my way to test her patience
in every way imaginable.

That was what I admired most. What I loved most about my beautiful,
intelligent, wonderful mother. The same mother who now lay several feet
beneath the ground, yet seemed to be everywhere I went.

It had all begun at my mother’s funeral. Her body lay pale, cold, and
rigid. Even so, there was something serene about her expression.
Something peaceful. Almost relieved. Draped over her carefully arranged
dress was a delicate piece of lace that Bianca had chosen for the
ceremony. My younger sister had always had a knack for these things.
Even amid the suffocating sadness of that funeral, she had managed to
turn our farewell into something beautiful. Perhaps someday, when the
pain was no longer so unbearable, we would be able to laugh together,
and I could tell her how clever she had been to arrange such a beautiful
goodbye for our mother.

But that moment was still far away. For now, all I could do was cry.

Silently, I lowered my head and rested it gently on my sister’s
shoulder, taking care not to hurt her. And I kept crying.

Then, a cold breeze suddenly swept through the closed walls and doors of
the funeral parlor, brushing against the back of my neck and raising
every hair on my body. I felt as though someone were standing behind me,
very close. A silent presence, its hand hovering just inches above my
shoulder. Almost touching me. As though struggling against an
irresistible urge to grab hold of me.

A shiver ran down my spine.

Slowly, I turned around, and horror washed over me. My mother was
standing there, just a few steps away. Her skin had taken on a sickly,
bluish hue. Her face was still recognizable, but something was terribly
wrong. Where her eyes should have been, there were only two dark, empty
hollows.

Her mouth hung slightly open as one arm stretched toward me. Her fingers
were curled like claws, as though trying to reach me, as though they
wanted to drag me somewhere distant and unknown.

I tried to scream. Tried to move. Tried to call out to Bianca, to anyone
who might be there. But no one around me seemed to notice the presence
of the creature that had once been my mother.

When I finally gathered the courage to look toward the coffin, now
behind me, I found it empty. My heart raced. I turned my eyes back to
the figure standing before me. Mother was still there, dead, sad, and
horrified.

The world went dark before my eyes, and I only returned to it when I
found myself in my office, standing open-mouthed in front of a patient.
Sitting across from me, Dona Eulália, one of my longest-standing
patients, watched me with curiosity and concern. Her brow was furrowed,
her eyes heavy with sadness, and she had already risen from her armchair
without my noticing. Her hand hovered close to mine, almost touching it,
in a way that was disturbingly similar to the gesture made by the
apparition that had just vanished from my mind.

She asked me calmly, “Are you all right, Doctor?”

I took a deep breath and tried to manage a reassuring smile. But before
I could say anything, I saw an expression of understanding spread across
my patient’s face.

I found myself wondering, “How did I get here?” and “Where is my
sister?”

Fifteen minutes past the scheduled end of the session—fifteen extra
minutes I had insisted on giving her—I walked Dona Eulália to the exit
of my office. The elevator doors closed slowly, and I hurried back to my
room. Once inside, I rummaged through my documents and searched my
computer until I discovered how much time had passed.

Ten days since my mother’s funeral. Ten days I couldn’t remember.

I walked over to the window. The sun was already disappearing behind the
buildings, casting its last orange rays through the office’s only
window. The rest of the room remained swallowed by shadows. A growing
gloom seemed to creep into the corners as night approached. All I could
do was tremble, gripped by an unreal cold that made my bones ache.

By the time I reached my apartment, night had fallen. I collapsed onto
the bed without even taking off my shoes. I tried to calm myself,
slowly, forcing myself to think rationally, as I always had, as my own
father had taught me to. Despite being the most emotional person in the
family, he had “the mind of a crow,” as he liked to say. After all,
crows were among the most intelligent non-primate animals in the world,
capable of making tools and blessed with an extraordinary memory.

For the next two hours, I wandered around the apartment with a glass of
white wine in one hand and a bowl of popcorn in the other, of which I
had barely eaten a handful, trying to make sense of what had happened. I
walked through the living room, the bedroom, the small office I had set
up in the guest room, and over to the window. Every so often, I stepped
out onto the narrow balcony between the living and dining rooms, a space
barely more than three feet wide where I tried to breathe and put my
thoughts in order.

When I finally settled onto the living room sofa, with the television on
merely to ward off the loneliness, I concluded that perhaps this was
simply my mind’s way of coping with grief. But deep down, it all still
felt strange.

The next morning, I woke up with my head pounding from drinking too much
wine. While showering, still struggling with the fog that had settled
over my memories of the past ten days, I decided I would go to work.
Dealing with my patients’ problems would help me forget my own, and
perhaps even do me some good.

In my building’s parking garage, I started the engine and pulled out of
my space, following the same route I took every day through the city’s
busy streets as I tried to organize my thoughts. I had driven a few
blocks when, suddenly, I heard my phone vibrating inside my purse, which
lay on the passenger seat. When I checked the screen, I was surprised to
see the name: Saulo (patient). I didn’t have any patients by that name.
I answered immediately.

“Hello?”

On the other end of the line, all I heard was heavy breathing.

“Saulo?”

I ventured to say the name displayed on the screen. No answer.

“Saulo, are you all right? Did something happen?”

The breathing continued. Slow, deep, labored. Then a voice emerged,
hoarse and strange, almost unrecognizable.

“Doctor…”

A shiver ran down my spine.

“Saulo?”

“You need to go home.”

The words came slowly, as though speaking required an effort.

“What happened?” I asked, my hands trembling once again.

“You have to go home,” he repeated, then added with difficulty, “She
told me to tell you.”

“Who told you that?”

There were a few seconds of silence.

“Her.”

“Who?”

The breathing returned, heavier than before.

“Go home, Doctor.”

I pressed the phone harder against my ear.

“Go home, Clara!”

My name came out distorted and drawn out, like a growl. Then the call
dropped. Strangely, the voice had sounded as though it hadn’t come from
the phone at all, but from somewhere inside the car itself. My eyes were
immediately drawn to the rearview mirror above my head.

And then I saw it.

Dead eyes. A gaping mouth, right behind me.

A scream tore from my throat, and I slammed on the brakes, nearly
causing a collision with the vehicle behind me. My car stopped at an
angle on the sidewalk. Panicking, I scrambled toward the passenger seat,
struggling to free myself from the seat belt, which seemed intent on
dragging me back inside the vehicle. When I finally managed to break
free, I threw myself through the open door. The momentum sent me
crashing onto the hard concrete of the sidewalk.

I lay on my back for long seconds, gasping for air, while pedestrians
walked past, watching me with curiosity, bewilderment, or simple
judgment. My heart hammered against my chest. I closed my eyes and took
a deep breath. Then I tried to gather my thoughts.

I knew that intense emotions could alter the way the brain interpreted
ambiguous stimuli. Bereaved people reported hearing voices, smelling
familiar scents, and even seeing deceased relatives. It was more common
than most people imagined. I had personally seen a patient swear she had
spotted a loved one across the street. So I clung to that explanation.
In truth, I needed to cling to it, because the alternative was admitting
that I had seen something impossible, and I wasn’t willing to do that.

I remained still for a few seconds before remembering what had brought
me there, to that sidewalk: Saulo. I couldn’t remember him, but if he
was my patient, I had to do something. I had to help him. Without
wasting any time, I grabbed the appointment book from my purse, which
had fallen onto the floor of the car, and searched for the patient’s
address. I couldn’t explain how, but the address was there, perhaps
because I had made a habit of keeping the addresses of patients who
required extra attention in case of emergencies. And there it was,
written in a strange handwriting that looked nothing like mine. The
letters seemed to have been formed with twigs rather than a pen.

Armed with the address, I returned to the car, fighting my own body to
control the trembling that still ran through my hands. I sat in the
driver’s seat, struggled to fasten my seat belt, and took a deep breath,
filling my lungs until the painful pressure in my chest eased a little.

Only then did I start the engine and head for Saulo’s home.

As I drove a little faster than usual, I had to change my route several
times because I was so nervous that I kept missing the streets I needed
to turn onto. A journey of just over four miles became nearly six,
leaving me feeling guilty. My delay could have dire consequences.

When I arrived at the address, I noticed a small yellow-fronted diner
that was closed despite the busy hour. That alone was strange enough.
The establishment occupied the ground floor of a modest building, with a
small apartment directly above it.

I walked to the side entrance and pressed the intercom several times. No
answer. When I placed my hand against the door to knock, it swung open,
sending a brief tremor of apprehension through me. Deep down, something
told me I shouldn’t go in, that I should return to the safety of my car.
Unfortunately, I pretended to shake off the fear and stepped through the
half-open door.

The unease that had been lingering in my chest since the near accident
intensified when I found a narrow, steep staircase that seemed to have
no end. As I hurried up the steps, I noticed the hallway lights
flickering irregularly, casting strange shadows across the
claustrophobic walls. When I reached the final flight, I found the
apartment door standing ajar as well.

“Saulo?”

I knocked twice out of politeness. No answer. Then I pushed the door
open.

The apartment was almost entirely shrouded in darkness. The blackout
curtains were closed, except for a small gap that allowed a narrow strip
of light from the street to slip inside. I took a few steps forward,
unsettled by the silence, especially in a place facing such a busy
avenue.

“Saulo? Are you here?”

Something moved in one of the corners.

I turned my head, but there was nothing there. Then I heard heavy
footsteps running behind me, echoing throughout the apartment. I spun
around again, searching for whoever was making them, but found no one.
Frightened, I immediately backed away toward the opposite side of the
living room. At that very moment, a door slammed so hard that the entire
apartment seemed to shake.

For the first time since my mother’s death, I realized I was truly
afraid. Terrified, actually.

“Saulo!” I shouted. “P-please, talk to me! I-I came to help!”

The footsteps returned. This time, they came from the kitchen, fast and
heavy, running back and forth. I stumbled toward the strip of light. As
irrational as it seemed, that small illuminated space was the only place
where I felt safe. I reached out, grabbed the curtain, and pulled it
wide open, searching for salvation.

Light flooded the room.

And that was when I saw him. Saulo stood near the door, motionless, his
mouth open in an impossible, gaping stretch. A horrible sound came from
his throat, like an old door creaking slowly open. My stomach lurched.

Only when he raised one hand did I notice the old, short-barreled
revolver pointed directly at me. Every instinct told me to get out of
his line of fire, run as far away from my patient as possible, and call
the police. But I remained frozen in place. I couldn’t do that.

“Saulo… please. Let’s talk. Tell me what’s happening.”

He didn’t answer. He continued making that grotesque sound, the gun
still held steady, aimed at me.

“Saulo, please, let’s…”

Then he spoke, his voice rough and unsettling.

“Go home, Doctor.”

“I will, if that’s what you want. But let’s talk first.”

“I DON’T WANT TO TALK!”

Whoever that person was, he was anything but Saulo, my kindest patient,
the man who had spent years struggling with all his might to fight his
own demons. No. That was the Devil himself, controlling the poor man’s
body as though it were some grotesque puppet.

I thought about moving, but this time I couldn’t, even if I wanted to. I
felt as though my feet had been cemented to the filthy floor of the
apartment. I’m going to die, right here, this very moment, I thought,
utterly terrified.

“Go home, Clarissa.”

Before I could react, Saulo abruptly raised the revolver, pressing the
barrel against his own temple. In the final fraction of a second, a
solitary tear rolled down his cheek, like a sad plea for help.

Then the gunshot echoed through the apartment.

For some time, which I couldn’t begin to measure, I wandered aimlessly
through Saulo’s stuffy, moldy bedroom. Despite wanting to scream and
cry, I kept my mouth shut in a feeble attempt to avoid the taste of
blood and the pieces of Saulo’s brain splattered across my face. Who
would have thought a bullet could make such a mess?

THUD.

When I looked down, I realized I had dropped the phone I’d used to call
emergency services. The metallic-colored device was marked with bloody
fingerprints. More of Saulo’s blood, the result of my failed attempt to
save my patient, even though I knew that by then not even the most
talented doctor could piece together the grotesque jigsaw scattered
across the apartment’s walls, ceiling, and floor.

A faint creak came from the living room, making the post-shot tremors
surge violently through my body once again. It felt as though my insides
were being brutally processed in a blender, every part of me slowly
destroyed until all that remained was a revolting, filthy pulp.

“Police! Is anyone there?” a man’s voice echoed from the living room.

Staggering, I made my way down the apartment’s narrow hallway, forcing
myself to breathe with every step. When I reached the doorway, I stopped
abruptly. The longer I looked at the horrible scene, the worse it
seemed. More grotesque. More bloody.

I only noticed where the police officer was standing when he approached,
moving slowly sideways to avoid the enormous pool of blood staining the
center of the living room. He kept one hand near his holster, clearly
assessing whether I posed any threat. Then he raised an arm toward me,
careful not to touch me. I wondered whether that was one of the
protocols they had to follow.

“Are you injured, ma’am?”

I didn’t answer.

I kept staring at the pool of blood in the middle of the room, at the
body lying in it. I knew the human body contained a considerable amount
of blood, but I had never truly understood what that meant. Knowing that
a human body carried around five liters of blood was one thing. Seeing
that amount spread out before my own eyes was something else entirely.

“Ma’am?” the officer asked again. “I need you to tell me whether you’re
injured.”

I felt a presence. Still staring into the room, I noticed a doorway I
hadn’t seen before. Something seemed to be vibrating. A force seemed to
emanate from within that space. Then I simply said:

“There’s someone in there.”

Another police officer was standing by the door. I hadn’t noticed her
presence either. She was a woman. Instinctively, she turned toward the
room on the other side of the living room. Moving quickly, she crossed
to the door and grabbed the handle. With a sharp motion, she opened it,
revealing a small room that was completely empty and dark.

“There’s nothing here, ma’am,” the officer said, clearly irritated.

The officer standing beside me, his face caught between sympathy and
nervousness, gave me a pitying look.

“Were you the one who called the police?”

For the first time, as I felt a part of myself returning to my body, I
simply nodded. The officer seemed not to understand, because his
expression remained unchanged. He questioned me again.

“Were you the one who called the police?”

I didn’t answer.

“Ma’am?” he asked again. “Was it you?”

I remained silent.

“Clarissa?”

And then, my body still trembling, I finally return to the interrogation
room at the police station. I’ve been questioned for hours by several
officers who react strangely to me; they seem disgusted by me, even
afraid. Can’t they see I was trying to help? Why are federal agents
involved in a suicide case? And why won’t they answer my questions?

Oh, my God. What have I done?
