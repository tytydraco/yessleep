Part 5

Before I get into what happened, I want to answer the question I keep
getting.

Why did I stay.

I’ve read every comment. Some of you think I’m brave. Some of you think
I’m an idiot. One of you asked who I was making this sacrifice for, and
I’ll be honest, that one sat with me for a while.

Here’s the only answer I have. I wanted to be a cop since I was a kid.
Somebody calls, you show up, you handle it. I was good at it before I
ever got to this town and I’m good at it now. The calls here don’t make
sense. Fine. They’re still calls. Somebody still has to show up.

That’s the whole answer. If you were hoping for something deeper, I
don’t have it.

At least that’s what I would have told you that night, sitting in the
parking lot with the engine running.

I know a little more now. I’ll get to it.

I turned right.

The drive to the building took eleven minutes. I knew that because I’d
made it five times before. Every time, the priest was already there when
I pulled up. Already parked, already standing by the front door like
he’d been waiting all day and didn’t mind. I never once saw him arrive.
I never thought about that until that night.

This time there was nobody standing at the door.

I wish I had a plan. I didn’t. I had a transfer form on the passenger
seat that I’d read four times, a radio that had played my own voice back
to me an hour earlier, and eleven minutes to think about the fact that
the man who ran this ceremony five times in front of me was gone, and
nobody had taught me a single word of it.

The transfer form was real, by the way. I checked everything. Real
letterhead and a signature from a captain I actually served under. Dated
that night. The town doesn’t bluff. That’s something I understand about
it now. When it offers you a door, the door works.

I left the form on the seat and got out of the car.

The front door of the building was unlocked. I took the stairs to the
third floor the way the priest always took them, slow, one hand on the
rail. The building was quiet.

The hallway on the third floor looked the same as every time before.
Doors on both sides, all of them shut, and the last door at the end of
the hall. 3C.

I heard it before I got there.

Voices. A lot of them. Overlapping, layered, like a crowded room with a
TV on. I stopped where the priest always stopped, a few feet back from
the door, and the voices stopped too. All at once. Like somebody lifted
a needle off a record.

I stood there. I didn’t know the words. I didn’t have holy water. I had
a flashlight and a gun and twelve years of showing up to things, and
none of that had ever opened or closed anything in this town.

So I said the only thing I could think of.

“It’s time. You know why I’m here.”

The door stayed quiet for a long moment. Then a voice came through it. A
child’s voice, small and wet, like the kid had been crying for hours.

“You’re not him.”

“No,” I said. “I’m not.”

“He’s not coming back. Nobody taught you the words. Nobody taught you
anything. They sent you here the same way they send you everywhere.
Alone and late.”

I didn’t answer that. Mostly because it was true.

The voice changed.

“Chris.”

It was Ian.

It was his voice. Perfect.

“Chris. It’s me. I don’t know where this is. It’s dark and there’s a
door and I can hear your voice. Everything it takes ends up behind this
door. The ceremony is the only time it opens. Please. The priest knew.
He knew and he left us here anyway.”

My hand was on the doorknob.

One second I was standing where the priest always stood, and the next I
was holding the knob, and it was cold, and Ian’s voice was right against
the other side of the wood, close enough that I heard him breathing
between the words.

And then it stopped being Ian.

“I’m sorry. I’m so sorry. I tried to save them. I tried.”

My voice.

The same thing it played through the Sergeant’s radio. The same thing it
said through this same door the first time the priest brought me here,
while I stood in the hallway feeling my skin crawl. Word for word. And
standing there with my hand on the knob, I finally understood what it
was.

It was never a prophecy. It was a lure. It cut those words out of me the
first time it ever heard my voice, and it kept them.

It almost worked.

What stopped me was a hand on my shoulder.

“Take your hand off the door, kid.”

Steve.

I hadn’t heard him come up the stairs. He stood in that hallway looking
at the door the way you look at a dog you’ve known a long time.

“You’re not supposed to be here,” I said.

“Neither are you. Yet.” He nodded at the door. “It do Ian for you?”

I told him it did.

“Figured. It did kids’ voices for me, my first time. Doesn’t bother
anymore. It knows I’m empty on that account.” He stepped past me, right
up to the door, closer than I ever saw the priest get. “It’s got nothing
left I want. That’s the qualification, if you’re wondering. That’s the
whole job posting.”

“Steve. What happens without the priest.”

He was quiet for a second.

“Somebody holds the door,” he said. “From the inside.”

I started to say something and he talked over me, which he’d been doing
since my first night, and I let him, which I’d been doing just as long.

“Thirty-one years, Chris. I’ve watched this town take pieces off better
men than me, on a schedule. I stayed because somebody had to teach you
idiots which calls to drive slow to. Well. You drive slow now. You
listen to dispatch. You made it further than anybody I’ve broken in, and
I’ve broken in eleven.” He put his hand flat on the door, casual, like
he was checking it for heat. “I’m due. Been due. The town’s been polite
about it and I’ve been ignoring it, and tonight the bill came in your
name instead of mine. That’s not happening.”

“Steve.”

“First one’s the worst,” he said. “This is my last one. Trade you.”

I said his name again. I didn’t have anything to put after it.

He opened the door.

I’m going to describe what I saw, because I’ve owed you people the truth
this whole time and I’m done being careful with it. I’ll tell it the way
I’d give a statement.

The room was a bedroom. Bare floor, one window boarded from the inside,
no furniture. The room was full of people.

Maybe thirty of them, packed shoulder to shoulder the way people stand
at a wake. An old woman by the window. A man in work clothes. Kids. All
of them facing the door, and all of them with their mouths open, and
none of the mouths matched the breathing. The breathing came from the
room itself. One breath, one set of lungs somewhere under the floor of
all those open mouths, and every person in that crowd swayed with it,
together, like weeds in the same current.

None of them had eyes. Just skin, smooth, like eyes were never part of
the plan, like whoever built these people worked from a description
given over the phone.

The old woman by the window was wearing my mother’s cardigan. The man in
work clothes had Ian’s haircut. I understood then that I wasn’t looking
at the people it took. I was looking at its wardrobe.

Steve walked in like a man walking into a break room. Every open mouth
in that crowd turned to follow him, and the room said his name in thirty
voices at once, and he said, clear as anything, “Alright. I’m here. Shut
up.”

The door closed on its own.

Every voice in that building stopped at once, and this time they stayed
stopped.

I stood in that hallway for a long time. Then I said the only thing I
had.

I said, “Thank you. And I’m sorry I never bought you a coffee. Not once.
All this time and I never bought you a single coffee.”

And through the door, I heard him laugh.

I drove back to the precinct. The transfer form was gone off the
passenger seat. I never touched it. I checked the whole car with my
flashlight like it was evidence, and it was just gone, and I understood
the offer had expired and was never going to be made again.

I felt something I had no business feeling about that.

Relief.

Now. The thing I said I’d get to.

Remember my first post? The very first thing I told you. Twelve years on
the job, three of them in this town. I wrote that sentence and it didn’t
even slow me down.

Here’s what I didn’t tell you, because I didn’t know it. Everything I’ve
written about in these posts, the first night, the accident on the road,
the ceremony, Ian, all of it happened in my first year here.

I’ve been here three.

After that night I did the math. Pay stubs and a calendar. There are two
years I can’t account for. Whole seasons I remember as weeks. I’m not
going to try to explain it, because I can’t, and because I think some of
you already suspected, reading between the lines, and were kind enough
not to say anything.

The town doesn’t keep you here. The road out is real and the transfer
was real. It just makes staying feel like your idea, and it makes time
cost less than it should, and by the time you finally count your pay
stubs, you’re not a transfer anymore. You’re part of it.

Two more things and then I’m done.

Not long ago the radio went off.

“Unit 1, come up on the air.”

“Go for unit 1.”

“Got a job for ya. Head over about 2 blocks. You’ll see a blue house
with its porch light on. A man will be waiting outside for you. He
called 911 stating his wife isn’t feeling well.”

Word for word. Three years apart, and dispatch read me my first call
like it was fresh off the printer.

I drove there at the speed dispatch gave me. The porch light was on. The
man was waiting outside, same as before, and he thanked me for my timely
response, and he called me Officers, plural. His wife wanted to play
hide and seek. She always does. The first time, that house had me
sprinting for my car. This time I stood in that hot bedroom and told her
to pick a good spot, and I counted, and EMS showed up and did their
thing, and on my way out the husband told me she likes me. I said good
night to them both, and I’m not embarrassed about that.

And this morning, before shift, I passed the locker room. There were
voices in there laughing. The same ones as always. The ones that were
real once.

One of the laughs was new.

I stood outside that door for a minute. I didn’t go in. It wasn’t time
for me to go in. I’ve gotten good at knowing what time it is in this
town.

Then I stopped by the Sergeant’s office. It’s still his. I don’t think
that door will ever belong to anybody else, and I’m fine with that. But
a while back, Ian showed me where the pen is kept, and he didn’t say
anything when he did it, and I understood.

There’s a new officer transferring in soon. Voluntold, I’d guess. Young.
Good record. The kind of cop who shows up.

I wrote the note and taped it to the door, where the notes have always
been, and I put the spare keys in the visor of the patrol car out back.

The note says:

“Welcome aboard. Coffee’s in the back. It’s terrible. Drink it anyway.
Listen to dispatch. Drive the speed they give you. The calls won’t make
sense. Show up to them anyway. That’s the whole job.

You’re going to have questions. Bring them to the locker room.

Somebody in there will always answer.”
