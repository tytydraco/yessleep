His whole body jolted. He scrambled up so fast he almost took me out at

We found photos tucked behind a loose cinderblock. One showed him as a
kid, maybe four or five, asleep against something huge. The thing
holding him had skin like pale rubber and six fingers. The boy was
smiling in his sleep.

Reassuring.

Then the line went dead.

I called Sam back. Voicemail. Tried again. Nothing.

My hands shook dialing 911. I told the dispatcher my friend was inside
the old slaughterhouse, that I’d just heard something happen to him over
the phone. She asked if I knew who did it.

I almost told her. About the photos on my phone. The pale limb with too
many joints. The six fingers. The boy curled against something that
looked like it was made of wax and bone. I said I didn’t know.

She asked where I was. Said cops were already rolling toward the
property. I told her I was home. She said stay there.

I didn’t.

I grabbed my keys and got in the truck.

Stupid, I know. I should’ve waited. Looking back, almost every choice I
made was wrong.

But Sam had been with me eleven years. Helped me start the company when
I had nothing. Worked for free when we were slow. When my wife left, he
came over with a six-pack and let me bitch on the porch for three hours.

Now he was under that building.

I’d heard him scream.

I wasn’t staying home.

Ten minutes down the road, my phone rang.

I swerved hard reaching for it.

Sam’s name glowed on the screen.

I answered. “Sam?”

Silence.

“Sam—where are you? Talk to me.”

Nothing.

I pulled onto the gravel shoulder, engine idling. “Sam, the police are
coming. Just tell me where you are.”

A rustle came through the speaker. Dry, like straw shifting.

I held the wheel tighter. “Sam?”

A low exhale. Wet. Soft.

Then a voice.

Not right—not like a person’s. Like something that had heard words but
never felt them in its own mouth. The spaces between syllables were all
wrong. Some parts hissed with too much air; others clicked deep in the
throat.

It spoke slow.

“Good.”

I didn’t move.

A few seconds passed.

“Good.”

Another sound—a scrape, like plastic dragging over mic mesh.

Then, softer than anything, a tone so tender it froze the blood in my
veins:

“Good boy.”

The call dropped.

I sat there on the shoulder, phone in hand, staring at nothing.

I called Sam again. And again. Must’ve tried a dozen times before I
reached the Coldwater gate.

He never picked up.

He still hasn’t.
