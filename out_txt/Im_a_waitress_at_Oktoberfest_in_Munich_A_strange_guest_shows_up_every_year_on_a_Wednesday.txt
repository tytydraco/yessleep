“You need strong arms and a stomach for the smell of puke.” That’s how
Gustav hired me.

After my sister’s accident, I dropped out of university and badly needed
money and a distraction. A friend gave me the number of Gustav, who ran
the biggest tent at the Wiesn (that’s what we call Oktoberfest here in
Munich), and put in a good word for me. And as a waitress at
Oktoberfest, you get exactly that: lots of money and lots of
distraction. I mostly worked the evening shift, which paid better, and I
quickly learned the unwritten rules of the tent. Walk the extra lap
instead of spilling precious beer. Give drunks (mostly men) clear
orders. Only switch to flirty waitress mode when you’re sure they won’t
misread the signals (I want your tip, not your number). It’s a bit like
being a zookeeper in a monkey house. You also need a feel for the room.
Which table is about to blow up, so you’d better call security? Which
guest is so drunk that you have to make sure he doesn’t puke on your
dirndl (the traditional dress)?

Most guests, tourists and locals alike, were fine. Except for one man
who came every Wednesday. He wore worn-out leather pants, a faded red
checkered shirt, a beaten-up felt hat with a broken brim and a huge
feather. His shoes caught my eye right away. Big, shapeless leather
shoes, with thick wool socks pulled up to his knees. He showed up at the
same time every Wednesday, ordered a Maß (that’s a liter of beer) and
drank it while watching the party around him with a strange smile. His
look was arrogant, as if he secretly judged people for having fun. When
he finished, he paid with old D-Mark bills (our old currency, from
before the euro) and disappeared. The first time, I refused to take the
money, but he said nothing and just grinned. His teeth were yellow and
he stank like rotten eggs. Gustav pulled me aside, rough and fast, and
told me to stop making a fuss and take the money. Money is money, he
said, and he could easily swap it for euros at the bank. I wanted to
know who the man was, but my boss gave me a look as if I had said
something forbidden. “Just do your damn job and cash him out.”

The strange man came every year, every Wednesday, drank his beer, paid
with D-Mark and vanished as quietly as he had appeared. The staff had
all kinds of theories. Some said he was a time traveler, maybe even the
founder of our tent, who jumped into the future every Wednesday to enjoy
his life’s work. Others thought he was an alien. The sensible ones among
us said he was just an old man with a pile of D-Mark bills at home who
had found a fun way to get rid of them.

It was the second-to-last week of the festival, so I decided to find out
who he really was. My sister always said my curiosity would kill me one
day. But I never could help it. He came, I brought him his beer, and
when he wanted to pay, I took the D-Mark bills with a smile and wished
him a nice evening. I followed him when he got up. He didn’t head for
the main entrance. He took a side door that led past the kitchen to the
offices. He walked down the hallway whistling, hands in his pockets, and
stopped in front of Gustav’s office. He went in without knocking, as if
it belonged to him. I crept up to the small window above the door,
climbed carefully onto a beer bench and tried to peek inside.

Gustav and the old man were arguing.

“Can’t we sort this out another way? I … I can talk to her!”

“We’ve played this game before.”

“Please, I …”

“You know exactly what happens now. We have a deal.”

Gustav sighed, sank into his chair and stared blankly into the corner.

My eyes fell on the man’s beige socks. They had slipped down a little,
and I saw thick, black hair on his legs. No, it wasn’t hair. It looked
more like … fur. Black, shiny fur. His worn leather shoes started to
crack, and something strange pushed through. Something hard, glossy and
dark yellow. Far too late, I understood. Hooves. Gustav looked away,
clamped his hands over his ears and whimpered, as if his whole body
hurt.

But all of that was nothing compared to the man’s gaze, which hit me
like a bullet. Suddenly every positive thought (and every neutral one)
vanished from my mind. Only the pain was left. Everything bad that had
ever happened to me was suddenly right there. My parents’ divorce when I
was eight. My gym teacher’s hands on me when I was fourteen. My sister’s
tragic death two years ago. Until now, a filter had softened every
negative feeling and thought, so that I could survive at all. His stare
and his grin tore that filter apart. All the misery of the world hit me
like a focused laser, with so much force that I fell over backwards. As
soon as I broke away from his eyes, I felt instant relief.

I ran down the hallway, past the kitchen, and pushed open the door.
Instead of the tent, there was another hallway. How was that possible?
That was clearly the door to the tent. So I turned around and ran past
the kitchen on the right side this time, to reach the tent from the
other direction. But when I went through that door, I ended up in the
storage room. That made no sense at all. The storage room is outside the
tent, on the opposite side. How did I get here if I never left the tent?

I turned around again and ran toward the kitchen. This time I ended up
in Gustav’s office. Gustav wasn’t there, and the strange man was gone
too. My sister stood there instead. She looked exactly like she did on
the day she died. Torn jeans, the thigh bone sticking out, the shoulder
twisted, the neck broken, so her head hung sideways off her shoulders,
like a doll with a snapped neck.

I screamed, but I knew instinctively that nobody could hear me.

“Why did you … do this?” my sister groaned.

The door I had come through was suddenly gone.

“Why did you give me your car?”

“I didn’t know!” I screamed and shut my eyes so I wouldn’t have to look
at her.

“You knew. You sent me to my death on purpose.”

“No, no, no.” My voice was hoarse and raspy.

“I didn’t know the brakes were damaged. I swear.”

My sister took a step toward me, dragging her broken leg behind her.

“Stop lying! You always hated me, because Mom loved me more than you.”

“Stop it, please stop!”

“I got the love you always wanted.” I covered my ears and turned away
from her.

“You were happy when I was finally gone.”

I shut my eyes and shook my head, hoping she would disappear.

When I opened my eyes again, I saw a door that hadn’t been there before.
And when my dead sister took another step toward me, I ran past her and
out of the office. Past the kitchen, and suddenly I was back in the
tent. Everything was where it belonged again.

The hallways, the doors, the guests.

And the filter was back. The bad feelings slowly faded, and I only felt
them dimly now. I remembered everything and I was still scared, but I
wasn’t so close to it anymore. With every minute, I gained more
distance. The shield built itself up again and protected me from the
harmful memories. It worked so well that I even finished my shift that
day. I told no one about what I had seen and lived through, not Gustav,
not anyone.

For the next few days I slept badly, but I recovered and was my old self
again. A week later, it was Wednesday again. And the old man came back
to the tent. I brought him his beer and cashed him out when he was done.

There’s only one thing I never did again: look him in the eyes.
