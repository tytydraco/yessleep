My mother’s last words were Don’t let them drain it.

She said them in the hospice in Lewiston at a little after four in the
morning, in a voice I hadn’t heard her use in twenty years: clear, flat,
wide awake. She’d been under for two days by then, morphine-soft, her
breath going in and out with that wet clicking sound the nurses call the
rattle and the families don’t call anything at all. I was dozing in the
vinyl recliner. I woke up and she was sitting straight up in the bed,
staring at me, and her eyes were so dark in that room that I couldn’t
see the whites of them.

“Don’t let them drain it,” she said. “Promise me, Annie.”

I didn’t know what she meant. I said, “Okay, Ma,” the way you do. She
lay back down. She was dead before the sun came up.

That was in June. In July the drought got bad. By August the state of
Maine had drained the Tenney Reservoir down to its bones, and I had
driven up there on purpose, and I had broken the only promise my mother
ever asked me to make.

I want to tell you why, but I’m not sure I know. I’ve thought about it
every night since, and the closest I can get is this: I think part of me
had been waiting to go back there my whole life. I think I’d
been called, the way the nuns used to tell us about vocations, a soft
voice in the back of your heart that you can ignore for years but never
quite stop hearing.

I think my mother heard it too. I think she spent sixty-five years with
her hands over her ears.

My mother was from Calvary Falls. Most people under sixty in this part
of Maine have never heard the name, and the ones who have say it the way
you’d say the name of a child who drowned at summer camp: low, a little
ashamed, eyes going off toward something else. The state flooded it in
October of 1958. Paid everybody off, knocked down what they could, then
opened the sluices on the Sabattus River and let the water come in brown
and steady over the rooftops while the whole town stood up on Haskell
Ridge in their Sunday coats and watched.

My mother was nine. She only ever told me about it once. I was ten
myself, sitting at the kitchen table doing long division while she
peeled potatoes at the sink.

“Nobody said a word,” she told me. “Three hundred people on that ridge
and not one of them said a single word. The only sound was the river and
a dog barking down in town, barking and barking, until it stopped.”

“Were they sad?” I asked.

She stopped peeling. She stood very still at the sink with the knife in
one hand and the potato in the other, and she thought about it for so
long that I put my pencil down. Then she said, “No, honey. They were
relieved,” and went back to peeling, and I never asked her about Calvary
Falls again.

I should tell you one more thing about my mother, because it matters,
and because I spent fifty-four years not thinking about it.

She had scars in her palms.

Round ones, one in the center of each hand, the size of a quarter.
Puckered and shiny and whiter than the skin around them, like old
smallpox vaccination marks. She said she’d burned them on a woodstove
when she was little, grabbing at the rail. She wore gloves most of her
life (church, grocery store, garden, even indoors in the winter), and
she never, not once, let me hold her hand with the palm flat against
mine. If I reached for it as a little girl, she’d curl her fingers
closed and take my hand by the wrist instead. Gently. Always gently.

When I was seven I woke up from a nightmare and padded down the hall to
her room. Her door was half open. She was sitting on the edge of her bed
in her nightgown, in the dark, with her hands in her lap, palms up. Just
sitting there. Staring down at them. Very still. And I heard a sound in
that quiet room, a soft, small, wet sound, like someone chewing with
their mouth closed.

She looked up and saw me and closed her hands so fast the knuckles
cracked.

“Back to bed, Annie,” she said. Her voice was perfectly calm. “It’s just
Mumma’s hands. Sometimes they get hungry in the night.”

I went back to bed. In the morning I’d decided it was a dream. That’s
how it works, when you’re seven. That’s how it works when you’re
fifty-four, too, if you let it.

The Diocese of Portland called me on the second of August. I spent
nineteen years doing paintings conservation at the Bowdoin museum before
the budget cuts, and now I’m the person parishes call when the Stations
of the Cross have gone brown with a century of candle soot and they
can’t afford anybody from Boston.

The monsignor said they’d found paintings in the church. Icons. Panels
set into the walls. “Possibly very early.” He said it in the careful,
sanded-down voice of a man repeating what somebody higher up told him to
say.

“How early?”

The silence on the line went on long enough that I heard him breathing
through his nose. Long enough that I heard, faintly, a clock ticking in
whatever office he was sitting in.

“Earlier than the church,” he said. Then, so quietly I almost missed it:
“Earlier than the Church.”

Capital C. I heard it. I swear to God I heard the capital C.

They sent a photographer with me. Danny Pelletier, twenty-seven, from
Biddeford. A big, sunny, good-natured kid with a farmer’s tan and a
silver crucifix on a chain around his neck that he wore outside his
T-shirt where everyone could see it, the kind of Catholic who’s still
Catholic not because he’s wrestled with it but because nothing’s ever
given him a reason to stop. He crossed himself every time we passed a
cemetery. He did it on the drive up, outside Sabattus, and caught me
watching, and grinned.

“Habit,” he said. “My memère used to whack me if I forgot.”

I liked him right away. I want you to know that before I tell you the
rest. I liked him very much.

Picture the ugliest place you’ve ever been. A mall lot in February, a
dump, the loading dock behind a slaughterhouse. Now drain every drop of
color out of it and stretch it to the horizon.

That’s the bottom of the Tenney. Mile after mile of grey-brown mud baked
into plates like the skin on a lizard’s back. Dead trees at the old
shoreline, silver and stripped, standing so still in the heat that they
looked painted on the sky. A rusted Hudson Hornet up to its axles with
its doors hanging open like it had been abandoned in a hurry. Foundation
holes. Chimneys. The steel bones of the Grange hall.

And the smell. Dead fish and rot, and under that something sweet,
cloying, like a bakery with something gone bad in the back. It got into
your clothes. It got into your hair. I’d shower at the motel in Turner
and smell it on my pillow.

No birds. I’ve lived in Maine my whole life and I have never stood
outdoors in August anywhere in this state without hearing some bird
complaining about something. Out on those flats there wasn’t a gull, a
crow, not one fussy little chickadee. Just the heat ticking in the mud
and the enormous white sky.

The flats were the emptiest place I’ve ever stood, and they
were crowded.

That’s the only word for it. Five miles of nothing in every direction,
and it felt like the back of a packed church on Easter. Shoulder to
shoulder. The air close and humid with breath. The feeling of a hundred
people standing just behind you, just out of the corner of your eye, so
close you could feel the heat coming off them, so close that if you
turned around fast enough you’d look straight into their faces.

I didn’t turn around fast. Neither did Danny. We walked out to the
church with our eyes straight ahead like kids walking past the bad house
on Halloween.

We passed the town cemetery on the way. I’d read that the state moved
all the graves in ’58. Dug them up and trucked them to Lewiston, every
last one. And they had, I guess, because the cemetery was just rows of
holes, sunk in and slumped with silt.

But in some of those holes the old coffins were still there.

Not moved. Left. Maybe a dozen of them, pine and black and soft as cork
with rot. And the lids on every one had been pushed up. Not pried down
from above by a shovel. Pushed up from inside and split down the middle,
the broken boards bent outward like the petals of a flower.

Danny crossed himself. He didn’t grin this time.

I looked into the nearest one, because I’m a conservator and I look at
things for a living. The inside of the lid was scored all over with
marks. Hundreds of them. Little crescent-shaped gouges, overlapping, row
on row, the way the edge of a cookie looks when a child has worked their
way around it.

Bite marks. Small ones. From the inside.

From the outside St. Agatha’s was a small country church. Granite,
square-shouldered, sixty-one feet long; I know because the police
measured it later. Sixty-five years underwater had taken the roof and
the door and painted every stone the color of a dead man’s thumbnail,
but the walls stood straight. The Quebecois stonecutters who built it in
1867 built it to last.

Inside, it was bigger.

I’m going to put that plainly. I spent the whole first afternoon trying
not to put it to myself at all.

We set up the work lights, two LED floods on tripods running off a
generator out in the mud, and they lit the near end of the nave like a
stage. The pews, toppled and half-buried in silt. The holy water font by
the door, still full of black water with a scummy skin on it. The walls,
crowded floor to ceiling with panels.

And past that, maybe forty feet in, the light stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. As if it had run into a curtain of black velvet hung
straight across the nave from floor to ceiling. Except there wasn’t any
curtain, because air was coming out of it. Cold air. In August, in a
stone box that had baked in the sun all day. It came across the floor in
a slow, steady draft that raised the hair on my forearms and smelled
like a walk-in meat locker and, faintly, under the meat, like
Sunday-morning frankincense.

Danny laughed, too loud. “Trick of the light. Humidity, right?
Scattering?”

He took a hand lamp and went to pace it off. I watched his light swing
away down the nave, smaller and smaller, like watching a lighthouse beam
from the deck of a ship steaming out into fog. A pinprick. Then nothing.

I stood alone at the edge of the light for four minutes. I timed it.
They were the longest four minutes of my life, and I have stood by a
hospital bed and listened to my mother’s breath stop.

During the third minute, from somewhere very far down in the dark, I
heard Danny laugh. A short delighted bark, the way you laugh when
somebody springs a surprise party on you.

And then I heard a lot of other people laugh with him.

Not loud. Soft. Dozens of them. Maybe more. The warm, low, indulgent
laughter of a room full of grown-ups watching a child do something
sweet. It rolled up out of the dark toward me and broke over me and went
past, and the draft behind it was colder.

When Danny came back, he was walking fast and trying not to. His face
was the color of skim milk. His lamp’s battery light was blinking red.
I’d charged it myself in Turner the night before.

“Hundred and twelve steps,” he said. “Then the light started going and I
figured—” He swallowed. “There’s a wall down there. Right? Got to be a
wall.”

“Did you laugh?” I asked him.

He looked at me. “What?”

“Down there. Did you laugh at something?”

He kept looking at me for a long moment. Then he said, “No, ma’am,” very
politely, and went to change the battery, and we worked by the door
after that, and neither of us said anything more about it.

That’s how it starts. Not with screaming. With two reasonable people
agreeing, without a word, not to say something out loud.

I stayed late that night, and the night after, and the night after that.

I told Danny it was the work. I told myself the same. I’d sit on an
overturned pew at the very lip of the light with my thermos of Dunkin’
going cold between my knees and look down the nave into the dark, and
the dark looked back.

I keep wanting to call it empty, and it wasn’t, not the way a field at
midnight is empty. It was empty the way a throat is empty. It had
a shape, and I could feel the shape all around me: enormous, going back
and back and down and down, a cathedral the size of a mountain folded up
inside a church the size of a barn. And here’s the thing I can’t make
anybody understand, not my sister on the phone, not the grief counselor
the diocese paid for: the bigger it felt, the tighter it got. I’d sit at
the edge of that endlessness and I couldn’t get a full breath. It
pressed on my chest like a hand. Like all the water that had sat on that
church for sixty-five years was still sitting there, every ton of it,
invisible and patient, pushing down.

Around midnight on the third night, I heard the Mass.

Far down. Very faint. A man’s voice, old and cracked, intoning Latin,
the Tridentine Mass, the one from before Vatican II, the one I
half-remember from when I was very small. Introibo ad altare Dei. I will
go in unto the altar of God.

And then the responses. Hundreds of voices. Low and wet and murmurous,
like a congregation speaking with their mouths full.

Ad Deum qui laetificat juventutem meam. To God, who gives joy to my
youth.

I sat frozen on that pew and listened to the whole thing. The Kyrie. The
Gloria. The Credo. It went on and on, an hour at least, and I knew the
shape of it in my bones even if I’d lost the words, and when it got to
the Consecration (the moment when the priest lifts up the host and
says Hoc est enim Corpus meum, for this is my Body) the whole
congregation down there went silent at once.

Then, in that silence, I heard the sound I’d heard coming out of my
mother’s bedroom when I was seven.

Soft, wet, patient chewing. Hundreds of mouths at once.

When it was over, a single voice said the final response. A woman’s
voice, closer than the others. Much closer. Maybe just past the edge of
the light.

Deo gratias.

Thanks be to God.

It was my mother’s voice. I knew it the way you know your own name. And
my mother had been dead for nine weeks.

I didn’t leave. I want to be honest about that. I’m a grown woman with a
car and a cell phone and a sister in Augusta, and I did not leave. I
came back the next morning and started cleaning icons.

They covered every wall. Egg tempera on wood, gold leaf gone green and
brown with sixty-five years of water. Saints. Saints and saints and
saints, rank on rank on rank going up into the dark where the ceiling
should have been, like a choir loft full of the dead.

Martyrs, mostly. If you weren’t raised by nuns, you might not know how
martyr icons work, so I’ll tell you: every martyr gets painted holding
the thing that killed them. It’s called their attribute. It’s how you
know who’s who. St. Lawrence holding the gridiron they roasted him on.
St. Catherine and her spiked wheel. St. Lucy holding her own two
eyeballs on a dish like deviled eggs at a church supper.
St. Bartholomew, who was skinned alive, holding his own skin draped over
one arm like a raincoat, the empty face of it hanging down by his knee.

And St. Agatha, patron of this very church, who had her breasts cut off
with shears by a Roman governor for refusing to marry him. She’s always
painted holding a silver platter with her two breasts on it, side by
side, nipples pointed up at heaven like little pink gumdrops, and her
face as calm as a summer pond.

I had a St. Agatha card when I was eight. Sister Mary Rose gave you your
pick of the laminated martyr cards if you got a hundred on a catechism
quiz, and the boys all fought over Bartholomew, and I picked Agatha
because she was pretty. I kept her in my jewelry box for twenty years. I
never once thought it was strange.

Every face on those walls had its eyes scratched out.

Not water damage. Gouges. Deep crossed X’s cut through the gold and the
gesso right down into the bare wood. Hundreds of faces. Thousands.
Somebody had gone around this whole church with a blade and blinded
every saint in it, one at a time, and taken their time about it.

But they’d left the wounds alone.

I found it on the second day, cleaning a St. Sebastian, the one tied to
a post and shot full of arrows. I had my loupe in and was swabbing the
grime out of an arrow wound, and the wound was beautiful. Painted far
more carefully than the face, the hands, the folds of the loincloth. The
work of a different hand. A master’s. I leaned in closer and closer, and
I saw what was inside it.

Teeth.

In every arrow wound, painted so small you’d need a jeweler’s lens to
see, there was a ring of teeth. White and perfect, each one with its own
tiny highlight, curving inward toward the center of the hole. Every
wound in that boy’s body was a mouth, and every mouth was open.

I went to the next panel. And the next.

Lawrence’s burns: mouths. Bartholomew’s flayed skin: the raw underside
dotted with tiny mouths, dozens of them, like the pores in a sponge.
Lucy’s eyes on their dish weren’t eyes; they were two small puckered
mouths, lipless, glistening even under sixty-five years of silt.

And Agatha. On her silver platter, the two severed breasts. The nipples
weren’t nipples. They were mouths, each one open in a small wet O, a row
of baby teeth showing inside.

I thought about my St. Agatha card. I thought about how many nights I’d
looked at it before I fell asleep.

Danny shot it all. Thousands of frames. He kept up a steady patter while
he worked: folk art, regional heresy, some crackpot twelfth-century sect
that came over with the Acadians, ha ha, the Church sure had its share
of weirdos. He talked the way you talk walking past a dog that’s
growling at you. Low and steady and never looking it in the eye.

He’d stopped crossing himself. I don’t know exactly when. I just noticed
one day that he hadn’t in a while.

On the fourth morning there was a new icon.

I’m sure of it. I’d photographed that whole section of the north wall on
the first day, top to bottom, every panel logged. That morning, between
St. Lucy and St. Apollonia (patron of dentists, they pulled out all her
teeth with pincers, and of course she’s holding one up in a pair of
tongs), there was a panel I had never seen before.

It was wet.

Not damp from the river. Wet. Fresh. The egg tempera still tacky, so new
I could smell the yolk in it, rich and sulfurous, like a breakfast plate
left out in the sun. The gold leaf on the halo wasn’t green or
tarnished. It shone.

It was a woman. Middle-aged, heavyset, in a long dark robe that, as I
looked closer, wasn’t a robe at all but a cardigan, a grey one, buttoned
wrong at the bottom, the way I’d buttoned mine wrong that morning in my
hurry to get out of the motel.

She had my face.

Not like my face. My face. The gap in my eyebrow where I fell off my
bike when I was six. The little mole at the corner of my mouth. My
mother’s heavy jaw.

She was holding a silver platter, the way Agatha holds hers. On the
platter were two hands, severed cleanly at the wrist, laid side by side,
palms up.

In the center of each palm was a round, dark wound. And in each wound, a
ring of little white teeth.

Her eyes were the only ones on those walls that hadn’t been scratched
out. Painted clear, wet blue. My blue.

I sat in the mud in front of that panel for an hour. I didn’t tell
Danny. I don’t know why. I think I was ashamed, the way you’re ashamed
of a dream you had about someone.

The next morning, when I came in, the gold on her halo had gone dull and
green, and her eyes had been scratched out with two deep X’s, and the
wood in the gouges was pale and raw and new.

The big icon hung over where the altar would have been. A Pantocrator,
Christ the Ruler of All, staring dead straight at you, one hand raised
in blessing, the other clutching a jeweled Gospel to his chest. Eight
feet tall. The only face in the church besides mine whose eyes hadn’t
been gouged. They were painted solid black. No whites. Two pits of
lampblack. I’d shift the work lights and swear I saw them track.

The paint was blistering in the lower corner. Underneath was another
layer. That’s normal. Churches get repainted. You find a medieval saint
under a Victorian one all the time.

I’m a professional. I knew what I was doing. I spent two days taking
that Christ off, a quarter-inch at a time, solvent and swabs and
scalpel, on my knees in the cold draft from the dark, with the slow
sound coming up through the stone into the bones of my legs.

I haven’t told you about the sound yet. It started the second night and
it never stopped.

Thoom.

Ten seconds.

Thoom.

Very deep, so deep you felt it more than heard it, like a bass note from
a car stereo three streets over. Like a heartbeat. Like something very
large, asleep, with a building on its chest.

What was under Christ’s face was a face with no features at all. A
smooth oval, the yellowish white of candle wax or rendered fat. And on
that oval, arranged in the shape of a cross, were mouths.

Thirty-three of them. Like the years of Christ’s life. And every one was
different, painted from life, I’m sure of it, by someone who’d sat a
long time studying each one. An old man’s mouth, lips caved in over
toothless gums. A young woman’s, full and rouged. A wet lipless slit. A
man’s mouth with a gold tooth.

And low down, near where a chin would be, a child’s.

A little girl’s mouth. Maybe nine years old. Gap-toothed, missing one
front tooth. And on her upper lip, on the left side, a small pale scar
shaped like a fishhook.

My mother had a scar shaped like a fishhook on the left side of her
upper lip. A neighbor’s dog bit her when she was six. I’d looked at that
scar my whole life. I’d kissed it goodnight. I’d watched the hospice
nurse dab Vaseline on it when her lips cracked at the end.

I sat back on my heels in the mud and couldn’t feel my hands.

Under the Gospel, Christ’s left hand wasn’t holding anything. It was
open, palm out, and the palm had a wound in it, a nail hole, and the
hole was a mouth. Under the blessing hand, the same.

And it came to me down there on my knees, the way the big things always
come, not as a thought but as a sick drop in the stomach like missing
the bottom stair in the dark: they didn’t paint over Christ. They
painted Christ over this. Somebody a long time ago looked at this thing,
at its hunger and its patience and its hundred little mouths, and
decided the way to hide it was to give it a kinder face.

And out of every face in the world, the one they chose was a man with
holes in his hands.

I heard Father Gervais in my head then, Father Gervais from St. Peter &
Paul’s, who smelled of Aqua Velva and Pall Malls, holding the host up
into the light at my First Communion while I knelt in my little white
dress with my hands folded so tight they ached:

Take this, all of you, and eat of it. For this is my body.

Two thousand times in my life, at least, I heard those words. Every
Sunday. Every Christmas, every Easter, every funeral. I heard them nine
weeks before, over my mother’s casket.

Not once in fifty-four years did I stop to ask who was supposed to be
doing the eating.

Danny found the journal in the sacristy. It was in a Sucrets tin, the
old flat kind with the hinged lid, wrapped in oilcloth and stuffed up
the stovepipe. It belonged to the last pastor of St. Agatha’s, Father
Lucien Thibodeau, and it covered the fourteen months before the flood.

We read it on the pew with our backs to the dark. Danny read the French
aloud, because his memère had made him learn it, and his voice got
quieter and quieter until I had to lean against his shoulder to hear.
The draft from the nave pushed at the pages. Every ten seconds the
heartbeat came up through the pew and into our spines.

I won’t give you most of it. I’ll give you the shape.

St. Agatha’s wasn’t built to worship God. Father Thibodeau was clear
about that from the first page. It was built on top ofsomething, the way
you’d pile rocks on a grave to keep the wolves from digging. The
Quebecois stonecutters who came down in the 1860s didn’t discover the
thing. They discovered the people who were already keeping it: an older
congregation, older than the Abenaki, older than anyone, a church with
no God in it, only a thing on a cross at the bottom of the hill and the
long patient business of feeding it.

The French did what Catholics have always done best. They didn’t destroy
it. They baptized it. They hung their own saints on the walls and their
own Christ over the altar and they said Mass, every Sunday for ninety
years.

Not as worship, Father Thibodeau wrote. As feeding. The Host is not the
Body. The Host is the bait. We lift it up and It comes up to meet it,
and while It is eating, It is not hunting.

Every generation, he wrote, someone in Calvary Falls received what he
called la grâce. The grace. It began in the palms, then the feet, then
the side, under the ribs, where the centurion’s spear went in. The
wounds opened. And something fed through them, gently, endlessly, the
way an infant nurses. The person who received the grace became very calm
and very kind and very happy. They stopped sleeping. They lived a long
time. Too long, he wrote, and underlined it twice. There was a woman in
the parish, Mme. Veilleux, who’d received the grace in 1871 at the age
of eleven. In 1957 she was still alive. She sat in the front pew at
every Mass with her hands in her lap, palms up. Smiling. Her palms made
a soft sound during the Consecration that the whole church could hear.

The town petitioned the state for the reservoir. Not the other way
around. They begged for it. Three hundred people met in the church
basement in the winter of 1956 and voted, by secret ballot, to drown it.
Not kill it. Father Thibodeau didn’t think it could be killed. He
thought it was older than the valley, older than the glaciers that cut
the valley, older than the cross and the man on the cross and the God
who sent him.

Danny read me the line that I hear now every time I close my eyes. He
read it twice, because his voice cracked in the middle the first time.

Notre Seigneur ne l’a pas vaincu. Notre Seigneur l’a imité.

Our Lord did not defeat it.

Our Lord imitated it.

Think, Father Thibodeau wrote, and the handwriting here was getting big
and loose, like a drunk’s. Think what we were taught. A god who lets
Himself be nailed open. A god whose wounds never close. A god who says
take and eat, and lives in us when we do. Did we think we invented that?
Did we think the Nazarene invented that? Someone in Judea came here, or
somewhere like here. Someone saw It and went home and tried to tell the
story in a way that men could bear. The Resurrection is not a victory.
The Resurrection is a thing on a cross that will not die and will not
stop being hungry. We put a beautiful face on it and we called it Love.

Then, near the end:

The grace has come to the Dumont girl. Thérèse. Nine years old. Her
palms opened on the Feast of the Assumption. Her father brought her to
me with her hands wrapped in a dish towel, and when I unwrapped them she
smiled at me and said Father, it doesn’t hurt, it’s like being
understood.

I pray the water closes her wounds. God forgive me, I think it will. I
think It will let them close, if It is sleeping. And I pray she never
comes back. Because if the water ever goes down, It will wake. And It
will remember every mouth that ever fed It. And It will call them home.

My mother’s maiden name was Dumont. Thérèse Dumont.

She went by Terry. She hated Thérèse. She said it sounded like a saint’s
name, and she said it the way you’d spit out a fly.

The last entry was dated the night before the flood.

Forty-one of the graced will not leave. They are in the church now,
kneeling. Mme. Veilleux is with them. They are singing. I have tried,
God knows I have tried, but they only smile at me and hold out their
hands. Tomorrow the water comes. I am going down to the altar tonight to
pray over It one last time.

It is very large down there now. The nave goes back much farther than it
did when I came. I walked for an hour and did not reach the end. I have
stopped trying to reach the end.

I think It only ever let us hold It because It was patient.

I think It can wait under the water as long as It likes.

I think It is the oldest thing in the world, and I think It loves us,
and I think that is the worst thing I have ever known.
