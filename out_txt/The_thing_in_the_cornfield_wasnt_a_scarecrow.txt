I grew up in rural Iowa. Not like, a small town rural but the kind of
rural where the nearest house is a mile away and your best friend is a
cow. Our property was surrounded by cornfields on three sides. My dad
farmed those fields. He had for forty years. My grandfather before him.
It was a family tradition. A lot of hard work. And a lot of quiet. Too
quiet sometimes.

I was twelve when the corn started to get weird. It was late July. The
stalks should have been chest high by then. Ready for harvest in a few
weeks. But this year it was different. Taller. Much taller. Like fifteen
feet tall. And thicker. You could get lost in it if you weren’t careful.
Which I often was not.

My dad said it was just a good growing season. Plenty of rain. The soil
was rich. But even he looked a little unnerved by it. He’d come in from
the fields later than usual. His brow furrowed. He’d never say anything
but I could see it in his eyes. Something was off.

One evening after supper my little brother Jimmy and I were playing
hide-and-seek. He was five. A little terror. He insisted we play in the
cornfield. I told him it was too dark. Dad would kill us. But he begged
and begged. So I gave in. Biggest mistake of my life.

We went out there just as the sun was setting. The corn was a dark green
wall around us. Rustling in the evening breeze. It felt different at
night. More alive. Like it was watching.

I counted to twenty. Jimmy had a good head start. I could hear him
giggling as he ran deeper into the stalks. I started walking. Calling
his name softly. “Jimmy. Come on out. I’m gonna get ya.”

No answer. Just the rustling of the corn. And then another sound. A
faint scraping. Like something dragging itself through the dirt.

I stopped. Held my breath. “Jimmy? That you?”

The scraping stopped. The corn went silent. Too silent. Even the wind
seemed to hold its breath. A shiver ran down my spine.

I called louder. “Jimmy! This isn’t funny!”

Still nothing. Panic started to bubble up. I told myself it was just my
imagination. The wind. A rabbit. But I knew it wasn’t.

Then I heard it again. Closer this time. A low guttural growl. Not
animal. Something else. Something I’d never heard before.

I spun around. My heart was pounding. I could feel my blood run cold. I
was sure something was right behind me. But all I saw was corn. Endless
corn.

“Jimmy!” I screamed. My voice cracked. Tears stung my eyes.

And then I saw it. Just a glimpse. Between two stalks. A shape. Tall and
thin. Taller than the corn itself. And it moved with a jerky unnatural
motion. Like it was struggling against invisible ropes.

It was black. Completely black. And it seemed to absorb the dim light
around it. I couldn’t make out any features. Just a form. A looming
shadow.

It turned its head. I could feel it. Feel its gaze on me even though
there were no eyes to see. A profound sense of dread washed over me. I
wanted to run. But my feet were glued to the ground.

The scraping started again. Louder. Closer. It was coming for me.

Then I heard a scream. A child’s scream. Jimmy.

That snapped me out of it. Fear for my brother overcame my terror. I
turned and ran. Not towards the house. Deeper into the corn. Towards
Jimmy’s scream.

The corn slapped at my face. My legs burned. I could hear the scraping
behind me. Gaining. And Jimmy’s cries were getting fainter.

I burst through a thick patch of corn. And then I saw him. Jimmy. He was
huddled on the ground. Tears streaming down his face. Pointing a
trembling finger.

“It’s coming” he sobbed. “It’s coming.”

I looked up. And there it was. Just a few yards away. The black shape.
It was huge. Towering over us. And now I could see more. Long spindly
limbs. Ending in sharp points. A head that was too small for its body.
And a mouth that seemed to stretch from ear to ear. Filled with jagged
teeth.

It didn’t have eyes. But it was looking at us. I knew it. I could feel
its hunger.

I grabbed Jimmy. Pulled him to his feet. “Run!” I yelled. “Run!”

We tore through the corn. The scraping was right behind us now. I could
feel its breath on my neck. Cold and foul. I thought we were going to
die there. Lost in the corn. Eaten by that thing.

Then I saw it. The edge of the field. The clear night sky. Hope surged
through me.

We burst out of the corn. My dad was standing there. He had heard
Jimmy’s screams. He had a shotgun in his hands. His face was pale.

He saw the thing. Behind us. Just at the edge of the field. He raised
his gun. Fired.

The shot echoed through the night. The thing shrieked. A sound that made
my teeth ache. It writhed. And then it dissolved. Like smoke in the
wind. Leaving nothing but the faint scent of something rotten.

My dad held us close. Shaking. He never spoke about it. Not once. But he
stopped farming that field. Planted soybeans instead. Never corn again.

Jimmy never played hide-and-seek again. And I never looked at a
cornfield the same way. Sometimes late at night I still hear the
scraping. And I still see that black shape. Just at the edge of my
vision. Waiting. For the corn to grow tall again.
