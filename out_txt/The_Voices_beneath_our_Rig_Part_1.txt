I’ve been working on an oil rig for a couple of years now, and I can
safely say that it’s far more dangerous than any other rig out there. We
have more casualties and a much higher turnover rate than any other crew
I’ve heard of.

When I first arrived, I figured it was just bad luck. Now I know better.

When I was younger, I never thought I’d end up here. I always thought
I’d be a successful businessman or the CEO of a powerful company. Not
stranded in the middle of the ocean, drilling for oil.

Funny how things work out. I used to think the worst thing about working
out here was the possibility of falling overboard.

The only reason I haven’t left already is that I’d have nowhere to go.
In all honesty, I only joined because I was in trouble with the police.
I almost killed a man. I didn’t mean to—I mean, I did, but I didn’t want
to. The man forced himself on my girlfriend, and I freaked out. He even
got her pregnant. I’m not a very emotional man, and I’m definitely not
violent, but I beat him until he could barely move.

I would’ve killed him. I wish I had. The only reason that man is still
walking the streets is that my girlfriend, Adriana, stopped me. She knew
it would be over for me if I did.

When the police came for me, I ran. I’ve been on the rig ever since.
It’s not glamorous, but I’ve gotten used to it. It’s not half bad. I’ve
met some good guys, and I still get to help Adriana and the baby.

I’ve always had mixed feelings about the baby. Adriana gave birth to a
boy she called Jackson. I know he’s done nothing wrong, but I can’t help
seeing the man I beat half to death staring back at me. She loves
Jackson more than anything, though. I guess something good can come out
of something bad.

I’m sure I would’ve gone crazy if they weren’t there. I wouldn’t be the
first to jump into the ocean below in a desperate attempt to end it all.

There have been far too many accounts of men jumping overboard. Good men
who jumped without any reason or explanation. They just jumped, all by
themselves. I hope their deaths were quick and painless.

I was told they’d lost their minds to the things down below. They said
that, at some point, whatever was down there would try to get me, too.
It would be like a nagging voice, begging me to jump. Every man had a
different experience with it. One said he heard his deceased mother,
while another said he heard God. They told me that if it ever happened,
I should go to one of the senior crew members, and they would help keep
my head above water.

I didn’t believe them. It sounded stupid at the time, just a way to
scare the new recruits.

When it did happen, I wasn’t as scared as I thought I would be. The
voice was oddly calming and inviting. I couldn’t even tell you what it
said to me, but they were the most beautiful things anyone had ever told
me.

It told me I couldn’t tell anyone about this, about our little secret.
So I didn’t.

For days, it talked to me, whispering sweet promises of a better place
than this, and I believed it. It would tell me to stand near the edge
and just look out. I swear I could faintly hear music.

Then, one night, it told me I was ready to join them.

I remember walking out onto the railing in the middle of the night, with
the voice begging me to jump. If I did, I would be free. I could see
Adriana and my baby again. At least, that’s what I thought.

I almost did.

I climbed onto the railing. I was about to vault over when a heavy,
gruff voice screamed my name. It was ear-splitting. I felt like a child
being scolded. I knew I was doing something bad.

The voice in my head told me to hurry, to jump before the man could stop
me from joining them. Panic rose in my throat, and I leaned forward.

Below me, the water was black and cold. If I jumped, no one would find
me. That thought scared me more than the thought of staying alive.

I hesitated.

Then thick forearms wrapped around me and slammed me onto the hard deck.
My teeth rattled. Something warm trickled down my forehead.

“I told ya to tell us when you hear ’em, ya damn fool!”

The man was livid. My ears rang. I was lost and confused, unable to
remember what I’d been doing out there.

I don’t remember much after that. It’s like someone took bits and pieces
of my memory. I was told that I’d been in and out of consciousness and
hadn’t moved much. They said I’d hit my head pretty hard. I know they
were lying.

I think I might have been sleepwalking. I remember lying in bed, then
suddenly finding myself in a hallway. I think I was trying to head back
outside, but I kept tripping over my feet.

I almost reached the door when a blurry figure, whom I assumed was one
of my coworkers, gently guided me back to my room.

Then I remember trying to get out again, only to find the man who’d
tackled me standing outside the door. He put me back in bed.

That’s all I really remember.

I haven’t heard the voice since. I’m glad, because I’m not sure I’ll be
so lucky if it happens again.

One of my buddies wasn’t.

I’ll call him Bryce. He wasn’t a new recruit or anything, and this
wasn’t his first time hearing the voice, either. He’d heard it about
four years before I joined the rig. Back then, he’d told one of the
older guys, and they’d kept a close eye on him until it passed.

Not too long after I joined, he disappeared.

We thought the voice only preyed on the recruits, so the older guys
didn’t need to worry. We were wrong. I guess we assumed that, having
heard the voice before, they wouldn’t be so easily tricked again. None
of them had heard it more than once, either.

In the days leading up to his disappearance, Bryce acted normally.
Nobody suspected he was planning on jumping. Sometimes, though, I’d
catch him resting on the railing. I wasn’t too worried about it because
I did the same thing.

It wasn’t until one morning that we realized he was missing.

We searched the decks high and low, checked all the places he usually
went, and asked around to see when anyone had last seen him. Nobody had
an answer. Eventually, we came to the only conclusion we could think of:
he must have gone overboard.

One of the first places I searched was his bunk. Lying on his desk were
a half-smoked cigarette, a quarter-full beer, and a half-eaten sandwich.
It was like he’d just up and left.

The only other option was to search the ocean, so one of the men sounded
the man-overboard alarm.

The sound was jarring and almost made me jump. It was the first time I’d
ever heard it, and I hoped it would be the last.

Men used searchlights to scan the water below, but we found nothing.

Five minutes went by, and still nothing. People were shouting at each
other and pushing past one another. It was hard to think.

Then another five minutes went by. Still nothing.

Then someone shouted, “I found him! I found him!”

We pushed past each other. I only managed to get a quick look at him
before being shoved aside.

Bryce was alive.

He was clinging to the crossbeam like a newborn baby clings to its
mother. The waves slammed him against the hard surface again and again.

There was no way a search boat could safely reach him. The boat might
have crushed him or anyone caught between it and the structure. So we
had to use a rescue basket suspended from a crane.

One of the men, whom I’ll call Max, went down to help him, but Bryce
didn’t move. Max had to grab him under the arms just to get a reaction
out of him.

When they both returned, we were shocked beyond belief. We’d somehow
managed to find him alive, shaking but alive nonetheless.

He was a sickly pale color and drenched. The tips of his fingers were
dark charcoal, and his eyes were glassy. The medical staff said he was
suffering from severe cold exposure.

We brought him into the infirmary, and he didn’t say a word.

The weird thing was that he had no visible injuries apart from the
effects of the cold. He had no gashes or wounds, and there was no sign
of internal bleeding. Hell, not even a bruise.

Throughout all of this, he just looked shocked. It was like he’d
realized he’d done something bad and was afraid someone would find
out—or like he’d realized he’d missed something important.

He went home for a while. I’m sure they took him to a hospital, but they
never told us much. Honestly, we were surprised when he came back at
all.

When he returned, he started speaking again. He said he had no idea what
he’d been doing out there. All he remembered was swimming in the ocean
and trying to reach for something.

We thought it was one of the pillars, but he swore up and down that it
wasn’t.

That was two years ago. I recently asked him about the incident, and he
said he had no idea what I was talking about.

I don’t know how he could forget something like that. It was pretty
traumatic for everyone.

I haven’t said anything about it since.

That’s all I plan on writing for now. I have more stories to tell, and
I’m sure I could get some of the other guys to share their stories, too.
Maybe I’ll start with the senior crew members. They’ve been here the
longest.

They’ve also been here long enough to know what happens when someone
doesn’t listen.

Until then.
