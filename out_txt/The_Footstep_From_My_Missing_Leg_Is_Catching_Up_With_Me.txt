When I cross my kitchen, I hear three footsteps for every two I take. My
right shoe comes down first. Then the shoe fitted over my prosthetic.
Then, somewhere behind me, a third foot strikes the floor.

The prosthetic lands flat and heavy, with a faint click from the joint
if I put my weight on it too quickly. The other sound has a heel and a
toe. It rolls naturally across the floorboards. It completes the rhythm
my footsteps used to make.

This morning it began in the bedroom doorway, about twenty feet behind
me. By the time I finished making coffee, it had reached the
refrigerator. I stopped walking after that. My mug is still sitting on
the counter because I would have to take six steps to retrieve it. I can
afford the six steps. I just don’t want to spend them yet.

The footstep seems to reset when I sleep. I don’t know whether sleep
itself does it or whether I simply have to remain still for long enough.
I haven’t been willing to test the difference. Every morning, it begins
several seconds behind me and some distance away. Every step I take
brings it a little closer.

It has never caught me. I need to make that clear. I don’t know what
happens when it does.

The first time I heard it, I was still using crutches.

I lost my leg after a driver turned across the bike lane without
looking. The paperwork calls it a surgical amputation above the knee. I
hate that phrase. It leaves out the road, the bike twisted beneath the
van, and the two surgeries they tried before they gave up on saving it.

I had been home from the rehabilitation center for four days. My sister,
Erin, stayed with me for the first three, sleeping on the couch and
putting everything I might need on the lowest shelves. On the fourth
morning she returned to work, and I decided to make my own breakfast.

It took me nearly ten minutes to get from the bedroom to the kitchen. I
would plant both crutches ahead of me, shift my weight onto my arms, and
bring my right foot forward between them. The rhythm was awkward but
predictable: rubber tips, right foot. Rubber tips, right foot.

Halfway down the hall, I heard another step behind me.

I told myself it was the medication. The prescription bottle warned
about confusion, and I had barely slept since coming home. The surgeons
had also warned me about phantom sensations: pain, pressure, even the
feeling of toes curling on a foot that was no longer there. An imagined
footstep didn’t seem like much of a leap.

That explanation held until I moved again.

Rubber tips.

Right foot.

A second later, the missing step answered from the hallway.

I made it to the kitchen without looking back again.

The extra step followed each time my right foot touched the floor. At
first it came from the far end of the hallway. Then the middle. Then
just outside the kitchen. There was no movement between those places. No
shuffle approaching me. It existed only for the length of each footfall,
and each time it returned, it was closer. I spent the rest of that day
on the kitchen floor.

My phone was in the bedroom. I told myself that exhaustion and pain
medication were doing something strange to my hearing. Eventually I
crawled back down the hall, dragging the crutches beside me.

The footstep didn’t follow while I crawled. That should have comforted
me. It didn’t.

The last one had landed just outside the kitchen, and I knew it was
still there. As I crawled past that spot, I imagined I could still hear
the soft pull of its sole lifting from the carpet. I locked the door and
pulled myself onto the bed. I stayed awake for hours. At some point,
without meaning to, I fell asleep.

The next morning I lowered my right foot to the floor. Four seconds
later, from the other end of the apartment, my left foot stepped out of
the kitchen.

That was how I learned about the reset. It took longer to understand the
rest.

My phantom foot itched sometimes. At night, my missing ankle would twist
into positions that made me grit my teeth. Those feelings were terrible,
but I understood them. They were damaged nerves and a brain refusing to
update its map of my body.

The footstep was different. It did not come from inside my head or from
the end of my leg. It came from a place in the room. I tested that
first.

I stood beside my bed and took one step. The sound came from the
kitchen. Another step brought it closer, though it was still inside the
kitchen. After six more, it had reached the entrance to the hall. I
turned around to face it. When I stepped back toward the bed, the sound
did not retreat. It came from the open space in front of me, closer than
before. It wasn’t following a path through the apartment, it was
following me.

I tried wearing headphones. I turned music up until the walls shook. The
step still reached me clearly, as though it were travelling through my
bones instead of my ears. I tried making noise when I moved. I struck
the crutches against the floor. I shouted. Once, I threw a drinking
glass into the sink as I brought my right foot down.

The other foot landed beneath all of it.

I recorded it on my phone. On playback there was a soft thump after each
step, but nothing that would convince anyone. Erin listened three times
and said it could be the crutch tip settling or the pipes knocking in
the wall.

I asked her to stand behind me while I walked. She watched me cross the
living room and said she didn’t hear anything. On my ninth step, the
missing foot landed between us.

Erin didn’t react. She was looking directly at the place where I had
heard it, but her face didn’t change.

I asked if she was sure.

She said, “I’m sure.”

I asked her to leave after that.

She thought she had upset me. She kept apologizing while she gathered
her things, and I let her believe that was why I wanted her gone. The
truth was that I couldn’t stand having her between me and the sound.

I started keeping records. Some mornings I still believed I was
measuring the progress of a hallucination. I counted anyway.

On waking, I would take a single step and count the seconds. Then I
measured how many steps it took before the delay shortened.

The results weren’t exact. Carpet seemed to make the distance harder to
judge. Pain or exhaustion made me walk unevenly. But the pattern was
consistent:

The first step of the morning arrived about four seconds late.

After fifty steps, the delay was closer to three.

At one hundred, it was less than two.

At approximately one hundred and fifty, I could feel the footfall
through the floor almost as soon as my own foot came down.

I never went beyond that.

At the time, one hundred and fifty steps seemed generous. More than
enough to cross my apartment, shower, make meals and get into bed. I was
still recovering. Most days I didn’t want to move any more than
necessary.

Then I received my prosthetic.

I nearly cried the first time I stood on it. My physical therapist held
both my hands while I shifted my weight onto the socket. There was pain,
but there was also the feeling of being upright again. Balanced. Close
to my old height. For several seconds I saw myself walking into
restaurants, grocery stores and movie theaters. I imagined going back to
work without crutches tucked beneath my arms. I imagined people looking
at my face instead of glancing down at the empty space below my shorts.

“Ready?” my therapist asked.

I nodded.

Right foot.

Prosthetic.

From the far end of the rehabilitation gym came a third step.

My therapist tightened her grip. “You’re okay. Keep your weight
centered.”

I told her I needed to sit down.

She thought the socket was hurting me. Three people spent half an hour
adjusting straps and checking the skin on my leg while I stared across
the gym.

There were twelve patients in the room. Therapists moved between
parallel bars. A man in a back brace walked slowly on a treadmill.
Rubber soles squeaked against the polished floor. I could still pick out
my missing foot among them. The prosthetic hadn’t silenced it. It had
only changed the rhythm.

Right foot.

Prosthetic.

Missing foot.

By the end of that appointment, it was close enough to walk beside me. I
slept in Erin’s car on the way home. When she woke me in the parking
lot, the sound was distant again. After that, I built my life around the
reset.

Sleep became more important than food. More important than pain
medication. More important than anything except keeping track of my
steps.

A full night worked best, but a nap could do it. Twenty minutes wasn’t
enough. An hour usually was. I bought a watch that tracked my sleep and
started setting alarms throughout the day. If I had physical therapy in
the afternoon, I slept through the morning. If I needed groceries, I
took a nap before leaving and another as soon as I returned. For a
while, it was manageable.

I went back to my office three months after the accident. My supervisor
moved me to a desk near the elevator, and facilities gave me permission
to use the accessible entrance. From the parking space to my desk was
eighty-seven steps if I led with my right foot and didn’t have to go
around anyone.

I practiced the route on a Sunday when the building was empty:

Eighty-seven steps in.

Eighty-seven steps out.

Too many for one waking period. I solved it by sleeping during lunch.

I would arrive before everyone else, close my office door and work until
noon. Then I’d recline my chair, put on an eye mask and take medication
that made me drowsy. Once my watch confirmed that I had slept, I could
move again. My coworkers thought the accident had left me exhausted.
That was true enough. What I couldn’t explain was why I refused to walk
to the conference room or why I kept a plastic container beneath my desk
so I wouldn’t have to use the restroom before lunch. People offered to
help. They brought coffee, printed documents and picked up food for me.
I became skilled at accepting favors without revealing what they were
actually saving me from.

It lasted six weeks.

One afternoon, the fire alarm went off before I had taken my nap.

Everyone stood at once. Chairs rolled back. Doors opened. My supervisor
appeared in my doorway and told me we had to leave. I had already used
more than a hundred steps that morning. The footfall was less than a
second behind me.

I said it was probably a drill. She told me smoke had been reported on
the fourth floor. I tried to wait for the elevator, but the system had
shut it down. The accessible evacuation chair was mounted beside the
stairwell. No one on our floor knew how to use it.

Two coworkers helped me down three flights of stairs.

I don’t remember how many steps I took. I tried to count, but people
were talking over one another and the alarm was too loud. The stairwell
filled with bodies. Hands pressed against my back. Someone carried my
crutches while I gripped the railing and forced the prosthetic down one
step at a time.

Right.

Prosthetic.

Left.

Right.

Prosthetic.

Left.

By the second floor, the missing foot was landing almost at the same
time as the prosthetic. There was only a fraction of a second between
them. Just enough for me to tell which was which.

At the landing above the lobby, something brushed the heel of my
prosthetic. I screamed and shoved the man in front of me. He fell
against the railing, but someone caught him before he went down the
stairs. People yelled at me, but I couldn’t stop. I took the last flight
too quickly, half hopping and half falling while two coworkers held me
upright.

Someone guided me to the grass. I sat down, tore off the prosthetic and
threw it several feet away. The fire department arrived. There was no
fire, only a burned-out motor in the ventilation system. We were allowed
back inside forty minutes later.

I refused to go.

I slept on the lawn until Erin came to get me.

I resigned the next morning.

My supervisor called twice. Human resources offered additional
accommodations, then medical leave, then a remote position. I accepted
the last one. I have worked from my apartment ever since.

That was five months ago.

The apartment looks different now.

I removed most of the furniture and pushed what remained against the
walls. There are stools at regular intervals so I can sit without taking
an extra step. I keep food in a small refrigerator beside my desk. A
rolling cart holds my medication, toiletries and anything else I might
need during the day.

I use a wheelchair whenever I can. Turning the wheels with my hands does
not bring the sound closer. Neither does crawling, though the pressure
on my residual limb makes that difficult. The foot only advances when I
walk. I order groceries and ask the driver to leave them just inside the
door. I pay extra for trash collection from the apartment. I bathe every
three days. Sometimes four.

Erin visits on Sundays.

She knows about the step count now, though she still doesn’t believe in
the footstep. She thinks counting gives me a sense of control. My
therapist calls it an obsessive response to trauma. Both of them want me
to see a psychiatrist.

I tried once.

His office was thirty-two steps from the elevator. He asked me to walk
around the room so he could observe my anxiety.

I didn’t go back.

The hardest part is that the system works.

If the foot had caught me in the stairwell, at least I would know what
it wanted. If it had stopped after I got the prosthetic, I could blame
grief or medication. If someone else could hear it, I could ask for
help. Instead, it obeys its rules.

It waits while I sit.

It approaches when I walk.

It returns to a distance when I sleep.

So I’ve made myself safe, if this can be called safe.

Most days I take fewer than forty steps. On a bad day I take sixty. I
have never again allowed it to come as close as it did in the stairwell.

Until today.

This morning the delivery driver left my groceries in the hallway
instead of bringing them inside. I called after him, but he was already
at the elevator. There was a prescription in one of the bags that I
couldn’t leave out there.

I had taken fourteen steps before the delivery arrived. My wheelchair
was beside my bed with one of its rear wheels removed. Its axle had bent
the night before, and Erin wasn’t bringing the replacement until Sunday.

The front door was twenty-one steps from the kitchen stool. There were
too many bags to carry at once, so I hooked them over my wrists and made
three trips, crossing the apartment six times. On the last trip, one of
the bags split. The milk slid across the floor, and the orange bottle
containing my medication rolled beneath the narrow table by the door.

Retrieving them cost me another eight steps.

That brought the total to one hundred and forty-eight.

I turned toward the kitchen stool. The missing foot landed directly
behind me. I took one final step and pulled myself onto the seat.

That was nine hours ago. I haven’t moved since.

The last footstep sounded as though it came from somewhere between the
kitchen table and the stool. I can’t see anything there. I’ve taken
photographs and used the camera on my phone to examine the floor behind
me. There are no marks, shadows or depressions in the wood.

My bedroom is nineteen steps away.

I could crawl to the bedroom. I’ve done it before. But getting down from
this stool isn’t as simple as sliding onto the floor. It is too high. I
have to plant the prosthetic, stand, turn and lower myself carefully.

It would require one step.

On any other morning, one step would mean nothing. Right now, with the
foot directly behind me, I don’t know whether I have one left.

The sound doesn’t reset when I lie down. It resets when I lose
consciousness. Even with my sleep medication, that can take twenty or
thirty minutes. Longer if I’m frightened.

And I am frightened.

I could try to sleep sitting here, but I’m prone to fall off the stool.
I don’t know if that would count as a step or not.

My sleep medication is beside the bed.

My phone battery is at nine percent. I could call for help, but anyone
coming through the front door would have to pass through the place where
the foot last landed. I couldn’t bear having Erin stand between us
before. I won’t ask a stranger to do it now.

Erin will come on Sunday, but today is Thursday.

So this is what I’m going to do.

I’m going to wait until I can’t keep my eyes open. Then I’m going to
stand, take one step and lower myself to the floor. If nothing happens,
I’ll crawl to the bedroom and take the pills.

If it catches me on that first step, I suppose I’ll finally know what
happens.

There is one thing I haven’t been honest about.

In the stairwell, I said something brushed the heel of my prosthetic.
What I felt was five toes sliding beneath it and beginning to curl.

Today, as I took that final step toward the chair, I felt them again.

They settled beneath my heel just before I sat down.

They haven’t moved since.

I think they’re waiting for me to stand.
