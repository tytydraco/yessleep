This one isn’t a ghost story. Nothing happened that I can’t explain,
except for one part, and that one part is the reason I’ve never really
shaken it. Seven of us went, and all seven of us still talk about it,
and none of us has a good answer for that one thing.

It was a few years ago, in December. A friend of mine had grown up going
to a beach town out on the coast, one of those places that’s alive in
the summer and closed the rest of the year. He described it like it was
paradise. A cabin near the water, a little harbor, fish and chips, a
lighthouse. Seven of us split the cost of a cabin at the state park for
a couple of nights. We were young and it was cheap and we wanted an
adventure. We got one.

The first thing that was wrong was how empty it was. Not quiet. Empty.
We drove into the town and there were no cars. No people on the
sidewalks, no lights on in the houses, nothing moving on the road but
us. In the whole campground there was one other vehicle, a sheriff’s
patrol car, parked up near the entrance. I remember being glad it was
there. It made the emptiness feel supervised instead of abandoned.

The campground itself was half underwater. The winters out there are
just months of rain that never stops, and the whole site had flooded.
The picnic benches were stacked and chained together. Every building was
locked except one bathroom. We couldn’t really walk anywhere without
wading, so we were more or less stuck in the cabin unless we drove. But
we were together and it was funny at first, and we made the best of it.

So we drove into town to look around. We got lunch at the one place that
was open, a little fish and chips shop, and we walked out to the
lighthouse in the rain. And that was where we saw the first person.
Other than us and the sheriff’s car, the first living person in that
whole town.

He was walking down the middle of the road by himself. Soaking wet,
moving badly, like something was wrong with his leg. He was talking, but
not to anyone. We were maybe sixty feet away and something about him
made all seven of us go quiet at the same time. Then he stopped, and he
shouted something none of us could make out, and he went down. Hard.
Straight down onto the road, and he stayed there, moving. We called for
help and we left. That was enough of the town for one day.

We went back to the cabin and tried to reset the night. We made dinner,
we put on music, we laughed about the lighthouse guy because laughing
about it was easier than the alternative. And for a couple of hours it
was actually fine. It felt like a trip again.

Around eight, a car came down our road too fast. An old beater, engine
knocking, and it parked crooked in front of the closed bathrooms and
just sat there. Nobody got out. It sat with the lights off for about
forty-five minutes, and then it drove away. We told ourselves it was
nothing. Somebody lost, somebody turning around. But everybody had gone
a little still.

Later, two of us walked over to the one open bathroom to shower before
bed. It was past ten and it was raining hard. And there was a woman
sitting on the bench outside the showers. Mid-thirties, dressed for a
summer night, not a December one, sitting perfectly calm in the rain
like she’d been waiting there a long time. I was young and stupid and I
actually asked her how the showers worked, because I assumed she was a
camper. She looked at us and she smiled and she said something I still
don’t fully understand. She said, “You two aren’t supposed to be here
yet.” Not angry. Almost gentle. Then she went back to looking at the
road.

We showered fast and we did not talk. When we came out she was standing
much closer to the door than she had been, and I told her to leave us
alone, and we walked back quickly. And as we walked, the beater car came
down the road again, pulled into the same crooked spot, and a man got
out and started arguing with her. He looked at us the whole time he was
talking to her. Then she got in his car and they drove off, and we told
ourselves, again, that it was nothing. Just people. Just a bad little
town in the off season.

Everyone went to bed except me and one friend. We stayed up talking,
low, the way you do when you don’t want to admit you’re on edge. And
around three thirty in the morning, we heard the engines. Not one car.
Several. Three big trucks and the beater, all of them coming down our
road together, and they parked in a line by the bathrooms, and men got
out with flashlights.

They started going cabin to cabin. Shining the lights in the windows.
Trying the doors, testing the windows, moving down the row toward us.
They were on the second cabin before we understood what we were
watching. We woke everyone up. Seven people, every light on, and we made
ourselves loud and visible on purpose, and when the men saw how many of
us there were, they stopped. They stood in the parking lot and talked
and pointed at our cabin, and we packed everything we owned into our
cars in about four minutes flat.

We had to drive past them to get out. They pulled in behind us and
followed us all the way from the campground to the highway, headlights
right on our bumper, and then at the on-ramp they turned around and went
back. We called the sheriff the whole time. The number for the patrol at
the campground, the one whose car we’d seen parked there since the first
afternoon. It rang and rang and no one ever answered. Not once, all
night.

Here’s the part I can’t explain. A week later, still rattled, I called
the county to report what had happened. I wanted it on record. I told
them the campground, the dates, the patrol car. And the man on the phone
was quiet for a second, and then he told me that campground had been
closed for the season since the end of October, and that there had been
no patrol assigned to it, no car, no deputy, nothing out there at all.
He was certain. I described the car. He said it plainly, like he’d said
it before to someone else.

There was no one out there. There hadn’t been anyone out there for
months.
