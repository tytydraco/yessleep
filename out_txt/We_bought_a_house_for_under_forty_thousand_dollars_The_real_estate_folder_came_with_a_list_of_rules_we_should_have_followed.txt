Mom woke up on a Tuesday vomiting a blackish foam that stained her
teeth. She shook in bed so hard the cheap particle-board headboard
banged against the wall. I didn’t look at her. I sat on the edge of my
mattress with my knees pulled up to my chin and stared at the baseboard
until the noise stopped.

A week later, the doctors discharged her. They told us the treatment
wasn’t taking, but they used that quiet, professional voice that meant
they just wanted the bed empty. They said she could go home to rest.

We shouldn’t have bought that house. It was the only thing listed under
forty thousand dollars within two hours of the city, sitting out past
the gravel pits where the air always smelled faintly of burnt tires and
stagnant ditch water. The paint was peeling off the siding in long, gray
strips.

The real estate agent—a man with dry, yellowish skin who wouldn’t make
eye contact—handed us the keys over the hood of his sedan. Tucked into
the back of the folder was a handwritten note on a yellow legal pad,
scribbled in a messy, childish hand: Living Rules. 1. Ignore the
secondary inhabitants. 2. Never leave doors open after the sun goes
down.

Mom laughed and signed the papers anyway. She had a smudge of blue ink
on her thumb and her hair was tied back with a rubber band. I was
twelve, and it just seemed like some weird joke the previous owner left
behind.

Before the sickness got bad, she used to sing while she cooked, humming
old tunes while her hands were dusted with flour. But this place sucked
the life out of her right away. By winter, the house had settled into
its own rhythm. The walls groaned whenever the wind picked up, and the
kitchen sink backed up with water that smelled like wet dirt. Mom spent
most days on the living room sofa with a fleece blanket over her legs,
staring up at the popcorn ceiling while the small TV played on mute.

The diagnosis hadn’t come as a sudden shock so much as a slow
confirmation of what we already knew from the way she stopped eating. By
spring, she was too weak to walk down the hall by herself. She didn’t
talk much anymore. She just went rigid when the pain spiked, her fingers
clawing into the fabric of the sofa until her knuckles turned white,
whispering apologies for things she hadn’t done.

That night, she managed to walk me back to my room. She tucked the
scratchy wool blanket under my chin, smoothed my hair down once, and
turned off the light.

She left the door cracked about an inch. Just enough for a strip of
yellow hallway light to cut across the floorboards.

I heard her crying in her room after that. It wasn’t loud—just that
muffled, rhythmic hitching she did into her pillow when she thought I
was asleep. I wanted to get up and go to her, to hold her hand the way
she used to hold mine when I had bad dreams, but a heavy, stupid
paralysis glued my ankles to the mattress.

Then the crying stopped. Dead quiet.

The thing was already inside the room when I sat up. It didn’t drop from
the ceiling or crash through a window; it just occupied the corner where
the shadows were thickest, smelling wet and metallic, like old pennies
left in the mud.

Its face was covered in matted, grayish fur that looked like wet carpet
fibers pulled from a drain, and its body was too long, folded impossibly
into the corner like a pile of heavy winter coats. Its limbs ended in
blunt, black-nailed fingers that twitched against the baseboards, and
its eyes were too bright, a flat, sickly green that didn’t reflect the
light. Its nose—a raw, wet slit—twitched every time I breathed, drinking
in the air like it was measuring the salt in my sweat.

I remembered the list from the real estate folder. Ignore them.

“Amy,” it said.

It didn’t sound like a voice. It sounded like dry wood snapping right
next to my ear.

When I didn’t answer, its head didn’t just turn—it ratcheted sideways, a
series of wet clicks echoing inside its skull until its jaw was resting
against its collarbone. A thin line of black, oily drool leaked from the
corner of its mouth, hitting the floorboards with a soft tink. It began
to crawl out of the shadows, dragging its heavy torso forward without
making a sound, its long fingers sinking an inch deep into the wood like
it was soft clay.

I pulled the blanket up over my nose and stared straight at the wall.
“Go away.”

“You looked,” it whispered. It was right at the edge of the bed now. I
could feel the freezing draft rolling off its hide, smelling of wet
earth and old copper. Its breath puffed against my bare foot, ice-cold
where it touched my ankle.

The floorboards groaned outside my door. The house always creaked, but
this was different; the whole structure seemed to lean inward, shifting
its weight to accommodate whatever else was moving down the hall toward
Mom’s room.

The next morning, the house was freezing. Mom was still in her bed,
breathing in that shallow, rattling way that had become our new normal.
I sat by her side with a plastic cup of water, watching the way her skin
looked gray and translucent under the bedroom light. When the home-care
nurse came by twice a week to change her bandages, I stayed out in the
hallway, staring at the peeling wallpaper and wondering how long a
person could haunt a place before the walls started to look like them.

Months blurred together like that. Weeks of cold broth, dirty sheets,
and the quiet sound of her fading out. I learned to measure time by the
color of the mold blooming along the window frame and the dry scratching
sounds behind the plaster—the creature pacing the studs, its claws
ticking against the lath like a clock counting down. Sometimes, when I
walked past the hall mirror, I’d see a flash of flat green eyes staring
out from the reflection before my own face caught up.

Behind the drywall of that hallway, we later found out from local
archives, a whole network of shallow utility spaces had been sealed off
decades ago by the county—spaces built over the old drainage basin where
the foundation settled unevenly into the silt. The real estate agent
hadn’t given us a discount because of the paint; he sold it cheap
because the previous three families hadn’t just moved out, but had left
behind clothes, half-eaten dinners on the table, and front doors wide
open to the night wind. They didn’t flee from robbers. They left because
the house was breathing for something else, using the open doorways like
valves to pump its livestock through the floorboards.

Until a stormy night in August. The rain hammered against the roof so
hard the plaster dusted down onto the floorboards. Mom’s breathing
finally stopped hitching and just… leveled out into a silence that felt
heavier than any noise.

When the county doctor finally came out to sign the papers and the men
from the funeral home carried the stretcher down the narrow hallway, the
house felt entirely empty.

I stood by the window of my bedroom, watching the taillights of the van
disappear down the gravel road. The rain had stopped, leaving the world
slick and glistening under a bruised sky.

The shadow was standing by the closet, half-hidden behind the door. It
had unfolded itself fully now, standing seven feet tall, its shoulders
jammed against the ceiling joists, shedding clumps of matted gray hair
onto the carpet.

“Why didn’t you stop it?” I asked. My voice sounded thin and stupid in
the quiet room.

It didn’t move for a long time. Then it turned its head—too far, with
that dry, clicking sound from its neck—and looked at me with those flat
green eyes. Its jaw split open wider than any human’s should, revealing
rows of small, flat teeth caked in dried black foam.

“She left the door open,” it said.

That was all.

I live with my aunt now, two towns over, in a secure brick apartment
building with heavy steel doors and bright fluorescent hallway lights.
Child services processed everything, and that cursed house was
successfully auctioned off to an unsuspecting developer last year. I
haven’t stepped foot near the gravel pits since the day of the funeral,
and though I still double-check my locks every night out of pure
habit—and keep every door firmly bolted shut before the sun goes down—I
am safe, I am miles away, and the nightmares have finally started to
fade.
