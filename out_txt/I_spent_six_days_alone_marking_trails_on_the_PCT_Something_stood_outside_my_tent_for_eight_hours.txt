Always carry a titanium camp hatchet tucked under the right compression
strap of your pack, never inside the main frame. If you have to unbuckle
heavy canvas to pull steel, you’re already thirty seconds behind
whatever is tracking you through the timber. Five miles north of the
Oregon state line on the Pacific Crest detour, I unbuckled my pack and
found my hatchet wedged upside down inside the hydration sleeve,
stripped of its leather sheath and coated in fresh pine sap.

That stretch of the PCT through the lower Cascades is mostly dense
Douglas fir and volcanic gravel. I was handling early-season boundary
checks for the Forest Service, spending six days alone to clear downed
branches and log coordinate drifts on a handheld GPS unit. Cell service
drops three miles past the paved turnout on Highway 22. After that, your
only link to anyone is an old battery-operated satellite beacon that
pings the ranger office every eight hours. Honestly, I didn’t think much
of it at the time.

On the third afternoon, things started getting weird near the
timberline. I reached a Forest Service boundary marker—a galvanized
steel pipe driven three feet into the dirt with a stamped brass cap. The
pipe had been pulled straight out of the hard clay. It was jammed
sideways through the fork of a western hemlock branch, eleven feet off
the ground. The mud around the tree didn’t have boot prints, tire
tracks, or ladder marks. The ground beneath the branch was completely
untouched. I stood on an overturned log, pulled the pipe down, and
logged the location. That’s when the handheld GPS screen flickered twice
and froze on the coordinate display, showing zero satellite lock.

By six that evening, the light faded behind the western ridge. I cleared
a flat dirt spot eighty yards from a dry creek bed and pitched my
one-man tent. Standard backcountry protocol means hanging all food bags
twelve feet up and four feet out from a trunk to keep black bears out. I
threw a throw-line over a high fir branch, hoisted my sixty-liter dry
bag up, and tied the nylon rope to a dead stump with a clove hitch.

At nine fifteen, every single sound in the woods cut out. In the
backcountry, night is never totally quiet. Wind moves through the
canopy, frogs call from the drainages, and small animals rustle the
brush. All of it stopped in a split second. The forest went dead quiet.
I sat inside my tent with my boots on and a headlamp strapped over my
wool cap.

I unzipped the front door mesh three inches to check outside. The air
was completely still. Cold mist from the damp soil hung in a flat sheet
four inches off the dirt. Through that narrow gap in the zipper, I
shined my flashlight down the access trail. The LED beam cut through the
dark, hitting tree trunks fifty yards out.

Eighty feet down the trail, right at the bend where the path drops into
the creek bed, a tall human shape stood on the bare dirt. It had no
jacket, no high-vis gear, and no pack. It stood facing my camp with its
arms hanging straight at its sides. I clicked the flashlight to its
highest setting—twelve hundred lumens—and put the center spot right on
its face. The light didn’t reflect off its eyes. It didn’t cast a shadow
on the fir trunks behind it. The shape didn’t blink, shift its weight,
or move at all under the direct beam.

I sat back on my rolled sleeping pad, picked up my camp hatchet, and
held it across my knees. I didn’t yell out. I didn’t step outside the
tent. For nearly eight hours, I sat there in total silence, watching
that spot through the three-inch zipper gap. The figure across the trail
didn’t move an inch until the first faint daylight hit the upper trees
at five in the morning. As the light changed, the dirt path near the
bend was suddenly empty. I never heard a single footstep moving off
through the brush.

When full daylight hit the forest floor, I unzipped the tent and stepped
outside. The birds were calling in the high branches again. I walked
down the path to the exact spot where the shape had been standing. The
ground there was damp clay from the morning mist. There were no boot
prints, no bare tracks, and no kicked-up leaves.

I walked back to camp to pack up my gear. The nylon rope I’d tied to the
stump was still secured with the exact same clove hitch. The line hung
straight up into the hemlock tree, but my sixty-liter dry bag wasn’t on
the loop anymore.

I searched the clearing. Sixty yards up an unmaintained timber ridge, on
a steep forty-five-degree slope through thick brush, I found the bag. It
was sitting upright on top of a flat granite boulder. The roll-top
buckle was still clipped shut. Inside, my food, spare socks, and
first-aid kit hadn’t been touched. But right next to the bag, pressed
into the green moss on top of that granite boulder, was a single set of
marks: two bare footprints pressed four inches deep into the living
moss, starting right on the solid rock and stopping at the edge, with no
tracks leading up to the boulder and none leaving it anywhere on the
ridge.

I packed the dry bag into my frame, strapped the hatchet back under the
webbing, and left without making coffee or eating. I hiked eleven miles
straight back to the Highway 22 turnout without stopping for water or
taking a single break. The handheld GPS in my pocket stayed dead the
entire walk, showing the exact same frozen screen until my boots hit the
asphalt.

At the ranger station in Detroit, I handed my logbook to the officer at
the desk. I wrote down the displaced boundary pipe and reported the dead
GPS unit. I didn’t say a word about the shape by the creek, the silence,
or the footprints in the moss. Out here, official reports require
evidence you can put on a table. I signed my shift release, handed over
the truck keys, and turned in my gear for the season.
