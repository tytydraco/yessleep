Who killed them?

So much time has passed that people don’t ask me that anymore, mostly
because I still fear for my safety, just as much as you should. Either
way, I don’t want to take this to the grave. I’m an old man now, on my
way out, so I’ll tell you what I remember.

It was a hot summer — your report should have the exact date. Tomás rode
in the back of the truck, watching the little favelas go by through the
window; almost none of them had any color, just gray brick and lines of
bare concrete peeking through. Carlos drove up front with Mario beside
him. I was in the back, staring at the floor of the beat-up unit they’d
assigned us — the worst piece of junk you can imagine, if you want to
know the truth. God knows where our taxes actually went. I was dizzy,
nodding off; we’d been driving for over an hour to reach our
destination. The road twisted the whole way — uphill, downhill, right,
left — and on top of that the heat that season was unbearable. The place
they sent us to didn’t have a single tree. Everything was buried under
those favelas.

The assignment: go investigate a reported case of human trafficking. The
place we were headed had a terrible reputation — murders in broad
daylight, strange disappearances, territory run by the cartels. When we
arrived, I couldn’t stop myself from throwing the door open and vomiting
in the corner of one of the houses. I had to brace my hands against the
wall; I felt like my soul was trying to climb out through my mouth. When
I finished, I felt that instinct every human being feels when they’re in
danger — the sense of being watched. I snapped my head up toward one of
the rooftops in front of me and, I swear to God, I saw a child duck out
of sight; out of the corner of my eye I caught a curtain falling shut.

A silence swallowed the whole place. Carlos asked if I was okay; I told
him yes, even though something inside me was already saying this place
was bad news. None of us had ever been there before, any of the four of
us, and you didn’t need more than a glance around to know it wasn’t
safe. It looked like a narrow gray labyrinth of houses. Carlos’s face
didn’t believe me, but his voice did — without meaning to, he had the
same feeling I did. Maybe all four of us did. We just didn’t give it the
weight it deserved.

We split up once we got to the area. Carlos came with me — I’d patrol
the main streets, and he’d cover the streets near a school where,
according to the reports, most of the disappearances were happening.
None of us walked in with our weapons drawn; the idea was to patrol
without drawing attention. Tomás and Mario stayed by a church, on the
far side of the sidewalk from where the patrol truck was parked.

Before we split up, I wanted to tell them about the bad feeling I had,
that I wanted to get out of there as soon as possible. I didn’t. Maybe I
told myself it was just my imagination mixing with the town’s awful
silence. You have the reports — I assume you at least read them, or
looked into this before coming to interview me. Well… doesn’t matter.
That was the worst day of my life. Reporters showed up to get the story,
and not one of them lifted a finger. Sons of bitches — I hope every one
of those bastards burns in hell. Anyway… where was I? Can you remind me?
Oh, right! Forgive me if I lose the thread — it’s been so many years.

Climbing the steep streets of that place, I noticed there wasn’t a soul
outside. I wondered where everyone had gone — was it somebody’s
birthday? Something about it turned my stomach. When I reached the top
of the street there was a small park, the closest thing the town had to
a center. I decided to sit and rest a while. Before sitting, I set my
vest down and unbuttoned my shirt halfway down my chest. The moment I
did, I caught — out of the corner of my eye — people peering out from
the rooftops of their houses. I knew then that the feeling of being
watched hadn’t been in my head. I pretended not to notice and leaned
back on the little bench, trying to rest. I kept my eyes half-open,
faking sleep, and sure enough — there they were, still watching. They
knew who belonged there and who didn’t.

After lying there a good while, I got up. I figured Carlos had finished
checking the streets near the school by now. The sun was sinking behind
the mountains you could see from up there, since the park sat on high
ground. A beautiful, deep-red dusk lit up the whole place; if you’d been
there, you’d have sworn it was somebody’s last day alive. I think it was
a sign from God, warning us something terrible was coming.

Heading back down the slope, I nearly split my lip tripping over
something. The shadows those houses threw across the street made it hard
to see where I was stepping, and my eyes hadn’t adjusted yet — I’d gone
straight from bright daylight into shade, hurrying to meet the others at
the church and tell them I hadn’t seen anything out of the ordinary,
just locals peeking out from rooftops like they were hiding something.

Exhausted, I reached the bottom of the street, ready to head straight
for where the truck, Mario, and Tomás were waiting. That’s when my heart
gave a hard, sudden lurch. I froze for a few seconds, sucked in a
breath, and a chill ran from my heel straight up to the back of my neck.
Something terrible was happening — I knew it — so I picked up my pace,
broke into a jog. All I wanted was to get out of there, immediately,
without leaving anyone behind. I changed direction and went looking for
Carlos, over by the school. I don’t… I don’t know how to keep going with
this part. It’s too much for me. Anyway.

I stopped caring about drawing attention. All I wanted was out, so
instead of jogging I ran. The road to the school wasn’t paved, littered
with loose stones; somehow I never tripped. I could see the school from
a distance — a big blue rectangle with white pillars, covered in
graffiti, ringed by six-meter fences topped with barbed wire to keep
people out. That wasn’t all — the closer I got, the more I could make
out shouting, wave after wave of an angry crowd. I stayed frightened,
confused, my heart hammering somewhere behind my ears. I couldn’t see
anything from that distance because, like I said, the dirt road leading
to the school had so many dips and rises. It was like a curtain — the
closer I came, the more the veil of dust and dirt lifted, revealing the
whole spectacle.

That spectacle, the one the crowd was roaring over with such fury,
turned out to be the great host himself — the great magician, Carlos —
performing an incredible act: how to take a beating from a mob and not
die. A pack of animals, that’s what they were. Carlos was screaming,
desperately trying to shield himself with his arms. One image that’s
never left me is watching a man land a hard punch to his ribs, hard
enough that Carlos spat saliva out of his mouth. I screamed at the top
of my lungs for them to leave him alone, that we weren’t there to hurt
anyone, that we posed no threat — that we’d come to help stop the very
problem plaguing the town. It did nothing. My voice just dissolved into
the roar of everyone else’s.

I never even got a clear look at him. The crowd shoved me back, slow and
violent. I couldn’t tell you how many there were — maybe the whole town,
only God knows. The mass of bodies closed over the one gap I’d had to
see through. Before it sealed shut completely, I saw Carlos, his head
soaked in blood, trying to explain something while an old man beat him
with a wooden plank. Carlos tried to shield himself again, uselessly —
blows from iron pipes, kicks, fists, more planks raining down on him
from every side. “It’s him! It’s him! Kill the rapist!” I heard someone
shout from inside the crowd.

Somehow I forced my way through, shoving with everything I had at anyone
in front of me. A few grabbed at my back, but I didn’t let them stop me.
When I reached the center of it, I found Carlos crying. He saw me, and
his eyes lit up like he’d just seen Christ himself. He crawled toward me
on his hands and knees, leaving a trail of blood from his stomach, his
clothes shredded, his body bare. He grabbed my knee and said, with what
little strength was left in him: “Tell them. Tell them why we came.
Please, tell them, they’ll kill me if you don’t.” My throat closed up.
He’d already lost most of his teeth to the beating. His eyes were
swollen shut — I think he lost his sight only minutes later. “Go on,
tell them.” A man wearing a copper ring drove a fist into his face,
bending his nose sideways, exposing bone underneath. Carlos collapsed a
couple feet from me, his hand slipping off my leg, his body twisting in
pain. “Mamá… ahh… Mamá.” I couldn’t hold back a tear. I grabbed the arm
of the man who’d just shattered his nose and hit him as hard as I could.
He went down instantly. Everyone turned and stared at me. For one
moment, everything went silent — it felt like the crowd had calmed, like
maybe this was over. I was wrong. It was only the beginning.

“The state sent us — is checking the streets reason enough to beat a man
half to death? Does that seem fair to you, you bastards?” I shouted.
“You’re his accomplice, aren’t you? Rats, all of you, all the same. Get
him too! He’s part of it — the girls, he’s responsible too!” I froze,
confused for a second. The sun had fully set by then; the streetlamps
were flickering on one by one, lighting up the scene. Then all the men
came at me at once — so many blows landed at the same time that the pain
blurred into one long, cold numbness. “Grab the other one, he’s getting
away.” Everyone turned, me included. I saw Carlos trying to run, his
whole body slick with blood, clutching at his pants trying to cover
himself, waddling like a penguin as fast as he could manage. It didn’t
matter. They all went after him. A stone sailed over the crowd and
caught him in the heel; he went down face-first into a mud ditch. A man
was on top of me, pinning me down, but he was distracted, watching the
twenty-odd men swarming Carlos. I took the chance — leaned in and bit
down as hard as I could, tearing off part of his ear. He howled and I
head-butted him. He dropped, writhing.

I got up and ran for the truck. I could see the church cross from a
distance, so I ran and ran. The crowd noticed and a few of them gave
chase, right on my heels. I had to warn the others what was happening —
that we needed to save Carlos, that the crowd was screaming something
about girls. Give me a moment, let me get some water — my throat’s gone
dry. Water tastes different when you’re old. It tastes like life instead
of some clear, odorless liquid. Do you see this scar on my foot? A
perfect circle. I earned that mark that night, a little souvenir that
said: come back here again and we won’t be as kind as we were the last
time. Anyway. Let’s keep going.

The church cross kept growing larger; I knew I was close, close to the
truck. Relief flooded through me when I turned the corner where I’d
thrown up hours earlier. The ones chasing me had fallen way behind. But
when I rounded that corner, I saw a massive fireball rising up right as
I arrived. Those animals had torched the truck. I knew, right then, that
whatever hope I had left was gone. Against the wall were Tomás and
Mario, both stripped bare. Like Carlos, they were covered in blood, but
theirs wasn’t from fists — it was from blades, shallow cuts, but deep
enough to send rivers of blood running down them. There were a hundred
of them, maybe two hundred, maybe more — and there were only four of us.
A few people peeked timidly from rooftops, filming the whole spectacle,
others screaming for them to let us go. The screaming ones were mostly
women — some covering their children’s eyes, while the kids themselves
watched, grinning, like it was some kind of entertainment. It wasn’t
long before the ones I thought I’d left behind caught up and slammed me
to the ground. My vision went blank. There was no hope left. I thought
that was going to be my last day, dying in a way I never imagined, in a
place I never imagined.

Once they had me down, they dragged me against the wall with the others.
I turned toward my partners; they turned toward me too — our eyes all
said the same thing: fear, and somewhere underneath it, total despair.
When I looked up again, I saw stones flying straight at me. They struck
my face over and over, clattering to the ground with a sound like
marbles. Eventually I just stopped feeling the pain. I’d accepted
whatever was coming.

After the stoning, through the barking dogs, the screaming, the blows, I
heard the sound of vans pulling up. A narrow path opened through the
crowd, and TV reporters and camera crews pushed their way through the
mob toward us. A striking woman, no older than thirty, fixed her hair
and the thin necklace resting against her pale, delicate throat. There
were other journalists too, but out of all of them, she was the one who
wanted the hosts of this spectacle. The rest were busy interviewing
locals. She gave the cameraman a thumbs-up and walked toward me. I
lifted my head with everything I had left — I barely had the strength to
move my own neck.

“We’re here reporting live on an incident that has drawn a large crowd.
Behind me, a police officer is being assaulted — if your children are
watching, I’d recommend turning this off.” She stepped aside, and the
camera swung onto me. For a moment I felt ashamed of myself — I didn’t
want my family, especially my mother, to see me like that. The
microphone moved from the reporter’s mouth to an older, heavyset
woman’s.

“Ma’am, what happened here? Let me guess — the police up to their old
tricks again?” the reporter said, sweeping her hair back off her
forehead.

“They abused some little girls, the sons of bitches. We’re sick of these
filthy pigs, all they do is tear this community apart. I hope every last
one of them dies.” The woman kept talking, but by then her words had
blurred into noise. My vision had gone hazy. I wanted to die.

The other camera crews turned to my partners, naked, a thin coat of
blood the only thing covering them. A man in the crowd with a baseball
bat forced Tomás’s arm out straight. Tomás fought it, but five men held
him down until his arm was fully extended, and the man with the bat
swung it down as hard as he could. Tomás’s arm folded in half. His
screaming drowned out the crowd itself. It was horrific — the bone had
splintered so badly the skin couldn’t hold, and it tore through, blood
pouring out like a burst pipe.

The photographs taken that night are proof of the hell we lived through.
The autopsies showed how far the bodies had deteriorated; the coroners
said they couldn’t be put back together, so cremation was the only
option.

After Tomás came Mario. The camera crews recoiled in horror as a
splintered piece of wood, studded with sharp nails, flew out of the
crowd and lodged itself in Mario’s face. His screams were worse than
Tomás’s. The dogs kept barking, louder and louder. When my blurred
vision drifted upward, I saw a white shirt swaying on a clothesline
overhead. For a moment the noise around me faded, and strangely, that
made me feel calm.

Someone grabbed my hair and held a phone up to my mouth. Every reporter
and camera was on me now. I didn’t understand why until Tomás screamed
at me to answer it, that answering and calling for backup might be the
only thing that saved us. On the other end there was nothing but silence
for a few seconds, until I felt a blow to the back of my head.

“This is Officer Manuel, they’re killing us, you need to send someone,
please. I don’t know what happened with these people — they’re accusing
us of abusing some girls, that’s not true, someone must have made that
up.” A woman answered on the other end, sounding like she had no
interest in taking the call.

“Did you identify yourselves from the start?” — the reporters pressed
their microphones closer to my mouth.

“Yes, we identified ourselves from the very beginning, we need help as
fast as possible, please.” My voice, weak before, sped up now.
Everything inside me wanted to scream for help at the top of my lungs.

“Well look… we don’t have many units available right now.” An
uncomfortable silence hung on the line. “I’ll see what we can do. We’ll
send units in a couple of minutes.”

And as you read in the report, no more units ever came. Not even
paramedics showed up. That’s the part that still stuns me — the TV crews
reached us first, with no trouble at all, pushing straight through the
crowd, even interviewing us, while the state units never made it. I
waited for hours, foolishly clinging to that flicker of hope. The whole
thing was broadcast live on every national news channel. My own country
turned its back on me, on all of us, officers who’d only come for a just
cause. That’s why I swore I’d never go back — because after surviving
that hell by some miracle, I got threats of every kind, that if I ever
said a word, they’d kill me. The government offered me a spot in their
“victim support” program. I didn’t take it. As an investigator, I’d
already seen enough by then to know the obvious: the government and
organized crime are in bed together. The rest… the rest you already
know. There’s nothing more to tell.

The old man looks down again at his bare foot, at the circular scar. The
interviewer does too, and the old man notices.

“Seems your curiosity’s got the better of you — I see you’ve stopped
writing down what I’ve told you. I think you’ve gotten so caught up in
this story that you’re not doing this for work anymore, but because you
actually care. Isn’t that right? Well. Let’s keep going.

When they pulled the phone away from my mouth, a swarm of people had
gathered around Carlos — they’d dragged him from the school all the way
to the church. I watched men pull gas cans from a black pickup truck,
others closing in with knives. The cameras all swung toward him at once.
It was horrific. They sliced his stomach open. Fat and intestines
spilled out, slow and wet. I threw up at the sight of it. Carlos tried
to hold his own guts in, tried to push them back inside himself. The
same man who’d cut him open moved behind him and drove the blade into
his back. Carlos’s hands flew to his spine, and his intestines dropped
to the ground. I think the intestines hurt him more, because when they
hit the dirt, he doubled over, struggling, trying to gather them back
up. That’s when the men with knives closed in on him completely; the
crowd in front of me blocked the rest from view. All I heard was a
choking sound, the kind you hear when farmers slit a chicken’s throat.

Mario stared at me, terrified, that piece of wood still lodged in the
side of his face. His chest heaved with sobs, his lips trembling like a
heartbeat. Tomás lay beside him in a pool of blood — I think he’d
already bled out and died. If you’re asking me, Mario got the worst of
it, between the two of them.

They stacked Tomás and Carlos’s bodies together, then tied Mario’s arms,
hands, and feet and threw him on top of the pile. The men poured the gas
cans over all three. Mario looked at me one last time, his chest still
shaking with sobs. Every last one of those miserable bastards struck a
match — it took a few tries before one caught. They threw it onto Mario.
A sound went through the crowd that I swear I heard, the second that
match landed on his forehead. Mario went up in flames, screaming like a
dying animal, kicking wildly. He rolled like a log for a few seconds,
slid off the pile of bodies, then kept rolling in a circle through the
crowd, trying to put himself out. It was obvious that wasn’t going to
happen. Tomás and Carlos’s bodies were already burning, filling the air
with an unbearable smell of scorched flesh. The beautiful reporter
couldn’t hold back her own sickness and vomited. Everyone watched Mario
roll from place to place, desperate to smother the flames — even the men
who’d been guarding me stepped closer to the crowd to get a better view.

I used that moment. With whatever strength I had left, I got up and ran.
It was so dark I could barely see, and suddenly a kid appeared in front
of me holding a metal pipe. I stumbled back, but he drove it into my
right foot with everything he had. I wanted to scream from the pain but
held it in. The boy smiled, jumping with excitement. So I pulled the
pipe out of my foot — it was hot; the little bastard had heated it up
first. The boy stopped smiling, stopped jumping. There was no one else
around, just an old brick wall, so with everything I had left, I started
swinging that pipe at him. He was pinned against the wall, gasping, half
his face already caved in from the blows. He raised a hand, trying to
signal for me to stop. I didn’t care.

Somehow I made it to the nearest highway, where some drunk college kids
picked me up and got me to the ER. And that’s how I survived that night.

This story is based on true events. Some names, places, and details have
been changed for privacy and dramatic effect. Inspired by the night of
Tláhuac, Mexico, 2004.
