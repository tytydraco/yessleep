The emptiness I feel has grown unbearable. Despite months of trying to
put it into words in sterile therapy offices, there is no relief. I’ve
been labeled delusional, quietly threatened with involuntary holds, and
handed prescriptions designed to blunt what they assume chemically is
acute grief. They nod, they scribble in their legal pads, but they don’t
listen.

​The account that follows will not sound real. Whoever you are, reading
this on some late-night board, I only ask that you don’t immediately
dismiss my words as psychosis or drug-induced delirium. I am writing
this because keeping it locked inside is killing me, and this forum is
the only place left where I can put down the truth before it burns out
completely.

​I spent my childhood learning to notice what most people wouldn’t. My
father was an outdoorsman, and some of my earliest memories are of
sitting with him on the ridgelines of Pennsylvania, watching the wind
ripple across the valleys below and turn the segmented farmland into a
vast patchwork quilt of gold and rust. He taught me to track the water
flow of hemlock-shaded creeks and respect the deep woods. By high
school, hiking and wild camping were second nature to me.

​It was eight years ago, and the leaves had just started to break from
their branches. Anthony, Robert, and I packed up for a weekend trip in
the Furnace Hills. The area had drawn our interest for months. Long
before European settlers broke ground for iron-mining operations, the
ridges had served as a frontier between the Lenape to the north and the
Susquehannock along the river valleys to the south. The history was
etched into the land; miles of unmaintained secondary growth choked off
trails and access roads.

​We arrived late Friday afternoon, just as the sky began to deepen. The
autumn wind had picked up, stripping the red oaks and showering the
trail in dry amber leaves. We left my car in a gravel cutout near some
municipal ballfields and hit the path. We walked quietly, listening to
the various songbirds and wildlife calls around us. Within twenty
minutes, we were far enough into the trek to lose cell service. We
rigged our hammocks and rain tarps between three sturdy trees, coaxed a
small ring of deadwood into a fire, and ate our foil-pack meals in
relative quiet.

​When the sun dropped fully below the ridge, the dark felt unusually
heavy. Firelight gives the illusion of safety, but beyond our ring of
light danced skeletal shadows. Letting your imagination wander for too
long was never a good idea; a stray shadow or whistle in the wind could
become something far more terrifying. No matter how experienced you
were, or how brave, the forest had a way of playing with your mind.

​Sometime around three in the morning, a rhythmic tapping against our
rain tarp woke me.

​At first, I thought of loose acorns shaken free by the wind. Then came
the sudden, violent crack of an overhead limb. Robert and Anthony shot
upright in their hammocks at once. We clicked on our flashlights and
swept the beams into the upper canopy.

​No wind, yet the heavy branches of the oak above whipped side to side
with unnatural momentum. The disturbance moved rapidly through the
canopy, snapping smaller branches as it receded into the dark. For a
split second, my beam caught two wet, amber pinpricks of light
reflecting down at me. Before I could shout, the reflection vanished.
When we dropped our lights to the dirt around our camp, the forest floor
was littered with freshly torn, unripe hickory nuts. We tried to laugh
it off. We excused it as an owl or some other animal and returned to our
rest, but the back of my neck stayed cold until dawn.

​“Wake up, man. Sun’s burning.”

​Anthony kicked the base of my hammock strap. I rolled out into the dewy
grass, groggy and parched. We choked down protein bars, packed our kits,
and moved deeper into the hills. Our goal was an unmapped cluster of
abandoned pit workings and ruined masonry furnaces we had tracked down
through Reddit posts and Google Maps.

​The forest was unnervingly silent. No blue jays, no cicadas, only the
crunch of dry leaves underfoot. After an hour of climbing, the terrain
cut off abruptly at the rim of an abandoned open-pit cut. Far below lay
a flooded quarry, its water a dead, ink-slick turquoise. Its surface was
glass, revealing the submerged wooden cribbing of an ancient shaft
hundreds of feet down, choked in pale ribbons of green algae.

​As I stared into that drowned entrance, an unnatural snare began to pull
at me. My pulse hammered against my eardrums. The water seemed to exert
an almost gravitational weight, dragging my gaze down into the lightless
opening until my boots shifted dangerously close to the gravel edge.

​Anthony’s hand clamped down hard on my shoulder harness, yanking me
backward. “You sleep at all last night? You look like death.”

​“Yeah,” I rasped, wiping cold sweat from my upper lip. “Just… stared a
little too long. Just some vertigo.”

​“We can double back,” Robert added, eyeing the drop with obvious unease.
“No shame in taking an easy weekend.”

​“No, I’m good,” I lied. “Let’s find the foundations.”

​We skirted the quarry wall, keeping our pace slow. The path eventually
leveled out into a straight old hauling road framed by towering
hardwoods. The canopy interlaced above us like a cathedral vault,
filtering sunlight into gold dust. It should have been picturesque, but
the stillness was suffocating.

​A heavy thrashing in the underbrush broke our focus twenty paces ahead.

​We froze as a massive white-tailed buck stepped out onto the center of
the old road. Its rack was wide and asymmetric, but it wasn’t grazing.
It stopped dead in the center of the path, turned its head with a
mechanical, jerky snap, and locked its black eyes directly onto us. The
same suffocating vertigo from the quarry crashed back over me. The
animal didn’t flare its nostrils; it didn’t stomp. It merely stared
through us, its jaw slightly slack, until it turned on a dime and
slipped silently back out of sight.

​Robert exhaled with a nervous, breathless chuckle. “Tell me that wasn’t
a skinwalker, man. It looked like its neck was broken.”

​“Chronic wasting disease,” Anthony countered, though he gripped his
walking pole like a spear. “Brain-rotted deer. Let’s not get poetic.”

—allarly afternoon, we struck the rusted perimeter of an old property
line. A sagging four-strand barbed-wire fence was nailed into the trunks
of rotting locust trees, bearing faded aluminum NO TRESPASSING placards
whose paint had peeled away decades ago. We followed the fence-line
until we found a deep animal run where foxes or coyotes had carved a
trench beneath the bottom strand. We stripped our packs, dragged them
through the dead leaves, and squeezed under.

​Beyond the wire, any pretense of a trail was gone. We pushed through
tangled briars and jagged shale ridges until we broke into a bowl-shaped
hollow. In the center sat the concrete husk of an old hoist house.
Nature had spent decades tearing it apart; the roof was gone, sumac
trees grew straight through the fractured floor, and faded layers of
local graffiti plastered the walls.

​Lined up along the rotting back wall, placed with deliberate precision,
were three pairs of antique leather work boots. Each boot had been
stuffed to the brim with smooth, fist-sized river stones, their laces
tied into tight, intricate knots. We didn’t touch them. We took our
photos in hushed tones, unsettled by the display’s deliberate,
ritualistic look, and moved on.

​Following a trickle of spring runoff upstream, we climbed a cliff face
with loose ironstone debris. Halfway up the rock face, buried beneath
thick curtains of poison ivy and wild grapevines, we found an entrance
cut into the cliff.

​It wasn’t a natural cave. It was an old mine adit, brutally reinforced
to keep the world out. Heavy oak timber, sheet metal, and industrial
chain-link had been bolted into the rock, the seams secured with crude,
oxidized field welds. Yet, near the lower right corner, the steel had
been torn violently outward; the metal jagged and warped as if peeled
back by heavy machinery.

​“Someone breached it,” Robert whispered, kneeling to inspect the rusted
gap. “Looks wide enough to shoulder through.”

​“We don’t cave without ropes,” Anthony said flatly. “Especially not in
abandoned iron cuts. Ground’s hollowed out underneath.”

​“We’re just checking the threshold,” I said, a strange, electric
anticipation in the pads of my fingers. “Ten feet in. Just a quick
peek.”

​Before Anthony could argue, a sound rose from the dark beyond the torn
steel.

​It was faint at first, then unmistakable: a sharp, desperate human wail,
echoing from deep within the subterranean rock—a woman’s voice, cracking
with panic, calling for help.

​Every survival protocol we had drilled for a decade vanished. In the
heat of adrenaline, you don’t think about Native folklore warning
against mimics, or the impossible logistics of how someone got past that
welded steel. We tore our flashlights from our shoulder straps, dropped
to our bellies, and squeezed through the rusted tear into the dark.

​The tunnel immediately narrowed into a ragged, moisture-slick crawlway.
The air smelled of wet stone, oxidized ore, and undisturbed earth. We
moved as fast as our knees would carry us, the cold stone scraping our
elbows. The cry for help had vanished, replaced by a rhythmic, metallic
tapping that seemed to reverberate straight through the bedrock.

Anthony led, Robert behind me, when the shale beneath my boots slipped
away with a sharp tear. There was no time to brace. The floor dropped
out instantly, plunging us into a sickening, weightless slide down an
invisible chute before we slammed hard against the limestone floor
fifteen feet below, the breath detonating from our lungs in a single,
ragged gasp. ​

“Everyone intact?” I wheezed, my shoulder throbbing where it had slammed
against the wall.

​“Yeah,” Robert groaned, shaking out a bruised wrist.

​Anthony didn’t reply. He was already on his feet, his high-lumen
tactical beam fixed unwaveringly on the chamber wall ahead of us.

​I dragged myself up, tracking his light.

​We weren’t in a raw mining drift. Set into the native limestone was a
massive timber doorway. The framing wasn’t crude industrial rough-cut
lumber, but heavy, blackened beams carved with deep, flowing motifs;
fluid lines that resembled both coiling serpents and unfamiliar
geometric script. Fitted flush within the frame was a single, immense
door forged from dense heartwood, banded with ornate plates of
green-tinted copper.

​Robert’s beam trembled against the carvings. “That wood… It’s too
ornate. That doesn’t belong down here.”

​“Could be an old blast vault,” Anthony muttered, though the uncertainty
in his voice was glaring. “A powder store from the 1800s.”

​“No powder house looks like this,” I whispered. I reached out a hand,
and even through my leather gloves, an unnatural, meat-locker chill
radiated from the timber. “Nobody builds a door like this to keep iron
dry. They built this to keep something in.”

I stepped forward and gripped the heavy copper ring set into the door.
The cold bit through my gloves instantly, sharp as dry ice, sinking an
ache straight into my knuckles. I threw my weight against it, but the
timber didn’t yield an inch.

​“Help me,” I muttered, my breath fogging thick in our beams of light.

​Anthony and Robert wedged their shoulders beside mine. For ten agonizing
minutes, we threw our combined strength against the wood, our boot heels
slipping on the slick limestone floor. With a screech like grinding
stones, the bottom of the door finally scraped inward. The seal cracked,
and a blast of subterranean air hit us; not stale or humid, but an
unnatural, freezing draft that made our teeth ache. It felt like opening
the hatch of an industrial freezer buried thousands of feet underground.

​We squeezed through the widening gap, our flashlights sweeping the
chamber beyond.

​The beam cut through a hanging vapor, illuminating an impossible
corridor. It was twenty yards of straight, precision-cut stone flanked
by square pillars, each capped with a mounted sconce carved from
unidentifiable bone. Between the pillars ran continuous, deep-relief
murals: processions of horned serpents, wolves, and towering antlered
shapes, all encircled by dozens of kneeling, faceless human figures
whose limbs bent at impossible, reverent angles. Down the center of the
flagstones ran a smooth, polished groove, worn deep as if centuries of
bare feet or dragged weight had followed that exact line toward an
identical door at the far end.

​None of us spoke. In all our years of scouting the ridges and old shafts
of Pennsylvania, we had never encountered anything that shattered our
understanding of history like this. No survey, no regional archive, no
local trail forum had ever breathed a word about an ancient temple
buried under the Furnace Hills. For all we knew, our boots were the
first to touch that groove in millennia.

​We followed the groove to the second door. It wasn’t sealed like the
first; it sat ajar by an inch and yielded easily to a light push. ​We
stepped inside, and when our flashlights found the far wall, our
understanding of everything shattered.

There in the depths was something I still fail to comprehend.

​It was vast and sickening, its bulk rising nearly to the vaulted stone
ceiling. The shape defied easy anatomy—a swollen, unsegmented pillar of
pale flesh that tapered into an eyeless brow, encased in overlapping
chitinous plates the color of stagnant motor oil. The light from our
flashlights seemed to fall into the greasy folds of sickly white skin
beneath the armor, swallowed rather than returned. From its back, long,
pale appendages pierced the surrounding rock like calcified taproots,
held fast by a network of pulsing, translucent fibers. There was no
mouth, no nostrils; only two massive, bulging compound spheres where a
face should have been, catching our beams and shattering them into
thousands of dead, identical points.

​At first, nothing moved. The paralysis that took me was absolute; every
survival instinct wired into my DNA screamed at me to bolt, to tear back
down the shaft until my lungs burst, but my body was no longer mine to
control.

​Then the mass shifted.

​As it peeled slightly away from the stone, the air pressure in the room
plunged with the violence of a deep dive. A silent wave washed through
the chamber; not a sound or a scent, but a smothering, absolute
stillness that scrubbed my mind raw. Panic vanished so fast it left me
lightheaded. I looked sideways at Anthony and Robert; their
white-knuckle grips on their flashlights had loosened, their shoulders
dropped, and their jaws slackened into the peaceful daze of men slipping
into a dream.

​The entity leaned downward, tearing two of its anchored limbs from the
rock with the wet, fibrous sound of bark stripped from its core. A draft
rolled over us; not the stench of rot, but rich damp loam, cold copper,
and cedar dust sealed away since the ice age. A rhythmic tremor vibrated
through the floor under our boots, a deep, synthetic breathing from an
organism that possessed no lungs. Its fractured gaze held us completely,
and instead of revulsion, a strange, weeping reverence took root in my
chest. We stood shoulder to shoulder in the cold mud, smiling back into
that impossible face, feeling safer than we had ever felt in our lives.

​I couldn’t say how long we stood locked in that fractured stare.
Seconds, hours, or entire days could have bled away in the dark. The
trance broke when the tapered tip of one pale limb drifted forward and
pressed lightly against the center of my brow.

​For a fraction of a second, white fire tore through my skull. It felt as
though a red-hot iron needle had been driven into the center of my
brain, searing through memory and thought alike. My vision was blinded
with an intense radiance that threatened to send me unconscious. I tried
to scream, but the air had fled my lungs.

​Then the agony vanished, and the pain dissolved into pure, weightless
ecstasy.

​When my eyes opened, the chamber had been remade. The stone architecture
remained, yet it breathed. A warm, liquid-gold sheen sweated from every
carved surface, illuminating colors that existed entirely outside the
visible spectrum; shades I had no human names for, vibrating with quiet
energy. Along the walls, the animal murals no longer sat static; the
stone carvings moved in continuous, rhythmic loops, living out an
ancient ritual frozen in time. The stagnant underground chill was gone,
replaced by a rich, humid fragrance of sweet grass, crushed pine, and
sun-warmed earth.

​And then there was the creature.

​The chitinous abomination was gone, peeled away by my rewritten senses.
In its place rose a majestic, towering monolith clothed in a velvet down
of iridescent rose and moth-pale silver, trembling softly with every
silent breath. Sweeping, feathery antennae curved like a delicate crown
atop its head. Its multifaceted eyes were no longer dead; they were
radiant spheres of spinning, kaleidoscopic amber, holding all of us
inside an infinite, loving embrace.

​A warmth so total, so absolute, poured into my chest that the cold mud
vanished beneath my boots. Gravity itself seemed to yield. Every sorrow
I had ever carried, every quiet terror of living, turned to vapor.

​By the time we made our way back out of the drift, dawn was breaking
through the ridge canopy. We walked in complete, reverent silence the
entire trek back to the car. There was simply nothing to say.

​The foreign spectrum of color hadn’t faded; it lingered in the emerald
moss clinging to the oak trunks, the golden mist rising from the valley,
and the clean morning air against our skin. Every physical sensation
felt rich and deeply intentional. For the first time in my life, the
quiet, persistent fracture of being a solitary human being vanished; I
felt seamlessly woven back into the world’s living fabric.

​Only after the engine turned over and we began the drive south did we
finally break the quiet. We spoke in hushed, trembling voices, piecing
the morning together. We had all felt the same violent terror, the same
sudden absolution, and the identical blinding flash of heat against our
skulls.

​We had always loved these woods. We had always found nature peaceful.
But what we carried home with us wasn’t peace; it was an entirely new
reality.

The blessing, as we called it then, didn’t fade when we returned to our
routines. If anything, it settled deeper into our marrow.

​For the first few months, life felt effortless. I had always been
socially guarded, burdened by a familiar, quiet anxiety that keeps you
from speaking your mind or reaching out. But since descending the
mountain, any fear or hesitation was gone. I spoke with an effortless
warmth that drew people in. Career doors that had been firmly shut swung
open; promotions followed without the usual corporate grind; and within
two years, I met the woman who would become my wife. When I looked at
her, I didn’t just see a partner; I saw someone outlined in that same
soft, golden luminescence we brought back from the vault.

​Anthony adapted with an aggressive, machine-like efficiency. He
channeled the euphoria into his ambitions, founding an equipment
logistics business that quickly began dominating regional contracts. He
became laser-focused, treating the creature not as a spiritual wonder,
but as an undeniable source of success. He tracked our calendar with
obsessive precision.

​Because there was a catch: the light had an expiration date.

​Roughly every four months, the colors would begin to dull. The foreign
shape peeled away from the edges of our vision, the persistent warmth in
our chests cooled, and a hollow, nauseating lethargy crept back into our
bones. The first time it happened, panic sent us careening back to
Lebanon County in the dead of night. We crawled through the jagged hole,
squeezed down the shaft, and knelt before the entity.

​It never demanded anything from us. It never moved from its stone wall.
It merely extended that pale, tapered limb, drove that split-second iron
spike of white fire through our skulls, and washed the world clean
again. We became regulars at a shrine with no scripture, kneeling in the
damp dark to receive our new god’s blessing.

​Yet, by the fourth year, the cracks began to show. And they started with
Robert.

​While Anthony and I flourished, Robert began to hollow out. He had used
his initial momentum to open a custom carpentry and framing shop, and by
all external metrics, he was thriving. But whenever the three of us met
for a drink, his laugh sounded strained, brittle. He was losing weight.
Dark purple crescents bruised the skin under his eyes, and his hands
possessed a subtle, involuntary tremor.

​“Doesn’t it feel like theft to you?” Robert asked one night during our
drive up the mountain, roughly five years in.

​Anthony glanced in the rearview mirror, his face illuminated by the
dashboard glow, entirely impassive. “What are you talking about?”

​“The work,” Robert said, his voice barely audible over the tires’ hum on
the highway “The contracts I landed this month. People look at my
furniture, and they praise the finish, the lines, the grain. They say
there’s soul in it. But I don’t feel like I made it. I feel like
something else is steering my hands, and I’m just taking the check.”

​“You’re overthinking it, Rob,” Anthony said, his tone clipped,
protective. “You’re doing the work. You’re swinging the hammer. The
creature takes away the doubt that used to paralyze you. That’s a gift.”

​“Is it?” Robert whispered, turning his forehead against the cold
passenger glass. “Or is it just numbing the nerve so you don’t realize
you’re bleeding to death?”

​During our visits, while Anthony and I closed our eyes and luxuriated in
the warm, floral-scented haze of the chamber, I started noticing Robert
lingering near the archway. He would stare at the entity with a look
that wasn’t devotion, but profound, sickened suspicion.

​Once, right after the creature touched his brow, I saw him flinch
violently; not just from the initial sear of pain, but from whatever
followed. His eyes darted around the shimmering walls, his jaw clenched
so hard the muscles knotted. While Anthony and I saw radiant moths and
amber light, Robert looked like a man trapped in a burning room.

​“I saw through it for a second,” he told me privately at a diner a few
weeks later, his coffee untouched and going cold. He was gripping his
fork so tightly his knuckles were white. “When the needle went in… for
half a beat, before the pink and gold rushed back. It didn’t look like a
moth, man. It looked like a swollen, lightless tick buried in the side
of the mountain. And we’re just… we’re just letting it lay eggs in our
heads so we don’t have to feel sad anymore.”

​“You’re spiraling, Rob,” I told him gently, feeling that boundless,
serene confidence that always made other people’s pain seem small and
easy to manage. “You’re having panic attacks. We’ve got a good life.
Better than we ever deserved.”

​He looked at me then, and the pity in his eyes chilled me. “You don’t
even know what’s real anymore. None of us do.”

​After that, the distance between us hardened. Contact dwindled to curt
text messages confirming visit windows. Adult life provided an easy
excuse: marriages, mortgages, expansion plans. But the truth was uglier:
Anthony and I didn’t want to look at Robert because Robert reminded us
that our paradise was synthetic. We started going to the mountain
separately. Anthony would make the trek alone every twelve weeks like
clockwork, keeping his engine running hot. I went whenever the gold
around my wife started to tarnish.

​Robert stopped returning our calls altogether around the start of the
seventh year. Anthony claimed he ran into him at a lumber yard in
Annville and said Robert looked gaunt, wearing heavy flannel in the dead
of July, mumbling about unsealing old mine records and the weight of
debt. We assumed he was finding his own way up the mountain on his own
schedule.

​We were wrong. He wasn’t feeding the habit anymore.

​He was letting the withdrawal tear him down to the bone, sitting alone
in the cold dark of his own mind, until he reached the point where he
couldn’t live with the silence, or the thing that stole it from him.

The end didn’t come slowly; it ripped through me like an electric shock
in the dead of night.

I was lying in bed beside my wife, drifting into sleep, when icy pins
clamped over the crown of my head. The darkness didn’t just welcome me;
it pulled me under like an iron weight. In my dream, I was running
barefoot through a fog-choked stretch of second-growth timber, branches
slashing across my cheeks and tearing at my chest. From somewhere deep
beneath the shale, a deafening horn sounded; a low, mechanical drone so
bass-heavy it resonated in my teeth. The forest floor suddenly gave way
beneath my boots, and I fell into an endless, lightless vertical shaft.

Right before I woke, the searing iron spike hit my brain again. But
unlike the creature’s touch, which instantly washed the pain clean with
honeyed light, this burn didn’t stop. It twisted, searing through my
skull like acid.

I woke with an axe embedded in my frontal lobe, gasping for air on wet
sheets.

The room was gray. Not the gentle gray of dawn, but a flat, corpse-like
slate that completely washed out the tapestries on our walls. I turned
my head on the pillow to look at my wife. The golden, shifting aura that
had crowned her for seven years- the divine light that made her seem
like something pulled from a Renaissance fresco- was completely gone. In
its place was a tired woman with sallow skin, dry hair tangled against
the pillowcase, and a jawline set in subtle tension.

An ungodly wave of panic slammed into my chest, violent and unfamiliar.
My hands began to shake uncontrollably; cold sweat pooled between my
shoulder blades. I dragged myself to the bathroom and looked into the
mirror, only to recoil in sheer revulsion. The person staring back was
gaunt, empty-eyed, and fundamentally hideous.

Over breakfast, I couldn’t swallow dry toast. My wife spoke about bills,
schedules, and weekend chores, but her voice didn’t sing to me anymore;
it was merely noise. Flat, irritating, rhythmic noise. A terrifying,
intrusive question gnawed at my ribs: Did I ever love this woman, or did
the creature drug me into compliance?

I tried to run, desperate to clear the poison from my head, but the
world outside was dead. The dog our neighbor walked didn’t possess
cloud-soft fur; it was greasy and smelled of stagnant water. The autumn
oaks weren’t a vibrant mosaic; they looked like brittle brown rot
clinging to skeletal frames. No matter where I went, that same
mechanical drone from my dream haunted me in whispers.

By noon, the tremors in my hands had traveled up my arms. What I was
experiencing felt like torture; migraines that made light unbearable,
cold sweats followed by waves of heat, and nausea that brought me to my
knees by the toilet.

I dialed Robert first; four calls, straight to voicemail.

Then I called Anthony.

He answered on the first ring. He didn’t even say hello. His voice was
cracked, raw, and panting.

“You feel it?” he hissed through the receiver.

“My head,” I managed, clutching my temples. “Anthony, everything’s gray.
Everything looks… wrong.”

“It’s gone. All of it. I can’t look at my files. I can’t look at my
people. It’s like my skull’s been scrubbed with bleach.” Anthony’s voice
dropped to a panicked whisper. “Robert’s family called me Tuesday. He
cleared out his shop last week. Took his tools and emptied his
commercial account. All he told them was he was about to do something
big.”

“We have to get up the mountain,” I choked out. “Right now.”

Two hours later, Anthony pulled his truck into the gravel lot by the
abandoned ballfields. Sitting directly across from us, coated in a dull
film of limestone dust, was Robert’s pickup. A faded municipal ticket,
curled and water-logged, was wedged under the passenger-side wiper
blade.

“He’s been here for days,” I said, my heart rattling against my ribs.

“Shut up and grab your pack,” Anthony snapped, his jaw tight, eyes
bloodshot and wild. He didn’t wait. He slammed his door and started up
the trailhead at a punishing, desperate march.

We didn’t pause for breath. The Furnace Hills weren’t majestic anymore;
they were an obstacle of jagged stones, dead thorns, and suffocating
briars. Every shadow seemed bent at a malicious angle, watching our
frantic retreat from reality. By the time we scrambled under the
barbed-wire fence, the sun had already slipped behind the southern
mountains, plunging the hollow into cold blue twilight.

We reached the ravine, hauled ourselves up the slick scree, and found
the entrance.

The welded sheet metal was torn further open, the fresh gashes in the
iron glinting silver in the twilight. We didn’t even discuss it. We
dropped into the dirt and crawled through the jagged gap, scraping our
forearms against rusted steel and wet stone.

The descent was a nightmare. The whispering had returned; not the
gentle, phantom hum of the entity, but the screeching voices of my own
failures, my wife’s hollow gaze, the terror of living without an
anesthetic. I slipped on the wet shale and tumbled down the slope,
scraping my ribs, but Anthony didn’t even turn around. He was already
across the floor, hurling his shoulder into the ornate, carved timber
door.

It swung inward, unobstructed.

We moved through the pillared corridor, our boots splashing through
something thick and slick that coated the stone flagstones. The smell
hit us before the light did. It wasn’t the cedar and crushed wildflowers
we had worshipped for nearly a decade.

It smelled of burnt gunpowder, sulfur, and stagnant pond water.

We stepped through the inner threshold, our headlamps cutting through a
dense, hanging haze. Anthony froze in front of me, his flashlight fixed
on the center of the floor.

I looked down, and my stomach violently purged.

Robert lay sprawled across the flagstones in a wide, dark lake of
congealed fluids. An AR-15 lay beside his outstretched arm, its bolt
locked back on an empty chamber, surrounded by dozens of spent brass
casings. His service handgun rested loosely in his left hand. A single
contact wound had destroyed the side of his head; blind cave spiders and
pale millipedes were already tracing paths across his gray collarbone.

Tucked into the breast pocket of his denim jacket, stained with dark
grease, was a folded piece of yellow legal pad. With trembling, numb
fingers, Anthony knelt and pulled it free.

There were only three lines scrawled in Robert’s jagged, aggressive
hand:

It didn’t fix us… comfortably numb.

A p…  fattened for the…

… feel the cold again before…

The hastily written note was obscured by various stains, leaving
Robert’s full message to die with him.

Anthony’s light slowly drifted up from the paper and swept the far wall.

The entity was dead.

It sat slumped against the limestone face, collapsed inward like a
punctured tire. Robert had emptied entire magazines into its bulk at
point-blank range. Shattered chitinous plates revealed pale, fibrous
meat that leaked a viscous, gray-green fluid onto the stone floor. But
the most horrific sight was its face. The massive compound eyes had been
pulverized into deflated, wet husks by rifle rounds.

Yet no defensive marks marred the walls. Its long, pale taproots
remained anchored deep into the rock face. One of its tapered, fibrous
limbs lay extended fully across the floor, the blunt tip resting just
inches from where Robert’s boots had been when he pulled the trigger.

It hadn’t fought back. It had tried to touch him. It had tried to soothe
the man who was murdering it.

“We have to call,” I whispered, my voice breaking into a hysterical,
breathless sob. “Anthony, look at him. His parents… we have to bring him
home.”

Anthony whipped around so fast his beam blinded me. His hand shot out,
gripping the fabric of my heavy coat and driving me back against the
cold stone pillars until the breath left my lungs.

“Are you insane?” he spat, his face inches from mine, his eyes wide and
bloodshot, trembling with the violent edge of a cornered animal. “Look
at this room! Look at that thing! Who explains this? How do we tell the
police about seven years of coming here? About Robert’s rifle? They’ll
pin his murder on us before the sun comes up. They’ll lock us in a state
hospital for the rest of our lives!”

“Anthony, please—”

“Shut up!” he snarled, shaking me hard. “Do you want to lose your house?
Your wife? Every single dollar you made? The second people know what we
are, it all burns down. You leave him. We seal the hole. We never speak
of this again.”

He shoved me away and grabbed a heavy iron pry-bar left behind in
Robert’s open rucksack. I didn’t fight him. The withdrawal was complete
now; the last embers of supernatural courage had turned to cold ash in
my belly. I was weak, cowardly, and sick.

We dragged ourselves back through the narrow crawlspace to the outer
adit. Working in the dark with the pry-bar and heavy timber wedges from
the old entrance shoring, we levered the rotted support beams out from
beneath the fractured cliff face. With a deafening, grinding roar, tons
of ironstone, shale, and topsoil collapsed over the breach, sealing the
adit behind twenty feet of stone.

We wiped our prints from the tools, threw Robert’s pack down a flooded
prospect hole on the ridge, and walked back to the truck in silence.

That was six months ago.

The acute tremors eventually stopped, but the light never returned. I
live in a world rendered in dull newsprint. My wife still asks why I’m
distant, why I look through her when she talks about her day, but I have
no words to give her. The mask I put on each morning is heavy and
cracked, but it’s all I have left to protect what’s left of my life.

I know what the therapists write down. I know the labels they place upon
me: major depressive disorder, psychotic break, grief-induced
dissociation. They tell me to take the SSRIs, to talk about my estranged
friends, to accept that young men sometimes disappear.

They don’t know that fifty feet below the earth, my best friend is
rotting in the dark beside an ancient, blind god that died trying to
comfort him.

I don’t expect answers on this board. I don’t expect anyone to believe
me. But if you ever find yourself hiking the old Furnace Hills, and the
forest suddenly falls dead silent; if you feel an unnatural chill
bleeding out of the rock, or find three pairs of boots filled with
stone; turn around.

Some things in the deep woods aren’t monsters because they want to tear
your throat out.

They are monsters because they show you what it feels like to be whole,
and then leave  to bleed out in the gray.
