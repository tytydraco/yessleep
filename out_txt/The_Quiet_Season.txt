Dad used to say you could tell how bad a night was by how quiet the
morning came. I figured he meant weather. He did not mean weather.

I found the first two does on a Tuesday, back pen, right by the stock
tank. Dead, both of them, but laid out neat. Like somebody tucked them
in. Two holes in each neck, about as wide as a pencil, and no blood. I
spent ten minutes on my bad knee crawling around looking for it. The
dirt was dry. The wool was clean. Nothing.

Wednesday it was four more.

I called Don Ignacio, who lives a mile up the road and has an opinion on
everything from the price of feed to the Pope. Coyotes, he said. I said
coyotes make a mess. He said, so? I said these ones didn’t. He was quiet
long enough that I checked my phone to see if the call dropped, and then
he said he’d come by Saturday and bring his dog.

Thursday I walked out at first light and it was too quiet. I’ve got
thirty-one head, or I did, and those goats scream at the feed truck, at
me, at the sun, at their own shadows. That morning I stood in the yard
and heard the windmill and a truck on the county road and nothing else.

The barn door was open. I’d latched it the night before, I always do,
with a carabiner since the hook broke in July. The carabiner was lying
in the dirt, still clipped shut. Whatever opened that door had ripped
the whole eye screw out of the post.

I didn’t go in. I stayed by the gate with Dad’s shotgun and looked
through the slats, and every goat I could see was standing dead still,
facing the corner under the hayloft. Not eating. Not shifting. Just
looking at the dark in that corner like it owed them money.

Chato pushed up against the back of my legs and I could feel him shaking
through my jeans. Chato is a heeler mix who once picked up a rattlesnake
by the head. I’d never seen him scared of anything.

“Vámonos,” I told him, and we backed to the house, and I didn’t turn
around until the screen door slapped shut.

I drove to Tractor Supply that afternoon and bought a trail camera,
eighty-nine ninety-nine plus tax, the kind deer hunters strap to trees.
Infrared flash, motion sensor. I mounted it on the center post of the
barn, aimed at the hayloft corner, and I remember feeling smart about
it. Evidence. Like I was on one of those shows.

Don Ignacio came by anyway, a day early, without his dog. He said the
dog wouldn’t get in the truck. He stood at the fence and told me about
his grandmother in Reynosa, who wouldn’t say the name of the thing out
loud after dark, because it listens for its name, she said. He told it
like a joke and then he stopped halfway through and didn’t finish.

“Lock your door, mija,” he said, and went home.

I ate cereal standing up at the counter. I put the shotgun on the
kitchen table, with the lights on, and sat there like I was waiting for
a bus.

11:40. I know because I looked at the stove clock right when it started.

I never heard the barn door. I never heard feet. What I heard, out in
the yard, was my father saying my name.

“Elena.”

He’s been dead two years this March. I held his hand at the hospital in
McAllen. I signed the papers and picked out the box.

“Mija.” Warm, tired, the exact way he used to holler across the yard
when supper was ready. “Help me out here. The light’s out in the barn, I
can’t find the switch.”

It was his voice. It was his words, even, the dumb little complaint he
made a hundred times. But there was breathing underneath it, long and
wet and whistling, and way too slow, and no man ever had a chest that
big.

Chato was under the table by then, making a noise like a rusty hinge. He
wasn’t barking. He was staring at the window over the sink.

“Elena.” Closer. The porch now. “Open up, it’s cold out here.”

I watched the doorknob. Cheap brass thing, Dad put it in back in 1998.
It turned a quarter inch and stopped against the bolt, and turned back,
gently, like whoever was out there didn’t want to be rude about it.

Then the voice cracked in half. It went high and thin and it wasn’t Dad
anymore, it was a kid goat, a little one, crying for its mother. Then a
second one started up. Then ten, twenty, the whole herd bawling in the
dark, and every single voice was coming from the same spot on the other
side of that door.

That’s when I got it. The goats hadn’t gone quiet because they were
scared. They’d gone quiet because something else was doing their
screaming for them.

It was practicing. It had been listening to me my whole life.

I didn’t sleep. When it got light the noise just cut off, like somebody
pulled a plug, and I waited another hour anyway before I slid the bolt.

Scratches on the porch boards, four of them side by side, deep enough to
put a finger in. At the bottom step, one print in the dust. Two toes
pointing forward, two pointing back. Bigger than my hand.

The barn was empty. No bodies. No blood, no hair, nothing kicked over.
Thirty-one goats just gone, and the straw in the pens hadn’t even been
disturbed. The camera was still on its post, little red light blinking
away.

I pulled the card and took it to the kitchen and put it in my laptop,
and my hands were doing something I didn’t like.

Hundreds of pictures. Moths, mostly. Wind in the dust. Then 11:38,
something at the edge of the frame. Tall, hunched, up on two legs, with
arms that hung past its knees and a line of black spines down its back.
The eyes lit up white in the infrared, flat and wide, and it was looking
right at the lens.

11:39, closer, head tilted. The mouth hung open and went back farther
than a mouth should. 11:40. 11:41, and the whole frame was one wet
ridged nose and a tongue that wasn’t a tongue, it was a hollow needle
uncoiling out of the mouth.

11:42 was black. Somebody, something, had turned the camera, slow and
careful, away from the loft and toward the house.

11:43 was the yard. The porch light. The kitchen window. And in the
window, tiny and bright, me. Standing at my own sink with a shotgun in
my hands and my mouth hanging open, listening to my dead father ask to
come in.

It kept shooting every thirty seconds after that, right up to 6:12 in
the morning, which was about five minutes before I opened the door. The
last picture was the porch, very close. The doorknob. And flat against
the screen, a long gray hand with too many knuckles, resting there the
way you’d rest your hand on a sleeping dog.

Waiting for me to come out.

I looked up from the laptop. Out in the yard the windmill creaked once.

Then, from somewhere real close, in my voice, my exact voice, breathy
and scared, something said:

“Chato. Come here, boy.”

And under the kitchen table, where my dog had been all night, nothing
moved.
