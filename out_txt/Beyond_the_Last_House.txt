My mother used to tell my sister and me a story about a woman who lived
beyond the last house in a village.

Mum never gave the woman a name. She only told us that her hearing was
so sharp she could hear a child crying three valleys away, and someone
whispering from six feet underground.

Every evening, the woman would stand outside her home and listen.
Sometimes, she heard someone who could hear her too. Whenever that
happened, she would call into the darkness and ask them a simple
question.

“Can you hear me?”

Mum always finished the story with the same warning.

“If you hear her, do not answer.”

My sister used to ask what would happen if someone did. Mum never told
us. She would only say that the woman wasn’t asking whether they could
hear her voice. Whenever we asked what she really meant, Mum said we
were too young to understand. By the time we were old enough, we’d
stopped wondering.

I thought about that story for the first time in years on a Friday
evening, while my sister and I were driving to collect a vanity I’d
bought online. It was old, ugly and way too big for my apartment, but it
was made from solid wood and cheap enough to justify borrowing Dad’s van
for the journey.

The seller had warned me that the signal was unreliable near her house.
She sent me written directions beforehand and told me not to trust the
navigation system once we left the motorway. I trusted it anyway.

The first part of the journey was normal enough. We passed through a few
small villages and a petrol station. After that, the navigation system
directed me away from the seller’s directions and sent me down a narrow
road with trees on both sides.

I kept going because the map showed the road joining the main route
again further ahead. About twenty minutes later, I realised we hadn’t
passed a house in a while. The trees had grown thicker and closer to the
road, and there were no signs and nowhere wide enough to turn around.

The estimated arrival time still said twelve minutes. Sara noticed it
first. She leaned towards the screen and watched it for another minute
before pointing out that the time hadn’t changed since we left the main
road.

I refreshed the route. The map loaded slowly, then showed us moving
across an empty patch of green land. According to the screen, we weren’t
even driving on a road.

I stopped the van and looked for somewhere to turn around. While I was
doing that, Sara stared into the trees on her side. Her expression
changed, but she didn’t say anything at first. When I asked what was
wrong, she told me she had heard someone whisper.

I lowered my window slightly and listened. There were no cars or wind,
and no movement in the trees. The only sounds came from the engine and
the navigation system repeatedly telling me to make a U-turn.

Sara said the voice had sounded close enough to be inside the van, but
somehow she knew it was coming from very far away. Neither of us
mentioned Mum’s story, but I knew we were both thinking about it.

I reversed until we reached a slightly wider area of the road and
managed to turn around. The map recalculated straight away and directed
us back the way we had come. We should’ve reached the main road within
fifteen minutes, but instead, we found another petrol station that
neither of us remembered seeing on the way in.

It stood alone beside the road under a rusted sign. We were running low
on fuel and Sara needed the toilet, so we decided to stop. We agreed to
stay together and leave as soon as I asked for directions.

The man behind the counter looked surprised when we walked in. He was
elderly, with thick glasses and a portable radio sat beside him. The
radio seemed to be tuned between stations, producing nothing but a low
hiss of static. I told him we’d been following the lane behind and asked
how to get back to the main road.

He glanced through the window and frowned. From where we stood, the lane
was clearly visible past the fuel pumps, disappearing between the trees.
He said there had never been a lane there before. I might’ve assumed he
was joking if he had smiled, but he didn’t. 

He stared at the road for a moment before telling us to leave through
the opposite side we came from and continue until we reached a
roundabout. As we turned to go, he asked whether anyone else was
travelling with us. Sara told him it was just the two of us. He looked
through the window behind us again and said he thought he saw a woman
standing beside the rear passenger door. We turned to look, but there
was nobody near the van.

We didn’t dismiss it as a reflection. We checked the van and the narrow
gap beside the shop before getting back inside. There was nowhere nearby
where someone could’ve hidden without crossing the open area.

Once the doors were locked, Sara opened the notes app on her phone and
typed:

Mum’s story.

I typed underneath it:

I know. Don’t answer anyone we can’t see.

We left and followed the road the man had pointed out. Less than a
minute later, the lights disappeared behind us. It was odd. There wasn’t
a turn, a dip in the road or anything that could’ve blocked our view.
One moment, the station filled the rear window. The next, there was only
darkness.

At the same time, the navigation screen changed. The destination address
vanished, and a new instruction appeared in its place.

Continue until heard.

I switched it off. We continued to drive without speaking. The road
stayed narrow and completely empty, with thick trees on either side.
There were still no houses or road signs, and strangely, the fuel gauge
didn’t seem to move either.

Sara suddenly pressed both hands against her ears. She said the woman
was calling again. I still couldn’t hear anything. Sara said the voice
was repeating the same question from somewhere ahead of us, but it also
seemed to be coming from every direction at once.

I kept driving because there was nowhere safe to stop or turn around. We
agreed that we would stay inside the van until we reached somewhere
populated. If anyone appeared on the road, we’d keep the doors locked
and drive past them. 

Eventually, a light appeared in the distance. At first, I thought it
belonged to another car, but as we got closer, I realised it was coming
from an upstairs window. A house stood alone beside the road, surrounded
by dense trees. 

A woman was standing outside. She wore a long grey dress, loose hair
hung around her face, and her head was slightly tilted.

Sara told me not to stop. I already had my foot on the accelerator. As
we passed, the woman turned her face towards the van. She was older than
I expected, with pale eyes that looked unfocused.

Her lips moved, and this time, I heard her.

“Can you hear me?”

The question was barely louder than a whisper, but I could still hear it
clearly. Neither of us answered.

The road carried on without any turns. I kept checking the mirrors,
expecting to see the woman following us, but there was nothing behind us
except darkness.

Ten minutes later, we passed the same house again. We never turned
around, yet the house appeared ahead of us in exactly the same place.
The woman was still standing outside, only now she was closer to the
road.

She repeated the question as we passed. The third time the house
appeared, the woman stood in the middle of the road, forcing me to stop.
She was barefoot and mud covered the bottom of her dress, but her feet
were perfectly clean.

Sara handed me her phone. She’d written one word.

Reverse.

Before I could move, the woman spoke. 

“Your mother taught you well.”

It was the first thing she’d said apart from the question.

Sara made a small sound before she could stop herself. The woman’s head
turned towards her straight away.

I reversed until the woman disappeared into the darkness. As soon as I
found enough room, I turned the van around and drove back the way we’d
come.

A road sign appeared ahead of us soon afterwards.

BELRIDGE - 4 MILES

I let out a sigh of relief. Sara started checking her phone for a signal
while I concentrated on keeping my hands steady on the wheel.

Then she noticed the time. The dashboard clock still showed 8:17, and
her phone showed the same.

We arrived at the petrol station at 8:17. Since then, we’d been driving
for what felt like more than two hours. I assumed the dashboard system
had frozen when the navigation failed, but that didn’t explain Sara’s
phone. 

The sign ahead still said Belridge was four miles away. It grew larger
as we approached, then slowly became smaller again, even though we were
still driving towards it.

Sara opened her messages to text Mum, but there was already a message
sent from her phone at 8:17. 

WE CAN HEAR HER.

Sara hadn’t written it. There was no response from Mum, and the message
hadn’t been delivered. We didn’t try to call her. By then, we understood
that anything reaching us through the phones couldn’t be trusted.

Then the road sign disappeared, and the house stood in its place. 

The woman was waiting outside the house, exactly where she was the first
time. I tried to ignore her and drive past, but the engine cut out
before we reached the house.

The headlights stayed on, lighting up her pale face as she walked
towards the van and placed one hand against my window. There was no
anger in her expression. Instead, she looked exhausted and afraid. 

That was when I heard it. A sound came from somewhere past the house. At
first, it was too far away for me to recognise. 

The woman closed her eyes and listened.

The sound became clearer. There was a horn, tyres dragging across wet
tarmac and the sound of metal folding in on itself.

Then we heard ourselves.

Sara’s scream came first. My voice followed, shouting her name over and
over while glass shattered around us. The sounds seemed to be coming
from miles ahead, but they filled the inside of the van.

I turned towards Sara and saw that the woman was sitting in the back
seat now. Neither of the rear doors had opened. One moment the seat was
empty, and the next she was there with her hands folded neatly in her
lap.

I grabbed the door handle to get out, but it wouldn’t budge. The woman
told us not to be scared of her. The sound ahead was what mattered. She
explained that the stories about her hearing had changed over time.
People believed she could hear across huge distances because they didn’t
have any other way to describe what she could do.

She didn’t hear people from miles away. She heard them from moments that
hadn’t happened yet. Sometimes the sounds came from a few seconds ahead.
Sometimes they came from years in the future. She heard children crying
before they were born, buildings collapsing before they were finished
and travellers calling for help before they became lost.

Whenever she heard a voice clearly enough, she called out to it. Most
people never heard her because they were still safely inside their own
time. The people who answered had already started slipping towards the
moment she was hearing.

That was what her question really meant. She wasn’t asking whether we
could hear her voice. She was asking whether we could hear past the
moment we were still living in.

The horn sounded again, and the woman looked towards Sara. She said Sara
had answered first. Sara denied it, but I remembered the whisper beside
the trees and the way she went quiet before telling me about it.
Eventually, she admitted that after hearing the voice, she had whispered
back. She hadn’t thought one word would matter. 

The woman told her that answering had not caused anything. It only
confirmed what she already feared.

She could hear our crash. 

She could hear me afterwards.

She couldn’t hear Sara.

The road and the house started flickering. For a few seconds at a time,
I could see another place behind them. A wet road. The bright side of a
lorry. The van entering a junction.

The navigation system had never sent us down a different road. We stayed
on the main route the whole time. Everything that happened after 8:17
had taken place in the fraction of a second between seeing the lorry and
being hit.

The petrol station, the endless road and the house weren’t real places.
They were the only way our minds could process the final moments before
the crash. We heard the accident before it happened. The woman had found
us in the space between those two moments.

Sara reached for my hand. Her fingers felt cold and strangely light,
like there was already less of her there than before. She asked the
woman whether there was any way to change what she had heard. The woman
listened before answering, but her face told us first.

She said she could hear me waking up in the hospital. She could hear Mum
crying beside my bed and police officers asking me what I remembered.

But she still couldn’t hear Sara.

The lorry horn grew louder until it hurt. The woman reached forward and
placed one hand on Sara’s shoulder. She told her she was sorry.

Then the house disappeared.

I woke up in the hospital the following afternoon.

The lorry had crossed the centre line and hit the passenger side of the
van. The front of the van was so crushed that they had to get me out
through one of the rear doors.

Sara died before the ambulance arrived.

There had never been a diversion, a narrow lane or a petrol station. The
seller confirmed that she’d been waiting for us that evening, but we
never reached the turn before her house.

Mum had called us seventeen times after the crash, but none of the calls
had connected. The message on Sara’s phone was still marked as unsent,
even though it had a timestamp of 8:17.

The police recovered my phone from under the driver’s seat. The screen
was shattered, but the audio recorder had been running before the
collision. I didn’t remember opening it.

The recording began at 8:12. For the first few minutes, Sara and I were
talking about the vanity and whether it would fit through my front door.
The navigation system told me to continue along the main road.

At 8:16, another voice spoke from the back seat.

“Can you hear me?”

Sara went quiet. Then a few seconds later, she whispered yes.

The lorry horn started less than a minute after.

After I left the hospital, I asked Mum to finish the story. For the
first time, she did.

The woman past the last house wasn’t there to lure lost children away.
She called because sometimes she heard them before they were lost, and
she needed to know whether there was still time to reach them.

Most people never answered. The few who did were already too close to
whatever she had heard.

That was why the warning had always been so simple.

If you hear her, do not answer.

Not because answering brings her closer, but because if you can hear her
at all, she might already be listening to the last sound you’ll ever
make.
