Raymond was trying to get me to eat a stuffed pepper out of a margarine
tub when the lights went out. He’d brought three of them, which meant
he’d already decided I was having one, and he was following me down
aisle eight with a plastic fork while I checked dates on pasta sauce. I
told him I’d eaten before work. He said that hadn’t been his question.

He was fifty-eight, and we’d worked together for six years. He knew my
mother’s birthday because I’d once forgotten it, and every year
afterward he reminded me early, then took credit for whatever I bought
her. He was also why I kept spare socks in my locker. You only drop a
gallon of pickle juice on your feet once before you start listening to
the older guy.

Our delivery was two hours late, and the rest of the crew had been sent
elsewhere for inventory. We had seven pallets to finish before the
bakery staff arrived at four. Raymond’s daughter was coming over
afterward to help paint his kitchen. He showed me the color she’d
picked, explaining that he’d paid forty-three dollars a gallon for
something called Quiet Linen.

“Apparently the old linen was making too much noise,” he said. He held
the phone under my nose until I looked properly, then went back to
peeling the lid off his tub. “Come on. My kid made these. You’re hurting
her feelings by proxy.”

I took a bite, and he waited for the verdict like he’d cooked it
himself. When I said it was good, he explained how he’d told her to add
more garlic. I knew that meant she’d specifically told him to stop
adding garlic to everything.

I was thirty-two and had been telling people this job was temporary
since twenty-five. I didn’t hate it, though. No customers, no traffic
going home, and usually someone I liked to work with.

Raymond kept a felt-tip marker in his breast pocket for writing cases
up, and it leaked a little blue square onto every shirt he owned. I
offered him a plastic pen holder once. He said he wasn’t becoming the
sort of man who wore a pen holder. Apparently being the sort with blue
nipples was fine.

The store was a rectangle, with registers across the front and stockroom
across the back. Grocery aisles ran between them, produce and bakery on
the left, freezers and dairy on the right. Meat was in the back right
corner. Behind bakery, the employee corridor held our lockers, break
room, restrooms, and office, with an exit at its far end. Other exits
stood beside produce, beside dairy, and next to the loading dock.

I knew where the exits were. People keep asking why I didn’t use one, as
if I spent the night looking for a key. The doors worked. Getting to one
was the problem. Raymond left his lunch on the meat-room worktop and
fetched the pallet jack. A fan rattled over frozen vegetables. The ice
machine dropped something with a hollow clunk, and Raymond’s pallet jack
knocked once every wheel rotation as he pulled it toward dairy. I could
tell where he was without looking up.

My scanner beeped twice whenever it couldn’t read a damaged label. Above
the cases the fluorescent fittings hummed, and a dented shelf clicked
when I put another jar on it. You get used to all of that. Most nights
the only time I noticed the refrigeration was when a unit stopped and
the whole place suddenly seemed much bigger.

There was a bang behind meat, and half the ceiling lights went off. A
few came back, buzzing. The front registers stayed lit, and so did some
freezers, but most aisle ends disappeared.

“Back lighting again,” Raymond called. A roof leak had been dripping
into a fixture near meat, and management had supplied a bucket and a
work order. He said he’d call the night manager. I told him the bucket
had apparently failed its promotion to electrician.

He laughed. I remember that exactly, because it was the last completely
ordinary sound he made. His laugh came out through his nose first, like
he was trying to stop it.

I put my scanner down and took the flashlight off my key ring. My phone
was charging in my locker behind bakery. Raymond had his, but instead of
a call I heard a solid scrape and the rattle of milk doors.

“Eric,” he called. He sounded pissed off. “Come look at this.
Something’s behind the yogurt shipper.”

I thought he meant a rat. I asked if I should bring a broom, and he
said, “Bring an actual light. Mine’s doing something weird.” I started
toward the rear cross aisle. My little flashlight showed the floor and
shelf bottoms, but Raymond was around the corner beside dairy. I could
hear his phone tapping against metal.

A deep breath went in just beyond the corner. It snagged halfway,

couldn’t get enough air behind it. His other hand caught a dairy

His hand went out of mine. I lunged after it and got a fistful of

intake that never became an exhale.

I crawled forward with the light extended. A broad surface shifted
behind the fallen display, flattening its metal legs with little pops. A
yogurt cup burst under its weight. I scrambled backward, leaving the
torn cuff behind.

I called Raymond’s name, quieter each time. There was a dragging noise,
a bump, then something like a person trying to swallow too much food. I
heard teeth touch. The noise stopped, started again, and ended in a
small breath through the nose.

“He got a finger in,” something said. Its voice was low enough to have a
rough vibration underneath it, but the words were clear. “In the floor
joint. Had to take that out before he would come.”

I stood up and hit the shelf with my shoulder. A can fell behind me, and
the thing went quiet. When it spoke again, the voice was several feet
higher. “You’ve got both your hands,” it said. The first word stuck, and
it made a small sucking noise before finishing. “Put them down where I
can get them.”

I ran toward the registers, where I could see light and windows. My
flashlight jerked over jars. Behind the shelf on my right, something hit
hard enough to knock a row of cereal forward. There were two heavy
movements, a lighter scrape, then a pause. I thought it was falling
behind until the next impact came ahead of me. It covered too much
ground between sounds.

“You pulled,” it said through the cereal. I heard the sticky separation
of lips, followed by a little tap against teeth. “That’s why his
shoulder went. You could pull harder than that.”

I didn’t answer. The front cross aisle was thirty feet away, under a
working ceiling light. Beyond it I could see the entrance and the
shopping carts parked exactly where we’d left them. The next step didn’t
come. I slowed, listening over my shoes. Nothing struck the shelf. The
cereal stopped moving, and I reached for a can without knowing what I
meant to do with it.

The gravy packets hanging at the aisle mouth lifted outward. It was on
my left now, between me and the registers. I’d been listening behind the
shelving while it took the shorter route along the next aisle.

I stopped beside a stocking cart loaded with canned vegetables. I pushed
it ahead as hard as I could, then ducked behind its rear rail. Something
hit before it had rolled its own length. The front wheels lifted, cans
bursting across the floor. Something caught my shirt and tightened the
collar against my throat. I kept going. The fabric pulled me upright,
then tore from shoulder to waist.

Warm, damp air touched my neck. Something hard slid across my collar. I
dropped to my knees, wriggled out of the shirt, and went under the cart
rail as it came down crookedly behind me. The shelving had aligned
midway breaks left from a remodel. I turned through the one in aisle
seven toward bakery. The cart crashed behind me, followed by another
impact as the creature tried following directly before going around.

I crouched under the bakery wrapping station. One torn sleeve was still
around my elbow. I pulled it off, pressed it against my mouth, and
listened. Front lighting reached the counter edge but left me in
darkness. A tray of discounted rolls sat beside my feet. One yellow
sticker was stuck over a plastic knot, and I wanted to fix it. My brain
had run out of useful suggestions.

A cart rolled a few inches near the entrance. Its front caster
chattered, stopped, then chattered again. I couldn’t tell if the
creature had touched it or if the collision had left it rolling. I
stayed where I was until my thighs began to shake.

“That comes off,” the voice said from the front cross aisle. Something
sounded clogged behind the words, and there was a wet little cough. “I
want the part you can’t take off.”

It passed bakery without coming in. Two heavy movements, then the
lighter drag. I lifted my feet onto the shelf beside the discounted
bread and waited until the sounds moved toward produce.

My locker was through the door behind me. Beyond it were the office
phone and west exit. I could be outside in twenty seconds if the
corridor was clear. I eased out and pushed through the swinging door.
Its hinge squeaked. I’d heard it a thousand times without once reporting
it.

The combination wheel slipped twice under my fingers. When the locker
opened, I called emergency services with my phone still on its charger.
There was a message from my sister asking if I could look at her car
Saturday. I gave the dispatcher our address and said someone had
attacked my coworker. She asked where I was. I told her about the bakery
corridor and west exit, and she said officers were coming.

The emergency lamp lit the exit door but little of the passage.
Something was against its small window, blocking the glass from inside.
I jerked the charger out and backed into the break room. I didn’t close
the door completely. Through the narrow gap I could see the last few
feet of corridor, and I needed to know if it came toward me.

The dispatcher asked whether I could lock myself in. I told her about
the second door into bakery prep, then made her repeat what she’d said
about the police. Hearing her get slightly firmer helped. She was
dealing with a frightened man in a place with an address. For a few
minutes I could be that.

I pressed my torn sleeve beneath my chin. There were paper cups beside
the break-room sink, and I filled one without letting the tap run hard.
My first swallow hurt. The second went down, and I drank the rest while
the dispatcher asked about Raymond. I could give her his name and where
he’d been attacked. When she asked if he was breathing, I found myself
checking the corridor gap instead of answering.

The thing stayed near the exit. It wasn’t searching the rooms or trying
handles. Once I heard a wet little knock against the wall, followed by a
long pause and an effortful breath. “He’s stopped making spit,” it said.
“This gets worse when they stop.” It sounded occupied, and that was the
only reason I could finish the water.

An officer texted while I was still on the call. He was on his way and
wanted to know how to enter after closing. I described the front doors
and their inside release. I could almost hear Raymond correcting me:
left of the lottery cabinet, not right. I deleted RIGHT and typed LEFT.
The officer asked me to stay behind a closed door if I could, and I told
him the break room had no lock.

I thought about Raymond’s daughter. I knew her car, knew which shifts he
swapped when she needed him, but didn’t have her number. I opened his
contact as if someone else might be listed underneath it. Then I went
back to the officer’s messages. There were two people I could still
help.

I texted WEST EMPLOYEE EXIT, INSIDE, then listened. It shifted against
the wall. Something scraped once and lifted, and the exit window briefly
cleared before a dark surface covered it again. It was staying between
me and the door. I could wait until it decided to come down the
corridor, or use the other break-room door while I knew where it was.

I told the dispatcher I was moving toward dairy, put the call on silent,
and slipped through bakery prep. Raymond always said a break room with
two doors meant twice as many people interrupting you. That night it
meant I could leave without passing the thing at the exit. I took the
aligned breaks halfway along the grocery shelving, keeping whole aisles
between me and the front. There was no pursuit. I reached frozen food
listening for it to start, and somehow its willingness to let me go made
each intersection harder to cross.

At the freezer bank I stopped to get my bearings. Doors reflected
ceiling lights in broken strips, making it hard to separate what was
ahead from what was behind. The fans covered everything quieter than a
footstep. Behind a chest freezer full of turkeys, I checked my phone.
The same officer asked my location. I typed RIGHT SIDE FROZEN FOOD, then
COWORKER DEAD BACK DAIRY. I looked at that before sending it.

My hands were cramping. Blood from my chin dripped onto my bare chest,
and my shoulder burned. I could still feel Raymond squeezing my fingers.
I rubbed my hands together, trying to make it stop. I texted that it
wasn’t a person. Asked about weapons, I typed BIG ANIMAL MAYBE. I needed
them expecting something worse than an argument.

The dairy exit was sixty feet behind me, hidden beyond an ice cream
cabinet. Raymond was back there too. I told myself I could stay against
the cold cases and reach the door without passing where he’d died.

I waited first. My phone said 1:26. A relay clicked and something
settled in the turkey chest, and I started to turn before catching
myself.

the ice cream cabinet. The circulation fans were behind me, blowing the

turning before.

I kept swallowing, which made my chin hurt. There was no way to shift

in that position until one leg felt swollen and distant, listening to
the fans while the card slowly faced me again.

“He tried to get his knees under him,” the voice said. It was only a few
feet away, beyond the cabinet. “Even after. Kept those working longest.”

Something knocked twice against the casing. I pressed my forehead to the
cold plastic and covered my nose. Trying to breathe quietly made me need
more air. “You’ve gone cold on this side,” it said. There was a thick
sliding noise, followed by a swallow that seemed to take effort. “But
the little holes will warm up when I put something in.”

It fell silent. For a while I heard the irregular breath, then the dairy

I thought it had left. There’d been no footsteps, but I knew it could be
quiet. My thumb rested on the flashlight button until it went numb.

The tiny sound came from above my right shoulder. Something hard touched
the upper edge of the turkey chest, just behind my ear. It made a
delicate ticking noise against the plastic, and a drop of warm liquid

I threw myself sideways as the lid slammed where my head had been.

the freezer corner, knocking my breath out. I hooked my right hand

even through the freezer chill.

“Turn it,” it said, very close. The words came through a thick, wet

My box cutter was in my trouser pocket, a safety model that only stayed

Behind me came a violent exhalation. There was no roar. I heard a low
scraping cough, a hard little clatter on the floor, and then the voice

I ran toward the dairy exit, close enough to see the push-bar sticker.
Something hit the ice cream cabinet behind me, turning it across my path

I took the dairy service door instead, hitting it with my good shoulder.
Beyond it was the milk cooler. I dragged empty crates against the door,

the milk dollies. My arm had four bleeding holes, but my fingers moved.
I squeezed between two loaded carts toward the stockroom entrance.

The sales-floor door shook once, then stopped. I heard the weight move
away. It knew there was another way out, and I hadn’t even reached it
yet.

Only emergency lights worked in the stockroom. Receiving and its outside

texted BACK LOADING DOCK NOW. The officer said they were approaching the
front. Between me and receiving stood the pallet of beans and rice I’d
left raised on a manual jack.

I stopped beside it instead of running past. I’d tried outrunning the
thing twice, and both times it had cut across my route. The overstock
rack beside the pallet had a low beam with empty skids beneath it. I
could crawl through there. It couldn’t bring its whole weight through
without hitting steel, and the loaded pallet stood across the nearest
way around.

The jack’s release handle was on my side. I pulled the load back a foot,

My left arm didn’t want to push. I braced the handle against my hip,

part.

The cooler door scraped behind me. I stepped into the space beneath the
rack and said, “Here.” The word came out too softly. I had to say it

It approached without speaking. A broad section of darkness passed

The load settled with a thick snap. My shoe was dragged sideways, and

shuddered in their cases, but the pallet stayed down.

I’d caught it, and the knowledge made me hurry. I banged my bare foot
against the upright, retrieved my shoe, and shoved it on without tying
it. The exit was beyond the baler. I got up and started toward it.

Something struck the overstock from the other side. The loaded pallet
jerked sideways, and a stack of paper-towel cases folded across the
route I’d just checked. They were light enough to shift, but the next
impact came down on top of them. A dark surface drew tight beneath the
broken pallet, then wrenched clear. I backed away before it could finish
turning toward me.

It made a low, furious sound with no words in it. I expected it to come
straight after me. Instead it stopped beside the rack, where something
had fallen during the struggle. I heard a bump and the slow drag I’d
been using to follow its movements. Something was lifted. Only then did
the breathing start again.

“You shut that on me,” the voice said. It was level with the top cases
now, thick and bubbling. “Let me do your little one. Let me hear it go.”
I could still see the emergency sign above the personnel door, but it
stood between me and that sign. The trap had bought time and hurt it. It
hadn’t bought a way past.

I went toward meat while it was occupied, favoring the cut leg. It
didn’t follow immediately. Behind the bread trays I sat long enough to
pull my shoelaces tight with one hand. I wanted to believe it couldn’t
move after what I’d done. Then the familiar heavy rhythm started again,
with a new dragging scrape under one of the impacts.

My phone vibrated. The officer said they were at the front entrance, and
I typed MEAT BACK RIGHT. When he called, I answered quietly and told him
I’d injured it. Saying that aloud steadied me for a second. Then he
asked what I’d injured, and I couldn’t tell him.

Two sets of swinging doors led through meat prep onto the sales floor.
From there I could reach the front along the right wall, passing dairy.
I couldn’t make myself picture that part. Glass crashed at the front.
The officer said they’d broken the narrow panel beside the entrance to
reach its inside release. He said they were entering and asked me to
keep talking. I told him not to call out. His ordinary voice coming down
an aisle frightened me almost as much as the creature’s.

Inside meat prep I wrapped blue paper towel around my arm. The clock
said 1:39. Raymond had been alive less than half an hour earlier. His
margarine tub was on the worktop, its lid tilted. I pushed it down all
the way around because his daughter would want it back. I couldn’t move
until I’d done that.

He’d once stayed two hours past the end of his shift when my car
wouldn’t start, waiting for my brother to bring jumper cables. I’d told
him to go home about fifteen times. He’d sat on the curb with a coffee
and said he was already sitting down, what difference did it make where?
I couldn’t stop thinking about that while I looked at his lunch. I
didn’t want to be someone who left him in there, even though I knew
there wasn’t anyone left to bring out.

I pulled a clean plastic apron from the dispenser, cut strips with the
box cutter, and bound the folded towel against my arm. It took several
tries to hold tension while tying the knot. I put another towel over the
cut below my knee and used a second strip there. The officer waited on
the line while I did it. His patience made the room feel briefly like
somewhere first aid could happen, rather than another place I was going
to be caught.

I put the cutter away. One finger twitched each time my pulse reached
the bandage. I tried bending them all, then stopped before I pulled
anything loose. I still needed that hand.

The creature had stopped in the stockroom. Through the swinging doors I
heard a wet hitch, a slow outward breath, then something small pressed
against something hard repeatedly. It wasn’t coming for me. I stood
beside Raymond’s lunch, listening to it work.

“Stay open,” it said quietly. The voice had gone indistinct at the
edges. “You’re splitting when I do it. Stay open.” I looked at the gap
beneath the doors. There was no fresh shadow crossing it. Whatever it
was doing mattered enough to make it stop following a bleeding man.

The officer whispered that they were at the registers. He asked me to
come forward only if I had a clear route. I said I wanted to check dairy
from the cross aisle first. I didn’t tell him I still needed to see
where Raymond was. He told me to keep the call connected, and I put the
phone in my pocket with its microphone exposed.

I eased around a wheeled meat rack and looked through the sales-floor
doors. Nothing moved between the cold cases. The light over the far end
of aisle eleven was still on, and for once I didn’t hear anything coming
to cut me off. I stepped out with my key-ring flashlight held low,
knowing I was spending the few seconds it had given me on something I
couldn’t justify to the man on the phone.

In the rear cross aisle I saw Raymond’s right shoe beneath a dairy door.
Beside the yogurt display, a wet streak led toward the stockroom. There
was no body. He’d bought those shoes three weeks earlier. A loose rubber
heel flap kept catching, and he’d shown me by scraping it against tile.
I’d told him to return them. He said that meant finding another pair he
hated less.

The scrape came again behind me. It had been there before I dropped the
pallet, that lighter drag after every pair of heavy movements. The
rubber heel catching and releasing.

The prep doors opened. I pointed my flashlight back, and its beam caught
Raymond’s face above the wheeled rack. His eyes were open. His head hung
sideways, with a fold in his neck where a neck couldn’t fold without
killing someone.

His mouth moved. I took a step, and the light shook down to something
dark and hard protruding through a torn opening below his jaw. His lips
stretched away from his teeth as pressure moved inside his face.
“There,” the voice said out of Raymond’s mouth. A line of saliva dropped
from his lower lip onto the rack. “You’ve stopped.”

His head rose, turning toward me. His cheek dimple pulled deep as
something pressed from within. His chest didn’t rise. Breath came from
behind him, into the opening in his neck, and his dead lips shaped more
words. “That’s better,” it said. The tongue moved wrong, pressed upward
against his front teeth by something I could see beneath it. “Keep
looking at this.”

The wet clicks were his teeth. The sticky sounds were his lips drying
and being pulled apart. It hadn’t been swallowing. It had been working
his mouth, clearing it, changing its hold when the words wouldn’t come.

I’d heard Raymond’s mouth tell me about his death. I’d hidden listening
to something use his face a few feet away, waiting for it to finish
eating him. My flashlight shook against the doorframe.

The rest of him hung behind the rack, his knees not quite touching the
floor. That was why his shoe had kept scraping. It had carried him
through every aisle it searched, dragging his feet when it lowered him
to speak near the floor. When it had gone silent beside the turkey
chest, it hadn’t been standing where its voice had been. It had put his
head there, and reached around the other side for mine.

Something dark slid over his forehead. His mouth opened wider, a wet
pressure stretching his cheek until I could see where it ended. The
voice failed halfway through a sound. The creature jerked him backward.
My phone slipped out when I staggered against the dairy doors, the
officer still speaking from its tiny speaker.

I ran into aisle eleven. The rack hit the endcap behind me, scattering
detergent. Raymond’s heel skipped rather than dragged as it came after
me. I cut through the midpoint break toward aisle seven. Each turn hurt
below my knee. I ducked behind a half pallet of oil as something struck
the shelving and bowed the price rail beside my head.

I heard it stop near the front and struggle wetly to make a voice. I
stayed behind the oil, pressing the bandage on my knee, until a police
flashlight swept the cross aisle. I raised my good hand, then lowered
it. The scrape was gone. I couldn’t tell the officers where it was, and
waving them closer would mean asking them to cross a space I wouldn’t
cross myself.

Then the voice came from aisle six. It was higher now, clearer, almost
human, and the change in it made my stomach contract. “Help me,” it
said. There was a little catch between the words. “I’m down here. I
can’t get up.”

A flashlight swept the cross aisle. The officer said, “Keep talking.
Tell me where,” and I understood what it had learned from my phone. It
could make someone come to it.

I shouted before I’d worked out the sentence. “That’s him. That’s my
dead friend. Don’t go to the voice.”

The flashlight stopped. The oil display jerked against my back. I
crawled clear before bottles came down, got up with oil under my shoe,
and slammed into a shelf. I caught the rail and dragged myself toward
the front. The officer moved along the cross aisle toward me. I pointed
behind me and shouted for him to stay there.

Something heavy lost traction in the oil behind me. A shelf groaned as
it caught itself. I reached the front cross aisle, where the officer’s
light hit my chest and face.

He was beside register six, his partner near the open entrance. I saw
red and blue light on the windows. Then the creature came out of the
next aisle ahead of me, having abandoned the slippery route.

The officer backed behind the register and fired twice. The noise
slapped my ears. Something struck the checkout housing, and he fell out
of sight. His partner dragged him toward the entrance. I heard him shout
that he could move. The creature was between us, and I backed into the
grocery aisle before it turned toward me.

My knee folded. I hit the floor with my hands out, and something caught
my ankle. It drew me backward until I caught a shelf upright with my
good hand. I kicked with my free foot. It found something that gave,
then returned hard against my sole. Raymond’s mouth made a little
clacking effort behind me.

“Not the foot,” it said. “The other end. I need the other end still
soft.”

It lifted my hips by the waistband. My hand slipped to the shelf’s base
plate. Cold skin touched my ear, and two hard contacts settled on either
side of my jaw. I understood before it pulled. I clamped my teeth
together so hard one cracked. It was opening my mouth. The thing holding
Raymond had found a mouth that still closed by itself.

My cutter was back in my right pocket. I reached for it, caught the
slider, and drove the blade toward the pressure beneath my jaw. I cut my
own chin before striking something tougher. I sawed, unable to see. My
jaw was opening anyway. A noise came out of me I’d never known I could
make.

One side let go. I twisted my head, felt something tear against my ear,
and my ankle was released. I crawled through the midpoint gap as it
struck the upright behind me.

I lay in aisle eight, breathing through my nose because my mouth was
full of blood. I couldn’t run enough turns to lose it again. I needed
something that would hold its weight for a few seconds.

Bakery had a rolling security grille over the service opening, running
in steel guides fixed to masonry. We left it up overnight for cleaning.
A hanging chain worked it from behind the left counter. I didn’t expect
it to stop the thing, only catch part of it. Behind bakery I could reach
the employee exit. I needed to get over the counter before it caught me.

I could still lift my right knee. I tested it once against the shelf,
then tucked the cutter away so I could use that hand to climb. It wasn’t
much of a plan. I kept starting over in my head and finding the same
problem: the chain would be on the left, which meant I’d have to turn my
back on it to reach down. There wasn’t another way to that handle. I got
up before I could decide to stay on the floor.

I yelled that I was going left. An officer answered from the entrance,
and I told him to keep his light there. The creature didn’t go toward
his voice. It had learned that too. I pushed jars off the opposite shelf
and went through the midpoint passage. It ignored the breaking glass.
Its next impact came directly behind me.

At bakery I hauled myself onto the counter. My injured arm buckled,
leaving my legs dangling on the sales-floor side. Something struck
beneath them. I pulled my knees up as the wood front cracked inward. I
fell into prep and caught the chain. The grille rattled down, slower
than I needed. Before it was halfway down, something hit the service
opening and pushed the counter against my thighs.

I dropped flat, still pulling. The shutter stopped against something
reaching over the worktop. A dark, wet curve blocked the light above me.
It flexed, and a hard point ticked against the laminate by my cheek.
“Open,” the voice said. Raymond’s head lowered into view on the far side
of the counter, upside down now. His lip was split through to the gum.
“This one’s going. You have to open.”

I pulled again. The grille settled another link and stopped. Its guides
creaked, and the chain bit into my fingers. It kept its weight high,
away from the gap underneath. The pallet had taught it that much.
Instead of reaching along the floor it pushed farther over the counter,
trying to get me before I could crawl away. More of it came beneath the
grille. I braced a foot against the counter and hauled.

The shutter dropped several inches with a thick tearing sound. Breath
spattered my chest. Raymond’s head struck the counter and dropped out of
sight. Then something caught my wrist beneath him. My arm jerked
straight. The grille lifted as it braced, and the first guide shifted in
the wall. I couldn’t pull away.

The chain loop dangled beside my elbow. I shoved my free hand through it
and pulled it over the pressure on my wrist. The thing tightened instead
of letting go. I rolled under the prep table, drawing the chain taut
against its mechanism. Links pinched my trapped hand. I thought I’d have
to leave it there, then the grille settled another notch.

The grip shifted. I dragged my hand free, leaving skin on the chain, and
crawled toward the prep door. Bolts began popping out of masonry behind
me. I went into the employee corridor toward the west exit. The lamp
still lit the bar. My open locker stood beside me, charger trailing onto
the floor.

The shutter tore loose: a bolt hitting tile, the bottom rail slamming,
then the rush of something getting free. I was halfway down the passage.
I couldn’t make that leg go faster.

I hit the push bar and fell outside as the alarm started. I caught the
door edge and pulled, but it struck the inside before the latch caught.
The door jumped out of my hand. I scrambled backward across asphalt.
Something scraped the frame and forced the door wider. The narrow
corridor had kept it from coming through at full speed.

I reached the corner. A police light hit me from the front lot, and I
raised my good arm. Behind me something heavy moved through the doorway.
Then I heard Raymond’s voice. It wasn’t the deep one, or the
almost-human appeal from the aisle. It sounded enough like my friend
that I stopped crawling.

“Eric, give me a hand,” it said. There was a familiar little breath
through the nose. “Come on. Don’t leave me like this.”

A flashlight swung toward the doorway. I grabbed the officer’s trouser
leg, shaking my head, pulling him down. He looked at my face and backed
us both around the corner. The closer pulled the door partway shut,
stopping against something. Through the black gap came a wet clack,
another try at that laugh, and my name. The officer dragged me toward
the lot. This time I kept moving.

I spent six days in hospital. They repaired tendons in my arm and
stitched my chin, ear, and leg. Raymond’s body was recovered from the
employee corridor after daylight. I haven’t asked for pictures.

His daughter came with the margarine tub someone had put with his
things. She knew he’d have made me try her cooking. I told her it was
good, and we talked about kitchen paint because we couldn’t get through
anything else.

There’s one thing I couldn’t tell her. I told the police, and the
officer heard part himself. She deserved one memory of her dad I hadn’t
put that mouth into.

At first it had needed to shift his tongue and get his teeth out of the
way between words. By the end it could ask for help clearly enough to
draw an officer down an aisle. Outside, it managed Raymond’s laugh.

I keep thinking about the quiet stretches. I thought it was listening
for me, but I’d heard it murmuring beyond the meat-room doors, stopping
and starting over. I understand why it sometimes let me get farther
away. It needed time with him. It was learning to make a dead man sound
like someone you ought to save.
