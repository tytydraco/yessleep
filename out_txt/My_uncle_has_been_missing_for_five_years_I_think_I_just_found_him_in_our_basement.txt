The man in our basement is skeletal. His tank top is stained yellow and
bug-eaten, his arms outstretched and pinned to the wall behind him with
two loops of rusted chain. His eyes are also yellow, sunken deep into
their sockets, and his teeth are black, tightly gritted. His hair has
fallen out, clumps of it collected in the hollows of his shoulders.

And the bugs. Clouds of them. Some of them clotted on his face, crawling
in his nostrils. Maggots, gleaming white, wriggle around in the puddle
that’s soaked into his lap.

I don’t know what to do. I don’t know if I can even believe what I’m
seeing. But he’s here, barely breathing, his eyes big and glassy. And
even though he’s looking at me, I know he isn’t seeing me.

If there was a man in that body, he’s not here anymore.

He’s almost unrecognizable, almost inhuman. The only reason I think it’s
Uncle Ross is the star-shaped scar on his forehead. The one my dad gave
him before I was born.

I guess I should try to explain. I’m sixteen. Only child. I think I had
a pretty normal life up until a few minutes ago, maybe even a great one.
My dad owns a restaurant chain passed down from his father, and my mom’s
a doctor, which means I’ve always lived in a house bigger than my
friends’ and had a pantry that looked like a grocery store. I’ve cycled
through my share of friends- kids who’d come over and jump on the
trampoline or play video games but never really seemed to like me. I was
okay with this. We’d talk about cartoons and Sonic or whatever, and they
would fade away, replaced by another boy named Tanner or Brayden or
Connor or something.

Seeing Uncle Ross, my dad’s older brother, was my favorite part of my
childhood. My dad called him “a drifter” and kept a hand clamped tightly
around my shoulder when Uncle Ross blew into town, but no matter how
hard my dad tried to keep him out, Uncle Ross would always end up in our
guest room for a week or two.

Uncle Ross was taller and heavier than my dad, covered from the neck
down in tattoos. From the head up, he looked like a refined gentleman,
with little round glasses and a carefully brushed mustache. And the
scar. A pink raised star that made me think of that Dr. Seuss book, The
Sneetches, the one where the birds strutted around with pink stars on
their bellies and were real dicks about it.

Uncle Ross would take me to museums and the skatepark. He would tell me
about his adventures with the Peace Corps, then drive me hours to the
little galleries that displayed his art. My dad hated his art, said
anyone could make the same scratches and scribbles, and at first I
agreed. But the more I looked, the more I understood it. How many shades
of blue it took for him to paint a single ocean wave. I wanted to be an
artist just like my uncle.

One day, when I was ten, I asked him about the star on his forehead.
Uncle Ross’s eyes sparkled. “From your dad.”

“Dad?”

He stooped down, both hands squeezing my arms. “It was a silly argument.
I’m not mad. I never wanted the business anyway.” He traced the star
with his fingertips. “He broke a bottle. The points are where the glass
got me. I think it makes me look very distinguished.” He did a bad
British accent that made me laugh even though I didn’t know what the
word meant.

That night, I waited for Dad to come home. I can still remember it: his
neatly tailored suit, his cologne thick in the air. It always made me
sneeze. “Dad? Did you hit Uncle Ross on the head?”

My dad, who’d just sat down on the couch, shot straight up. “That’s not
true! I never put a finger on him!”

I didn’t understand why he reacted like that at the time. But as I got
older, I did. You don’t drive a bottle into someone’s head to prove a
point. You do it to kill them.

We moved a few months after that night, into a three-story mansion with
a pool and a game room. Uncle Ross took me out for hot dogs, and I never
saw him again. My dad shrugged this off. Reminded me that he was a
“drifter,” after all. At first, I thought about Uncle Ross all the time,
but then those thoughts got buried under stress about school and girls
and trying to find actual friends. I spent a lot of time in the game
room. My dad didn’t let me into the basement. He said it was “his space”
and that men have to “respect each other’s spaces.”

Sometimes I stood in front of the basement door and tried the handle.
Sometimes I would think I heard sounds, the faintest jangling or
scraping. I’d press my ear to the door and listen, but as much as I
strained, I could never be fully sure of what I’d heard. Eventually I’d
get bored and go back to playing pinball.

The basement has been locked for years. I thought I put two and two
together recently. Thought I understood why my dad had been so adamant
about me not being allowed into the basement.

It had to be a wine cellar.

It was easy to get a copy of the key. I slipped it off his key ring
while he slept and ran it to the key copier at the store across the
street. I thought I had it all planned out. I’d wait until he was
supposed to be out of town, and then I’d have my pick of all his
expensive wine. Surely he wouldn’t miss just one bottle.

The door led to a staircase. At the bottom of the stairs, there was
another door, this one much thicker than the first. My heart started to
beat faster because the air was too heavy. It smelled bad. Far worse
than I expected for a fancy wine cellar.

I tried the key, and I think part of me hoped it wouldn’t work.

But now I’m here. Uncle Ross, or whatever’s left of him, blinking up at
me.

I want to throw up, but I can’t. Even with the smell. Even with the
bugs. It just won’t come out. I’m rooted in place, trying to square what
I’m seeing with reality. This can’t be real. But I can see his chest
moving. I can see the star bobbing on his forehead.

A stair creaks behind me. I whip around, and there’s my dad in his gray
suit and shiny matching tie. He holds a handkerchief that smells
strongly of his cologne to his nose. His eyes are clear as he looks at
me.

“You would’ve found out eventually.” He lifts up his keys. “I didn’t try
that hard to keep it from you.”

My voice shakes. “Dad?”

His head swings over toward his brother. “I just couldn’t kill him. I
should’ve done it years ago, but I couldn’t do it. I still can’t.”

I want to say something, but I can’t. I just stand there staring at the
man who was my uncle. A fly lands on his eyelid and rubs its legs
together.

Dad smiles at me. “But maybe this is better.”
