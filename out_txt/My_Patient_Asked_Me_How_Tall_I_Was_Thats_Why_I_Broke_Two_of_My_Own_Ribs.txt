Content warning: abduction, violence, and self-inflicted injury.

The man who tied me to a chair is still out there, and we don’t know if
he’ll come to our house. My wife and our boys are staying at her
sister’s.

The chair was in a barn, next to a stone trough. The door was on the
other side of the room. I knew I couldn’t reach it.

I wasn’t trying to reach the door.

There’s a mark on my chest where a tube went in. It started with a
patient named Gabriele.

I’ve changed the names. The investigation is still open.

I’m a nurse on a locked psychiatric ward in southern Italy. Gabriele was
twenty-six and had advanced cystic fibrosis. Steroids for an allergic
reaction in his lungs had left him unable to sleep and convinced people
were poisoning him. Our doctors and his lung team agreed on a short
admission in a private room. Once the steroid dose came down, the
delusions passed. He stayed longer than he needed to. Pulmonology had no
isolation room free, and a patient with lungs like his couldn’t be put
in a shared one.

Every morning he spent about forty minutes inhaling medication and
clearing mucus from his lungs. At first I stayed because he needed
someone nearby. Later I stayed when I could, even after he was done.
Some mornings we talked. On others he was too tired, and I sat there
doing paperwork.

He was a fan of a soccer team I couldn’t stand. He also noticed that I’d
make coffee, leave it on the counter and remember it an hour later.

“You could skip a step,” he said once. “Make it cold to begin with.”

One morning my wife sent me a video of our younger boy learning to ride
a bike. He got going, looked back to see if she was watching and rode
straight into the grass. He stood up laughing, with dirt on both knees.

Gabriele asked me to play it again. The next morning he wanted to know
if my son had learned to brake.

“No. He’s learned to blame the grass.”

I’d mentioned that I used to play soccer. Gabriele decided I must have
been a goalkeeper and asked how tall I was. I told him. He said his team
would have put me in goal anyway, which started another argument about
his team.

His father came by after his shifts in intensive care. No one else came.
I mostly saw them together when Demetrio stopped me at the door to ask
whether Gabriele had eaten or finished his treatments. Sometimes he’d
ask while the empty meal tray was still beside the bed.

A few days later Ferruccio came back from his break smelling of
cigarettes. I told him I could smell him from Gabriele’s doorway. He
asked if I’d finally decided to try one. I said I’d never smoked.
Gabriele was in his room, turning a paper cup in his hands. I didn’t
think he was listening.

Another day I complained about the photo on my new ID card. Gabriele
asked if I’d registered as an organ donor when I renewed it. I said yes.
After a pause, he made a joke about the camera trying to improve my
face.

Around then, he began going quiet when his father came in. I thought he
didn’t want Demetrio to see how much time I spent in his room.

Gabriele was already waiting for two new lungs. When his breathing
worsened the following week, his lung team arranged a transfer to the
transplant center. By the time I reached his room, he couldn’t finish a
sentence. He kept pulling at his oxygen mask. When I got him to look at
me, he left it alone long enough for it to help.

“Just look at me,” I said. “You don’t have to talk.”

I stepped away to speak to the transport team. He caught my wrist.

Nunzia, our head nurse, was beside us.

“He only wants Matteo,” she said.

I finished the transfer notes, and Nunzia sent me with him. The doctor
assigned to the transfer had been pulled into another emergency, and no
one else was immediately available. Demetrio was an intensive care
anesthesiologist and had done critical-care transfers before. The
transport coordinator wasn’t happy about sending Gabriele’s father, but
after a call between the two hospitals, they cleared him to go with us
as the physician.

Before we left, Demetrio held the metal end of his stethoscope in his
palm to warm it. Gabriele’s eyes were shut. His father warmed it anyway.

On the road Gabriele pressed a hand against one side of his chest. His
oxygen saturation dropped. He tried to tell us something and couldn’t
get enough air to say it. The driver pulled over. Demetrio listened to
his chest and said he could barely hear air moving on one side.
Gabriele’s blood pressure was falling too.

Demetrio stopped with the stethoscope in his hand.

“Demetrio.”

He didn’t answer. His hands were shaking.

“Help him.”

He opened the emergency kit and relieved the pressure building around
Gabriele’s lung. I held the mask in place. We went to the nearest
emergency department, where they put in a chest tube. Once he was stable
enough to travel, we continued to the transplant center.

Demetrio hardly spoke for the rest of the trip.

At the center, other doctors took over. I bought a coffee from a machine
in the hall and left half of it on a ledge when someone called me back.
Gabriele’s breathing had worsened again. They were about to sedate him
and put a breathing tube in his throat.

He caught my sleeve.

“I’m sorry.”

I thought he meant the mask, or the trouble he’d given us in the
ambulance. I told him there was nothing to apologize for.

He watched me until the medication took effect.

Later a transplant doctor told us the center was requesting emergency
priority for him on the national transplant list. The request still had
to be reviewed. Even if it was approved, she couldn’t tell Demetrio
whether suitable lungs would become available in time.

Demetrio thanked me for staying with his son. A little later I saw him
on the phone at the far end of the hall.

I expected him to stay there. He said he needed to collect Gabriele’s
things from our hospital, where he’d left his car, and rode back with us
in the ambulance.

I sat beside the empty stretcher. Demetrio pointed at my seat belt.

“Put that on.”

I did.

“You have children?”

“Two.”

“Then you understand.”

I thought he meant leaving Gabriele at the center.

The road home was nearly empty. A van passed us, slowed down and stopped
across our lane. Our driver braked hard. While he radioed that the road
was blocked, a man got out of the van and came toward us, waving for
help.

The driver stepped down. I saw him fall, but I didn’t see what hit him.

Demetrio got out on the other side and went down beside him. By the time
I reached them, the driver was breathing but wouldn’t answer me.
Demetrio lay with his eyes shut and blood at his temple. His eyelid
tightened when I tried to lift it. I thought he was coming around.

There was no sign of the man from the van. The van sat across the lane
with its doors open. I climbed into the cab to use the radio and found
the handset ripped from its cord. I looked up and down the road. Nothing
moved.

Dispatch had our location from the driver’s call, but they didn’t know
he was hurt. My phone had no signal. I didn’t want to leave the two men
on the road, so I only went a little way uphill, looking back at them
every few steps. I checked the phone again. Still nothing.

A hand landed on my shoulder.

I started to turn. Something hit the back of my head.

When I woke, my wrists and ankles were tied to a chair in an old farm
building. A strap crossed my chest. I could hear an engine idling
outside. I pulled at one of the ties until I tore the skin underneath
it.

There was a stone trough close to the chair. The door was farther off.

A man outside was arguing with someone. I couldn’t make out everything
he said, but he’d heard that the ambulance had been found and wanted to
leave.

The door opened.

A man came in with his face covered and a medical bag in his hand. There
was dried blood at his temple. He took out a stethoscope and held its
metal end in his palm.

“Demetrio?”

He took the covering off. Then he listened to my chest. I held my
breath. He waited until I couldn’t hold it anymore and listened again.

“Is the driver alive?”

He raised one finger to silence me and kept listening. Then he took the
stethoscope out of his ears.

“That’s why I told you to put the belt on.”

I thought about Gabriele asking my height. About Ferruccio’s cigarettes.
About my ID card.

“You looked up my blood type.”

“Yes.”

“That’s all you know? You don’t know if my lungs would be right for
him.”

“No.”

He clipped an oxygen monitor to my finger. Red light showed through my
nail. He watched the reading for a few seconds.

“You want me to get to a hospital with a brain injury,” I said. “While
my lungs still work.”

He didn’t answer.

We’d been on our way home. Whoever found me would take me to our
hospital, and an injury like that would go straight to his unit. His
colleagues would keep my heart going for the transplant team. He’d
trained some of them himself.

That was why he’d let me see his face.

“You can’t make the transplant team give them to Gabriele.”

“I know.”

“They might give them to someone else.”

He gathered the monitor’s cord into his hand.

“Then I’ll have killed you for nothing.”

The man outside called for him. Demetrio told him to wait.

I said Gabriele’s name. Demetrio kept looking at the monitor.

I tried my wrists again. The ties held.

Gabriele needed both lungs. If I damaged one of mine badly enough,
Demetrio might have no reason to go on with his plan. I might also die
before anyone found me.

My son still hadn’t learned to brake.

I looked at the trough.

I threw my weight sideways. The chair went over with me, and my ribs
struck the stone edge.

At first I couldn’t tell how badly I’d hurt myself. Then I tried to
breathe and couldn’t get a full breath in.

Demetrio was on the floor beside me. He cut the strap across my chest
and listened with his stethoscope. He hadn’t warmed it this time.

“Call them,” I said.

He checked the monitor again.

“Call them.”

I was afraid he’d leave me there now that my lungs were damaged. He
stayed where he was, listening to my chest while I struggled to breathe.
I couldn’t tell whether he was trying to help me or assess what was
left.

The man outside shouted, “I’m leaving.”

Demetrio went to the doorway and held up his phone until he got a
signal. He put the call on speaker and told emergency services where to
find me.

“Is he conscious?” the dispatcher asked.

“Yes.”

He cut the ties around my wrists and put the phone beside my hand, the
call still open. Outside, the van’s engine revved.

“Why me?” I asked.

Demetrio stopped at the door.

“I didn’t choose you.”

He looked back at me.

“He only ever wanted you.”

The van pulled away. I stayed on the line and asked about the ambulance
driver.

The driver survived.

I remember the noise of the rotor and someone counting my breaths. The
helicopter took me to our hospital, and I ended up in Demetrio’s unit
after all. One of the doctors who looked after me had trained under him.
She couldn’t meet my eyes.

Three of my ribs were broken, two of them against the trough. I also had
a collapsed lung, a concussion and a drain in my chest for several days.

Each morning a physiotherapist had me cough into a pillow pressed
against my ribs. The first time, it took me forty minutes. Gabriele had
spent forty minutes like that every morning since he was a child.
Ferruccio came on his day off and stayed until I was done, and a while
after that.

Demetrio and the man with the van are still missing.

An employee at our hospital later admitted he’d let Demetrio see a blood
bank record from a surgery I’d had years earlier. It showed my blood
type and nothing more. Demetrio had looked at it before Gabriele’s
breathing got worse, while Gabriele and I were still talking about my
son’s bicycle.

Gabriele was alive. The emergency request had been approved, but there
were still no lungs for him. Once he was awake enough to write, he asked
for me. Ferruccio drove me to the transplant center after my chest tube
was removed.

Gabriele still had a breathing tube in his mouth and a drain at his
side. There was a small whiteboard beside his hand. The only chair was
against the wall. No one had pulled it up to the bed. I tried, but it
hurt too much. Ferruccio moved it for me.

I’d had questions ready on the drive. Before I could get the first one
out, Gabriele wrote on the board and turned it toward me.

Does it hurt?

I didn’t answer.

“Did your father tell you to ask how tall I was?”

Gabriele nodded.

“Did he tell you why?”

He wiped the board and wrote:

I didn’t ask him.

“What about the cigarettes?”

The marker squeaked on the board.

I told him what you said.

“And the donor question?”

He told me to ask.

I looked at him. He held the marker but didn’t write anything else.

“When did you realize something was wrong?”

He waited a long time before putting the marker to the board.

Before the ambulance.

He wiped it clean, then wrote again.

I told him you had children. I thought he would stop.

Demetrio had known the answer when he asked me on the way back.

I thought of Gabriele catching my sleeve before they sedated him. I’d
told him he had nothing to apologize for.

“You could have warned me.”

Gabriele nodded.

“Why didn’t you?”

He looked down at the board. Ferruccio was standing near the window. I
could hear someone moving equipment in the hall while Gabriele wrote.

I didn’t want you to stop coming.

He set the marker down.

I looked at my wrists. I thought about the driver on the road, and my
wife taking our boys out of the house because we didn’t know who
Demetrio might come after next.

“You should have told me,” I said.

Gabriele nodded again. He didn’t try to pick up the marker.

“I could have died.”

He shut his eyes. A tear ran down toward his ear.

I put my hand over his. My sleeve slipped back, and he saw the marks
from the ties. He tried to pull away. I held on.

“Look at me.”

He did. I was still angry with him. I couldn’t say anything for a few
seconds.

“I forgive you.”

He tried to say something around the tube, but no sound came out.

“You don’t have to talk,” I said.

I kept holding Gabriele’s hand until his fingers warmed against mine. By
the window, Ferruccio wiped his cheek with his thumb.

After a while Gabriele pointed at my phone. I knew which video he
wanted.

My son started pedaling. Halfway across the frame, he looked back to see
if someone was watching and rode into the grass. Gabriele watched him
get up laughing.

He reached for the marker.

Did he learn to brake?

“Not yet. He still blames the grass.”

His shoulders shook, and the ventilator beeped at him. I laughed too,
and I had to press my free hand to my ribs.

Then he looked at me. He was still holding my hand, and his eyes dropped
to my chest as I breathed.

“A little,” I said.

I waited for the machine to lift his chest.
