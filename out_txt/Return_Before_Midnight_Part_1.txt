Everywhere you turn, there’s at least one person who can’t leave the
’90s behind.

“The ’90s were the last good decade,” they’ve repeated since 2000. “Back
when you actually owned what you bought.”

And many more things you’ve probably heard thousands of times.

Though I wish my memory could get that rinse of rose tinted nostalgia,
my brain won’t let me forget what really shaped those times. Especially
for me.

Satanic panic. Religious hysteria. Occult paranoia.

Regardless of what the label of the day was, the sentiment was always
the same. Secular music was the devil. If you played D&D, you were
participating in the occult. The warnings were repeated so often that
eventually, even our parents weren’t sure what they were saying anymore.

Thirty four years later, I can still hear the high pitched yelp my
mother accidentally let loose on my tenth birthday.

My Uncle Troy was always the classic counterculture guy. Whatever was
loved, he hated. Whatever was hated, he loved.

And by God, he loved Velvet Crucifix.

I felt the familiar rectangular shape beneath the wrapping paper.
Another cassette. I assumed it was another tape from the same Christian
rock band my parents listened to and, as far as I knew, nobody else did.

To say I tore through the leftover snowmen paper from last Christmas
would be a lie. I slowly peeled it away, making sure to keep the smile
going for the camera. One strip at a time, the light blue paper gave way
to the clear plastic of the cassette case.

Something bright red stared back at me through it.

I stopped peeling.

Then I tore faster.

The J-card sat perfectly pressed against the inside of the case. Across
it was a bright red ram’s head, its horns curling around an upside-down
cross. Beneath it, in scribbled lettering, was the song title:

Mourning star

Mom saw the look on my face.

“What’d you get?” she asked in that typical voice parents use when they
already know exactly what you got, but still want to hear you say it.

I turned the cassette around to show her.

That’s when I heard the yelp.

The sound itself isn’t what stuck with me. It was everything that came
with it.

The quivering lip. The pinpoint pupils. The slight trembling in her
hands.

Then came the hysterical rambling, words tumbling over one another so
quickly that I couldn’t make sense of any of them. I’d never seen anyone
react like that before.

Especially Mom.

Needless to say, I never saw that cassette again. Or any cassette after
it. No Walkman. No cassettes. No CDs. Nothing.

For the next eight years, the house was a war zone fought in absolute
silence. My parents built higher walls, terrified that a slip in
vigilance would invite the devil right back through the front door,
while I learned to weaponize my own resentment.

Uncle Troy drifted in and out like bad weather, never staying long
enough to start a war of his own, but always leaving behind the
distinct, electric scent of transgression. He didn’t need to say
anything directly to me. Just his existence was proof that another world
existed outside our stifling, paranoid kitchen.

It made everything worse. I believed they had stolen my childhood,
stunted me socially, and used the devil and evil as an excuse to
suppress me. For years, I thought they had taken something from me that
I could never get back.

Now, strangely, I miss the ignorance of feeling forsaken.

By the time I turned eighteen, the suffocating atmosphere had done its
work. I joined the army. Not due to a sense of duty for my country, or
even to do good. I just wanted a ticket out, a socially sanctioned way
to throw myself into the fire.

Yet with 9/11 and the invasion of Iraq, I survived. A 23 year old
veteran, and still forsaken.
