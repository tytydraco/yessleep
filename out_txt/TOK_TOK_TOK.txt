Some sounds are frightening because of what they might mean. Others are frightening because of where they come from.

I grew up in a comfortable subdivision where most of the families were
well-off. The houses were spacious, the properties were generously
sized, and the neighbors weren’t particularly close to one another. Many
of them were established professionals—doctors, lawyers, and families
who had lived comfortably for years.

Our house and lot had come from my father’s parents. Despite the size of
the neighborhood, there was still plenty of greenery around us. There
was even a vacant lot behind our house, covered in trees and tall grass.

During the day, it was just another empty piece of land. At night,
however, it became something else.

I was never particularly fond of the sounds coming from that lot. The
crickets, frogs, geckos, and occasional rustling of leaves and tall
grass made the darkness feel strangely occupied.

I’ve always been sensitive to sounds, even as a child. I tend to notice
little noises that other people might easily overlook. I could
distinguish the familiar sounds around our house, which was probably why
I became so aware whenever something sounded different.

I knew those nighttime noises were ordinary, but that never made me feel
any better. Perhaps that sensitivity was also why I could recognize the
faint knocking that night, even though it wasn’t particularly loud.

There was also a story adults used to tell children to keep them from
wandering outside or opening the door to strangers.

They called it the Toktok Gang.

The name supposedly came from the sound they made when knocking on
someone’s door.

Tok. Tok. Tok.

There were different versions of the story. In some, they were simply
pranksters who knocked on doors before disappearing into the darkness.
In others, they were criminals who went around checking whether anyone
was home before breaking in and stealing valuables. The more frightening
versions involved violent crimes, even murder.

I never knew which parts, if any, were true. Looking back, I suspect
some adults and yayas had exaggerated the stories to keep the children
they cared for inside the house and away from strangers.

Whatever the truth was, the story worked.

I grew up believing that three knocks in the middle of the night were
something you should never answer.

Our family also had several dogs. They were alert and would bark
whenever someone approached the gate. In a way, they were our alarm
system. If someone came near the house, we would know.

Which is why I still remember that particular night.

It was moonless. I woke up sometime after two in the morning and glanced
at the clock.

2:17 a.m.

Something felt different.

The usual nighttime sounds were gone. No crickets. No frogs. No rustling
from the vacant lot behind our house. Even the dogs were quiet.

There was no barking, no movement, and no familiar sound coming from
outside.

Then I heard it.

Tok. Tok. Tok.

The sound wasn’t particularly loud or forceful. It was faint, but
distinct and deliberate, as if someone was making sure we could hear.

My first thought was the Toktok Gang.

I was still a child, and my instinct was to get up and open the door. I
remember starting to move when my father suddenly whispered:

“Shhh! Huwag mong buksan.”

He said it quietly, but firmly enough to make me stop.

I stayed where I was.

It was just the three of us in that room: my father, my pregnant mother,
and me. None of us got out of bed. My father didn’t investigate, and my
mother remained beside us.

For a moment, everything went silent again. I waited, listening, perhaps
expecting someone to speak or walk away.

Then came another set.

Tok. Tok. Tok.

Six knocks in total.

I pulled closer to my parents. I remember waiting for the dogs to bark.

But they never did.

There were no footsteps, no voices, and no indication that anyone was
outside.

Eventually, I fell asleep.

The following morning, nothing appeared to be wrong. Nothing was
missing, and there were no obvious signs of an attempted break-in. We
had no explanation for what had happened.

Over time, I heard that other families in the neighborhood had
experienced similar incidents. Some said they heard knocking at their
doors in the middle of the night. Others couldn’t even determine where
the sound had come from.

Years later, I brought up the incident with my mother. She is deeply
religious, and during our conversation, she mentioned something she had
heard about three knocks being associated with the Holy Trinity. Some
people, she explained, interpreted such knocking as a mockery or
imitation of something sacred.

I don’t know whether she believed that was what happened to us or
whether she was simply sharing a belief she had heard over the years.

I have never been able to settle on an explanation myself.

Maybe it was someone playing a prank. Maybe it was a stranger testing
the house. Maybe there was a perfectly ordinary explanation that we
simply never discovered.

I still remember my father’s warning that night. I remember the silence
that followed, the dogs that never barked, and those six faint knocks
that none of us dared answer.

It would have been less frightening if the knocks had come from our
gate, or even our main door. At least then, I could have imagined
someone standing outside. A stranger. A prankster. Maybe even the Toktok
Gang from those stories adults used to tell us.

But the knocks hadn’t come from either place.

They had come from our bedroom door.
