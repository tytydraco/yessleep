[Part 1]

“Chicken pad kra pao with a fried egg, extra spicy, no MSG, please,
Auntie.” I was ordering from the owner of a tiny made-to-order
restaurant.

“Yeah, yeah, I know. But how’s it supposed to taste good without MSG?
Just a little, come on.” She shot back at me in a clipped voice.

“Whatever you think is best, Auntie,” I said.

Okay. I’d finally made it back to Thailand. I figured the first thing I
had to do was find the food I’d missed the most: real, authentic Thai
made-to-order food. I hadn’t had any in over a year.

It didn’t take long. The plate landed in front of me: rice topped with
chicken pad kra pao and a runny fried egg, the smell of fresh holy basil
hitting my nose like a punch. I didn’t waste a second. I grabbed my fork
and spoon and stabbed the yolk so it ran all over the rice, because,
well, that’s the only way to eat a fried egg. Then I took a bite.
Savory, balanced, and burning hot. This was the flavor that Thais and
foreigners alike fell in love with.

But even while I was eating, I couldn’t stop thinking about what had
happened on the plane. Do you think I dreamed it? What the hell was
going on? Everything felt shuffled around. Someone in the comments told
me it was called “Quantum immortality” which I’d never heard of. When I
looked it up, it said that if you die in a quantum dimension, your
consciousness carries you into a universe where you don’t. But how could
that be possible? Don’t scientists say that’s pure superstition?

“How much, Auntie?” I asked.

“Sixty.”

It felt like the price had gone up. Wasn’t it usually forty or fifty
baht? But I didn’t argue. I paid and walked to the airport entrance,
where a long line of taxis was parked. I decided to take a taxi, because
there weren’t any motorcycle taxis at the airport. Weird as hell.

The first taxi in line was a green-and-white Toyota with a dent in the
back door and a jasmine garland swinging from the rearview mirror. The
driver was leaning against the hood, smoking. When he saw my suitcase,
he flicked the cigarette away like I’d just won the lottery.

“Where to?”

I told him the district. He grimaced.

“Meter’s broken. Four hundred baht flat.”

You have to understand something. In California, I once paid sixty
dollars for a ten-minute Lyft ride and almost cried inside. Four hundred
baht was only about twelve dollars. After a year in America, that price
didn’t sound like getting ripped off. It sounded like a discount coupon.

“Sure,” I said.

He loaded my suitcase into the trunk, and I climbed into the back seat.
The seats were covered in glossy blue vinyl that squeaked every time I
moved. The car smelled like jasmine, menthol, and an air freshener that
had died years ago. A small amulet hung next to the steering wheel. The
radio played softly, a DJ cheerfully arguing with a caller about whether
traffic was worse than last week.

“Studying abroad?” the driver asked as he pulled out.

“How did you know?”

“You’re staring at everything like you’ve never seen it before.” He
laughed. “My daughter wants to go to America too. She’s only in sixth
grade, but she already has opinions about it.”

“What kind of opinions?”

“She says the snacks are better over there.”

“She’s not wrong,” I said. “They’ve got forty kinds of potato chips, but
not a single decent bowl of rice.”

He liked that answer. He slapped the steering wheel and started telling
me about his daughter. I let him, because it was kind of nice to hear
Thai that wasn’t coming from inside my own head.

Here’s the thing about seat belts in the back of taxis in my country:
technically, they exist. But they’re usually shoved deep into the crack
of the seat, under snack crumbs and maybe even a coin from the nineties,
and nobody has ever pulled one out willingly. But after what happened on
the plane, I wasn’t in a joking mood. I dug around for the buckle and
clicked it in.

“Oh, safety first!” the driver said when he caught it in the mirror.
“Good job, kid.”

We got on the expressway. It was late afternoon, the sky a dusty orange.
The driver kept going on about his daughter. “…so I told her, study
hard, but don’t forget where you come from. I told her every day, until
she said, ‘Dad, you say that every day…’”

His phone buzzed in the cup holder. He glanced down.

Shit.

I don’t know what made me look up. Maybe it was the sound: a horn, long
and furious, from the right. I turned, and there was a massive
ten-wheeler truck filling the whole window, drifting into our lane like
we didn’t exist. I could see the fender. I could see a faded sticker on
its side that read A mother’s love never runs out. I could see the
driver in the cab, mouth hanging open, one hand raised like he was
trying to wave us out of the way.

Then everything slowed down.

I’d read about this. Before death, the brain stretches time and pulls up
the good memories. I even told you about it at the very beginning,
remember? But what I never knew was that it would feel this calm. The
horn stretched out into a low drone, like a whale song. I thought about
my mom. I thought about a bowl of creamy tom yum goong Mama noodles. I
thought, what an idiotic way to die. I’d survived the plane. Actually
survived it. And now I was going to die in a car with a jasmine garland
hanging from the mirror?

Then the sound cut out.

Not just the horn. Everything. The engine, the radio, the tires grinding
on the road. The same silence as on the plane. Quiet like someone had
hit mute on the whole world.

I closed my eyes.

Why does it only happen to me?

“…but don’t forget where home is.”

I opened my eyes.

What the fuck.

We were still on the expressway, same lane. No truck. The sky was the
same dusty orange. The seat belt was still across my chest, and I was
gripping it with both hands so hard my knuckles were pale as uncooked
rice.

“Sorry, what?” I asked. My voice came out an octave higher than normal.

“I said don’t forget where home is. That’s what I tell my daughter.”

It was almost the same sentence he’d left hanging. Almost. But the voice
was wrong. Deeper, a little rougher. Like the same song played in a
different key.

I looked up at the rearview mirror.

The driver in the mirror had a long face, thin glasses, and no mustache.
The guy who’d flicked away his cigarette had a round face and a beard
thick enough to see from space. They wore the same green polo and tilted
their heads the same way when watching the road, but they were not the
same man.

I’d like to officially announce that my brain was about to explode.

“You okay, kid?”

“Did we… almost hit a truck?”

“A truck?” He frowned at the mirror. “No way, not on this road. They
don’t allow trucks at this time of day. I think you dozed off. You were
mumbling something.”

“I wasn’t asleep.”

“Sure, whatever makes you feel better,” he said, in the tone of someone
who didn’t want to argue with an exhausted teenager.

I slowly scanned the car, like I was afraid it would notice me looking.
The jasmine garland was gone. A plastic bobblehead dog hung there
instead. The amulet was still there, but bigger, hanging from a gold
chain. And the seats… were red.

Red. Not blue. Definitely red.

Blue to red. I’d seen this movie before, and it ended with my underwear.

“Um…” I said. “How much is the fare so far?”

“The meter’s running right there,” he said, pointing to the dashboard,
where orange digits read 312. “Another twenty minutes or so, depending
on traffic.”

Wasn’t the meter broken? I distinctly remembered it being broken.

On the radio, the DJ had stopped talking about traffic. Now he was
reading the news in a bright morning-show voice. He said the name of the
prime minister, and it was a name I had never heard before. I’d been
abroad for a year and a half, so maybe I’d just missed the news… maybe.

I stopped caring about everything else. All I wanted was to get out of
that car as fast as I could.

Whatever was happening to me wasn’t normal. I figured I should probably
go see a psychiatrist.

The driver turned onto my street at 5:52 p.m. The meter read 312 baht,
exactly as he’d said. I handed him a five-hundred-baht note and told him
to keep the change. I guess I should go say hi to my family first. I’ll
come back and keep writing.

See you.
