Most childhood memories fade with time. But some linger, sitting in the
back of one’s mind until something brings them rushing back to the
surface like a hollow ball released from underwater.

One such memory recently came back to me. It originates from when I was
seven years old. Back then, my mother volunteered to water the plants at
my elementary school every Saturday morning. She usually brought me with
her.

An empty school on a weekend is a strange place. Without any kids or
teachers, the dark hallways always felt enormous and eerie. The overhead
lights were left off, leaving the long corridors that stretched along
each of its three floors in shadow. For much of the time, the only
consistent sound was the wet slosh of mom’s large green watering can as
she worked her way from the classrooms up to the new glass solarium on
the third floor.

There was one rule everyone at school knew about, although nobody ever
seemed able to explain why it mattered so much: never be alone in a
classroom or hallway for more than a few moments.

The teachers said it was so nobody would get lost. That never made much
sense to us. The school wasn’t particularly difficult to get lost in,
and there were adults everywhere during the week. Still, everyone
followed the rule. Even the teachers did. I remember seeing
Mrs. Campbell stand outside her classroom waiting for her students to
arrive rather than entering alone.

Naturally, this led to rumors.

Some kids said there was something in the school that hurt children who
were alone. Nobody could agree on what it looked like or what happened
if it caught you. Most of the stories were probably made up. At seven, I
wasn’t particularly worried about them. Whatever “hurt” meant, I assumed
it couldn’t be anything too serious if the adults were still willing to
let us come to school every day.

During those Saturday watering sessions, Mom usually did all the work.
Sometimes, I’d help out a little, but the plastic can was heavy and
awkward for me to carry. So, I was usually left to my own devices,
though I always stuck with her. While she watered, I’d generally have my
nose buried in a Redwall book, and then I’d trail a few paces behind her
as she moved from one room to another.

If I strayed from her or let her get too far ahead of me, her voice
would echo down the hall, “Bailey, come back, stay where I can see you.”

I always listened and stayed close to her.

Until that rainy Saturday.

~

Mom offered to let me stay home that morning. She’d never done that
before. She didn’t believe the rumors, and I imagine she wanted to give
me a chance to avoid getting soaked in the storm. Plus, my stepdad was
down in the basement tearing out drywall for a remodel, so, while he
wouldn’t be watching me, I wouldn’t be alone.

I jumped at the chance, eager to watch TV while curled up on the couch
instead of returning to school on a weekend.

I watched as mom zipped up her oversized yellow rubber raincoat – the
classic kind with a corduroy collar and heavy brass snap buttons – and
stepped out to her station wagon.

As I sat there, a strange wave of unease hit me. Something quiet told me
that I’d rather be with her than without her. Acting on impulse, I
grabbed my own raincoat, sprinted into the rain, and tapped on the car
window. She unlocked the door, and I scrambled inside.

I rarely picked up on adult emotions back then, but the way her eyes lit
up stuck with me. Even at seven, I could tell how much my choice meant
to her.

The drive over felt safe despite the storm. With mom behind the wheel,
the thunder outside was just background noise.

We pulled into the school’s empty parking lot and used the side entrance
near the cafeteria. Mom used a key from the heavy brass keyring the
principal let her borrow to unlock the heavy metal door. She held it
open for me as I rushed into the cold air inside.

Once we were in the building, mom stepped into the custodial closet at
the end of the first-story hall to fill one of the school’s large green
watering cans at the mop sink. “Stay right next to me,” she called out
over the sound of the running faucet.

“I am,” I said, leaning against the doorframe and looking down to shake
the rain off my jacket.

While I was doing that, I remembered something.

My apple.

My homeroom had a behavior chart posted near the teacher’s desk. Each
student had an apple with their name on it, and whenever somebody
misbehaved, the teacher moved their apple farther up the chart. If it
got high enough, you got a note sent home or a call to your parents.

Mine was much farther up than everyone else’s, a fraction of an inch
from a phone call home. Usually, the teacher reset the chart at the end
of the day on Friday. But the day before, she’d forgotten. I knew
exactly where my apple was, and I knew Mom was going to see it when we
got to that classroom.

If I snuck away, I could move it myself.

I glanced toward the custodial closet. Mom had only just started filling
the watering can. It usually took a minute – I had just enough time to
dart up the stairs, make the change, and come back. If she noticed, I
might get scolded for straying a little, but I could make up an excuse.

I took one step into the hallway. Instantly, however, I spotted mom, out
of the corner of my eye, standing in the doorway, holding a watering
can.

She beckoned for me to follow her. The rush of emotions that followed,
both frustration that my attempt to sneak out had so quickly been
thwarted and relief that she seemingly hadn’t realized I’d been trying
to sneak away, buried the sense of ‘strangeness’ I felt at seeing her
still wearing the yellow coat – with the hood up over her face, no less.
It was odd, too, that she’d moved so quickly.

I nodded and fell into step behind her. We soon reached the first stop,
the planters outside the main cafeteria. Mom lowered the watering can
while I leaned against the cinderblock wall and read a few lines of my
book. Next, we visited the cast iron plants inside the front office
before heading to the staircase.

Mom paused at the large potted ficus sitting in the dark alcove near the
main stairwell before heading upward. The heavy brass keys on her belt
clanged with every step. It was the exact same metal chime I had
followed a dozen times before. I pushed myself off the wall and followed
her up the stairs, exactly like I always did.

We reached the second-floor landing, where the long hallway stretched
out into shadow. My mom kept moving toward the classrooms, her boots
squeaking softly on the polished linoleum.

We passed the music room. Through the small square window in the door,
the upright piano looked enormous and dark in the corner, totally silent
without Ms. Jenson there to play it.

During the week, that room was full of noise, but my favorite part was
when Ms. Jenson had all twenty-five of us hide behind the piano and the
risers to ‘trick’ our homeroom teacher into thinking we’d all gone home
early. Back then, sitting in the dark, holding our breath, and trying
not to giggle felt eerie and thrilling. But looking through that glass
now, with the room completely empty, the quiet felt heavy and cold.

I kept my head down, flipping to the next chapter of my book as I
walked. The brass keys just kept clanging against her hip, a steady
rhythm guiding me as she turned down the corridor toward the library. I
adjusted my grip and kept pace a few steps behind her.

Mom pushed through the library door and headed for the tall potted fern
tucked beside the checkout desk.

In the dark, the room felt huge. Looking at the reading carpet, I could
almost hear Mr. Hinkle’s booming, ridiculous voices during story hour,
followed by the chorus of giggles echoing off the tall shelves. But,
today, the empty beanbags and shadowy aisles had a lonely, desolate aura
to them.

My mom reached into the fern’s fronds, her yellow coat rustling in the
dark. She kept the watering can tipped over the soil for a long, quiet
stretch – far longer than usual. At first, I figured the plant was just
really dry, but as the seconds dragged on, a small prickle of unease set
in.

“Mom?” I asked, stepping closer. “Isn’t that enough?”

She didn’t turn around, but her voice came from beneath the high hood,
low and coarse, roughened by something in her throat.

“Almost done, sweetie. Stay close.”

She lingered there, frozen over the plant, before abruptly snapping back
to her full height.

At this point, I started to worry. Her voice sounded strained, like her
throat was raw. I wondered if she was coming down with something, or if
sprinting through the cold rain earlier had already made her sick. Her
movements, too, seemed ‘off’. I wasn’t sure what to make of it.

She didn’t say anything else, just turned and walked back out into the
hallway toward the next row of classrooms. I followed, opening my book
and trying to focus on the paragraph I’d been reading, but the words
wouldn’t stick. Every time I looked up from the page, my eyes went
straight back to the yellow coat swaying ahead of me.

As we moved down the hall, the uneasy feeling in my stomach only grew. I
was hit by a realization so terrifying that my homeroom, and the apple
chart I’d been panicking about, completely faded into the background as
we walked right past. Namely, I hadn’t actually seen her face since we
left the custodial closet. Every time she reached for a planter or
turned a corner, her body was angled just enough away that the deep
yellow hood blocked my view, casting her face in complete shadow.

I hesitated, unsure of what to do. I knew that I didn’t want to go up to
the third floor. But where else was I supposed to go? Back down to the
dark cafeteria by myself? Outside into the freezing rain? I took a small
step backward, my eyes locked on the empty landing above, waiting for
her boots to stop moving.

“Bailey.” Her raspy voice echoed down the concrete well. I looked up.
She had stopped on the landing above, her back turned to me, her deep
hood casting a shadow that completely hid her face. She held the heavy
door open, waiting. “Come on,” she called.

“I…um…can I just sit in the library and read?” I asked.

“Please, honey,” she implored, “Stay with me. Stay where I can see you.”

The familiarity of her words soothed me, or at least some small part of
me. That was what Mom always said when I wandered too far away. I took a
deep breath and began climbing the steps.

The third floor was even quieter and darker than the rest of the school.
Just outside the staircase sat the closed metal doors of the building’s
old elevator, its floor indicator resting silently above the frame. The
air here felt cooler and carried a faint damp smell from the glass
solarium at the far end of the hall. The figure moved ahead of me down
the corridor, her boots making a soft, sticky sound against the tile.

We moved into the first classroom, a fourth-grade room. Mom headed for a
tall pothos sitting on top of a filing cabinet in the corner. I stayed
back by the door and flipped open my book.

That was when the silence of the room really hit me.

Down on the first and second floors, every time she lifted the watering
can, I’d hear the heavy slosh of water shifting inside the plastic. I’d
hear the quick, wet patter as it hit the soil. But here, as she tilted
the green spout over the leaves, there was nothing. No sloshing. No
dripping. Just the scrape of rubber sleeve against plastic.

The can had gone empty, and she’d never refilled it. Yet, she leaned it
over the plant for ten long seconds, as if watering it, before pulling
it back.

She turned and brushed past me into the next room, her head tilted
slightly as if listening for something. I felt a wave of unease as I
followed her. She stopped beside a small, shriveled African violet
sitting on the teacher’s desk.

I stopped a few paces inside the doorway. For a moment, I reassured
myself that I must be wrong. I must have imagined her doing things that
made no sense. Surely, Mom wasn’t pouring imaginary water from an empty
can. I had to be mistaken, right?

Then, I watched, holding my breath, as she tilted the green spout over
the leaves, holding it there for several long seconds. But, again, no
water came out. There was no sloshing inside the plastic can, no wet
trickling into the dirt, no sound at all. Just an empty container over a
dying plant.

She pulled the spout back and turned toward the doorway. I watched her
for a moment, trying to see her face beneath the hood, but the darkness
swallowed it completely.

“Um, Mom, are you okay?” I asked.

She didn’t answer. She simply turned and walked back into the hallway.

I hesitated again, looking back toward the staircase. The thought of
going back down alone made my stomach tighten. I remembered the rule
everyone at school knew, and suddenly the old rumors about something
that hurt children who wandered away didn’t seem quite as ridiculous as
they had before.

Ahead of me, the yellow coat stopped. “Bailey,” it said with a
commanding intonation. I hurried to catch up.

Her figure waited at the end of the hallway, just outside the last
classroom. I slowed as I approached, keeping several paces between us.
Beyond her, the corridor ended at the double doors leading into the
solarium. She pushed open the door to the art room and stepped inside. I
followed reluctantly, my eyes moving between her and the dark hallway
behind me.

The room was empty. The high countertops and rows of tall wooden stools
looked like strange, crouching shapes in the dim light. I imagined the
forms of my classmates sitting on those stools, their eyes trained on me
in judgment as Mr. Jones gave me a stern lecture.

I’d gotten in trouble in this classroom recently. I’d drawn my dad,
translucent and in hospital garb, hovering above my mom and I as we ate
at the dinner table. “It’s supposed to be a realistic image of your
family,” Mr. Jones had chided. “Not something weird that you made up.”

“It’s not imaginary,” I’d pouted. “He visits us as a ghost.”

“Your dad’s alive,” he’d retorted. “Right?”

“Yes,” I conceded. It would remain true for nearly a year. Soon, he’d
start showing signs of being sick, but hadn’t done so yet. As far as I
could tell at the time, he was perfectly healthy. “So, how could your
dad be a ghost if he’s alive?”

I didn’t have an answer to that. Nor did I have an answer for the
counselor I was required to visit for the next few weeks.

Mom walked toward a hanging spider plant near the far window, her
movement stiff and robotic. As she lifted the empty watering can to it,
I watched the green spout hover over the leaves. Nothing came out. There
was no sloshing inside the plastic, no dripping onto the soil, no sound
at all. She held it there for several seconds before lowering it again.
I stayed near the doorway, increasingly unsure of what I was supposed to
do. Every instinct told me that something was wrong, but I still
couldn’t make myself leave her.

Then I heard something in the hallway.

I turned toward the open doorway. At first, I saw nothing except the
dark corridor stretching toward the stairs. Then lightning flashed
through the windows, and for the briefest moment I saw something
standing near the far end of the hall. It was barely more than an
outline, a tall, narrow shape against the darkness, with the vague
suggestion of a head, shoulders, and arms. I couldn’t make out a face. I
couldn’t even tell if it was facing me. I stared at it, holding my
breath, until the darkness swallowed it again.

“Mom?” I whispered.

She turned toward me. Her face remained hidden beneath the deep yellow
hood, but she lifted one hand and beckoned me farther into the room.

“Stay close.”

The familiarity of those words calmed me, just as they had downstairs. I
stepped away from the doorway and moved toward her, keeping my eyes on
the hallway until the last possible moment. When I finally looked back,
there was nothing there.

I didn’t see the shape again. Not then, anyway.

The rest of the watering continued much the same way. Mom moved from
classroom to classroom, lifting the empty can to each plant and holding
it there for several seconds before moving on. I followed behind her, my
book still open in my hands, though I hadn’t read a single word since we
reached the third floor. Every now and then, I would glance back toward
the hallway, expecting to see that strange outline standing there again.

It never did. Eventually, the fear began to recede, replaced by a
different kind of confusion. Maybe I really had imagined it. Maybe the
lightning had made a coat rack or some other object look like a person.
The school was full of strange shapes in the dark, and I was seven years
old. I wanted to believe that explanation.

Mom continued toward the solarium. I followed at a distance, still
trying to make sense of everything I had seen. I just knew that I wanted
to go home.

She pushed open the double doors and stepped into the solarium. The rain
was louder here, drumming against the glass walls and the sloped ceiling
overhead. The room was full of plants, arranged in long rows beneath the
windows. Mom walked slowly between them, stopping at each one to lift
the empty watering can. Again and again, she tilted it over the soil and
waited, motionless, before moving on.

I stood near the doorway and watched her. Something about it bothered me
more than it had in the classrooms. Maybe it was because there were so
many plants here, and because she seemed to be spending longer and
longer at each one. She would lift the can, hold it there, and then
stand completely still as though she were waiting for something to
happen. There was no water. There wasn’t even the sound of her
pretending anymore. Just silence.

I looked up when lightning flashed outside. For a moment, the entire
solarium filled with pale white light. The overhead skylight caught the
flash and threw it across the glass, illuminating everything in the
room.

For the first time since the figure emerged from the janitor’s closet, I
saw its face. It was swollen and water-softened, the gray skin sagging
loose around her jawline and mottled with deep bruises. The water had
eroded the soft tissue around her cheeks, leaving raw, darkened patches.
Her eyes were sunken and milky, fixed in an unblinking stare. As her
blackened mouth opened, heavy water spilled over her torn bottom lip.

I stared at her, completely frozen. I didn’t understand what I was
looking at. I only knew that this wasn’t my mother, and that I wanted to
get as far away from it as possible. I ran.

I pushed through the solarium doors and sprinted down the hallway, my
shoes slipping against the tile as I headed for the stairs. I didn’t
know where I was going beyond wanting to get away from the thing that
had been impersonating my mother. The stairwell was the only place I
could think of, and I remembered that the old elevator was just outside
it. Maybe I could get downstairs and find a way out.

Just as I approached the staircase landing, something emerged from the
darkness below.

This was not the figure in the yellow coat. It was taller and thinner,
with unnaturally long arms hanging almost to its knees. Its movements
were jerky and uneven, as though its limbs weren’t quite connected
properly. I couldn’t see its face, only a pale, indistinct shape where
one should have been.

I froze. The thing stopped on the landing below me.

For one terrible moment, neither of us moved. As I struggled to process
what was happening, it lunged toward me.

A strike across my chest sent me sprawling backward against the floor.
Before I could get up, the creature was on me. One of its hands closed
around my arm, and another pressed against my chest.

I screamed and kicked, but it didn’t seem to notice. Instead, it began
dragging me back toward the stairs, back from where it came.

Then that gravelly voice came from down the hallway. “Don’t you harm
Bailey!” The creature froze. The hand around my arm loosened, and its
head turned toward the voice. I saw the yellow coat standing beneath the
doorway to the solarium.

I didn’t understand what was happening. I only knew that its attention
had shifted away from me, and that the pressure against my chest was
gone. The yellow coat stepped backward toward the art room.

“Bailey won’t be alone for long,” she said. “But I will be.”

The elevator ‘beeped.’ The creature turned to the elevator, then to her,
and watched as she disappeared into the art room. The creature burst
after her, leaving me behind.

I scrambled backward, too frightened to move any farther, and then the
elevator doors opened beside me.

“Bailey!” It was mom. My real mom.

She stepped out and dropped to her knees beside me. She grabbed my arm
and pulled me against her. “Dear lord, Bailey, what’s happening? And
where have you been?”

I couldn’t answer. I just clung to her as she dragged me into the
elevator. As the doors began to close, I looked back toward the art
room. The hallway was empty. The yellow coat and the creature were gone.

The next thing I remember clearly was the elevator stopping on the first
floor. Mom pulled me through the doors and kept one hand around mine as
we hurried down the dark hallway. I could barely keep up with her. My
chest hurt where the creature had struck me, and my arm was throbbing
where its fingers had closed around it. We ran out the side entrance
into the freezing rain and scrambled into the station wagon. Mom locked
the doors before starting the engine, then drove several blocks to a gas
station and pulled beneath the bright fluorescent lights.

Only then did she stop and look at me. There were already dark marks
forming around my arm, four long bruises where the creature had grabbed
me.

Mom stared at the marks for several seconds. “Oh my god, who hurt you?”
she whispered. “What happened?”

I tried to explain. I told her about the thing on the stairs. I told her
about the yellow coat. I told her about the face I had seen in the
solarium and the creature that had grabbed me.

She listened without interrupting. Then she reached across the seat and
pulled me against her.

“I didn’t leave you,” she said. “I would never leave you.” I remember
believing her.

The police arrived not long afterward. They searched the school from top
to bottom, but they found no sign of an intruder, no damaged doors, and
nothing that could explain the bruises on my arm. The solarium was
empty. The art room was empty. The stairwell was empty. They only found
my book lying on the third floor hallway.

They took our statements separately, and that was when we discovered
something strange. My mother said that she had been in the custodial
closet filling the watering can when she heard me call her name. She had
stepped into the hallway and found it empty. She called for me several
times, and when I didn’t answer, she became frightened. She went outside
to see if I’d returned to the car and, when she didn’t find me, came
back in. She was searching the nearby area when she heard my cries
emanating from above. She hopped onto the elevator immediately, and she
was now convinced that an intruder, maybe a child predator, had snuck
inside and led me away from her.

For the rest of that school year, a police cruiser was parked outside
the cafeteria entrance every Saturday morning while Mom watered the
plants. At school, there was a silent agreement to obey the rule – no
entering classrooms or hallways alone – even more stringently than
before, and nothing happened again.

Eventually, the bruises on my arm and chest slowly faded. But the
memories remained, especially the horrible face beneath that yellow hood
and the monster in the school.

My mom and I stopped talking about the school as I got older. Life moved
on. My mom and stepdad divorced a few years later. Once again, it was
just Mom and me.

She retired a few years ago. After that, she bought a small secondhand
motorboat and spent her weekends cruising the quiet inlets near home.
She kept that old yellow raincoat all those years, and I still saw her
wearing it whenever the weather turned cold. She invited me out with her
half a dozen times, but between work and family, I always found an
excuse not to go.

Looking back, I wish I hadn’t.

The first sign that something was wrong was the silence. My mom checked
in with me constantly, so when three days passed without a call or text,
an uneasy knot formed in my stomach. I started calling her friends and
neighbors, but nobody had seen her since Friday morning, when one of
them had spotted her heading down to the dock, bundled up against the
chilly morning fog in that same old yellow raincoat.

I called the local authorities and a search was organized. It took
nearly two weeks before they found the boat, jammed deep into a secluded
patch of reeds in the marsh.

The investigation pieced together what had happened. While attempting to
clear a tangled line from the propeller, Mom had slipped and fallen
overboard. The engine was cut, but the impact against the hull had
knocked her unconscious. The current carried her away from the boat and
deep into the marsh. Her life jacket had kept her afloat. The head
injury had not.

By the time they found her, she had been in the water for nearly two
weeks. The official cause of death was drowning following an
incapacitating injury.

When I went to the viewing room, the medical examiner warned me that the
water had severely altered her appearance. He told me I didn’t need to
see her.

I insisted. When he pulled back the sheet, he stepped aside and watched
my face carefully. To his surprise, I didn’t scream or turn away.
Standing there, looking down at my mother’s ruined face beneath the
sheet, I finally began to grasp what had happened that Saturday morning.

The figure in the yellow coat hadn’t been trying to lead me somewhere.
She had been keeping me close to ensure I wasn’t alone.

Something had happened to me once. The moment I slipped away from the
custodial closet to move my apple, the spirit that haunted my school had
found me. I was meant to wander off that morning, just as I had intended
to do. But Mom had come back to stop that from happening.

Maybe she didn’t fully understand what she was doing. Maybe she only
remembered one thing: that I couldn’t be allowed to wander away from her
again. And, when the monster came for me again, she knew exactly what to
do.

I knew this, because I’d seen the decayed face that lay before me
before. It was the same face I’d glimpsed through the flash of
lightning, the same face I had followed through the school all those
years ago.
