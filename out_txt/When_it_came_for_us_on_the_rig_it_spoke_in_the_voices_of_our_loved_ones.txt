I have alerted the proper authorities. They will find us in the morning.

What I write here is not for them.

This is for Mitch. For his family. For anyone unlucky enough to get
caught alone out here at sea. The wind speaks to you, you know. Reminds
you of all that you’ve forgotten. Fills your ears and head until the
secrets leak out your eyes. People think that the ocean is an escape. A
place to dissolve one’s reality in the waves. But the truth is out here,
you can’t hide.

Because, can you really hide from yourself?

I’m employed by a company I won’t name here. Four other men and myself
tend to an out of date oil rig in the Black Sea, preparing the structure
for use between shift changes. I’ve  worked there for the last twenty
years. In my younger days I figured college was a suckers game, so I got
directly into the dirty business of making money.

Before I knew it, I had more cash in the bank than I knew what to do
with. But I had already traded my life to get it.

I’ll skip describing the next fifteen years worth of backbreaking labor,
marriage troubles, divorce, and seeing people die. It’s horrible, but
you get used to it in a fucked up kind of way. You have to, or else your
brain would shatter.

What happened here started and ended on the second day of our last shift
together. About a week ago. We were still settling in, getting used to
the routine and tasks we were assigned to complete. One of them was
moving the rig to a new oil reservoir five miles east. Mitch and I were
bunk mates. When it happened, we were off-duty. He was looking at some
nudie mag with all the shame of a highschool pervert. I never was really
into that sort of thing, even though Mitch had often showed me articles
he found “interesting.” Instead, I was busy doing the crossword.

“Five across, a sound of warning.” I looked up at Mitch.

“Alarm?” Mitch flipped out the centerfold and stared at it with a
lackluster expression. The man had a thing for words, mundane and weird.
Using him was the equivalent of asking google. This was an old ritual of
ours, one I appreciated on the lonely nights out here.

But as I was about to try the word, everything shut off.

It was dark, entirely and completely. There were no windows in the bunk,
the only source of light being the fluorescent bulb on the ceiling. The
hum of the platform ceased, and in that quiet, I was reminded just how
loud our lives actually were. I had forgotten. Once our ears adjusted,
the room was filled with only the trembled sound of breathing, and the
ceaseless churn of the waves below.

In the dark, we fumbled for the exit. I grabbed ahold of something
fleshy. “That you, Mitch?”
“Get off me you sonofabitch.” His tone stayed light, but I could tell he
was anxious. You get to know the little things about people at sea.
Especially when you’ve faced death together.
We moved toward the door, and found the handle. We opened it into even
more darkness. The emergency lights were shut off as well. There was no
whine from the speakers indicating a system failure. It was all strange,
and put a pit in my stomach.

“Explosion?” Mitch’s voice came from behind me. Disembodied like a
phantom.

“If it was, it was quiet as hell.”

There was a small glint down the hall. I grabbed at Mitch until I
connected with his shoulder and pulled him forward. We reached the door,
pushed it open, then stepped onto the outside railing.

It was nearing sunset. Clouds covered the sky entire, except for a thin
line of sunlight on the horizon. Soon, the dark shapes overtook it, and
the world became a resolute gray, growing darker by degrees. The first
raindrops struck the deck like ricocheted ordinance. We squinted in the
dim light as we assessed the damage to the platform.

There was none that we could see. No smoke, no orange glow, no shouting
and panicked voices. Just the wind and the waves bashing up against us.

“Clint.”

I turned to look at Mitch. “Yeah?”

He was fixed on something in the distance, his eyes squinting.

“Mitch.”

He turned to me. “What?”

“You just said my name.”

“No I didn’t.” Mitch’s eyebrows scrunched down. “Did I?”

“Knock it off.” I might not have shown it, but I appreciated his effort
at a joke. His humor was one of the many reasons I liked him as my bunk
mate. And then…something was wrong inside me. Like there was fire and
water churning into steam and bursting out my skin. I wanted to run, to
hide. 

Like there was something looking for me, and I shouldn’t let it see me.

I swallowed it down. There was work to be done, and that was all that
mattered. I went across the scaffolding for the upper deck ladder.
“Let’s get to the control tower. See what knocked out the power.”

By the time we arrived, Cregger, our engineer, and Captain Donahue were
already assessing the situation. By that, I mean Cregger was smashing a
fist into a console screen while the captain watched with a hand on
lips, his eyes empty and mind elsewhere.

Donahue saw us arrive and nodded, pinching his smooth chin. He was the
only one on the platform that kept up with shaving while at sea. “Backup
generators are shot.”

“Goddamn it.”

Cregger finished his temper tantrum by kicking the console he was
punching. “I told them. I told those fucking corporate ass puppets that
we needed a backup for the backup half a year ago. And now it’s us
eating the shit. Of-fucking-course.”

“How did it go down?” Mitch took one of the consoles and started
flicking buttons, tapping the screen.

“We were hoping you’d know.” Donahue grabbed a binder off the wall and
thumbed through it. “Cregger and I were going through a system check and
everything just switched off.

“Same here.” Mitch bent down and opened a panel underneath the console,
exposing the wires like a gush of small blood vessels. He was our
electrician, but even this gesture felt like him grasping at straws. No
short in a console was enough to take down an entire platform.

“Maybe when Frank and Eric get here they’ll know what happened.”

They didn’t show up for almost an hour. In that time, the sky went
completely black. We were alone out in the ocean, with no lights except
a few oxygen candles. All our flashlights were shorted out, and the chem
lights stored up here wouldn’t glow. There were no stars. Our platform
was darker than the deepest cavern. The rain grew into a solid wall of
water that drenched every inch of the man-made structure. None of the
radios worked.

Each minute Erik and Frank didn’t show made the feeling of unease in the
room spread like a cancer. Discussion turned from how to communicate
with the mainland, to what was keeping our shift mates from joining us.

We were in the middle of discussing who would go looking for the two
missing men when there was a slamming noise on the door. Through the
window, the silhouette of one person carrying another flashed
specter-like in the guttering flame. Mitch opened the door, and Erik
stumbled in, drenched, dragging Frank. “Get over here!” Erik slammed
Frank onto the ground and pushed his weight up against the door. For a
moment, we all stood shocked. Even Cregger’s mouth hung open, mid curse.
The same question was on everyone’s minds: why was Erik handling Frank
so roughly?

Then, we didn’t have to ask.

Frank raised up from the ground, dried blood on his left temple, a gash
on his balding forehead. Despite his injury, he leaped up towards Erik
and threw his fists at his nose. It took me, Cregger, and the captain to
keep him down. Frank thrashed, and moaned great sighs of pain.
“Christie…Christie wait…Oh, God, Christie…”

Donahue’s eyes went wide, like he recognized the name. But then it
passed, and he was back to his impassive self. “How long has he been
like this?” He reached for the medical bag slung on Erik’s shoulder.

Erik let the strap go free. “Since the lights went out. We were down by
the bilge, and he just started going crazy. Almost threw me overboard.”

“How’d he get that?” Cregger pointed to the gash.

“Me. Hit him in the head when he wouldn’t listen.” Erik examined his
forearms, each dripping small lines of dark blood. His forearms were
scrawled with fingernail scratches. “Jesus fuck.”

The captain found a syringe, tapped it a few times, then jammed the
needle in Frank’s neck. He pressed the plunger. The man’s eyes were
crossed with mania and his mouth wet with spittle, but his muscles
relaxed as the drug took effect.

We tied him up and put him in the corner. Mitch watched Frank while the
rest of us discussed what to do. We let him know about the radios, but
Erik knew of an extra in the lifeboat, near the bottom of the platform.
If we could get to them, then maybe we could call for a repair team, or
even an evac. Cregger and the captain went to the back closet to get the
spare batteries. Erik had brought a flashlight with him they wanted to
test. In the meantime, the injured man wrapped his bleeding forearms in
gauze, cursing each time the white round slipped between his fingers.

“Clint.”

I turned to Mitch. “Yeah?”

He was looking at Frank. My bunk-mate had a strange expression on his
face, his eyebrows scrunched up like he was angry, or confused. A pulse
of sympathy went through me. Mitch was the kind of man that got lost in
his own anxiety. Sometimes, I wanted to turn off his brain, give him a
rest.

“Mitch?”

He turned to me. “What?”

“You said my name.”

“No. Did I? Sorry. Can’t remember…” he focused on Frank again, his eyes
searching the already bloody bandage on top of his head.

Behind me, Cregger made all sorts of foul oaths towards god, the sea,
and a wide variety of mothers. None of the batteries were working. A
small pile grew to the side as he slid the cells into the flashlight,
clicked the switch, then pulled them out and threw them down when the
light didn’t come on. “Goddamnit…goddamnit…goddamnit!”

“We’ll go old fashioned.” Donahue took another oxygen candle, and an
acetylene torch from the emergency kit. He grabbed another candle, and
held it out to me.

Something about that little metal square spooked me. The thought of
being in the pitch dark of the platform with nothing but a strobing fire
put chills through my neck. Erik must have had the same feeling because
he shook his head. “No way. I’m not going.”

“Get a grip.” Donahue slipped the oxygen candle in his pocket.

“No. I’m not going back.”

Cregger took a step away from Erik and gave him a side eye. He took a
candle. In the corner, Frank stirred.

“There’s nothing–”

“There’s something down there! I’m not going back near it!” Erik backed
away from the box, his back pressed to the wall. “Frank swore he was
hearing things. Kept saying that…ah hell. I’m not going.” He winced and
rubbed a hand against an earlobe.

Donahue took a moment to observe Erik, then glanced at Frank, Mitch,
Cregger, then myself. He wasn’t a man easily ruffled. If there was
something that needed to be done, he would do it. That was his way. But
when we made eye contact, I saw hesitancy. Erik wasn’t a superstitious
person. He was straightforward, and if we’re honest, boring. The man had
less imagination than a rock.

But even he was certain that something was wandering the dark hallways
below.
“How about you keep an eye on Frank?” The captain reached out to put a
hand on Erik’s shoulder, but then thought better of it. He leaned in
close to Cregger and spoke in a whisper I could only just make out.
“Stay and keep an eye on them.”

He took the box of candles and offered them again to me. I took one, and
tucked it into my raincoat. Donahue tapped Mitch on the back with the
box, and he jumped. He calmed himself, then without really looking or
comprehending, he took his share.

Armored up with candles and a singular crowbar, the three of us left
Cregger, Frank, and Erik and stepped out into the rain.

It was like walking into the ocean itself. The three of us were doused
instantly, and it was hard to breathe with all the water spilling into
our noses and mouths. Shuffling with our arms interlocked, Donahue’s
freehand gripped the railing. Step by step, inch by inch, the corrugated
metal path led down to a slippery ladder, then to the door where Mitch
and I had entered the deck. The last vestiges of flickering candlelight
petered out from the command tower as we descended. The only things that
felt real then were our hands, and the cold kiss of rain.

In the dark, Donahue’s hand hit up against the metal door until he found
the latch. There was a grating and twisting sound above the storm, and I
was pulled into a quieter dark. The door closed.

There was fumbling, and the wet slipping of polyester. Finally, a click,
and a pale blue flame appeared. Mitch and I gathered at it with our
candles, getting the slightly wet wicks to fizzle, then light. It was
not enough. Our three candles made a shifting pool of reddish light that
only extended about three feet in front and behind, our shadows dark and
shifting cutouts on the walls. I had lived on this rig for what amounted
to a quarter of my life. But even with my bunk only a few feet from me,
everything felt unfamiliar. I could just as well been in the bowels of
some unknown tomb. The pipes and wires gave off the illusion of the
vessels and bones of some great beast’s interior.

Donahue found the emergency evacuation sign. An arrow pointed in the
direction we should go. “Stay close. Can’t waste a candle if one of us
gets lost.”

We turned left down the hall.

Each footstep echoed more the deeper we went down the twisted tunnels.
Our feet moved quickly. The candles only burned for about fifteen
minutes at a time. No one spoke after descending to the second level.
Perhaps we were too busy listening for things. The sounds of other
footsteps. Other breathing. But still, I kept an inner mantra. We are
alone down here. We are alone down here. We are alone.

It did little to calm me.

Nobody did anything to acknowledged the silence or the dark hallways
other than to point out the signs that directed us to the lifeboat. But
once we descended down to the third level, Mitch couldn’t take it
anymore. “Who’s Christie?”

I flinched at the words. It was like being slapped. The small portion of
fear I had tuned out returned now tenfold. Donahue blinked, which was a
tremendous show of shock for the captain. “What?”

“The girl Frank kept talking about.”

Donahue narrowed his eyebrows, as if considering his words. He opened
and closed his mouth once, and then twice. Then finally, he gathered the
necessary language. “His wife.”

“Shit.” Mitch was quiet, and we all strained our ears. Only the plunk of
leaking rainwater filled the space. “Must love her.”

“She’s dead.”

I glanced at Donahue. The man’s face was stoic, but I could tell the
fact bothered him. It bothered me too. The way Frank was crying out…it
was like he had seen her in the flesh.
But that couldn’t be.

Mitch saw the sign first, and pointed to it. A small icon of a lifeboat
along with an arrow. It was stored on the fourth floor, just down the
next set of stairs. We could get the radio and get out of this mess. I
relaxed, my shoulders loosened.

“Clint.”

I turned. “Yeah, Mitch?”

“What?”

“You said my name again.”

“No, I didn’t.”

I had just about had it with the joke. “Mitch, quit fucking around.”

“I wasn’t.”

“Then why’re you saying my name?”

“I didn’t!”

I leaned forward to punch Mitch, but Donahue stepped between us. “He
didn’t, Clint. He didn’t say your name.”

We were all quiet for another moment, realizing what this meant. The
blood left my face. Instinctively we huddled, turning our backs to the
others, staring out into the dark. In the light of our near-dead oxygen
candles, shadows moved like sea beasts in old film, jerky and unnatural,
watching with sightless eyes.

Donahue went stiff. “Claire?”

“Captain?”

There was no hesitancy. He sprinted into the dark, dropping his candle
with a flare on the ground. As he disappeared into the shadow, he cried
out again. “Claire!”
He turned a corner and was gone.

“Aw shit.” Mitch hesitated, looking at me. Then he took off after the
captain.

“Hey!” My feet were rooted to the metal grate. I waited one moment for
them to return. Then two. I waited too long.

They were gone, and I was left alone in the dark with my single candle.

As I stared after my shipmates, the light in my hand dimmed. The candle
was almost gone. What could I do? It was an impossible choice. Either I
go after Mitch and Donahue, or just get the radio myself. The captain
had the extra candles. If I wasn’t careful, I’d run out before I got up
top. They’d never find me then. 

We needed that radio.

“Donahue?”

No response.

“Mitch?”

Nothing. Just darkness, and a loud creak as the frenzied storm outside
bashed against the ship.

I put a hand around the flame of my candle, and continued down the
stairs and toward the lifeboat. In only a few moments,  I arrived at the
door. Opening it let in a roar that blew my hair back, and extinguished
my candle in a rush of water. I tasted salt, and smelled dead fish.
The corrugated metal platform surrounding the orange boat was rough on
my fingertips as I crawled to the boat. Oblivion grazed against my palms
as they searched for the craft rocking to and fro in the screaming wind.
Somehow I found the door, and managed to get inside. It went quiet
again, but the plastic reverberations of rain filled my head. Some more
searching revealed a canvas pack, where I found the radio, as well as
some unbroken chem lights. I snapped them against my leg, praying to a
deity I hadn’t believed in for years. The inside of the craft was filled
with a soft orange light. A sob of relief escaped me. At least these
still worked.

In the glow, I tried to turn on the radio. Nothing. Not so much as a
burst of static.
I told myself all sorts of lies. That Cregger could get the radio
running again. That all I needed to do was get it to him. I wrapped the
box tight in my coat. With the chem light between my teeth, I opened the
lifeboat door and stepped out into the rain, back through the door, and
into the interior platform.

The silence constricted my throat. I wasn’t sure if I knew how to
breathe anymore. Holding the glowstick aloft, I tried again to call out.
“Captain?”

Nothing.

“Mitch?”

Only drips.

I was faced with the same impossible decision. Go to the surface, or
search for my crewmates. But I had the chem light. And that gave me
false courage. I took a deep breath and began to wander the darkened
halls.

“Donahue? Mitch?”

The metal structure groaned again like some beast in the midst of
digestion.

The hallway I saw my shift mates disappear down was ominously darker the
second time I arrived. I went down the path, and it eventually ended in
a fork. Not sure which way to go, I listened. Strained my ears until it
felt like they would bleed. Silence again. But something beyond it. A
sustained hum, changing pitch every few moments. A rattle within it,
liquid and bubbling. It sounded like sickness. Want.

I smelled rotting meat.

“Clint.”

Mitch’s voice froze every part of my insides into a solid mass. This was
a mistake, the primal part of my brain told me. But if there was a
chance…I swallowed. “Where are you?”

“Turn the corner. I’m with Donahue.”

I didn’t move.

“He’s hurt.”

Mitch’s voice was quiet. Fearful like I had never known him to be. I
would never forgive myself if it turned out he was calling for help.
Behind me, I heard that noise again, raspy, but I needed to get to Mitch
and Donahue.

I turned the corner, and started down the hallway.

“This way, Clint. Hurry.”

The end of the hallway lay obscured, out of the reach of my chem light.
An orange halo of sight surrounded me, but even now the stick in my hand
flickered. I shook it, restoring its glow.

Step by step, inch by inch, I walked down the hallway. The far wall took
shape. There were feet there, limp against the floor. “Captain?”

Donahue was leaned up on a wall, his forehead against it. His body was
still, and there was the sound of dripping liquid. His hands lay at his
sides and his shoulders slumped. The crowbar leaned against his leg,
intertwined in his loose digits. There was something unnatural about his
position, his bent knees. His candle lay extinguished at his feet. Mitch
was nowhere to be seen.

I went closer, and felt the urge to vomit. A sharp knob jammed through
Donahue’s eye socket, holding him in place. Blood dripped down his
cheek, his shirt, his pant leg. His jaw hung slack in a death mask of
surprise.

“One more corner, Clint.”

I spun, trying to see in the dark, raising up my light. Mitch’s voice
came from a branched hallway just behind me. My brain rushed with
adrenaline. How did I miss that? A dark void just off to the side. “Come
out.”

“I can’t. Legs broken.”

There was pain and weakness in his voice. But something else too I
couldn’t place. Something hungry. I hesitated. “What happened to
Donahue, Mitch?”

“He tripped.”

I stared at that void, waiting for more words. A further explanation.

“Why won’t you be with me, Clint?”

I stifled a sharp inhale. My fingers clenched around the glowing stick
in my palm.

“Doesn’t it get lonely here?”

Donahue’s body spasmed, but stayed upright. The voice. Oh it was sweet,
soft. I should’ve known then. Should’ve ran. But you haven’t heard it.
You haven’t felt its pull. No one knew about those things that I thought
about Mitch. The private desires in the dark. Not even he had known. I
had hidden them within me so deep, maybe to keep even myself from from
finding them. And now here he was, speaking them aloud. Speaking them to
me. He wanted me. As I wanted him. It was written thick in the husk of
his voice.

I left Donahue’s body, stepping toward the void.

“Mitch, what’s going on?” My mind was queasy with desire and fear.

“This is truth, Clint. I’m making myself free. Making us free.”

One step, then another. “Just a bit further.” The hunger was stronger
now, pushing out the fear and terror that had been in his voice just
moments ago.

My legs shook, my heart burst. I wanted to break out in a run, turn the
corner, pull myself to him. Like I had dreamed on more than one night
before. My muscles tensed, my body ready to move.

“Clint…”

It was Mitch again. But his voice came now from further back in the
hallway. It was weak, his throat full of gurgles. As if he were fighting
the imminent collapse of his lungs. There was a sharp sound, a crack.

“Clint.” Calm and steady again. No gurgles.

The sound I had heard at the junction came to me again, that strange
shifting pitch. It was faster now. There were drips, heavier than the
slight plinks of water that now echoed through the space. It spoke of
things more viscous.

“Come.”

My legs relaxed, my heart stilled for three beats. Then sprinted
forward.

Past the opening and back to the deck.

It wasn’t him. It wasn’t Mitch. I told myself this over and over.
Recited the words as I blindly retraced my steps, keeping hold of the
radio. Footsteps echoed behind mine, that same raspy noise, now all too
recognizable as breathing. Mitch’s voice cried out incessantly, begging
me not to leave him alone in the dark. But it was too clean, too full of
want. I closed my mind to it, sprinted around turns and up stairwells.

I felt something grasp my shirt, and nearly lost my footing. A door
appeared on my left, and I flung myself through it.

I was back in the rain. Mitch’s voice ceased. Only the rush of the storm
filled my ears.
Ascending nearby stairs, I could see the glow of the control tower
further up. It quivered like a lidded eyes in the throes of a dream. The
light was redder and more polluted than before, like it was shining
through flesh. 

As I made my way there, dread grew in my chest.

Before I reached the tower, a dark figure burst from the door and
sprinted off. I watched them go to the side of the platform and leap off
into the void. “Wait!” My voice couldn’t carry over the storm. They were
already gone. I knew their body would be turned to gelatin by that high
of a leap.

I stared at where the leaper had disappeared. The control tower’s
windows were covered in smeared red. I didn’t want to enter. But I
couldn’t survive in the storm. Couldn’t survive below deck.

After ascending the stairs, I reached out for the metal door handle. It
creaked open, each inch an agony.

It revealed a room covered in blood.

A naked body lay on the console covered in red finger smears. Intestines
poured out of the horrible wound in his stomach. A single name, written
over and over again covered the walls. 

Christie.

The radio fell from my limp hands, clanging to the ground.

“Fuck!”

My heart nearly stopped, and I spun to see Cregger in the corner, his
ears jammed full of what looked to be chewed paper. “What happened?”

He didn’t respond. He couldn’t hear me. The engineer crawled up to me,
his movements jerky and uncoordinated. He plucked up the radio, and
tried to turn it on. When it didn’t light up or emit static, he smashed
it on the deck. “Where’s Donahue?” He spoke loudly, even though in the
room, it was silent save our two heartbeats, and the striking of water
on the window.

“Dead.”

He stared at me for a long moment, then tackled me to the ground.

“What the–”

Cregger wrestled my chem light away from me. I didn’t even realize I
still had it in my grasp. Once he got it, he ran out the door, into the
storm.

I called out to him. “There’s something down there!”

If he responded, I’ll never know. The storm was keening out in awful and
soul-shattering gales. As his form disappeared below the edge of the
deck, I thought I heard one word. “Lifeboat.” Cregger was gone.

I waited out the storm in the control room. I tried not to look at the
body. The platform rocked in the waves, upright but drifting. Hours
passed. Then, as suddenly as it came, the storm ceased. Light returned
to the deck in a gray haze.

Through the windows, I saw him, distorted by the congealed viscera that
coated the glass.
Mitch.

He knelt on the tarmac, his arms outstretched. His throat was bruised,
like old fruit. His forearms bore deep slash marks up their length, two
pools of blood forming beside him like perverted fountains. The razor
still lay between his index and middle fingers. I rushed to his side,
but he was already so pale, so weak.

“Stay with me.”

“Jules…” His eyes looked at mine, already far away. “…Jules…”

His head slumped forward, and he was gone.

It’s been only a few hours. The power came back. All systems in order. I
wandered the halls, and found nothing but Donahue’s dead body. The
Lifeboat is missing, the ropes that moored it, cut. We have drifted, our
GPS indicates, only about two miles off our original destination.

Rescue services will be here soon. They’ll find four bodies wrapped in
canvas on the deck.

To the families of Cregger, Donahue, Frank, and Erik…I’m sorry you will
never have satisfaction beyond this when it comes to your loved one’s
deaths. They were good men, and did not deserve this fate.

To Becca and my children…I’m sorry I never told the truth until now. You
deserved to know a long time ago.

To Jules…

I am sorry for your heartache. I have felt it.

I can only hope that you can live with it.
