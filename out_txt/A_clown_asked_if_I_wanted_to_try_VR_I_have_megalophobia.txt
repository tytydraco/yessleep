I’m typing this from a hospital bed. My head is wrapped in bandages,
there’s an IV in my arm, and the nurse keeps telling me I need to sleep.
I can’t. Every time I close my eyes, I hear that sound again. So if you
ever see a circus with a clown offering free VR trials, don’t go inside.
I’m serious.

“Greetings, master. Might I tempt you with our circus of illusions? ’Tis
a waking dream like none you have known.”

That’s what he said to me. He talked like someone doing a Renaissance
fair impression, and I remember thinking it was ridiculous. I write
horror stories for fun, and I’d spent most of that afternoon trying to
finish one called Black Light Torch. It wasn’t going anywhere. I’d been
staring at the same paragraph for almost an hour. I just wanted to go
home.

The clown grabbed my arm.

“Can you just shut up and let go of me?” I said.

His smile disappeared. Not slowly. It was just there one second and gone
the next.

“Pray forgive me, good sir! The first trial is free of charge, and I
daresay you shall crave the rest once you have tasted the finest of them
all.”

I almost laughed. The poster beside him showed a roller coaster. I’d
never tried VR before, and my friends had been telling me for months
that I was missing out, so I figured whatever. One free ride couldn’t
hurt. Then I noticed the picture on the poster again.

“There’s nothing giant in it, right?”

The clown tilted his head. He actually thought about it for a moment
before smiling again.

“Nay, good sir. Nothing of the sort.”

That should have been enough to make me leave.

I should explain the giant thing. I’m a megalophobe. Basically, I’m
terrified of enormous things. Not heights. Not exactly. It’s the size of
something that gets me. It started when I was ten. I watched Godzilla in
a theater, and something about seeing that thing on a screen surrounded
by people just broke something in my brain. I still remember the noise
the audience made when he appeared. My imagination does the rest. I’ll
be walking through a parking lot at night and suddenly imagine something
enormous standing behind the buildings, something so tall I can’t see
its head. Sometimes I can almost feel it looking down at me. That’s why
I avoid giant-monster movies when I can, which is a little inconvenient
considering I write horror. I read Lovecraft. I watch monster movies. I
study how they’re written. But there’s always been a screen between me
and the thing.

Until that night.

The clown put the headset on me. His smile had returned, and it stayed
there while he adjusted the straps. About halfway through the ride, I
hated him. The roller coaster came over a hill and there it was: the
Titanic. Not a model. Not something that looked big because of the
perspective. It was enormous. The entire ship was rising out of black
water directly in front of me, and the coaster was heading straight
toward it. I could see individual bolts. I could see rust. I could see
the metal moving. My body just shut down. I curled up in the seat and
ripped the headset off. I remember throwing it at the clown. I remember
swearing at him. Then I walked out.

I spent the next few hours wandering around the circus because I didn’t
want to admit how badly it had gotten to me. Eventually the sun went
down. By then I thought I was fine.

I wasn’t.

The parking lot was almost full. There were cars everywhere, which
should have made me feel better. Instead, the open space between them
made my stomach tighten. I stood at the edge of the lot for a few
seconds before forcing myself across. When I reached my car, I put my
hand on the door handle.

That’s when I saw my reflection in the window.

My face was there.

And behind it was another face.

Smiling.

The clown.

I turned.

Something hit me in the side of the head.

Then everything went black.

I woke up in my driver’s seat. My head was killing me. The windows were
completely black, and I couldn’t see anything outside. I tried the door.
Locked. The passenger door was locked too. I started pounding on the
windows, but nothing happened. My vision was blurry. At first I thought
maybe I’d damaged my eyes, but when I raised my hands to my face, I felt
something around my head.

The headset.

For a second I couldn’t understand why it was still there. Then I felt
the straps. They weren’t just tight. They were inside my skin.

I ran my fingers along the edge and felt little knots, one after
another, all the way around my scalp. I realized what they were.

Stitches.

Someone had sewn the headset to my head.

I screamed. I grabbed the foam and pulled. Every stitch pulled at once.
It felt like someone had hooked a bunch of hot wires underneath my skin
and yanked them. I let go. My hands were covered in blood.

I tried smashing the headset against the steering wheel. Nothing. The
dashboard. Nothing. The window. Nothing. It wouldn’t break. It was too
thick and too heavy, like it had been built specifically so I couldn’t
get it off.

Then the screen turned on.

The roller coaster started moving.

The cars clicked up the lift hill. Click. Click. Click. Every movement
pulled against my scalp. Then we reached the top. The Titanic was
waiting below.

It was even bigger than before.

I could see every bolt. Each one was wider than my head. Rust ran down
the hull like blood. The ship groaned. I closed my eyes, but the sound
didn’t stop. It was inside my head.

Then the ship disappeared.

I was standing in a city. Everything was broken. Dust floated through
the air and settled on my tongue. The ground shook once, then again.
Closer.

I knew that sound.

I’d heard it when I was ten.

That low roar from the theater.

Except there were no hundreds of people around me this time. There was
nobody.

Something moved above the buildings. I didn’t look up. I didn’t need to.
The skyline disappeared behind something enormous.

Then the city dropped away.

Water rushed around my legs. Warm water. Something moved underneath it,
slowly, like something waking up. A tentacle came out of the water. It
was ridged and covered in slime. It curled around one of the ruined
buildings, and the entire building disappeared inside it.

I knew what I was looking at. I’d read about things like this. I’d
written things like this. Except I’d never imagined what it would feel
like to actually be there.

Then everything went quiet.

I was standing on a shore.

There was a dog.

A little stray. Wet. Skinny. One ear folded over. It walked toward me
with its tail hanging low, then pressed its head against my leg.

I laughed.

I couldn’t help it. It was the first normal-sized thing I’d seen in what
felt like hours.

I knelt down.

The dog looked at me.

And then something was wrong.

Its head was higher.

I blinked.

It had grown.

I stood up.

It kept growing.

Its fur began pulling apart. Red scales appeared underneath. Its body
stretched upward until I couldn’t see the top of it anymore. Wings
unfolded behind it. They covered the sky.

I couldn’t move.

Something bright gathered behind its teeth.

I felt the heat on my face.

Then the world turned white.

I was in space.

There was no ground. No sky. Nothing to stand on. Just stars. Planets.
Galaxies. Everything.

I looked down for my hands and couldn’t see them. I tried to understand
how big the universe was, but my brain couldn’t do it. The thought just
broke apart.

I screamed.

There was no echo. No sound coming back. Nothing.

And somehow, in that moment, I understood. There wasn’t going to be a
trick. There wasn’t going to be a way around it. I had to get the
headset off.

I grabbed the strap. I planted my feet and pulled.

The pain was unbelievable. I screamed until my throat hurt. One stitch
came loose. Then another. Then another. Blood ran into my eyes, but I
kept pulling. Finally, the last stitch gave.

The headset came off.

I was back in my car.

The windows were black. The parking lot was silent. The headset was
sitting in my lap. There was blood on it, and thread was still hanging
from the straps. I could feel every hole in my scalp.

For a few seconds, nothing happened. I just sat there and breathed. The
air was normal.

Just air.

Then I heard it.

That sound.

The one from the theater.

A low, enormous sound directly behind my car.

The locks clicked open.

I didn’t look behind me. I didn’t look in the mirror. I threw the
headset onto the passenger seat and started the engine. I drove.

I don’t remember much after that. I don’t remember parking at the
hospital. The nurses told me I walked into the ER myself with blood all
over my neck. They took me into surgery within an hour.

The surgeon said they removed forty-one stitch holes from my scalp.
Forty-one. Some still had thread in them. She said the stitches were
neat. Careful. Professional, almost. She asked me who did it.

I told her about the clown.

She didn’t write anything down.

Security checked my car. The headset wasn’t there. Neither was the
clown. There wasn’t even a circus. According to them, the fairground
next to the parking lot had been closed for years.

The doctor says I have a concussion. She says I lost a lot of blood. She
says the medication can make me hear things and that the dizziness and
hallucinations should go away. I told her I understood.

I wanted to believe her.

It’s after midnight now. My room is on the fourth floor. The blinds are
closed. A few minutes ago, my bed shook.

Once.

Then again.

Slowly.

The water in the cup on my tray started rippling. I pressed the call
button. The nurse came in. She looked at the cup, then at me. She put
her hand on the bed rail and told me the floor was completely steady.
She checked my IV, told me it was probably the medication, and left.

The cup is still moving.

The ripples are getting bigger.

And the stars outside my window are disappearing.

One by one.

I can hear someone outside.

A voice.

Faint.

Almost playful.

The clown.

“I daresay you shall crave the rest, good sir.”

It’s probably the painkillers.

That’s what I keep telling myself.

I haven’t turned my head.

I’m afraid that if I do, I’ll see what’s standing behind me.
