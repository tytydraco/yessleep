Last spring I decided I needed a haircut. Something clean, that would
make the rest of me matter less. I found a place a few blocks from my
apartment; a barbershop that smelled like soap and shaving cream. No
fancy shampoos nor offerings for a subscription model, merely a couple
of black chairs, as well as a bald man who looked like he’d been cutting
hair since the day I was born.

I sat down and told him what I wanted; just a little off the top. He
nodded and worked in silence. My hair looked better. Sharper. For a
moment I felt like a slightly different person from the neck up. But
when I looked in the mirror again, the old dissatisfaction was still
there. The barber must have noticed. He asked, plainly, if I needed help
with anything else.

Maybe I only answered because of the way he asked. He was so ordinary,
so polite and professional. I confided that there were other things I’d
been insecure about as of late. He said he could help. I told him I
wasn’t interested in plastic surgery. He said it wasn’t surgery. Just a
little off the top.

I asked what that meant.

He said it would be simple. A cut.

He took the shears and, with no ceremony, removed a thin ridge of flesh
from the side of my face. It came away like cut clay, no blood, no pain.
The piece peeled free in one clean motion. He plopped it on a tray as if
it were nothing more than excess hair.

When I looked in the mirror, that side of my face looked closer to the
one I’d been carrying in my head for years. Cleaner. Defined. Beautiful.

I asked him to do the other side. He did. I asked him to take the soft
places from my stomach. He did. Every imperfection, every insecurity, I
had him cut it all away that night, all for thirty dollars (plus tip).

I went back the next week, and the weeks after that. Soft places trimmed
down. Angles sharpened. Each visit I left looking more like the version
of myself I could stand. The weak places along my jaw disappeared under
the shears. My stomach flattened in increments. I started recognizing
myself. I could hold my own gaze for several minutes at a time before
looking away.

A few people noticed. A coworker complimented the haircut. A woman said
I looked sharper, better than I ever had. She asked if I’d been working
out. My conversations lasted longer. Eyes lingered. It felt like proof
that, for the first time, something was paying off.

But it started to feel like something else. I began to wonder whether
anyone was actually looking at me, or only at the surface that kept
being refined. Those thoughts came and went. I kept going back. There
was always one more correction. Cut the fat from the stomach. A cleaner
line at the chin. Just a little off the top.

The ideal kept moving. The goalposts shifted every time I got close.

The barber never rushed. He worked with a precision I’d only seen in
doctors. Occasionally he stepped back to study the shape of my body. He
spoke only when necessary. When I asked how the flesh came away so
cleanly, he only said that some things separate easier when you know
what to cut. When I asked what happened to the pieces, he said they’d
been taken care of. I should have left it there.

One afternoon the shop was empty. The barber was slow coming out of the
back room. Curious, I stepped toward the open doorway. I’d only meant to
call his name.

The room held every piece that had been taken from me, arranged into a
fleshy mass, formless, folded in on itself, stacked and pressed into
heaps that took up most of the far corner. Some sections still held
older contours; the curve that used to sit above my hips, the thickness
under my jaw, the slack on my arms. The mass shifted. Breathed. It had
no face, yet I knew it could see me.

I stood longer than I should have. The barber appeared behind me. He
asked, in that same mild tone, if I’d been satisfied.

I didn’t know how to answer.

That night I left, and I didn’t go back for a while. The changes held
for a time. I still felt the relief of the cleaner lines when I caught
myself in the mirror. Then they started slipping. My chin softened
first. My jaw followed, gradually. I almost talked myself out of
noticing. One morning the angle wasn’t so sharp. The next day the skin
was looser, warmer, almost melting. The trim around my stomach began to
fill again. It felt as though my body was trying to restore itself,
trying to put the pieces back.

It itched. The surface underneath did not feel like healing skin. It
felt harder. Like something malignant, spreading faster each night. I
could feel it settling, resettling, searching for every shape that no
longer belonged.

I stopped sleeping well. When I did, I dreamed of the mass in the back
room folding in on itself.

The changes accelerated. My face began to blur again. My muscles waned
and sagged. Something inside me no longer felt like my own tissue,
denser in places, grainy in others, coalescing into something
uncomfortable and barbed. It lasted for weeks, and it kept getting
worse. My body, my face, the perfect face I’d almost had, grew more
malformed, more unbearable every day. The only way to keep what I wanted
was to let him cut again.

I went back on a random Tuesday. The shop was quiet. The barber looked
up from his chair. He didn’t ask where I’d been. He only nodded toward
the empty seat.

I sat down. He worked with the same careful attention as before. The
shears took away thin layers. Each piece peeled free without mess. The
face in the mirror was closer again to the one I’d been chasing. That
familiar relief settled over my shoulders. Underneath it, something
colder stirred.

Before I left, I asked about the mass in the back room. He wiped the
shears on a cloth and said it was still there, waiting. He asked if I
thought I would be finished soon.

Again, I didn’t answer.

I’ve gone back four times since then. The intervals between the
mutations are getting shorter. The body tries harder every time to
reclaim what was taken. The itching starts sooner. The shifting grows
more insistent. I can feel each place wanting to soften back into its
old shape, pressing upward, trying to remember exactly where it belongs.

When he works, I catch myself wondering how large the mass has become.
Whether it holds any recognizable shape anymore, or whether those
details have been buried under mounds of flesh.

I haven’t looked again. I don’t know if I could stand it.

I’m closer to the face I’ve always wanted.
