Any unauthorized person found past the secondary perimeter fence of the
Blackwood timber tract had to be logged, brought into the staging
office, and kept under direct physical observation until county
transport showed up. You didn’t take your eyes off them. If you broke
visual contact during a holding period, company policy was immediate
termination and a formal negligence inquiry.
It was about twenty to two in the morning when the gate sensor at access
point four went off. My shift partner, Wayne, grabbed the keys to the
company truck and headed down the haul road. I stayed behind in the
guard shack to log the yard dials and nurse a mug of burnt coffee. We
were covering six thousand acres of replanted pine up in northern Maine.
During the winter shutdown, the job was basically making sure nobody
stripped the copper off the heavy equipment and checking the pipeline
pressure on the irrigation line. That line ran right past the north
parcel where a timber harvester burned down to the frame two winters
back.
Around five after two, the base station on my desk clicked. Wayne’s
voice came over the speaker, sounding tired and flat. He told me he had
a trespasser near the burned-out skidder. A young woman, bare feet,
wearing a heavy ripped canvas work coat, sitting on an old fuel drum. He
said she had circular rust-colored marks around both ankles, the kind of
ring patterns you get from steel transport chains. He asked her how she
walked through seven miles of snowed-in fire breaks without boots, but
she wouldn’t answer him. She just sat there staring straight down at the
slush.
Wayne drove her up to the shack about ten minutes later. The rule was to
put any trespasser in the auxiliary holding room, which was just a
windowless concrete utility bay behind the front desk with a heavy steel
door, a deadbolt, and a wired-glass observation slot. Wayne walked her
in. Her clothes were soaked in frozen mud, and she had thick black dirt
under her fingernails. When Wayne pointed at the steel bench and told
her to sit, she sat. She put her hands on her knees, kept her head down,
and didn’t make a sound.
Wayne stepped back out into the main room and threw the deadbolt. He
unzipped his parka, sat down at the dispatch terminal, and picked up the
landline to call the county sheriff. He held the receiver to his ear,
waited a few seconds, hit the switch hook twice, and set it down. Just
dead air. He pulled out his own flip phone, but the screen said
searching for service with zero bars.
I picked up the Motorola mic and keyed it to call the regional hub on
the high-frequency band. Nothing. No response tone, no repeater chirp,
just a dry, flat carrier hum. Wayne checked the panel behind the desk.
Every single breaker switch was thrown forward in the on position. The
electric clock on the wall kept ticking along, second by second, but
every piece of communication gear in the room was completely dead.
I walked over to the observation slot to check on the girl. She hadn’t
moved an inch. She was sitting stiff on that steel bench under the
single fluorescent strip. On the concrete around her feet, somebody had
drawn four thick, chalky lines. They formed a square around the bench
legs, maybe three feet wide. It looked like heavy white grease, the kind
we used on haul-truck wheel bearings, only there wasn’t a single grease
tube or machine part in that room.
Wayne grabbed his five-cell Maglite, unlocked the deadbolt, and pushed
the door open an inch or two. He asked her what she put on the floor.
She didn’t look up. Her voice was flat, totally level. She said they
would be at the north gate before three. Wayne asked who she was talking
about, but she went right back to staring at the concrete.
Wayne pulled the door shut and locked the deadbolt. He reached into his
pocket, flicked open his utility knife, and set it on the desk by the
radio. He told me he was going to take the truck down to the paved
county route to flag down a plow or get a signal once he climbed out of
the basin. He said to keep my back against the front wall, leave the
holding door locked, and watch the yard lights.
He walked out at twenty-five after two. I watched his headlights cut
across the pines as he made the turn, and then the rumble of that diesel
engine just died out in the trees.
The shack went dead quiet. All I could hear was the wall clock ticking.
Around twenty to three, the overhead light in the main office cut out
with a sharp pop. The emergency backup light above the exit door didn’t
kick on. The desk lamp stayed dark. The thermostat on the wall was still
glowing green, reading sixty-four degrees, but the temperature in the
room dropped fast. Within a couple of minutes, I could see my own breath
hanging in the dark.
I moved behind the counter and picked up the thirty-six-inch pipe wrench
we kept under the desk. I looked through the glass slot into the holding
room.
The bench was empty.
The girl was gone.
The light in the holding room was still buzzing, shining down on that
steel bench. Those four white grease marks were still on the floor,
completely clean. No smeared tracks, no shoe prints, no wet spots on the
cement. The only window in the room was a small piece of reinforced wire
glass set into solid concrete, bolted tight from the outside. The bolts
hadn’t been touched. I checked the deadbolt on the metal door. The
cylinder was fully thrown into the frame, locked tight from my side.
I didn’t turn that key.
At twelve minutes to three, the radio on the desk made a rough clicking
sound. A voice came out of the speaker. It was Wayne’s voice. The tone,
the speed, the pitch, everything sounded like him, but there wasn’t any
engine noise behind it. It said the exact words he told me an hour ago:
I have a trespasser by the burned-out skidder.
The line clicked off. Five seconds went by, and it clicked on again.
Same voice. Same flat speed: I have a trespasser by the burned-out
skidder.
I didn’t touch the mic. I walked over to the north window and looked out
into the yard. The high pole lights were out, but the snow gave off
enough light to see the whole gravel lot.
Right at the tree line, about two hundred yards away, a dark human shape
was standing on top of the snowdrift. It was tall, taller than Wayne,
standing straight up with its arms pinned flat against its sides. It
didn’t shift its weight. It didn’t lift an arm. It didn’t turn its head.
It just stood on that drift, dead still, looking right at the shack.
Behind it, back in the pines about thirty feet deep, three pairs of
white reflections were sitting fixed in the dark. They stayed level,
about six feet off the ground, not blinking and not moving.
I backed away from the window, sat on the floor behind the metal file
cabinet, and set the pipe wrench across my boots. The radio didn’t click
again. The phone stayed dead. The room stayed freezing.
Just after six in the morning, the light outside started turning gray.
The overhead fluorescent tubes buzzed and snapped back on. The electric
heater kicked on with a loud clank, rattling the tin vent and pushing
warm air across the desk.
The county deputies got to the gate around twenty to seven. Wayne never
reached the paved road. They found the company truck four miles down the
haul route, pulled neatly onto the shoulder with the engine shut down,
the keys on the dash, and both doors locked from the inside. Wayne
wasn’t there. His winter coat and his phone were sitting on the
passenger seat.
Two deputies checked the holding room. The white grease marks on the
floor were gone, just dry gray concrete. The snow outside the fence was
completely flat, with no tracks leading away from the building, and the
technicians didn’t find a single short in the shack’s wiring. They wrote
the whole thing up as a voluntary disappearance.
The company shut down operations on the Blackwood parcel two weeks
later. They fired the private security contractor and nailed plywood
over the shack windows. Nobody has worked that ground since.
