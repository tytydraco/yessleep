Has anyone here heard of something called “junk journaling?”

Think scrapbooking, but with everything other than photographs. In fact,
the more disposable and useless something is, the better. A coffee
sleeve from meeting an old friend. A crumpled boarding pass from a
memorable trip. Even the wrapper from a stick of gum a kid offered you
on the subway.

The trick is finding a way to make the junk worth looking at again.

A feather you picked up on a walk with someone you love looks pretty
ridiculous taped to a sheet of paper. But glue down just the spine and
use watercolor to paint the rest of the bird around it, and suddenly it
holds more memory than the feather ever could on its own.

Things most people would throw away can become beautiful if someone
cares enough to find the right place for them.

Knowing my wife, it’s hardly surprising she would be drawn to a hobby
like this.

Anna has always been a lover of the unloved. You know the type. She’s
the girl who likes spiders and rats more than puppies and kittens, not
because she has anything against puppies and kittens, but because, as
she once explained to me, “Everybody already loves those.”

People aren’t much different. She works part-time for the City of
Baltimore supporting homeless outreach, then spends a frankly
unreasonable amount of what should be her free time volunteering at
shelters, soup kitchens, and whatever charity has managed to get its
hands on her phone number.

And, of course, she married me. Unloved is sort of my default setting. I
was abandoned before I could open my eyes and spent the next eighteen
years bouncing between foster homes, group homes, and juvenile
facilities. By the time I aged out, almost everything I owned fit into
two garbage bags.

The only thing from those years that didn’t stay behind was my foster
brother, Roderick. Rod never found his Anna. The things I spent my
twenties trying desperately to outrun, he ran toward instead: drinking,
arrests, drugs, lost jobs, stretches without a permanent address. I love
him. I’ve tried to help him. I’m also ashamed of how often I’ve seen his
name on my phone and let it ring.

Anna never does that. He’s eventually figured out that if he needs
something, all he’s got to do is call her, and she’ll invite him over or
pressure me to take him out to lunch. I’ve started preferring the
latter: less silverware goes missing.

Anyway, a few months ago, Anna found out that her general inability to
consider anything worthless apparently had a name and a thriving online
community.

She showed me a video about junk journaling one night, and that was
pretty much it. Within a week, we had a drawer full of receipts,
wrappers, torn packaging, bits of ribbon, dried flowers, and other
things I previously would have thrown away without a second thought.

And when I saw the first journals, I thought they were adorable.
Creative ways to remember everyday events with everyday things. 

Then one day, she came home absolutely devastated. One of the homeless
shelter regulars, a sweet but schizophrenic octogenarian named William,
was found unresponsive that morning. There was no family to call to deal
with his body or his few belongings; to the city, they were equally
worthless. 

Baltimore used to bury its unclaimed dead in a public cemetery, but
eventually the land became too valuable to keep burying people no one
was expected to visit. These days, they’re cremated instead.

As far as anyone could tell, Anna’s friendship with him made her the
closest thing he had to next of kin. When the city was finished with
William, Anna asked for what remained.

There was paperwork, a waiting period, and an uncomfortably callous
amount of bureaucracy, but eventually Anna brought William home in two
containers. His possessions filled a canvas tote bag. William himself
came in a small, unadorned, corrugated cardboard box.

I remember her setting them beside each other on the kitchen table and
staring at them for a long time.

“That’s it?” she finally asked.

I waited to see if she’d elaborate.

“Eighty-two years,” she said, eyes as wide as dinner plates. “And that’s
everything?”

I didn’t know what to say, I mean, there was nothing to say, so I put my
hand over hers and tried to grip it reassuringly. “It’s not everything,”
I finally offered. “You remember him.”

The big brown eyes landed on me. “For how long, August?”

That was the first time she made a journal for a person. She started
with William’s bus pass, then an empty cigarette pack, and eventually
managed to incorporate every single item in that tote bag, plus pieces
of the canvas bag itself as backing or to cut into shapes of doves and
crosses. 

It was breathtaking, but haunting, too. When she showed me the finished
journal, I had the distinct feeling I was looking at something I had no
permission to see. Some unearned intimacy I was invading. I’d only met
William a handful of times, but by the last page I knew that he drew a
star over the i’s in his name, that he’d once been married, that he
liked terrible knock-knock jokes, and that somewhere along the way he’d
learned enough French to write a few words of it in the margins of
things.

I held Anna tight and kissed the top of her head. I told her the truth:
she’d taken the scraps of a life that hadn’t been particularly kind and
made something beautiful out of them.

A few days later, I saw she was working on a new journal, with the
scraps of content to include piled around her in an organization only
she could decode. “It’s Madeline,” she said without looking up from the
double-sided tape she was untangling.

“Madeline…” I spoke the name as if doing so would conjure the memory I
was clearly supposed to have.

“God, August, come on, Madeline!” she snapped. “The woman who liked
birds?”

How could I forget. “Liked birds,” was a hell of an understatement. I
met her when I was once waiting to pick Anna up after a particularly
long Saturday shelter shift, and she crawled up on the hood of my car
and started whistling and flapping her bare arms like wings. And every
inch of those arms, flab and all, was covered in bird tattoos. Some
looked professional, older faded ones, and the rest looked like she
either did them herself or hired a kindergartener to stencil them. She
warbled and cawed until Anna gently guided her back inside, her face
absent of any of the judgment I felt impossible to restrain. But that
was Anna.

“Of course, Madeline. I remember now. Baby, I’m so sorry. What happened?
Do you want to talk about it?”

She shook her head and took a deep breath. “She’s gone.”

“I see, yeah. Okay. Well, I’ll leave you be to work on this, then. Let
me know if you need anything.”

I tried to slip away politely but quickly from Anna and her new journal,
uncomfortable by the feeling of trespassing I felt reemerging. She’d
lost homeless friends before, many in fact, but it seemed like this new
creative outlet was allowing her to grieve more fully, more connectedly.
After peeping at the private nature of William’s journal, I had no
interest in participating further beyond moral support.

Anna called out right as I rounded the railing of the bottom stair.
“Actually, your brother called.”

I sighed and shuffled back over to her. “He’s just ‘Rod,’ you don’t have
to call him my ‘brother.’”

That got her attention. She tightened her finger’s grip on the tape and
shot a glare at me. “Don’t start with me, not today. Roderick is your
brother. He wants to meet. Call him back, set something up, ASAP. And if
you insist on going out again, wherever you go, bring me back
something.”

I knew she didn’t mean a scone or a cup of tea. “Anything for my junk
junkie.”

Within two weeks, she had destroyed our YouTube algorithm. We share an
account on the TV, so suddenly half my recommendations were women
explaining how to keep pressed flowers from turning brown, how to stop
embroidery thread from fraying, how to keep thin leather from curling as
it dries, how to seal delicate paper without discoloring it. I
complained exactly once, and Anna informed me that this was the price of
being married to an artist. She was right, of course.

She always is.

At my request, she’d moved her crafting into the spare room, rearranging
the furniture to accommodate a makeshift workshop. We’d always talked
about fostering someday, so eventually the room was supposed to belong
to somebody who needed it more than Anna’s glue guns did. For now,
though, it was hers.

Things continued that way for a couple of months. I saw Anna less and
less as she closed herself off into her crafting space for whatever
hours she wasn’t working or volunteering. Others in my shoes might’ve
been worried, but this was just Anna. She never gave anything less than
her all, and this was a new way to honor the people and things she felt
were too readily discarded. Something else to care for in the way only
she could. I missed spending time with my wife, but figured the novelty
would eventually run its course. I should’ve known better.

Yesterday, I stopped by the shelter to pick Anna up. She wasn’t ready.
This wasn’t unusual. I found myself standing around near the front desk
while she finished up with someone in the back, making small talk with
one of the volunteers whose name I couldn’t remember.

I asked how everyone was doing. She gave me the kind of answer you give
someone who doesn’t actually know anyone.

Then, mostly because I couldn’t think of anything else to say, I
mentioned Madeline.

“I’m sorry about what happened to her.”

The woman frowned. “What happened to Madeline?”

“She died.”

Her expression changed. “Madeline isn’t dead.”

I didn’t know what to say, so I just responded, “Yeah, she is.”

“No. She just stopped coming in. That happens.”

“Anna said she was gone.”

“She is gone. Nobody’s seen her in weeks. But no reason to assume the
worst.”

I don’t remember what I said after that. On the short drive back to our
apartment, I tried delicately bringing the subject up with Anna.

“By the way, you never did tell me what happened to Madeline.”

She looked out the passenger window. “What do you mean?”

“I was talking to one of the volunteers while I waited for you. She
didn’t even know Madeline was dead.”

Anna was quiet for a second, then she nodded. “Not everybody knew her
like I did.”

“Apparently. She thought Madeline just stopped showing up.”

“That’s what people usually think.”

“So what happened?”

She turned to me then. “August, you barely knew her.”

“I know. I just thought you might be ready to talk about it.”

“I’m not.”

“Okay.”

A few seconds passed before she reached over and rested her hand on my
knee. “I’m glad you remembered her, though.”

And that was the end of it.

But it bothered me. Today is Saturday, so I had the house to myself. I
lasted until about ten before I went into the spare room. Even then, I
wasn’t looking for anything horrible. I wanted to know where Anna had
gotten Madeline’s things. That was all.

The room looked exactly as it always did. Morning light through the
blinds. Jars of buttons, ribbon, dried flowers, beads. Little scraps of
paper stuck to the carpet where the vacuum couldn’t reach them. It
smelled faintly of glue.

William’s journal was on the bottom shelf of the bookcase. Madeline’s
was beside it. There were others after hers.

I sat cross-legged on the floor and pulled Madeline’s into my lap. For a
while, I couldn’t open it.

Yesterday I’d been worried Anna was lying to me. This morning that had
hardened into a different suspicion, one that made me feel sick in a
much less ordinary way.

Madeline was missing. Anna had her things. I wanted to know why. I
needed to know why.

I opened the cover.

The first page held a grocery receipt, folded into a little pocket made
from the corner of an envelope. Inside was a handwritten list in
bleeding blue ink.

MILK

SOAP

PEANUT BUTTER

BIRD SEED

The next page had a bus transfer worked into the trunk of a tree. A torn
piece of a fast-food wrapper had become its leaves. Anna had cut a blue
foil candy wrapper into tiny birds and perched them among the branches.

This was what she did. She took garbage and made it mean something.

A few pages later I found a hospital bracelet. Then the corner of a
birthday card with “MADELINE” still visible in somebody else’s
handwriting. It was so faded, I could barely make it out; she must’ve
kept it for years. 

A shoelace. A library checkout slip. A piece of floral fabric that might
have come from a shirt. Things Madeline could have given her. Things
Madeline could have thrown away. Things Anna could have picked up. I
could explain every one. That helped.

Then I turned the page and found the raven.

It occupied almost the entire right-hand page, wings spread wide against
a blotchy pale background. Black feathers. Crooked feet. Head turned
sharply to one side.

It wasn’t particularly well-done. That struck me because everything Anna
made was well-done.

The proportions were slightly wrong. One wing sat higher than the other.
Several of the feathers crowded together near the tail. The black ink
had faded to gray in places, while other lines remained dark and heavy.
It looked amateurish.

Familiar.

I leaned closer. Anna hadn’t drawn it. 

I looked at the raven’s background. At first I thought she’d found a
scrap of unusually thin leather and cut around the design. It was a
pallid peach color, roughly oval, and stretched flat beneath a clear
layer of sealant. The edges had been trimmed into a decorative scallop,
almost like one of those old-fashioned postage stamps.

I touched it. It gave beneath my fingertip, but just slightly. I pulled
my hand back.

There were pores.

Tiny ones. Hundreds of them. Little dark pits interrupting the surface
in ways no paper ever would. Near the raven’s tail, several nearly
invisible hairs had been trapped beneath the sealant.

My stomach tightened, and my mind gave me a single word: Leather.

It’s leather. It had to be leather. Leather can have pores, right? And
follicles? And Anna had been watching videos about leather. I felt
relieved. 

Then I noticed the freckle. Small. Brown. Just below the raven’s left
wing.

And I remembered it. Not the freckle, but the raven itself. Madeline on
the hood of my car. Bare arms beating at the air while she whistled at
nothing. Birds from wrist to shoulder. Some beautifully done, and others
so badly drawn I’d wondered if she’d had a child tattoo them.

And on the soft underside of one upper arm, a crooked black raven with
one wing higher than the other.

I looked back down.

One second, the journal was in my hands, the next, it was lying open on
the carpet between my knees. The raven landed face-up. I stared at it. A
piece of Madeline’s arm stared back.

I crawled away from it. There’s no dignified way to describe that. I
didn’t stand or scream; I pushed backward on my hands and heels like an
animal until my shoulder struck the bedframe.

The journal stayed where it was, and the raven’s wings remained spread.
And because my brain apparently hated me, I remembered Anna’s search
history in a new light.

How to keep thin leather from curling as it dries.

I turned to the wastebasket, ready to release the toxic bile rising from
my nauseous belly, but I couldn’t throw up. My heart raced in my ears
and I felt my head get hot with blood, but the sick stayed within me.

I stayed there on my knees, leaning my forehead against the cool edge of
the bedframe, trying not to look behind me. Eventually, I had to.

The journal was still open where I’d dropped it, the raven’s wings
spread wide.

I should have left. I should have called the police. Instead, I crawled
back to it. I think some part of me was still looking for a way to be
wrong.

I picked up the journal by its edges and turned the page.

Anna had made another bird. This one was small enough to fit in my palm,
its body cut from black paper and its wings built from dozens of fine
black and gray thread. She’d braided some together near the body, then
teased the ends apart into individual feathers.

How to stop embroidery thread from fraying.

I touched one. It bent around my fingertip. Not thread. Hair.

I jerked my hand away so hard I tore one of the paper feathers beside
it. For a second, that stupid little rip was what horrified me most. I’d
damaged it…I’d damaged her. I stared at the torn paper, actually
thinking about how heartbroken Anna would be when she saw it.

Then I realized I was sitting on the floor of our future child’s bedroom
worrying that I’d damaged the junk journal my wife had made out of a
missing woman.

I turned another page. Then another. I stopped trying to identify
everything. That became important very quickly. Some things I
recognized, some I could guess, some were better left as shapes and
textures without names.

One I couldn’t avoid naming. Anna had made a little bird’s nest from
strips of brown paper and dried grass. Three white shapes sat inside it
like eggs. Two were beads. The third had a root.

I stared at it for several seconds before my tongue found the
corresponding place in my own mouth. A tooth. Human. Yellowed near the
base, with a tiny silver filling still embedded in the crown. I didn’t
touch that one.

I kept turning pages.

There were other things.

Slivers of something hard and white worked into the frame around a
pressed flower.

A thin, curved clipping I initially took for translucent plastic.

Something beneath a layer of tissue paper that had a shape I recognized
just enough to turn the page before I could recognize it completely.

And through all of it, Madeline’s ordinary life. Except now, Anna had
found a place for every piece. That was what finally broke something in
me.

She hadn’t hidden Madeline between the pages, she’d arranged the essence
of her.

The loud woman on the hood of my car had been scattered and strange and
impossible for me to understand. In here, she made perfect sense. Every
ugly little scrap of an ordinary life had been cut and folded and fitted
against the next until nothing was wasted and nothing was out of place.

Even Madeline herself made perfect sense.

I thought of the raven, without the courage to flip back to it. There
was one explanation I kept trying not to think about. Unclaimed meant
cremated. I knew that because I’d watched Anna bring eighty-two years of
William home in a cardboard box.

If Madeline had died unclaimed, there shouldn’t have been a raven left
to cut out.

If she’d been claimed, somebody besides Anna would know she was dead.

I looked around the room. Pressed flowers beneath wax paper. Bottles of
sealant. Scissors. Tweezers. Cutting mat. Everything was exactly where
it had always been. Nothing in that room had changed. Only the names of
things.

Thread. Hair.

Leather. Skin.

Junk. Madeline. 

I closed the book carefully, wincing as the items inside creaked under
the new pressure. Forcing myself to breathe deeply, I turned back to the
neatly stacked journals where I’d pulled this one from. I made myself
read the other spines. My eyes skipped past every name but one.

Tucked at the end was not a bound journal, but an overstuffed portfolio,
a name written in permanent marker along its folded edge. The letters
buckled where the contents had cracked and warped the thick paper.

RODERICK.

Like moving through a dream, I saw myself reach for the folder and place
it on the carpet in front of me. My vision grew blurry, and I put a hand
firmly against the smooth front flap in a futile attempt to keep the
dizziness at bay. 

Nothing inside was adhered; everything was binder-clipped in little
stacks on each intended page. There was a spread dedicated to our foster
home: a drawing I’d made of us when I was six or seven that I didn’t
notice was missing from my office drawer, an old photo booth strip of me
and Rod as teenagers, a piece of the shoebox I’d kept them both in, cut
into a neat rectangle for backing.

I turned the page. A movie ticket from the first time Rod came to visit
after I’d gotten my own place. A note he’d written me years later. A
coaster from a bar we used to meet at before I stopped drinking with
him. I knew where those had come from, too.

Me.

Anna had gone through the little collection of my life I’d managed to
keep and pulled out every place that Rod touched it.

The styrofoam coffee cup I’d brought home after Anna pushed me to meet
him a few weeks ago.

Wherever you go, bring me back something. I had, of course.

Then I reached things I hadn’t saved. Beneath the flattened styrofoam
was the ATM receipt from three days ago. I stared at the timestamp. Rod
had called asking for money. I’d said no, felt like shit, stopped for
cash anyways, and handed him forty dollars in a parking lot.

I’d thrown the receipt away before I got home. I was sure of that. Yet
Anna had it. Smoothed flat. Saved. “WITHDRAWAL: $40.00”

I kept going. A beer bottle label. A plastic coffee stirrer. The
cardboard sleeve from the brand of frozen burritos he practically lived
on. A scrap of paper with a phone number in Rod’s handwriting. A loose
button I recognized from the coat he’d worn all winter.

Childhood. Foster care. His twenties. Last month. Last week. Three days
ago. A grocery list I’d made for a dinner he was supposed to come to
tonight. 

Then I reached whole pages with nothing at all.

I knew what a finished one looked like now. I figured Madeline’s had
started the same way. Ordinary things. Things she touched. Things she
used. Things nobody else would have thought to keep. Then the things
that couldn’t be collected while she was living an ordinary life.

I shut Rod’s portfolio. I couldn’t look at the empty pages anymore.

It was almost noon, which meant Anna would be home soon from her morning
shift at the shelter. I went downstairs and sat in the kitchen,
considering my options. I had to call the police, there was no doubt
about that, but the phone felt like lead in my hand. I stared at the
clock once it struck 12, watching every tick of the second hand until it
was nearly 1 pm. Thinking. Buzzing, with equal parts confusion and fear.
Finally, the front door swung open, and Anna walked in, grocery bags in
hand.

“You’re late.” My eyes remained fixed on my coffee mug, unable to meet
hers. 

“Oh yeah, I had to go to the grocery store for dinner tonight.”

I watched her pull out chicken drumsticks, flour, eggs, and buttermilk
from one brown paper bag, then fresh zucchini and squash from the other.
She put the chicken into a brine and carefully covered it with plastic
wrap, then put it in the fridge. Then she wiped a drying kitchen knife
with a dish towel and began chopping vegetables with a peaceful smile on
her face.

I watched my wife with wonder as she wordlessly put together the pieces
of Rod’s favorite foods.

I couldn’t think of a way to begin asking what I needed to ask, so I
started at the beginning.

“When William died, did they give you his body?”

She held her knife in place mid-cut and looked at me with surprise.

“What? No. His ashes. You know that.”

“Right.” Breathe, breathe you idiot. “What about Madeline?”

Now she put the knife down altogether, which released a tension in my
arms I didn’t know I’d been holding.

“What about her?” she asked.

“Is she dead?”

A pause. “Yes.”

“Did you kill her?”

Another, longer pause. “Yes.”

The blunt confession should have sent a shockwave through me. It didn’t.
There was nothing left to discover, only to understand.

“Why?”

Anna looked down at the half-cut zucchini. “Madeline was going to
disappear someday. Everyone already expected her to, wanted her to.
That’s why nobody is looking for her now.”

“That doesn’t give you the right to slice up a woman! Put pieces of her
into your sick little books!”

“I’m no butcher, Auggie. I’m the only one who refuses to throw these
people away. Nobody else sees what’s still there. Nobody else cares
enough to make something beautiful out of it.”

“When did this start?”

For the first time, her eyes welled with tears. Real tears; she knows no
other kind. I had to fight the reflex to go to her and hold her tight.

“By the time William died, it was too late to do much of anything with
what he had left. I did my best with what I knew, but I was still an
amateur, and there’s not much that can be done with what’s left in the
pockets of a dead man.” She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand.
“It woke me up. No more of my friends will die in obscurity. I’m doing
what I always have done; I’m helping them.” The earnestness of her voice
was so captivating, so raw. I felt my heart ache with pangs first of
understanding, and then instantly, of regret. 

My head was swimming as I remembered the half-finished journal with
Rod’s name, filled with the rubbish of his rubbish life. My sadness
turned to something darker.

“You know Anna, I’m amazed you remembered to get all the right
groceries, considering where you put the list.”

It took a second for the realization to register on her face. “I see.
That’s why you’re upset.” 

She walked from the kitchen island over to the bar where I sat and
pulled up the other stool next to me. “That one isn’t ready yet, my
love.” Anna reached for my hand, but I pulled it away. As strange as it
is, I immediately felt immature in my reaction, like I was overreacting.
She pursed her lips at my flinching, and continued. “I’m sure it must
have been off-putting to see that before you could see the finished
product.”

“Oh, no, I’ve seen your finished product. I saw the skin, the teeth, the
hair, the shards of bone. And you will not touch Rod. Not now, not
ever.”

The look of patient exasperation she gave me was like one you would give
a child who refuses to hold his parent’s hand. “August, listen to me. I
know how much you love your brother. I love him too. But Roderick has
been suffering for his entire life. And he will continue to suffer. The
path he’s on…I see where it leads. I see it every day. I see the
kindest, most compassionate people live in squalor and sin and crime
just to scratch the itches that will never go away.”

I sat in a stupor of conflicting thoughts, unable to articulate any of
them before another stomped it out. Finally, one emerged. “Why don’t I
have a book? I was discarded too.”

Not waiting for me to pull away this time, she reached forward to grab
my hands and held tight. “Auggie, baby, no. You’re not disposable. Those
people made a mistake. Look at you! You have a home. You have friends.
You have a career. You have me. If you disappeared tonight, people would
notice.”

“Rod has me,” I said.

She exhaled slowly and asked, “Does he?”

I’m not a cryer; never have been. One thing foster care and juvie
teaches you is to hold those emotions in like your life depends on it.
Because usually it does. But in that moment, I wept. I wept for the
mischievous boy Rod once was, I wept for the misguided man he became, I
wept for all the things Rod would never be, could never be. And most of
all, I wept for my part in it. 

Anna watched me sob, helpless. “My sweet August. Can’t you see that this
is the best way to turn his life from something painful to something
magnificent? We can give him that.”

Suddenly, her eyes lit up. She looked toward the front door, then back
at me. Then the warmest, gentlest, most perplexing smile spread across
her face. “I had a different plan for tonight. I wanted more little
reminders of how he is now. But I think…” she gazed absently back at the
partially prepared food in the kitchen and nodded. “I think I have
enough.”

Now I am upstairs, writing this.

Rod got here about twenty minutes after Anna and I spoke. I heard him
knock. I heard Anna let him in. He asked where I was, and she told him I
needed a little time alone.

He asked if he should leave.

“No,” she said. “August wants you here.”

I almost went downstairs.

Before I came up, Anna had asked me one question. “Do you want me to let
him go home tonight?”

I couldn’t answer.

“August?”

I thought about the portfolio. The drawing of two little boys. The
coffee cup. Forty dollars. All those empty pages.

Then I thought about every phone call I hadn’t answered. Every promise
Rod made. Every promise he broke. Every time I’d wondered whether the
next call would be from a hospital, a jail, or somebody telling me there
wouldn’t be another call.

Mostly, I thought about how tired I was.

“I don’t know,” I told her.

She nodded and kissed my forehead. “Then go think.”

So I did. That’s what this was supposed to be. Me putting everything in
order so you guys could help me decide what to do. 

Call the police. Get Rod out. Give my brother one more chance, because
however many I’ve given him already, there is always supposed to be one
more.

But I’ve been up here for almost an hour. For most of it, I could hear
them downstairs. Rod laughing. Plates clinking. The television. Then it
got quiet. 

It’s been quiet for eleven minutes now. The police are three numbers
away. My brother is one flight of stairs away. Instead, I’ve spent those
eleven minutes writing.

So I think I finally understand. I didn’t come up here to decide, or ask
for your help. If I wanted Rod to leave this house, I never would have
left him alone with her.

Anna has spent years trying to help me keep my brother. Answer his
calls. Give him money. Invite him over. Forgive him. Try again.

And again.

And again.

Maybe this is the only way left to help him.

I just heard footsteps on the stairs. The spare room door opened. Anna
is calling my name from it. I know what she’s carrying.

There were a lot of empty pages in Rod’s book this morning. I should go.

It’s time to help my wife make something beautiful.
