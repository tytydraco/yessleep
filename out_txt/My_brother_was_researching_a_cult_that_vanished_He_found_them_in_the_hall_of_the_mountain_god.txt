The fondest memory of my childhood was the sound of strange instruments
coming from my brother’s room. He was a music prodigy of sorts, and
could play twenty-two different instruments by the time he left for
college. He had perfect pitch and a knack for improvisation that made
him phenomenal to listen to even if he was just picking up the hurdy
gurdy or the string bass. He had a mind for music, and even one that
went beyond convention. One time, he told me how much he wished he could
have a violin with a fifth string.

Being four years his junior, I idolized him the way a younger sister
does. I tried my own hand at music, but alas, I had no ear for pitch. My
sense of rhythm remained intact, though, and so I found my own calling
fulfilled in the realm of dance. I would practice my steps in the
hallway outside his room as he practiced his solos over and over.

It crushed me when he left for college. I mourned the departure of the
music into my pillow as he spent the morning packing his car with all
his instruments. The thought of a silent house terrified me.

He eventually came to my room with a rhythmic knock on the door. As I
turned further into my pillow, he came and sat on the edge of my bed.

“Alice,” he said. “I have something for you.” 

I made a half-hearted turn back to him and looked up at him through
tears.

“I know it’s not as good as the real thing, but hopefully twelve hours
of jam sessions can hold you over until Christmas break.” In his hand
was his cheap MP3 player with a pair of wired ear buds. I had a matching
one as a kid.

It wasn’t the same. He was an entire forty-eight states away and it
wasn’t the same to spin in the hallway when the music wasn’t coming from
the other side of the door. It wasn’t enough either. The melodies grew
stale as I knew where they ran, his improvisation no longer a shock to
the senses.

My brother didn’t come home for Christmas. He was on tour with the
concert band. I was forced to dance to a lifeless version of the
Nutcracker Ballet in the empty warmth of a quiet house.

He wasn’t planning to come home for spring break either. More touring.
My heart was broken, so I was astonished to see him pull into the
driveway in early March then. As I threw my arms around him, he returned
the hug less than enthusiastically. 

The Covid pandemic had hit. His university sent everyone home for the
semester. My melancholy was replaced with his. The music that emanated
from his room was now more often in a minor key, too fast or too
aggressive for dance.

My initial excitement at his return turned to confusion and sadness as
he became more and more withdrawn. It ended in grief that I had even
wished for him to come home. He retreated to his room more as the
semester wore on. He played less. I danced less. Once online classes
ended, a new sound came from his room. Not a strange instrument, but
just his voice. Talking.

He had started a podcast. He had identified a gap in the market, a topic
no one had covered yet: the music of cults. Now instead of practicing
for hours on end, he spent his time scouring the internet, headphones
on, listening to some strange chant or band. I jumped in my room several
times when he’d abruptly play a throat singing tune over and over
between the clacking of his keyboard.

Research turned to scripts and scripts turned to episodes. I couldn’t
stand to listen to them because I had already had a preview into the
recording process.

One day, I was feeling particularly lonely, and so I poked my head into
his room. He sat at his computer, brow knit in concentration with his
headphones on as he typed away. I was about to ask if he could play me
something when he noticed me and took his headphones off.

“What’s up?” he said, his face a mask.

“Oh, nothing. What are you working on?”

“A new episode.”

“Another one?”

“Yeah I found something really interesting. I don’t know if anyone has
come across this before.”

“Don’t you ever just want to play music instead?”

“Here, you should listen to this.”

He pressed a few buttons on his stereo and unplugged his headphones.

“What do you hear?”

I listened. It sounded like a bunch of rumbling thunder in the distance.
“Is that thunder?”

His eyes lit up. “It’s a rockslide. But it’s, like, rhythmic. Here, this
will make more sense.”

He fast forwarded. A violin piece ended, but instead of going to the
next piece, it turned to what  sounded like wind noises. Maybe some
creaking wood. And footsteps on a rock path.

“Is this a nature walk?” I said.

“This European orchestra sells this music. They all have interludes that
are just this.”

“Just walking and nature noises? I thought you were making a podcast
about cults.” I said, impatiently.

“They are a cult. Look at this crazy website! They’ve written all over
about how they know where to find true knowledge in ancient Norse
religion, and that it will save them from Ragnarok. They’re talking
about playing music for the gods.”

“That sounds stupid.”

“It’s insane! They said they all moved to Sweden to prepare for Ragnarok
together and that they’ve been searching for the gods. Trying to find
them with music,” he said. His eyes were so full of life. 

“Robert, you can’t be serious.”

“They are making an audio map of their search for the gods. And it
seriously sounds like they find them. Here, listen to the end.” He
reached to fast forward.

“That’s the dumbest thing I’ve ever heard.” I said, standing in my
frustration.

“You wouldn’t get it, ok? You didn’t lose what I did! You’re not a
musician, and you won’t ever be, so leave me alone. I’ve got work to
do.”

The insult stung. I left the room, tight-lipped and tight-fisted.

I left him alone for long enough that two weeks later, he was asking for
money from our parents to buy a plane ticket for a backpacking trip to
Sweden. He promised that his fifty new subscribers would create more
than enough revenue to pay them back. My  parents gave him the money
anyway.

I watched from my room as he filled a borrowed blue hiking backpack with
all manner of survival gear that seemed alien in his nimble hands. 

As he hugged Mom goodbye before Dad took him to the airport, I looked
from the top of the stairs outside my room. I was almost happy to see
his dreary presence leave the house. His tired eyes turned to me one
last time. There was something insatiable in them, like he was a fish on
a hook desperate to not let go.

“Bye, Alice,” he said. I thought about running down the stairs and
throwing myself around him to stop him from going. I didn’t.

A month passed. He didn’t call or text. He missed his flight home. My
parents were distraught. They froze in time while they waited for
updates from the Swedish authorities gathering information on his
whereabouts. All the while, I was eaten from the inside while I matched
their pallid faces of stone. No information came. It was like he was
swallowed by a mountain.

My first year of college went by in a blink online. All the virtual
dance recitals in the world of my bedroom could never match the fervor
of the studio. I longed for the more real, more authentic experience of
the studio and stage. But even more, I longed for the feeling of
spinning in the hallway while some strange instrument told a story from
the other side of the door.

After finishing my paper on choreography styles for my Dance 1 final,
the emptiness in my soul was getting the best of me. I went to my
brother’s room. It was exactly as he had left it. My parents, in states
of grief, had treated the objects in the room as if they were boulders
too heavy to lift.

I moved to his stereo and flicked it on, the last cassette tape playing
automatically. The sound of a low wind rose and fell for a few minutes.
As the tape whirred to a halt, I reached to turn it off.

I heard it then. The slight, low scraping of stone on stone. It was like
a note within a note, pitches wavering back and forth in the scraping.
The sound repulsed me, making me want to cover my ears. At the same
time, it was like nothing I had ever heard. It burrowed its way into my
ear and stuck there. There was music in the chaos of the stone. 

The tape stopped. And I knew why my brother had left in an instant. My
head was spinning. That mystery, that tone was unlike anything I had
ever heard. It called to mind caverns deep in the earth full of echoing
sound and mountains moving as though they were human. Somewhere in the
depths of my mind, I knew I had to know where that tone originated.

I looked at the tape. I traced it with some extensive internet searching
to find the website he had mentioned. Neon and pastel colors clashed in
simple HTML script that ran down the page. Nonsensical letters Google
identified as Swedish surrounded clip art of runes, amateur nature
photography, and random people posing with instruments. At the bottom of
a webpage that must have been fifty miles long, there was a picture of a
cassette tape and a link to an order form that looked like it might give
my computer malware. More importantly, it had an address for a remote
town in Sweden.

Before the sound stopped ringing in my mind, I was booking a plane
ticket to Stockholm. My parents had silently agreed to the premise of
the trip, though it seemed like they didn’t even hear anything I said. I
packed a hiking backpack I borrowed from a friend. Dad took me to the
airport in silence.

I had always wanted to visit Europe. I had dreamed of dancing on the
great stages there, the opera houses of Austria, Prague, Italy, and
elsewhere. Once I was out of Stockholm, my daydreaming quickly faded to
reality. The train crossing the mountains was headed for a town with a
name I could barely pronounce, the scribble in my pocket being my means
of communicating my destination to the ticketing agent.

It was a picturesque trip with a more picturesque destination. Modern
asphalt streets bordered cobblestone sidewalks that led up to cute
houses and shops. Grassy hills morphed into harsh stone mountains on the
horizon. Down one of the cobblestone sidewalks lay the store that had
sent the tape to my brother.

It was a quaint music store. Records filled boxes on tables, and CDs
lined shelves. A few stereos and speakers collected dust as a turntable
played soft classical music. My brother would have known the composer.
An older woman with round glasses sat behind the counter who looked up
from a book and greeted me in Swedish as I walked in. She switched
easily to English after I asked. “Welcome, dear, how can I help you?”

“Hi, my brother ordered a tape from here online. I was wondering if you
knew anything about it? The website said it shipped from here.” I set
the off-white tape from the counter on the table.

The woman’s eyes grew large behind the visage of her glasses. “That
orchestra’s nonsense recordings? I wondered who ordered that. One day I
got the email and sent it off. I never found out how they recorded so
far from a city. The conductor came to me one day and asked if I would
be willing to distribute their music. Maybe, twenty years ago? I only
ever shipped a few until this one.”

“And the orchestra? Where are they?”

“They’ve been gone for almost two decades. They lived here until one
morning when they just left. They were staying at the inn. My friend
Twila there would be able to tell you more. Tell her Catherine sent
you.”

I set off from there, my feet ringing on the cobblestones. I reached the
inn at the center of town. I entered the large oak doors of the hotel
and made my way into the warm foyer. 

A fire crackled in the corner. A dark wood counter sat in front of some
beautiful stairs to the second level that looked like they were out of a
musical. An elderly man and woman sat in the foyer, the man reading and
the woman knitting. They looked up as I walked in.

“Hello, how can I help you?” said the woman, recognizing my dress and
gear as American.

“Hi, Catherine said I could stay here for the night, and that you might
be able to help me.”

“Yes, Cat’s a good friend. What brings you to our little corner of the
world?”

“I’m here looking for my brother.”

“Your brother?”

“Yes, he was investigating some orchestra that stayed here.”

“Oh, them…I best put on some tea.”

Twila introduced me to her husband Yergen as she busied herself with tea
and something for me to eat.

The inn was empty save the three of us and a cat as we settled into the
dining room. The soup and tea that Twila made warmed me as the cool
breeze from the windows ruffled the curtains.

“Have you lived here your whole life?” I asked, trying to ease my
internal tension.

“Yes. We do not leave the valley often,” said Yergen.

“And business is good?”

Twila entered with a plate of scones and a loaf of crusty bread.
“Business was best when the orchestra stayed here. About twenty years
ago, they booked all our rooms.”

“There were forty or fifty of them?” Yergen said to his wife.

Twila nodded. “We opened up a storage room for one or two who trickled
in. They arrived gradually. They had passports from all over Europe and
America.”

“It was a zoo with instruments strewn about the hotel, practicing at all
hours of the night,” said Yergen. “That conductor of theirs, a strange
fellow.”

“That’s a picture of him over there, along with the rest of the
orchestra.” Yergen pointed to a picture on the wall. 

I walked over to it. A regular group of adults stood on the steps of the
inn wearing hiking pants and carrying backpacks. A tall, thin man with a
big nose stood in the center, wearing a suit and bowtie and holding up
his baton like a wand.

“Were they recording somewhere?” I asked.

“That was the strangest thing. There is no concert hall or studio in
this town,” said Jergen, “so we do not know what they were doing.”

Twila nodded. “They kept saying how excited they were to get new
instruments. I did not understand because they had expensive kinds
already. But they must have been right because they left them here.”

“When they disappeared?”

“Yes.”

“The police looked for them, surely?”

“Yes, but there was a rockslide that slowed them down. It happened the
night they left.”

“We’ve only ever had a few. One then and one last year,” said Yergen.

Twila’s eyes got wide. “The other was the night your brother went
missing.”

A wave of emotion came over me as the wind chilled me to my core. The
strangeness gripped my mind as despair echoed through my soul.

“My brother…” I managed to croak. “He stayed here?”

Twila and Yergen’s expressions softened. “Yes,” Nora said. “He was our
only customer while he was here. He played so beautifully on the violin
for us.”

“He hiked into the mountains for nearly three weeks before he
disappeared that night,” Yergen stood and closed the window as a slight
breeze came in. “We still have something of his. Come. I will show you.”

I followed him up the old oak stairs, creaking under us as they bore
tired weight. I placed my bag in my room and followed Yergen down the
hallway on a soft rug to the last room. He took a key from his pocket
and unlocked the door. I stepped inside.

The room smelled of old leather and fabric. Dark shapes stood piled and
stacked in the corners of the room like golems relegated to furniture
dressing. There must have been dozens of instruments. Horns, strings,
winds, even drums. A tuba clung to the dying light between the monoliths
of two upright string bass cases. A pyramid of the cases of flutes,
oboes, and picallos adorned a table. They sat in solemn silence, as if
they were waiting for someone to enter the velvety depths of their cases
and return with the gleaming treasure within.

At the foot of the bed sat a more modern case. A less-aged crown of dust
adorned it, though I didn’t need that to identify it was my brother’s.
It still bore his blue luggage tag.

As we closed the door on those dark shapes, the violin case in my hand
felt heavy. As I listened to the plodding steps of Yergen down the
stairs, I set the case on the bed and unzipped it. The violin was as
pristine as the day my brother had left. I ran my finger along the
forgotten curves of the instrument. As I plucked the loose strings, they
played notes octaves below their intended tune, clicking against the
wood. Part of me wished that I could emulate my brother, retune the
instrument, and play into the small hours of the morning. The other part
of me closed the case and stowed it on a shelf in the corner of the
room, out of sight.

Around midnight, I was lying awake, my thoughts stagnating about how to
retrace my brother’s steps, when it was like the air in the room
shifted. I looked over at my water glass on the nightstand and watched
the surface waver. My hand rested on the corner post of the bed, and
like a far off train approaching, I felt a low, growing vibration. There
was a low rumble that seeped through the cracks in the walls. It
crescendoed into a thunderous crashing like the world was coming undone.
It shook the pictures on the walls and made the whole building creak.
Under the crashing was a terrible grinding of stone sliding against
stone, and I heard what I knew was that awful symphony recording in the
rhythm and pitch, pressing its way into my brain, speaking melodies in
my ears no one should know from a mind no sane human could understand.

As soon as it began, it stopped. The silence was still as death. The
melodies echoed in my brain, entrancing me. Somehow, I drifted off to
sleep. Perhaps it was because I knew which way I had to go to follow my
brother.

The valley was green, the sky was clear, and the forecast showed no
weather events approaching when I set out the next morning. It was
summer, meaning I had extra hours of daylight, and I had three light
sources for night. I had a first aid kit just in case.

As I walked, I found myself humming a tune that felt familiar, but I
couldn’t quite place where it was from. It came in time with my
footfalls. As I ascended into the mountains, the wind joined the song
and the trees groaned in percussive creaks. It pushed me forward, even
as I gasped for air in the altitude.

I reached a fork and took the northward trail that would loop me around
the mountain, toward the face of the rockslide. I wound through fields
of slate grey boulders set at strange angles like some alien chess
pieces on an unseen board. A few unconscious steps sent gravel
skittering down the mountain, persisting until I re-entered the
dampening forest. The wind howled. As the sun reached its apex in the
grey sky, I rounded the mountain’s north face and beheld the destruction
of the night before. The path ahead disappeared. I stood at the foot of
a pile of rock and earth, hundreds of feet tall. The roots of trees lay
upside down, bare to a sky they had never seen. At random, small trails
of earth broke off and trickled down the pile, as if the earth was
breathing.

I started up the slope. Climbing over boulders to find steady ground,
each step was a plea to the earth to be strong beneath my foot. It was
slow going. When I turned to see myself one hundred feet from the
bottom, the sun had already canted forty five degrees in the sky. I kept
on, nourishing myself with food and water at regular intervals. The more
I climbed, the worse the exposure got. The wind pulled at my coat and
hair, biting at my cheeks and ears with a surprising alpine chill. My
legs burned, my hips seized up, and I felt my natural dance agility slip
away with each step. The skin and nails wore down on my fingers;
graceful hands were calloused and bleeding. The slope grew narrower. As
I crested another boulder, I cursed my stupidity and my hubris. The
mountain’s exposed face rose before me, a solid wall of rock, impassable
to me.

I turned back to look over the remains of the mountainside. Beyond its
base to the south lay the town. It sat like a model on a table, far out
of reach.

Rock scraped on rock behind me, the tape’s ending made manifest. It made
all the flesh on my body go cold. Wind like breath blew on the back of
my neck. It whistled a pitch I couldn’t match. I turned back to the
vertical grey face, waiting to see if it might collapse on top of me.  A
seam of white stone ran and branched like a river. That river fell away
into an opening between two giant chunks of stone protruding from the
mountain. Beyond the entrance, blackness swallowed the sunlight. The
cave was large enough to shim in sideways. With a deep breath and the
wind in my ears, I turned on my flashlight and entered the mouth of the
mountain. 

My progress was slow. I didn’t realize the mental shift needed to pass
from the open daylight of the world into the pitch black gullet of the
mountain. I squeezed through rocks two inches on either side of my head
or scrambled on hands and knees under a constricting ceiling. The path
wound downward. The whistling always accompanied me.

The ceiling ducked again. I crawled over the rough stone, thankful for
my tough pants and boots. I stood on the other side and looked in front
of me. It was pitch black. Confused, I checked my light. It shone on my
calloused hands. I reached forward into blackness and didn’t feel the
wall or the ceiling. I inched my foot forward slightly.

I shrieked as the sandy stone edge gave way under the weight. The sound
died in the void ahead as I flailed to regain my balance. My heart
thundered in my ribcage as I looked down over the edge that showed no
signs of a bottom. As I stood and my breath quieted, that void produced
no sound. Absolute silence made my ears ring. I felt very small.

Glancing around for a way forward, I saw that the ledge I was on
extended to the right around a corner. I crept along the edge, doing my
best to lean back against the wall to steady myself. It was slow, but
once I rounded the corner, the ledge widened out ahead.

Though I now had about three feet of space to work with, I still clung
to the wall with everything I had, swollen fingers searching for
handholds. I turned another corner. The wall fell away. The path
widened, and my light caught on something made of wood.

My flashlight followed nonsensical angles of roughshot pine beams
upward. The structure I was staring at was enormous. Only when I was
pointing my flashlight vertically did I see that the ceiling was beyond
the reach of the light. I searched to the edges of the massive stone
ledge upon which it sat. It was deeply silent save the creaking of wood.
The base was wide, extending upward in what seemed like stacks of
elongated wood boxes with small openings in them no one could fit
through that faced the chasm.

There was a break in the boxes. A bench made of wood had cabinets with
switches and buttons in front of it. The wood floor underneath was
uneven. The wood was older than any of the boxes, like it might split if
I touched it. The boxes continued after it. I stood before the creaking
monoliths and observed the wood grain of trees that didn’t know light.

Something moved behind me. Stone scraped on stone. I turned, my breath
catching in my throat. All I saw was the path continuing forward. The
stone beneath my feet turned smoothed, almost polished. The dark abyss
still cried its appetite to my left. As I turned the corner, the sound
of wood creaking was gone. My ears rang. 

The stone steps I found ahead descended in perfect dimension. I fixated
so fully on them as they took me down and down that when the path
flattened out, I ran into a wall. I jumped at the impact and fumbled
with my flashlight. My fingers slipped against the smooth metal as I
watched the light dance in the abyss. Upon regaining control and
pointing it forward, my blood ran cold. I hadn’t run into a wall.

The statue before me had skin like polished grey marble, contrasting the
sandstone brown and slate grey of the cave. It was a woman, with
clothing cut from an obscure century. My mind ran from Greek myth to
Chinese tombs in an attempt to identify it. The woman’s face held a
plain expression, so mundane for such accurate depiction.

The pose was active, as if it were preparing to climb the stairs. Behind
the feet were two streaks of ground stone like chalk dust. The sculpture
had been slid to this location from deeper in the cave. As my light
followed the streaks on the ground, it landed on the feet of another
statue, a man also facing the stairs. As the light hit the face, I saw
another behind it. And another. And another. And another.

The rock tier I stood upon curved in an arc into the darkness. It held a
row of statues, the first three facing the stairs, the rest looking down
upon another terrace of statues. They continued beyond the reach of my
beam, like marble soldiers at attention, facing the abyss. I started
down the stairs to the next level, now enraptured to know what they all
stood to bear witness too.

I descended past eight levels of statues before reaching a level floor,
a section of stone twenty feet wide before it dropped into the abyss. As
I turned from my vantage point, I could see each individual face of the
statues. Their eyes fixed on the spot I stood.

The statues each held something in their right hand. I don’t even know
how to describe the intricate yet natural look of whatever they held.
They were shapes too strange to make sense of.

In the darkness to my left, stone scraped on stone. I turned my beam
towards it. Nothing there. My heart pounded in my ears. I panned my
light further to my left, trying to assure myself it was my imagination.

A second scrape rattled my teeth. It did not stop as my flashlight
continued its frantic search, but got louder as it made its way towards
me. My heart was hammering in my chest in time to the steady rhythm of
stone on stone, the same sound echoing in my mind from the tape. I tried
to step backward but tripped. Scrambling around to face the coming
noise, I shielded myself with my flashlight and scooted backwards toward
the steps.

A statue was slowly walking towards me. His feet slid across the stone
in a marchstep, one arm stretched out to the side, beckoning the other
statues to look.  I watched their faces follow my scramble backwards at
the edges of my light. Stone heads turning on stone shoulders scratched
a new note that harmonized with the step of the statue. He was almost
upon me.

The statue stopped. He turned to the abyss. He bowed. He turned to the
rest of the statues. His other hand raised. It held a short length of
thin stone.

At the raise of his hands, I heard the shift of every figure behind me
as they moved. They raised the objects in their hands, some to their
shoulders, some to their faces. Their eyes fixed on the statue at the
front. I knew the face. The conductor. He raised his baton.

A long scraping sound filled the cavern. The baton rose and fell, and a
different sound, like low wind through trees joined the chord. It fell
again, and a terrible ticking of stone on stone set forth the tempo of
the stone orchestra at a hectic pace.

My flashlight illuminated them. A section blew into tubes with multiple
ends and impossible angles. A man with a needle of stone dragged it over
a plate of marble, creating a high scream. Percussionists threw stones
against a great rock face in booming succession. It all came together,
louder, more frantic, the statues wavering from side to side in unison
as the incomprehensible sound grew louder and louder until it felt like
my head might explode. Deep tones shook the cavern, and I knew whatever
godforsaken instrument I had passed previously had started its melodic
line by the rushing of wind and the creaking of wood that followed it.

My hand felt behind me across the smooth floor for the stairs as my mind
came undone at the volume of the music. Something in it compelled me to
run, while a new scrape lulled me to sleep, the high screech brought
tears to my eyes, the booming percussion forced a laugh from my throat,
and the driving rhythm showed me the steps to a dance.

Then something, not from the orchestra, but from the abyss, joined the
song. Perhaps a laugh, perhaps a cry, perhaps a roar, it was the loudest
sound that I had ever heard.

My flashlight found the edge of the platform. The world shook around me,
my fears of the cave collapsing causing my mind to refocus on survival.
As the flashlight quaked between the grey stone and black abyss,
something moved in the searing darkness. Colossal, gargantuan, swirling
in the blackness sent a brush of wind across me, damp air caressing my
skin. I was still at the movement. Four pillars of stone twice my height
rose over the edge and curled with great thunderous cracks to grasp the
stone lip. I scrambled backwards, falling on the base of the stairs. 
The four giant fingers ground against stone as the palm came into view
behind them, a massive chunk of smooth stone. The tips looked like horse
hooves. The music lulled in intensity, the driving rhythm of stone drums
ceasing. The groping hand unfurled. From the gentle, smooth precision, I
thought it was offering me to dance.

From behind me, a melodic line started in the orchestra. It made my
heart leap. Its strange, scraping tune made my feet itch to perform
unknown steps, to drop the flashlight and run into the darkness,
trusting my feet would find stone below every turn and stride. 

I turned to the source of the melody, enraptured by its tune. In the
middle of the orchestra, one figure moved a bow over something that
resembled a five stringed violin made of stone in passionate strokes,
movements growing quicker, more frantic.

His face was stone, yet I could see my brother’s brow knit in
concentration, his eyes crazed with the light of the cave. His figure
rocked and shook as if the stone shell might break from his skin. Faster
and faster he bowed, faster he shook, the orchestra holding for the
masterful solo of screeching stone.

All at once, the melody burst back into the full orchestra, and I lost
all desire to dance. As the percussion again started its frantic beat, I
had only one instinct: run. I took one look back at my brother and then
turned away.

I scrambled up the steps. A giant thunderclap sounded behind me as the
mountain shook. The hand had missed crushing me by a few feet and
cracked the stairs below me. I sprinted up the rest of them, then turned
to shuffle my way along the thin ledge, my hand clawing the rock to keep
my light steady on the path in front of me. The music grew louder, and
wind spiraled up from the cavern like a thunderstorm. Great crashes
echoed behind me, as the roaring from deep in the mountain continued.

I ran past the wooden structure which blared chords I could not fathom.
The female statue sat at the keyboard as two other statues worked the
bellows of the enormous organ. Each note filled me with a primal fear
that made my bones feel cold and brittle. It pushed me onward, the wind
in the cavern still growing. I climbed the path back toward the
entrance. When I found the tunnel, small stones crumbled below my feet,
threatening to carry me over the edge. The music was at its climax, the
loudest and most roiling peak it could reach. The stone hand slammed
against the walkway behind me, shaking the mountain. Deep and unknown

the chasm, illuminating dark churning shapes moving in patterns in the
depths. Great cries joined the music, calling me to come dance with
them.

I thought of my brother. I thought of the cold smooth skin. I thought of
the frantic spasms. I turned and stumbled over rivers of gravel out of
the cave, the music gradually fading as I cut my hands on the rough
walls. The light up ahead called to me as I grew closer. The mountain
was shifting around me. Its entire weight need only move a few
centimeters and my body would lie buried and forgotten to the world. My
legs pushed faster.

I burst from the cave into the night air and light of a full moon.
Behind me, the crack I had entered through shifted with a great groan
like that from the cavern, stone sliding on stone, and closed. The
cavern was sealed. My brother was left in the heart of the mountain.

As I turned to survey the world from my perch, my ears recoiled in the
silence. Nothing but the slight wind touched them. It was deafening.

Silence persisted on the journey down the mountain and back into town.
It followed me on the plane back to America. It tagged along as I packed
up and went to college the next semester.

The years pass, but visions of my brother in the heart of the mountain
still haunt me, playing a five stringed instrument in some strange
orchestra for beings that dance in deep darkness. The music still echoes
in my ears. In the silence I hear it. In the darkness I see it. The hall
of the mountain god.
