I shouldn’t be telling you this. 

But I’ll be honest—it’s not for noble intentions. 

This line is just too long, and my thinking is if more people know
what’s going on, the less likely they’ll show up here. Less people,
shorter line. Shorter line, higher chance of getting my haul. 

Where am I?

It’s past midnight, and I’m in a parking lot behind some foreclosed
store on Melrose (a shopping district in LA, for those who don’t live
here). I’m a reseller—I schlep to all corners of the city to find
designer pieces I can resell on all my marketplace apps. It’s an honest
living. 

Well, dishonest living if you deal in designer knock-offs. Which I
don’t, it’s not good money. 

The good money, the real money, are from pop ups like these. 

But this isn’t just any pop up. 

This is a Cat°9 pop up. 

Never heard of them? That’s okay—no one else really has either, and
that’s by design. They have no boutiques, not even a website. In the
reseller community, Cat°9 is a bit of a black box. 

Even securing a ticket for one of their pop ups can be a ruse. 

They’ll sometimes drop fake addresses to weed out the casual buyers.
Knowing the real address for their pop-ups is a reseller mark of honor. 

Why all the obsession, why all the secrecy? 

I want you to imagine an avant goth brand like Chrome Hearts or Rick
Owens—then dial that to eleven. Cat°9 isn’t just some edgy, underground
brand with “opium” pieces. If you’re truly a Cathead, like I am, you see
that Randy Park is a twisted genius. I’ll drop some RP lore for you. 

The dude was an iconoclastic skater from the OG Dogtown days, who
swapped a skateboard for a sewing table and became a cult fashion
designer. The brand gained its notoriety after this famous actor in the
90s OD’d on an 8-ball wearing one of his signature gothy pieces. 

But what really makes these items fetch for thousands, and why we’re all
here—is the secret sauce. Rumor is that RP bleeds his own blood into
every piece he makes, for every season. 

And I’ll let you in on a secret, it’s not his blood. 

“Let’s make a deal, we’ll split the haul if we’re the last two
standing.”

I turn around to see a lanky guy with an oversized Supreme hoodie
puddling around his elbows. His rat-like face is tucked under a camo
hat, but I’ve seen him around at a few fleas in la. 

“Thanks, but I’m good, bro,” I say, putting him down not so gently. 

You hear trades like this all the time in line. But it’s against the
rules. 

SCREEEEEEEEEEEEEECH! 

I swivel my head to the direction of the sound, a roll up door being
hoisted by chains. 

Two freakishly tall Cat°9 models emerge out of the lightless store,
bound in the brand’s signature draconian garb. Both are men, with shaved
eyebrows and faces like molded clay. 

Immediately, the in-line chatter dies, replaced with a focused quiet. 

The two giants stalk down the line, checking each of our ticket
numbers. 

As I pull out my phone, I’m embarrassed to see my hands already
trembling. I pray that the others in line can’t see this. Eventually,
one of the models scans my phone with flinty eyes. 

Up close, these guys appear even taller—like walking, leafless trees. 

My ticket number is #21 (not revealing my actual number, of course). 

The models move wordlessly down the line, and I can see the coiled panic
on a few of the attendee’s faces. It’s easy to spot the newcomers; the
way they read the red, jaggedly-stitched rules on the model’s leather
jackets. Yes, you read that right. There are rules, but only two:

“WINNER MOVES UP”

“LOSER MOVES BACK”

Clap. Clap. Clap. Clap. Clap. Clap. Clap. Clap. 

Our eyes glue to the roll up door, where two female Cat°9 models step
out of the darkness. 

Ensnared on their faces are RP’s “Scold’s Bridle Mask” (easily retails
at $899), a beautifully designed headpiece that looks like a face stuck
inside a bear trap. A web of chains droop from their bony shoulders as
two flip scorecards clap against their skin, “self-paddling”
intentionally. 

I squint at the pair of scorecards, which each display a ticket
number—like a macabre raffle. 

#4 and #35. 

Neither of the chosen numbers are mine. 

The models strut down the pop up line like a runway, their faces frozen
in zen-like repose, skin reddening with every successive clap. Each of
them carry a matte black Cat°9 shopping bag. 

A newcomer in front of me turns, his eyebrows touching with confusion: 

“What’s in the bags?” he asks, almost innocently. 

“Merch,” I answer, simply. 

We all watch as the models gracefully place the two bags in the parking
lot. Then, in synchronized movement, they resume their cat walk until
they re-enter the foreclosed store. 

SCREEEEEEEEEEEEEECH!

The roll up door shuts them inside, with the two male models remaining
outside like sentries. 

I feel my veins surge with adrenaline.

#4 steps out of line, enviously the closest toward the front.  

I twist my head to see #35, much further down the line from me, step
out. 

The two “called” attendees approach the bags left in the parking lot. 

One of the Cat°9 models steps in between them in the way a referee in a
boxing match would. 

With guillotine swiftness, the model raises his arm—then “chops” it
down, signaling the start. 

Go. 

The air crackles with electricity as the two attendees bend down and
frantically rip open their respective bags, unboxing and tossing Cat°9
black wrapping paper into the chilly night air. 

#35 pulls out RP’s “The Executioner Boot” (retailing at $3k), a pair of
menacing platform boots with a reinforced grill at the tip like you’d
see on an old train. We can’t help but awe at it. 

But the boots are two sizes too small for him; he squirms on the
ground—trying to shove his large foot into the boot like the ugly
stepsister jamming her warted feet into the glass slipper. 

I look over at #4, his face paling at his own bounty, or lack thereof.
He holds up “The Astor” opera gloves (retails at $1.5k), a pair of lamb
leather gloves that stretch out beyond the elbows like Batman’s. He
flexes his fingers in them, cording his body into a boxer’s stance. 

My eyes jump back to #35, who in a stroke of desperation, slips his
hands into The Executioner’s like boxing gloves. Then, he adopts a
fighting stance, mirroring #4. 

They approach each other awkwardly, and the first punches are
thrown—none landing. 

I can see it on the newcomer’s face in front of me, it’s the same look I
had the first time. Can this be real? Is anyone going to stop them? But
I’ve learned never to interfere, not unless you want the haul. I’ve seen
others in the past try, only to get a lifetime ban from future pop-ups. 

#4 connects a blow to #35’s face. Blood fountains down his torso. 

But somehow, #35 manages to keep standing—guarding his face with the
Executioner’s. 

When he sees an opportunity, #35 makes a valiant swing for #4’s ribs.  

And this time, the boots connect, dropping #4 to the ground, winded. 

#35 is quick to pounce, but before he can deploy a barrage of locomotive
punches…

…a COP CAR cruises past, on a customary late night patrol. 

The fighters immediately see it, and disperse from each other—folding
back into line in the same fashion as a stage being cleared after a
scene finishes in a play. The models stand still. 

The cop car encircles the outer perimeter of the open parking lot,
observing us. 

No one moves a muscle. To the officers, this is just any amusing
overnight pop up line. 

The two fighters discreetly face away from the watchful eyes of the
police, hiding their injuries. 

But once the coast is clear, #35 and #4 return to their match. I can’t
imagine how #35 must be feeling, having to start all over again. They
move slower, they’re exhausted. 

You can see the sweat glisten off their faces from the streetlights.
Eventually, #35 makes a daring move—charging full steam ahead at #4 and
tackling him to the hard cement. 

The violence unfolds so fast, you almost blink it away. 

#35 pummels #4 with punk-like ferocity. My ears fill with the wet sounds
of the poor guy’s face becoming nothing more than a smear under his
luxury platform boots. 

A horrible silence falls. 

Winner. 

When #35 sweatily removes his Executioner’s, the model takes it from him
and inspects it; all the new dents, tears, and bloody scratches from the
brawl. His eyes narrow at the underside. 

The model plucks out an errant tooth from #4 that ended up wedged in
between two grooves in the heel. Then, he plucks it out and wedges it
into a different groove. Pleased.

It’s the same deal with #4. The other model removes The Astor gloves
from his unconscious hands, inspecting all of the new “imperfections”
with an alien like remove.   

I hear a ripple of groans as #35 moves four spots ahead in line, a look
of triumph on his face. 

It’s at this moment that a couple of the attendees decide this isn’t
worth it, and bounce. 

But not many leave. 

Because we all want the same thing, it’s why we’re all here. 

The haul. 

SCREEEEEEEEEEEEEECH!

The roll up door. Then, that taunting sound. Clap. Clap. Clap. Clap.
Clap. 

I can make out one of the numbers on the model’s scorecards from here. 

#21. 

My number. 

Then another: #22

My eyes screw shut at the realization. 

“We can still make a deal,” the lanky reseller whispers from behind me. 

I face him, a rigid, hard-fought determination on his face. 

“#22?” I inquire, almost pitying. 

“My lucky number,” he confesses with a bitter smile. 

I feel the expectant gaze from the others in line as we step toward our
given Cat°9 bags on the ground. My eyes flick to #4’s slug trail of
blood on the pavement. Could I be next? 

The model stands in between us, a black tower of a person. 

After what feels like an eternity, he drawbridges his arm down—signaling
the start of the fight. 

“#22” is quick to his knees unpacking his bag, unsheathing wrapping
paper with the dexterity of the ones who wrapped it. He must have a
retail background, I think to myself. 

Stop. 

It’s not smart to view anyone in line as anything more than their ticket
number. 

They’re either in front of you, or behind you. That’s it. 

I push away these thoughts as I tear open my bag. Cloaked in black Cat°9
wrapping paper, I pull out a heavy, folded garment. “The Heretic” temple
flight jacket (retails at $5k). It’s a beauty, festooned with biker
leather straps and zippers—resembling those looney bin straightjackets. 

But my heart sinks when I realize it literally is a straightjacket. 

As I slide my arms into its contorted sleeves, fear swells in my body. 

How am I supposed to fight with my literal arms tied behind my back? 

I look up to see #22 unfolding his item—which is immediately met with
oo’s and aww’s. 

He wasn’t kidding about that lucky number, he was gifted “The Iron
Apega” tecsweat, the meanest looking thing that’s probably ever spun out
of a sewing machine (and fittingly priced at $10k). I swallow with
complete dismay at its thorned shoulder pads and urchin-like spikes. 

We square up, and #22’s eyes have turned a shade darker, one of
animalistic focus. 

“Hey, I want to make that deal—I’ll let you hit me, then I’ll hit you,”
I bargain. 

“Deal’s off the table,” he says, a sick grin scything his face.  

He approaches me, sizing up all of my weak spots. Then, the first spiked
punch is thrown. 

I duck, his fist only meeting air. 

Peals of dark laughter spill from the line as they watch me pathetically
scamper away. 

I need to come up with a plan, fast. Think, think. 

My eyes dart around—we’re in an open parking lot but yet it feels
claustrophobic. 

#22’s footsteps are only a few breaths behind me. I turn around to see
him, his jaw hangs low like a junkyard dog. But he looks at “The
Heretic”, not me—his eyes only dollar signs now. 

I think of #35. You have to get creative here. 

Maybe there’s something in these zippers, or pockets?

Freeing one hand from the jacket, I eagerly unzip all the squares on The
Heretic. 

But I come up with nothing, just my fingernails scraping empty leather. 

#22 manages to grab one of the loose straps on my jacket, pulling me
in. 

My eyes widen at the bouquet of spikes on The Iron Apega. They look even
sharper up close. 

Suddenly, perverse inspiration strikes. 

Like a rabbit trapped in a snare, I unspool myself from half of The
Heretic, leaving #22 holding nothing more than well-stitched leather.
Then, I twirl the fabric around my arm like you would with a wet towel.
With the loose leather straps from my jacket—it’s a makeshift whip. 

Fitting, too. 

Cat°9’s logo is a nineteenth century woodcut of a naval flogging with a
“cat o’nine” whip. 

I swiftly start whipping the tendrils of buckles and straps on #22,
belting his skin painfully. 

He covers his face, his hands bruising and breaking from the metal ends.
I soon see blood. 

Then, my mind simply blacks out. 

What comes over me isn’t me, but someone else—someone I’m scared to say
exists in me. 

#22’s screams ring out, but I don’t hear them. Only the sound of my own
thundering heart. 

Winner. 

I walk 22 spots ahead in line, a profound feeling washing over me. It’s
not relief, or adrenaline—it’s euphoria. The rush of having earned your
spot in line. It’s here you begin to see RP’s vision. 

It’s not his own blood, sweat, and tears in his pieces. 

It’s his Cathead’s. 

What time is it? 

I don’t even know anymore—or even care. 

The front of line euphoria dulls with each passing bout. I’ve returned
to the middle of the line. 

I rub my eyes, it can’t be much longer. My body is screaming for sleep,
but I’m too awake.  

It’s amazing what people are able to withstand. I gaze at a few of the
newcomers still left. The shock on their faces has worn off, now each
ensuing fight is met with an ambivalent shrug. 

SCREEEEEEEEEEEEEECH!

Clap. Clap. Clap. Clap. Clap. Clap. 

My face twitches at the sight of my number. 

I peel out of line, and head towards my bag on the ground, routinely. 

But when I turn my head at my opponent, my pace slows. 

It’s #35. 

There’s a hungry, deadened look in his eyes. 

The model stands in between us with runway coldness. He ominously lowers
his arm down. 

Go. 

We crouch down and start opening our bags, but our movements are
sluggish—the agonies of the night finally catching up to us. I can’t
help but glance in his direction, spying his haul. 

In the pre-dawn light, #35 unpacks a black jeweler’s box. He delicately
pops the lid to reveal a set of luxury rings: a “Poacher Elephant Tusk”
ring ($3k), a “Pagan” ring ($4k), and a “Boiler” ring ($3k). He slips
all the rings onto his knuckles, turning his hand into a retail claw of
death. 

Now, onto my haul. 

I remove the wrapping paper, and my heart leaps at the size of the box. 

What luck!

Hope floods through my body. Maybe it’s some type of jacket spiked with
barbed wire, or a coat with mousetraps? It’s at this moment that I pray
for Park to deliver on his warped genius.  

But when I take off the lid, my face deflates. It’s just a smaller box. 

I feel the tug of panic. It’s okay, maybe it’s some type of
post-apocalyptic metal hat, or a pair of gloves studded with tiny animal
bones. My breath quickens as I remove the lid. Please, Park.

But it’s an even smaller box. 

My mouth dries, I stare at it like I expect it to talk. 

It’s the size of a jeweler’s box. My hands tremble as I raise to inspect
it. Oh, I see. Maybe this is a jeweler’s match? Do I get my own ring
set, like #35? I clasp the lid of the smaller box. 

Then, I slowly remove the lid—in the same way you don’t let a captured
cricket escape from the palm of your hand. I feel the heat of stares
from the remaining attendees in line, rapt at this. 

I close my eyes, mouthing a silent prayer. 

Don’t fail me now, RP. 

When I look down, my whole face slacks. 

Nestled like a pearl in an oyster, is an item that retails for $1.2k. 

A diamond safety pin.
