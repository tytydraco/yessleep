A few months ago, my twin brother Jonah called to tell me that our
father had passed away. He and I were never close. Our childhood had
been anything but happy. Our mother died in a car accident when we were
young, and after that, we were left with a silent, alcoholic father who
barely felt like a parent at all.

When I was sixteen, a brutal argument with him pushed me over the edge.
I left home, left Ravensville, and started a new life in the city. Ten
years passed, and I never once came back. Until a few months ago.

Jonah picked me up at the almost-empty bus station in his old car. On
our way to the cemetery, we didn’t speak at all, except when he reminded
me not to smoke in the car.

He had been handling the funeral arrangements alone all morning. I’d
missed the morning train and ended up taking a bus that moved slower
than a damn tortoise. I missed the funeral. So he accompanied me to pay
our last respects to our father, just the two of us.

We looked so much alike that it was almost impossible to tell us apart.
The only real difference was the faint scar running down one side of
Jonah’s sharp face. I had also grown some facial hair over the years,
which made the resemblance less obvious now, but beneath it, we still
had the same pale skin, the same straight nose, and the same piercing
light brown eyes we inherited from our mother.

The old house still looked the way it had the night I left it ten years
earlier. Nothing much had changed. Except for the small garden in the
front yard, which had disappeared after our mother’s death, everything
else was pretty much the same.

Jonah parked in front of the garage and immediately hopped out of the
car to light a cigarette. He then walked up the steps, rummaging through
his pocket for the key. I followed him without a word. He went straight
to his old bedroom, leaving me alone in the living room with the echoes
of the past lingering in every corner.

“Whiskey?” he suddenly called out as he came back carrying two small
glasses, setting them down on the table.

“Thanks,” I muttered quietly.

“I’ve got some wine stashed in the attic too, if you’re into that,” he
said as he sank into a large recliner in the corner.

“So… when can I see your family?” I asked him.

He stopped halfway through lighting another cigarette. The question had
caught him completely off guard.

“Why?”

“Why not?” I doubled down, taking a seat in the other corner of the
living room, across from him. “Unless they don’t even know I exist, I
see no reason why I shouldn’t be introduced to them while I’m in town.”

“They’re aware I have an estranged brother who lives in the city and
that’s pretty much it.”

“So?” I pressed.

He lowered the lighter and stared at me for another second. Then he let
out a short, humorless laugh and finally lit the cigarette, exhaling a
thick plume of smoke that curled lazily toward the ceiling.

“Yeah, sure. Why not. We’ll see.”

There was something strange about the way he said it. Not quite
reluctance. Not quite indifference, either. I couldn’t tell which
bothered me more.

“I’m planning on staying here for a week or so,” I said. “At least until
we’re done with Dad’s stuff and whatnot. Then I’ll be out of your hair.”

He took another drag and looked away. “You’re not in my hair. Never
were.”

Ten years ago, this house was the only family I knew. Now Jonah had a
wife, two children, and a life that had moved on without me. I wondered
how much of it had ever included me.

“How ’bout tomorrow we go camping in the woods again, like we used to?”
he asked suddenly. “You know, after everything settles down. Before you
return to the city.”

I looked at him, bewildered. I hadn’t expected that at all. I froze with
my glass halfway to my mouth.

“What for?” I asked, frowning. “To relive our blissful family memories?”

“You remember how much dad loved taking us out there when we were
little,” he said, ignoring me.

I swallowed hard. “Bad idea,” I said quickly. “We never got along. We
always fought whenever we were left alone together. The only reason we
didn’t kill each other back then was because Dad was around.”

“We’re not kids anymore,” he said. “Besides, I’ve been meaning to do
something like that for a while. You can’t always find the time to be
alone with your own thoughts once you have a family to take care of.”

I took another sip of whiskey and slowly shook my head.

“Just one night.” He leaned toward the open window beside him and
flicked some ash outside. “We’ll just crash by the lake and head back
the next morning.”

I narrowed my eyes at him before shaking my head again. “Uhm, I don’t
know, man…”

He set his empty glass on the table, got up from the recliner, and
started walking away.

“Do it for Dad, then.” His voice was barely audible.

“Why?” I asked. “Did Dad ever tell you to ask me to do this if I ever
came back?”

He stopped in the doorway. One hand rested against the frame as he
hesitated. I held my breath, waiting. Then, slowly, he turned ever so
slightly without looking back at me.

“No. I just thought it would be cool.”

I sighed and lit a cigarette. “Just one night.”

“You’ll let me pick the campsite,” he said, “and you’ll do exactly what
I say while we’re out there.”

“Fine.”

I grinned. Maybe I had been too pessimistic all this time. Maybe our
relationship wasn’t too damaged to repair. Maybe it was still
salvageable after all.

The next morning, Jonah and I found ourselves trudging through the
forest, following a narrow, winding trail beneath towering pine trees.
Branches snapped and crunched under our boots with every step. The
forest felt calm and strangely quiet beneath the thin veil of mist still
hanging in the air.

After a few hours, we reached a grassy clearing surrounded by shrubs,
and Jonah decided we should rest there for a while.

The sun already hung high overhead as we circled around a massive tree
whose branches crouched so low they almost touched the ground, its long
leaves fluttering in the cool breeze. Beyond it, we came upon a small
spit of grassy clearing crammed with blooming dandelions, dried leaves,
and scattered twigs.

I was about to take some pictures of the dandelions when the wind
suddenly changed course, carrying with it a suffocating smell of rot. It
hit me so suddenly that I stopped in my tracks.

“What the hell is this smell?” I grunted, instantly pulling the collar
of my shirt over my face.

But Jonah had already stopped walking. He stood a few yards ahead of me,
staring down at something near the edge of the clearing. He didn’t say
anything. He just stood there, completely still.

I lowered my shirt and jogged toward him, and my stomach twisted when I
saw what had caught his attention. A shriveled-looking deer carcass lay
on its side beneath a huge boulder, its body strangely sunken beneath
the hide, which clung tightly to its withered frame.

“Must have been lying out in the open for weeks,” I muttered, wincing at
its head. The flesh around its face had begun to break down, and a faint
vapor rose from the carcass in the heat. But there were no obvious signs
of a predator attack. Apart from its oddly deflated appearance, the rest
of its body was mostly intact.

I frowned, holding my breath as I walked closer. Once I was beside him,
I followed his gaze and finally saw what he was honing in on.

There was a small, gaping hole in the top of the deer’s skull, almost
perfectly circular. The bone around it was remarkably undisturbed, with
no cracks or splintering anywhere along the edges. Apart from the hole,
there were no other obvious injuries on the animal. I stared at it for a
moment, trying to make sense of what I was seeing.

“Is that a bullet hole?”

He didn’t answer immediately. He just stood there, staring at it. His
expression had gone blank, but not with confusion. It was as if he were
looking at something he had seen before, something he wished he hadn’t.

He looked at me. “You ever seen a bullet hole this clean?” he said.

“What do you mean?”

He looked back at the hole, but instead of answering me, he quickly
eased his backpack off his shoulder and unzipped one of the smaller
compartments. He reached inside and pulled out a small pistol. I stared
at it, completely caught off guard.

“I’m sorry, are we going into battle or something?” I asked.

He ignored me. He checked the gun briefly, making sure the magazine was
seated before looking toward the trees. Then he raised it and slowly
scanned the surrounding woods, keeping the muzzle pointed ahead of him.

“In a forest this old, brother, you never know what you might run into.”

“What’s that supposed to mean?” I followed his gaze, bewildered.

“Better to let whatever’s in the forest know we’re armed,” he said, “and
that we’re not a pair of brainless idiots who came out here unprepared.”

I stared at him for a moment before spitting onto the ground, trying to
steady my racing heartbeat.

“So what killed the poor thing?” I asked again, nodding toward the dead
deer.

There was something deeply wrong about the way it had been left. There
was no obvious gunshot wound anywhere else on its body, no torn flesh,
no sign that a predator had fed on it. Its hide was almost completely
intact.

“And there’s something else,” Jonah said. He crouched beside the carcass
and pointed toward the wound. “There’s no blood.”

“Jesus.”

“Blood, organs, everything… it’s all gone.”

“Yeah, but what did this?”

He didn’t answer. Instead, he began scanning the surrounding woods, his
eyes moving from the deer to the trees and then deeper into the forest.

“Let’s keep going,” he said finally. “Stay close. Don’t wander off.” He
stood straight and started toward the treeline without another word,
leaving me staring after him for a moment.

I decided not to argue with him anymore and pushed myself to keep pace,
even though my feet were aching so badly they felt as if they were about
to burst through my shoes.

We eventually reached the small river, and I told Jonah I needed to stop
walking for a while. Otherwise, I felt like my feet were going to come
right off my legs. I crouched at the water’s edge and splashed the cold
water over my face and neck, washing away the sweat and grime that had
accumulated during the hike.

“Maybe we should set up camp here,” I suggested, glancing at him. “We’ve
wandered deep enough into the woods anyway.”

He just nodded silently.

We found a relatively clear patch beneath the trees and began pitching
the tent. As I pulled the gear from my backpack, I noticed the sky above
the canopy had suddenly darkened. Thick clouds were rolling across the
sky, swallowing the last traces of daylight and casting the forest into
a deep, murky gloom. The woods seemed to change with them. Darkness
slowly spread through the woods like a heavy black blanket. Wind
hammered against the tent while we lay there listening in silence.

Sometime close to midnight, I woke up. A warm golden glow flickered
through the fabric of the tent. I sat up and saw Jonah sitting beneath a
large oak tree nearby, feeding a crackling fire with dry branches we had
gathered earlier. The forest had gone eerily silent.

“Hey, Sleeping Beauty,” he called out after noticing I was awake.
“Hungry? I made dinner.”

“I knew you weren’t one to waste a dead deer.” I yawned loudly.

“I don’t remember you ever being this unfunny,” he muttered, spitting
into the dirt.

“What time is it?” I asked, stretching before crawling out toward the
fire. “Man, this feels good.” I rubbed my hands together near the
flames.

“Twenty-three to midnight.” He handed me a can of beer and a steaming
bowl of soup.

“We were out that long?”

“You were. Snored like a dying moose. I barely got any sleep.” He shook
his head.

“Sorry, man. Haven’t slept that deep in ages.” I slurped the soup
greedily with my eyes closed, savoring the warmth spreading through my
body.

“What’ve you been doing in the city, anyway?” he asked as he cracked
open a beer.

“Oh, you know… stuff. I pick up odd jobs here and there. Got bills to
pay and two hamsters to feed.”

“Good for you.” He snickered.

I took a sip of beer and let out a long sigh. “I used to love it when he
took us camping like this,” I said quietly. “I’d always beg him to stay
out longer.”

He nodded slowly. “He told me once that when he was much younger, he
used to go camping and fishing alone for days.”

“Dude had guts. No electricity, no phones, no GPS. What if he got hurt
out here?”

“But I remember him telling me once that, during one of his solo trips
on the north side of the lake, close to that old ghost town, he saw
something. Something that scared the hell outta him.”

“Oh yeah?” I frowned. “I don’t remember him ever telling me that. What’d
he see?”

“He didn’t say. But whatever he’d seen scared him so badly that he never
went near the north woods again.” His expression darkened slightly.

I laughed and shook my head. “Man, Dad used to make up the creepiest
stories just to freak us out.”

“Maybe. But he said even the animals avoid that place. Whatever’s out
there, it’s bad. We shouldn’t go near it.”

The image of the dead deer flashed through my mind again as I rummaged
through my jacket pockets.

He tossed his empty beer can into a plastic bag before stretching with a
yawn. “I’m gonna crash. I’m wiped.”

“You don’t wanna hang out a little longer?” I asked while pulling a pack
of cigarettes from my jacket. “Share a smoke?”

“No,” he mumbled curtly.

“Well, I could use some more ghost stories for a change.”

“Very funny,” he grunted from inside the tent, his voice muffled by the
canvas. He sounded deliberately unimpressed, though I could hear the
faintest hint of amusement beneath his flat response.

Around two in the morning, I woke up half leaning against the oak tree.
The forest was dark and eerily silent. Then I heard distant rustling and
soft crackling, like something moving through the undergrowth. It took
me a few seconds to realize the sound wasn’t coming from the ground. It
was coming from somewhere above me, up in the trees. I looked up
immediately, and my blood froze. Faint flashes of white moved through
the branches. For a moment, I just sat there, half paralyzed with fear,
trying to make sense of what I was seeing.

Then I heard Jonah mumbling in his sleep inside the tent, and the sound
snapped me out of my thoughts. I quickly slipped back inside and found
him still fast asleep. I gently shook him awake. He opened his eyes and
immediately frowned at the sight of me hovering above him. I could feel
how tense my own face was. My breathing was quick and shallow, and I
struggled to keep my hands steady.

“What’s going on?” he whispered groggily. “Why are you…”

“Shhh…” I pressed a finger against my lips and glanced nervously toward
the entrance of the tent.

“What is it, Marcus?” he asked while rubbing his eyes.

“There’s something out there.”

“What do you mean something?” He sat up with an annoyed frown, clearly
irritated about being dragged out of sleep.

“I don’t know,” I whispered. “I thought it was a bear or something, but
its skin looked too smooth. It’s just out there in the dark, up in the
trees, watching me.”

“What exactly did you see?”

“I don’t know, man. But there’s definitely something in the trees.”

He shoved my hand away from his shoulder, irritation flashing across his
face.

“If this is your idea of a joke, it’s seriously not funny,” he muttered.
“Goddamn it, Marcus. You always pull crap like this. Always ruin
everything, just like when we were kids.”

The words stung more than I expected.

“If you don’t believe me, go look for yourself,” I said quietly as I
shifted aside to give him room.

Still grumbling under his breath, Jonah crawled toward the front of the
tent and peeked through the slightly opened flap. Outside, the fire
still glowed faintly in the darkness while the forest beyond remained
completely still.

“I don’t see jackshit,” he whispered back at me while I crouched beside
him.

“At the edge of the forest,” I whispered back. “Up in those pine trees
over there.”

He squinted into the darkness beyond the weak firelight flickering in
front of us. For several seconds, nothing happened. The forest beyond
the clearing was almost completely black. The faint glow from our dying
fire barely reached the nearest trees, leaving everything beyond them
swallowed by thick shadows.

“There’s nothing there,” he whispered.

“Look higher,” I murmured. “Near the top of those pines.”

He tilted his head upward and narrowed his eyes, searching between the
tangled branches. Still nothing. For a moment, I began wondering if I
had imagined the whole thing. Maybe it had been a trick of the shadows.
Maybe exhaustion and too much beer had finally gotten to me.

Then he suddenly went still.

I followed his gaze. Two large, round eyes slowly emerged from the
shadows high among the trees, catching the faint orange glow of our
fire. They were staring directly at us.

“Owl?” he murmured. “Or maybe a racc— “

His voice died instantly. The fire suddenly dimmed, and the glowing eyes
blinked. Then I saw it too. A long pale arm emerged slowly from the
darkness as the thing began climbing down from the tree. Its limbs
looked bony and wrong, almost human but not quite. Every hair on my body
stood upright.

“Marcus,” Jonah hissed sharply. “The fire.”

“What about it?”

He did not answer. Instead, he lunged out of the tent and rushed toward
the dying flames, throwing more twigs and dry leaves onto the fire. The
flames burst brighter almost instantly.

“Dude, what the hell is going on?” I shouted as I scrambled out after
him.

“Keep the fire going,” he hissed while digging frantically through
nearby bushes for more dry kindling. “Help me. Hurry.”

I glanced back toward the trees where the thing had been standing.
Nothing. It was gone.

“It won’t come close while the fire’s burning,” Jonah went on while
gathering armfuls of dry branches.

“What do you mean?” I asked him.

“We’re almost out of dry wood,” Jonah said shakily. Sweat streamed down
his face despite the cold air, glistening along his forehead and
temples. He glanced nervously at the dwindling pile beside the fire.
“The fire’s gonna die in an hour.”

“What are we gonna do now?” I asked, still staring into the woods. I
could barely make out the trees beyond the firelight, their dark trunks
and branches fading into the deeper blackness beyond our campsite.

A long hoarse howl suddenly echoed through the forest somewhere beyond
the darkness. It sounded too close. My stomach dropped instantly.

“What… was that?” I whispered.

“Marcus go grab my gun in my backpack,” he hissed. “Quick.”

I looked around wildly, my eyes glued to the darkness between the trees
as if expecting something to burst out at any second. He grabbed a
burning branch from the fire and hurled it deep into the darkness. A low
guttural growl echoed back from the trees. Then came the sound of quick
shuffling footsteps retreating into the forest. Silence followed and he
turned toward me, his face pale and tight with fear.

“Now’s our chance,” he said.

We scrambled back toward the tent. I nearly tripped over one of the logs
scattered around the fire as I stumbled through the entrance.

“Backpacks,” Jonah hissed. “Get your backpack.”

“I’m trying.”

My hands were shaking so badly that I could barely grab the thing. I
fumbled with the straps, then yanked it toward me, nearly tearing the
zipper open in the process. Something inside spilled onto the floor, but
I didn’t stop to see what it was. Jonah was already crawling across the
tent, frantically shoving whatever he could reach into his own pack. His
movements were clumsy and frantic, nothing like the calm, methodical way
he usually did things.

“Leave the tent,” he said. “We don’t have time.”

I didn’t need to be told twice. I slung my backpack over my shoulder and
scrambled out of the tent. Jonah followed close behind, carrying his
pack and the burning branch he’d pulled from the fire. He fired another
shot into the darkness as we ran, and then we disappeared into the
woods.

Almost an hour later, we were still sprinting through the woods.
Branches slapped against our faces while mist clung to our clothes.
Behind us, that awful howl still echoed through the forest every now and
then.

“There’s a ranger tower up ahead,” he panted between breaths. “We’ll be
safe there.”

I glanced over my shoulder. For a split second, I thought I saw
something pale slipping through the bushes behind us. A hand. Long
fingers. Thin as branches. I blinked, but there was nothing there.

“Keep moving,” Jonah said.

The next couple of miles seemed to stretch endlessly. My legs were
burning, my lungs felt raw, and I could barely feel my feet anymore.
Every few minutes, that awful howl rose somewhere behind us, reminding
me that whatever we’d encountered was still out there. Then, through a
break in the trees, I saw something towering above the forest canopy. At
first, I thought it was another enormous pine. Then I realized there was
something built around its trunk. A wooden structure, high above the
ground, partially hidden among the branches.

“There,” Jonah shouted, pointing upward. “The ranger tower.”

I followed his gaze. At first, I could barely make it out through the
trees. The cabin stood high above the forest floor, wedged between the
massive branches of an enormous pine. Its old wooden structure had been
built directly around the trunk, almost as if it had grown out of the
tree itself. Heavy beams supported its weathered floor and walls. There
were no lights in the windows, no signs that anyone had been there in
years.

“Marcus,” Jonah yelled suddenly, pointing toward the base of the tree.
“Climb up.”

I looked over and saw a crude rope ladder hanging from the trunk, its
rough wooden rungs leading all the way up toward the cabin.

“Hurry,” he said breathlessly.

I grabbed the first rung and started climbing. Jonah scrambled up behind
me, pulling himself onto the ladder as quickly as he could. The old
ropes creaked and strained beneath our combined weight, making the
wooden rungs sway with every movement.

We jumped onto the wooden platform and rushed inside before slamming the
door shut behind us. Jonah immediately lit the fireplace in the corner
before collapsing against the wall, gasping for air. I stayed near the
window, peering down into the darkness below. Nothing moved.

“We’re safe as long as the fire stays lit,” he said between breaths. “We
wait here until morning. Then we figure out a way back.”

“How come you know this place exists?”

He was quiet for a moment. He looked toward the dark window behind me.

“Dad told me. He used to come here a lot.”

“Why?” I asked, unable to hide my frustration any longer.

“After you left, he spent a lot of time in the forest. Camping by
himself, hunting, sometimes just disappearing for days. He kept going
out there, even after all these years.” He poked at the logs in the
fireplace, sending a few glowing embers drifting upward.

“You think that thing attacked him? Was that what he’d seen all those
years ago?” I asked quietly.

“Maybe.”

“What is that thing, anyway? Some kind of animal or what? What is it,
Jonah?”

He swallowed hard before answering. “Something older than these woods.
It was here long before anyone settled this valley.” 

“How do you know all this?” I asked.

“I listened, Marcus,” he replied softly. “Back when we were kids. I
listened to stories told by the old folks in town. The ones whose
grandparents were among the first settlers here. They said that when the
settlers first arrived about a hundred years ago, they heard those same
howls almost every night. Then livestock started disappearing. They
thought it was a wolf or maybe a bear, so they sent five armed men into
the forest to hunt it down.”

“What happened to them?”

“They never came back,” he said quietly, lighting a cigarette. After
that, more people started disappearing too. Anyone stupid enough to
wander outside after dark.”

The cabin suddenly seemed strangely quiet, save for the occasional pop
of burning wood and the faint hiss of embers settling in the fireplace.

“That’s when the stories started. People believed something was living
out there. Near the lake. Something that hunted people and fed on them.”
He took another drag from his cigarette before speaking again. “One by
one, families packed up and left. They moved away from the lake and
built a new settlement farther east. That’s how Ravensville started.”

“What do you think happened to all those people?” I asked suddenly. “The
ones who vanished?”

He stared into the flames before answering. “Once that thing got a taste
for human flesh, it stopped hunting animals,” he said softly. “That’s
why it started creeping into town after dark. Anyone caught outside past
sunset just disappeared.” He looked toward the fireplace.

“So we’re safe as long as the fire keeps burning?” I glanced nervously
at the flames dancing nearby.

“If you want the logical explanation,” he said, leaning forward
slightly, “those people probably just left. Bigger dreams. Better
opportunities somewhere else. Isn’t that exactly what you did?”

The words hit harder than I expected. I flinched slightly. For several
seconds, neither of us spoke. We just stared at each other through the
dim orange firelight.

“You know why I left,” I finally said.

“Do I?” he said quietly while flicking ash into the fire.

“My relationship with Dad was already falling apart before I left. He
never got over Mom, and I…”

“You left me, Marcus.” His jaw tightened while his eyes glistened in the
firelight. “You walked away and left me to rot in this dead-end town.
You left me to take care of him while I was still grieving. I was
sixteen. Sixteen, Marcus. And I had to carry everything alone.”

“I’m so sorry.”

“It’s ten years too late for sorry.” He shook his head slowly while
staring into the flames. “You never even visited. Not once. You never
wrote. Never called.” His voice trembled.

“Jonah, I…”

He suddenly stood up and marched toward me. His face was tight with
fury.

“I took care of him,” he hissed. “I stayed. I fed him. I cleaned up
after him. I watched him rot away piece by piece. But the only name he
ever said was yours.”

I swallowed hard. “I swear to God, Jonah. If I could go back and change
things, I would.”

“When you left, it was like losing mom all over again. That’s what broke
him. That’s what killed him.” He went on, both eyes locked onto mine.
“You didn’t just leave him. You let him die slowly, day by day.”

“That’s not true,” I whispered with clenched teeth, tears burning in my
eyes.

He opened his mouth, probably to say something else that would cut even
deeper, but then a loud howl echoed through the forest. This time, it
sounded much closer, so clear that I could almost feel it vibrating
through the walls of the whole cabin. I instinctively recoiled from the
door.

“There’s something else I haven’t told you,” he said quickly, pulling
out his gun with a trembling hand.

I didn’t answer him. My mind was still spinning from everything he had
just said to me, and I wasn’t sure I could take anything else.

“It’s about Dad,” he went on quietly. “And it’s the reason we’re here
tonight.”

“You’re right. This is all my fault. I killed Dad. Right? Is that what
you wanted to hear me say?” I said in a hoarse voice. 

“No.” His voice had become so quiet that I could barely hear him over
the wind outside. “Dad didn’t die of a heart attack.”

For a moment, I just stared at him, certain I had misheard.

“What did you say?”

“That thing killed him.” His grip tightened around the gun. “That thing
out there. It killed our father.” He swallowed hard and looked away.
“They found him near the lake. He was…”

His voice trailed off. For several seconds, he said nothing. His face
had gone pale, and I could see him struggling to get the words out.

“What the hell are you talking about?” I asked, inching closer to him.

“That deer we found in the forest today…” He took a deep breath and
finally looked me in the eye. “They found him like that.”

I stared at him. “You’re joking.”

“I wish I was.”

“No…” I stammered.

“There’s a hole in his skull,” he continued, his voice barely above a
whisper. “A clean hole, right through the bone. They found him exactly
the same way we found that deer.”

I opened my mouth to answer him, but before I could say a word, a loud
thud slammed against the cabin. The whole structure shook violently,
rattling the shelves and sending dust drifting from the ceiling beams.

Both of us recoiled in horror. For a second, neither of us moved. The
only sound was the soft creaking of the wooden planks outside, as if
something heavy was slowly shifting its weight along the porch. My
stomach dropped. That thing had found us.

“I’m gonna kill it,” he said, his face hardening. “I’m gonna kill that
fucking thing for what it did to Dad. I’m gonna smash its ugly face into
its own fucking skull.”

He strode toward the door, pulled it open, and burst outside.

“Jonah… no.” I reached for his hand, my whole body seized with terror,
but he shoved me away.

I grabbed a burning log from the fire and followed him outside. Jonah
stood on the wooden platform, gun raised as he scanned the darkness. At
first, I could see nothing beyond the faint firelight from the log in my
trembling hand. Then my eyes adjusted.

At the far end of the platform, beneath the low-hanging branches, I saw
the creature crouching there. It was almost motionless, its enormous
black eyes gleaming as they stared at us. It hissed, then let out a
deep, guttural growl, its gaze shifting between Jonah and the burning
log as if it understood what the fire meant.

“Jonah…” I whispered, my voice barely more than a breath. “Get back
inside.”

The creature slowly lifted its head, and I got a better look at it.

It looked vaguely human but horribly distorted. One arm was missing,
ending in a blunt stump just before the elbow. Its hunched back rose
beneath pale, hairless skin, while its limbs were far too long, giving
it a grotesque, almost spider-like appearance. The skin stretched
tightly over its frame, revealing a network of pulsing veins beneath.

Jonah steadied the gun with both hands and aimed at its chest.

“Stay back,” he muttered.

The creature hissed again, lowering its head as its lips slowly peeled
away from its teeth. And Jonah squeezed the trigger. The gunshot
exploded across the platform, and the creature jerked violently
backward. It opened its mouth impossibly wide, far wider than any human
jaw should have been able to stretch, and for a brief second I saw what
was inside.

There was only one tooth. It was long, thin, and horribly sharp,
protruding from the roof of its mouth like a pale spike. I stared at it
in disbelief as the creature released a shrill, furious screech that
seemed to shake the entire tower.

The creature lunged forward. Jonah fired at almost the same moment, and
I swung the burning log at it. The log struck the wooden wall with a
dull crack before falling onto the platform between us.

For a moment, nothing happened. Then the flames caught. Dry sticks and
pine needles ignited almost instantly, the fire spreading across the
platform. The creature recoiled with a shrill, ear-piercing shriek, its
enormous eyes widening as it stared at the flames. Jonah fired again and
again.

The gunshots thundered through the forest, each followed by another
furious shriek. One bullet struck its shoulder, twisting its body
sideways, while another tore into its pale torso. It staggered backward,
snarling and clawing at its wounds as the fire spread around its feet.

“Marcus, the fire!” Jonah shouted.

I looked down and realized how quickly the flames had grown. What had
started as a small patch of fire was already racing along the dry wood,
crawling beneath the railing and spreading toward the other side of the
platform.

“Get back!” Jonah yelled.

We both started moving toward the opposite end of the platform, but the
creature suddenly crouched low. For one terrible second, it simply
stared at us through the flames. Then it lunged.

It moved so quickly that I barely saw it coming. It passed through the
fire without hesitation, its pale body briefly disappearing behind the
flames before emerging on the other side. Jonah raised his gun, but the
creature was already upon him. Its impossibly long arm shot forward, and
its spindly hand closed around his throat. Jonah gagged as his feet left
the floor. The gun slipped from his hand and struck the platform with a
metallic thud. I rushed toward him, but the heat drove me back as he
clawed desperately at the creature’s wrist.

The creature opened its mouth, its jaw stretching unnaturally wide and
revealing the single long tooth protruding from the roof of its mouth.
Then it swung Jonah sideways with terrifying ease and hurled him over
the railing.

“Jonah!”

I ran to the edge and looked down, but there was nothing I could see
beyond the darkness beneath the tower. My brother’s body had simply
disappeared into it. 

Something inside me seemed to break. I turned around. The creature was
staring directly at me, slowly stepping forward as the flames crackled
between us. My eyes dropped to the gun. I lunged for it. The creature
hissed and reached through the smoke, but I grabbed the gun and rolled
away as its hand slammed against the boards where my head had been.

I raised the gun and fired twice. The first shot struck its face, the
second tearing through the side of its head. It recoiled with a
deafening shriek, dark fluid pouring from the wounds as it staggered
backward.

I fired again. The shot struck near its jaw, snapping its head sideways
before it collapsed onto its back.

I jumped through the flames and grabbed the burning log from the
platform. The glowing end struck its chest, making it scream and writhe
as it tried to rise. I raised the gun again and fired.

The final shot slammed into its head. Its body jerked, then collapsed
against the boards. One arm twitched, its long fingers scraping weakly
against the floor before going still.

I stood there with the gun raised, staring at it through the smoke and
flames. Its mangled face was barely recognizable beneath the blood and
dark fluid as the fire continued spreading around its motionless body.

“Jonah…” My voice cracked as I lowered the pistol.

I don’t remember how I got down from the tree so quickly. Somehow, I
made my way back to the forest floor, stumbling through the darkness
with the gun still clutched in my hand.

I found Jonah lying motionless on his stomach a few feet from the base
of the tree. For a moment, I couldn’t move. The sight of him lying there
made something inside me sink. Right then, I realized my nightmare had
only just begun.

“Oh, God. No. Please…” I ran toward him, sobbing like a little kid, and
dropped to my knees. “Jonah.”

My hands were shaking as I rolled him onto his back and pulled his head
onto my lap. Above us, the burning cabin crackled and groaned, casting
an uneven orange glow through the trees. In the flickering light, I saw
dark blood running from his nose and mouth.

“Jonah…” I whispered, brushing the blood-stained hair from his forehead.
“Please, hang on. I’m gonna get us out of here.”

I knew it was a lie. I didn’t know how we were going to get out of the
forest or if he would survive the next few minutes. I clutched him
tighter, trying to convince myself everything would somehow be okay,
even as I felt the life slowly draining from him.

Then his eyes opened. He stared up at me without seeming to recognize
me, his unfocused gaze drifting past my face as his lips trembled.

“Marcus…”

I grabbed his hand and held it tightly between both of mine. It felt
frighteningly cold against my skin.

“I’m here,” I whispered, leaning down until my forehead rested against
his.

I could feel his fingers barely moving inside my grip, as though he was
trying to hold on to me but no longer had the strength.

“Marcus…” His voice was almost impossible to hear.

“Yes, Jonah.” I lifted my head and looked into his eyes, those same
light brown eyes we had both inherited from our mother. “I’m right
here.”

His lips parted slightly. “Please…”

And then his breathing stopped. I waited. I kept staring at his face,
convinced that he would take another breath, that his chest would rise
again, that he would open his eyes and say my name one more time. But he
didn’t. I pressed my trembling fingers against his neck, searching
desperately for a pulse even though some part of me already knew it was
too late. There was nothing.

I pulled him closer and buried my face against his chest, and for the
first time since I had left Ravensville ten years ago, I completely
broke down.

“Jonah…”

My voice disappeared into the darkness beneath the burning cabin.

I stayed beside Jonah until the darkness began to fade and the first
pale light of morning appeared between the trees. Eventually, I forced
myself to leave him and made my way back to Ravensville through the
thick forest, following the small river that took me back to the
overgrown trail the trail we had taken the day before.

Strangely enough, it didn’t take long before the forest started to feel
familiar again. I recognized certain trees and bends in the river,
remembering places I hadn’t seen since I was a child. Then I heard the
distant whistle of a train cutting through the thickest parts of the
forest, and somehow, that familiar sound made me realize that I was
finally getting close.

Looking back, I realize Jonah’s invitation to go camping was never
really about spending time together or repairing our relationship. Even
now, there are still so many questions I don’t have answers to.

I made my way upstairs to my father’s old bedroom and took a shower,
standing beneath the warm stream of water and crying until my eyes hurt.
I stayed there for a long time, trying to wash away the blood, dirt, and
sweat from the night before, but nothing seemed to make me feel any
cleaner. When I finally stepped out, I dried myself and stood in front
of the mirror for a long time.

I cut my hair short and shaved off my beard and moustache, leaving my
face clean-shaven for the first time in years. I stared at myself in the
mirror for a while, almost as if I were seeing my own face for the first
time. Then I put on my father’s old clothes, got into Jonah’s car, which
was still parked in the front yard, and drove straight to his house. It
didn’t take me long to find it. Ravensville is a small town, after all.

As soon as I parked in front of the small white house and got out of the
car, the beige front door swung open.

She stood in the doorway, her thick black hair gleaming in the morning
sunlight. The most beautiful woman I’d ever seen.

She looked at me for a moment, then smiled. I smiled back. She planted a
quick kiss on my face and stepped aside. I followed her into the house,
my fingers briefly touching my cheek.

For the first time in ten years, I was finally home.
