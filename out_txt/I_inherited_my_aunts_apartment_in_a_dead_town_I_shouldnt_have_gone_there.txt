This happened in the early 2000s. I was eighteen at the time, but I
remember it like it was yesterday. It was October, in a small town
somewhere in the Urals where my distant aunt, Nina, used to live. I had
only seen her a couple of times in my life. She had a son who, a year
earlier, killed himself by jumping out of their apartment window. A week
before I got there, she died too. There was no one close to her left, so
she left the apartment to me.

I packed quickly without thinking too much. My mom spent the whole
morning walking around the apartment like I wasn’t just leaving for a
trip, but for something much longer. Something final. She told me the
journey would be long and put a bag of homemade pastries into my
backpack, saying it was a three day train ride and you never know what
might happen. I just smiled. Three days on a train sounded like an
adventure.

I left from Volgograd. The station was loud and crowded, people pushing
past each other, announcements echoing, the smell of cheap coffee and
metal everywhere. Everything felt normal, but underneath it there was a
strange feeling, like I was going somewhere you don’t come back from the
same.

The first day went by fine. The second dragged on. Outside the window
there were fewer towns, more forests, abandoned stations, broken
platforms with no one on them. By the third night I could barely sleep.
I woke up because it had become too quiet.

The train was still moving, I could feel it, but there was almost no
sound. No clatter of wheels, no voices, no movement, not even breathing.
I sat up and realized I couldn’t hear anyone at all. I looked at the
window. It was pitch black, and in the reflection I could only see
myself, even though there were two other people in the compartment with
me.

I decided not to turn around. I lay back down and closed my eyes.

In the morning everything was normal again. People were talking, the
attendant walked through the carriage serving tea, someone was laughing.
But the pastries my mom gave me didn’t smell like food anymore. They
smelled damp, like they hadn’t been in my backpack, but buried somewhere
underground.

When the train finally stopped, it took me a moment to realize we had
arrived. No one rushed to get off. People moved slowly, like they hadn’t
slept or weren’t fully awake. I stepped onto the platform last. The air
felt cold and wet, with a metallic taste. Not like after rain, but like
places where no one has lived for a long time.

The station was small. The name sign was crooked, the paint peeling so
badly you could barely read it. The lights were on even during the day,
dim and yellow. The strangest thing was the silence. Not like the
silence on the train. Deeper. Like the whole town didn’t make any sound.

I told myself it was early and people were still asleep, but when I
reached the road, it felt worse. The town looked normal, apartment
blocks, shops, bus stops, but everything was faded, like an old
photograph. The windows were dark. Not a single one open. The yards were
empty. No cars, no kids, not even dogs. Just the wind pushing dust and
scraps of paper across the asphalt, and somehow they made no sound.

As I walked, I couldn’t shake the feeling that something was watching
me. Not from the windows. From somewhere lower, from the entrances, the
basements, or even from underground.

At the bus stop there were three people already standing there. They
weren’t talking, just staring in the same direction, waiting for the
bus. I stood next to them, and after a couple of minutes I realized none
of them had blinked once.

I looked away, and one of them quietly asked if I had come from the
train. I nodded without looking at him. He said it must have been a long
trip. His voice sounded dry, like he hadn’t spoken in a long time. I
wanted to say something back, but he didn’t continue.

The bus arrived without a sound. The doors opened on their own, and it
was darker inside than outside. As I stepped in, I caught a glimpse of
the driver. He was completely still, like a mannequin. I sat by the
window, and when the bus started moving, I noticed the reflection showed
the entire interior except for the driver.

The cemetery was on the edge of town. People got off one by one along
the way, silently. By the time we reached it, it was just me and the
driver. I stepped outside and a cold wind hit me immediately. The
cemetery was right across from the apartment building.

That was the first moment I felt real fear.

The ground was black and wet, like it had just been dug up, not just at
my aunt’s grave but almost everywhere. The crosses were crooked and old.
Some markers didn’t even have names, just dates. Sometimes the same
dates. The wind moved through the trees, but the leaves barely reacted.
There was a heavy, sweet smell in the air. When they lowered the coffin,
I couldn’t shake the feeling that the ground was taking it too quickly,
like it had been waiting.

There were only seven people at the funeral. Distant acquaintances and
my aunt’s sister, who hadn’t spoken to her in over ten years. After a
small wake at a nearby café, I went to a notary and handled the
paperwork. That evening, when it was already dark, I went to the
apartment.

The building looked abandoned, like no one had cared about it for years.
Only two apartments were still occupied. The rest were boarded up, doors
broken, windows covered with planks. On the first floor lived an old man
named Zakhar, around seventy, a former sailor. My aunt’s place was on
the fourth floor.

When I approached, he was sitting outside smoking. Somehow he recognized
me. He told me not to go out at night and said that not only people walk
around here. I shrugged it off. He invited me in to drink in memory of
my aunt, and I agreed.

As we entered the building, I heard a long, drawn out howl somewhere far
away. It lasted too long and sounded too deliberate. Inside, the hallway
was dark, the light flickering, the walls smelled damp and stale. His
apartment looked normal, old furniture, a carpet on the wall, but
everything was clean.

We drank. First to meet each other, then silently for my aunt. That’s
when he started talking. He said something was wrong with the building,
that over the last three years people had either died or left, no one
stayed without a reason. His voice was calm but sounded hollow, like it
didn’t belong to him. He told me to sell the apartment and leave as soon
as possible.

I smiled. He didn’t.

He said he used to think the same way until one night he went outside to
smoke. It was around midnight when everything suddenly went completely
silent, no wind, no insects, even his own breathing sounded wrong, like
it didn’t belong to him. Then he saw something crawling along the wall
of the building. Long and thin. Not human. It stretched upward and
scratched at the windows of the empty apartments.

I stopped smiling.

He said he ran inside, locked himself in, put bars on the windows, and
kept a shotgun nearby. He added that the night my aunt died, he heard
something crawling along the walls and calling out.

Right then a clock struck midnight and I flinched. He told me to stay
the night and not go upstairs, but I refused. I don’t even know why.

I took my backpack and went up. Above the first floor it was completely
dark. I had to feel my way along the railing. The lock on the fourth
floor resisted, like it didn’t want to open. When it finally did, the
door creaked loudly.

Inside it was pitch black. I flipped the switch. The light flashed once
and burned out.

I walked into the kitchen. There was a clear outline of a body on the
floor marked in chalk.

I froze. Then I realized how quiet it was. The same silence he had
described.

Then I heard it. At first a faint rustling. Then a scraping sound. It
was coming from inside the wall, like something was slowly moving
through it.

I stood still. The sound got closer. Then suddenly it was right behind
me.

I turned around. The door to the hallway was slightly open, and
something was moving in the gap. Too long. Too thin. It slowly stretched
into the apartment, clinging to the frame like it had no bones.

I couldn’t move.

Then it turned its head.

I couldn’t see a face, but I knew it was looking at me.

Then it spoke.

Quietly. Hoarse.

In my aunt’s voice.

It said that I had come.

I don’t remember how I ran out or got downstairs. I only remember
pounding on Zakhar’s door. He opened immediately. We sat in silence
until morning.

As soon as it got light, I ran out and went to a church, even though I
had never believed. The priest looked at me for a long time, then told
me to leave this place and gave me a cross.

I left that same day. I put the apartment up for sale through an agency.
A month later they called and said it had been sold. Then Zakhar called
me. I had given him my number. He said a young guy had moved in.

The next night he disappeared.

Zakhar said he heard something crawling along the walls again, and
something calling for the guy.

A week later the police called me.

They found Zakhar in his apartment.

The door was locked from the inside. The bars were intact. The shotgun
was next to him.

And on the wall there was a white outline.

Full height.

Like he had been standing there.

And someone had traced him.
