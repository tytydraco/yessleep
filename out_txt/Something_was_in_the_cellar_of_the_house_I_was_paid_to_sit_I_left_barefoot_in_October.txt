I never should have taken that overnight shift at the Ashgrove place,
but the pay was good and I needed the money badly enough not to ask why
a house-sitting gig was paying three hundred a night.

I’m going to tell this the way I remember it, which isn’t perfect. Some
of it I’ve gone over so many times in my head that I’m not sure anymore
what I actually saw and what I’ve built up from thinking about it too
much. But the important parts, the parts that still wake me up at three
in the morning — those I’m sure of.

I was twenty-six, living in Muncie, Indiana, working part-time at an
auto parts store and picking up odd jobs through a guy named Reggie who
did estate work — clearing out houses, minor repairs, that kind of
thing. He called me up in October and said he needed someone to stay
overnight at a property out past Yorktown, an old farmhouse that had
been sitting empty for eleven months since the last owner died. The
family wanted someone there because kids from the high school kept
breaking in, and insurance wouldn’t cover an empty structure without
some kind of occupancy.

I drove out there on a Thursday with a duffel bag, my phone charger, and
a six-pack I didn’t end up touching. The house sat back off County Road
500, one of those long gravel driveways lined with dead cornfields on
both sides. Two stories, white paint peeling everywhere, a wraparound
porch that sagged on the east side. Reggie met me there to hand off the
key and walked me through quick — kitchen, living room, two bedrooms
upstairs, a cellar door in the kitchen he told me not to bother with
because the stairs down were rotted through.

“Just don’t go poking around too much,” he said. “Old lady who lived
here, she was funny about her house. Family’s still touchy about people
going through her things.”

I didn’t think anything of it. I said sure, locked the door behind him,
and settled into the living room with my phone and a space heater
because the furnace hadn’t been serviced in who knows how long.

Around eleven, I heard the first sound. Something upstairs, a single
heavy step, like someone had shifted their weight from one foot to
another. Old houses do that. Wood contracts in the cold. I told myself
that and I believed it, mostly.

Then it happened again. And again. Not random creaking — footsteps, slow
and deliberate, crossing from one end of the upstairs hallway to the
other, pausing, then coming back.

I sat there for a long time before I made myself go check. I want to be
honest that I almost didn’t. I almost just left. But I needed that three
hundred dollars more than I wanted to admit, so I grabbed the flashlight
off my phone and went up.

The hallway was empty. Both bedroom doors were shut, same as when Reggie
had shown me through. I checked each room. Nothing. I told myself it was
settling, went back downstairs, and tried to sleep on the couch with the
space heater running and every light in the living room on.

I woke up sometime after two because the temperature in the room had
dropped so much I could see my breath. The space heater was still
running, still warm to the touch, but it wasn’t doing anything. My body
felt like I was lying outside in December.

That’s when I heard someone in the kitchen. Not footsteps this time —
the sound of a chair being pulled out from the table, slow, the legs
scraping across linoleum, and then nothing. Like someone had sat down.

I stayed on that couch for what felt like an hour, not moving, staring
at the doorway to the kitchen, telling myself if I just waited it out
until sunrise I could leave and never come back and Reggie could find
someone else to do this. But the chair scraped again. And again, in that
same slow rhythm, like someone getting up and sitting back down, over
and over, patient about it.

I finally forced myself to look. The kitchen was empty. The chair was
pushed in exactly like it had been when I arrived.

I should have left right then. I didn’t, because my car keys were in my
jacket, which I’d left hanging by the front door, and getting to that
door meant walking past the cellar.

I made it about halfway across the living room when the cellar door came
open on its own. Not slammed — it swung open slow, like something on the
other side had turned the handle carefully. Cold air came up out of that
opening, and underneath it, faint, I could smell something like wet
earth and something else I didn’t have a word for then and still don’t.

I heard breathing come up out of that cellar. Not wind through a gap in
the boards. Breathing, uneven, like someone dragging air into lungs that
didn’t want to work right anymore.

I grabbed my jacket off the hook without stopping to put it on and went
straight for the front door. It didn’t open. I want to be clear I know
how that sounds, but I checked that lock three times and it was
unlocked, the same simple deadbolt Reggie had shown me, and it would not
turn. My hands were shaking bad enough that I told myself that was the
problem, that I just wasn’t doing it right, and I kept trying while
behind me I heard something come up out of that cellar and onto the
kitchen floor.

Whatever it was didn’t sound like it was walking right. The rhythm was
wrong, dragging on one side, a scrape and a step and a scrape.

The deadbolt finally turned. I don’t know if it was ever actually stuck
or if I’d just been too panicked to work a simple lock, and that
uncertainty bothers me more than almost anything else about that night.
I got the door open and I ran across that porch and down the gravel
driveway in the dark without my shoes, which I’d left by the couch, and
I didn’t stop running until I reached the county road and flagged down a
truck heading toward Yorktown.

The guy who picked me up, older fella, didn’t ask a lot of questions.
Just looked at me shaking in his passenger seat, no shoes, no
explanation that made sense, and drove me the rest of the way into town
without a word until we hit the gas station on Main Street, where he
told me to go on inside and get warmed up.

I called Reggie the next morning and told him I wasn’t going back for my
duffel bag, my shoes, or the rest of the money. He didn’t argue with me.
He just went quiet on the phone for a second and then said, “Yeah.
That’s about what happened to the last two guys too.”

I asked him what he meant by that and he changed the subject.

I still don’t know what was in that cellar, or what had been sitting in
that kitchen chair, or why the front door wouldn’t open until it
suddenly did. I’ve thought about driving back out there just to see the
place in daylight, tell myself it was nothing, that the cold was just
bad insulation and the sounds were just an old house doing what old
houses do.

I’ve started that drive twice. Both times I turned around before I hit
County Road 500.
