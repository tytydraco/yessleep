The Idyllic Beginning: (Part 1)

June 3rd

Mom bought me this diary at a gas station somewhere past the third exit
sign for a town called Little Wren. She said, and I quote, You’re going
to want to remember this summer. I think what she meant was I need you
to stop complaining in the car, but sure, Mom. Sure. I’ll remember it.
I’ll remember every mile of corn.

I’m Dev, by the way, in case future me forgets who’s writing this.
Sixteen. Exiled to my grandmother’s village for two months because my
parents are renovating the kitchen, which is a phrase adults use when
they actually mean we need a break from our son. I get it. I’m a lot. I
contain multitudes, and most of those multitudes are sarcasm.

The village doesn’t have a real name I can pronounce, so I’m calling it
Wrenhollow, because it sounds like something out of a book where
somebody’s aunt turns out to be a witch. It has one church, one bakery,
and a square with a fountain that hasn’t worked since possibly the Cold
War. There’s moss growing in the shape of a frown on the fountain’s
stone lip. I took that as a warning and ignored it, because that’s what
teenagers do.

I’m staying with my grandmother. I haven’t seen her since I was maybe
seven, so my memory of her is basically a smell of cinnamon and a voice
that called me little frog for reasons nobody has explained. She met me
at the bus stop today holding a sign that said DEV in glitter glue, like
I was an airport pickup for a toddler. I loved it immediately. Don’t
tell her that.

June 4th

Grandma’s real name is Ilse, but everyone in the village calls her Nana
Ilse, including grown adults who are clearly not her grandchildren. She
lives in a cottage that looks like it was drawn by someone who really
believes in chimneys. There are four of them. The house does not need
four chimneys. The house has maybe six rooms.

Breakfast this morning was something called a placek, which is a cake,
but she serves it like a religious rite, with a specific fork and a
specific silence while you eat the first bite. I didn’t ask questions.
When someone hands you cake with that much ceremony, you eat the cake.

She’s exactly what you’d want in a grandmother if you built one out of a
catalog. Round glasses. Cardigans in colors that don’t exist in nature.
She calls me little frog constantly now, and I’ve stopped fighting it,
because fighting it only makes her say it more, gleefully, like she’s
found a weak spot and intends to press on it for the rest of the summer.

Here’s the thing I noticed, though, and I’m only writing it down because
this diary was clearly bought under duress and I have to justify its
existence somehow she writes a lot. Like, a lot a lot. There’s a
notebook on the kitchen table, always the same one, fat and
soft-cornered like it’s been handled every day for years. Every time I
walked past it today, it had moved. Not far. Just enough that I noticed.
Once it was by the sugar bowl. Once it was under a dish towel, like it
was hiding.

June 5th

Met a kid named Tobiah at the bakery. He’s my age, has a haircut that
looks self-inflicted, and immediately asked if I was the grandson from
the city, which apparently is what the entire village has already
decided to call me. News travels here at the speed of gossip, which, it
turns out, is faster than the internet.

Tobiah gave me the tour. The tour consisted of the church (old, smells
like wax and regret), the square (fountain, frown-moss, one bench that
wobbles), and something he called the reading house, which is apparently
the village’s library, tucked behind the church like it’s embarrassed to
be seen. He said it in a weird tone. Like when someone mentions their ex
at a party. I asked what the deal was and he just said, It’s closed most
days, and changed the subject so fast I got whiplash.

Village kids, man. Full of secrets they’re dying to keep.

Anyway, Wrenhollow is objectively charming in the way that places are
charming right up until you learn what’s under the charm. There’s a
story everyone tells about the fountain, some medieval nonsense about a
girl who wished so hard for something that the well granted it and then
never stopped granting things, on and on, forever, whether anyone wanted
it or not. Tobiah told it like a ghost story, complete with spooky
voice. I laughed. He didn’t.

I’m noting that down here, not because I believe in cursed fountains,
obviously, but because if this book somehow becomes evidence in my
eventual true crime documentary, I want it on record that I was warned.

June 6th

Grandma’s notebook again. I know, I know, I sound obsessed. I promise
I’m not obsessed. I’m bored, and bored teenagers notice things, that’s a
scientific fact I just made up.

This morning I woke up at seven, which for me is basically still
nighttime, and she was already at the kitchen table, writing. Not
humming-along-to-the-radio writing. Not grocery-list writing. She had
both hands wrapped around the pen like it might try to escape, and her
whole face was scrunched up in concentration, and she didn’t even look
up when I said good morning. I said it twice. Nothing.

I made toast and sat across from her for a solid ten minutes, and in
that whole time she filled almost two pages. Her handwriting isn’t even
neat when she does that. It’s slanted and cramped, running right off the
edge of the paper like the words are trying to escape before she can
finish the sentence.

When she finally noticed me, she smiled like nothing had happened,
closed the notebook, and said, Sorry, little frog, just getting some
thoughts down. Then she made me eggs and asked about my life back home,
completely normal, completely warm, exactly like the grandmother I met
at the bus stop.

But here’s the part I keep chewing on. When she closed the notebook, her
hand was shaking. Just a little. The kind of shake you get after holding
something heavy for too long.

I didn’t say anything. What was I going to say Hey Nana, your journaling
looks like a hostage situation I have manners, occasionally.

June 8th

Skipping a day because nothing happened, which, weirdly, felt like its
own kind of event. No notebook on the table. No writing marathon. Just a
completely ordinary Nana Ilse who taught me how to make pierogi and told
me stories about my dad as a kid, most of which made him sound
significantly more annoying than he currently is, which I appreciated.

I brought up Tobiah’s reading house thing, casually, over dinner,
because I am a master of casual conversation. She went quiet for a
second, just a second, the kind of pause you’d miss if you weren’t
already looking for weirdness in this house.

That old place, she said. Best left to the dust. Then she asked if I
wanted more pierogi, and obviously I did, because I’m not an idiot, and
the conversation ended there.

I want to be annoyed at myself for how much I’m noticing. This is
supposed to be a boring summer. Cornfields and cake and an old lady who
calls me little frog. That’s the deal. I did not sign up for a mystery.
And yet.

June 10th

The notebook thing happened again tonight, worse than before. I got up
for water around midnight, and the kitchen light was on, and she was
there, still in her nightgown, writing so fast her wrist must have been
screaming. The pages weren’t even lining up right anymore. Some of the
words ran over each other like she was writing two sentences at once and
couldn’t tell them apart.

I stood in the hallway for a second before she noticed me. Just a
second. Long enough to see her lips moving along with the pen, mouthing
words I couldn’t hear, like she was reading something out loud that only
existed as she wrote it.

Nana

She jumped like I’d shot off a firework next to her ear. The notebook
snapped shut so fast the pen went skittering off the table and onto the
floor, and for one weird, unguarded second, before the
cinnamon-and-cardigan grandmother reassembled herself on her face, she
looked at me like she didn’t recognize me. Like I was a stranger who’d
wandered into her kitchen at midnight.

Then it passed. She laughed, called herself a silly old woman, said
something about not being able to sleep and needing to get her thoughts
out before they kept her up all night. She sent me back to bed with a
kiss on the forehead and a promise of pancakes.

I lay awake for an hour after that. Not because I was scared. I want to
be clear about that, for the record, in this diary that I am absolutely
not taking seriously and definitely didn’t just spend an entire page
being honest in.

It’s because of the pen. When it skittered off the table, it rolled
toward me, and I picked it up to hand it back to her.

It was warm. Not room warm. Held warm. Like it had been in someone’s
hand for hours, except she’d only been writing for the length of time it
took me to walk from my room to the kitchen.

I gave it back. I said goodnight. I did not mention the warmth.

I’m mentioning it now, though. To you, diary. Whoever’s reading this
eventually, if anyone ever does.

Wrenhollow is charming. The cake is incredible. My grandmother calls me
little frog and I’ve decided I love it here.

I am also starting to keep this diary for real now. Not because Mom told
me to.

Because I think, somehow, I might need the record.
