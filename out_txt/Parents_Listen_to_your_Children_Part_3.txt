I told him.

A few of you asked in the comments if I would. I did, the morning after
I posted. I’m starting there because it’s the one thing I got right this
month, and I need something on the board before I confess the rest.

I know you waited a while for Part 2, but I’d finished writing it before
the sun came up that Saturday. I sat on it until that night because I
wanted every fact down before I uploaded it. This one took longer. About
a month. Some of y’all have messaged to ask if we’re still alive. We
are. I kept waiting until I understood what was happening before I
posted again, and I’ve given up on that.

It’s Thanksgiving night. I’m back at Stephanie’s kitchen table, same
chair, same cup of coffee gone cold. Eli is asleep on the floor beside
the couch in a sleeping bag her dad lent us. I’ve gotten up to check on
him five times since I started typing. He’s breathing.

I wanted to tell him immediately, but how do you go about telling your
son you killed any chance he had at a sibling? I couldn’t bring myself
to do it for a full day.

That Sunday morning, Stephanie went out for groceries, and Eli and I sat
on her couch with a box of dry Cheerios between us. He ate them one at a
time. I held a handful and didn’t eat any.

I’d practiced it in my head all night. None of it survived the first
sentence.

“Buddy, you know how Mommy was sick?”

He nodded and ate a Cheerio.

“Before she got sick, Mommy had a baby in her tummy. A little sister for
you.”

He stopped chewing. “In her tummy?”

“Right in there.” I had to stop. He waited. He learned patience through
the same thread I did. A dying mother in a room we could not leave.
“Then Mommy got sick, and the doctors said she couldn’t get better and
keep the baby too. So we had to let the baby go.”

“Go where?”

The heat ticked in the wall. He didn’t notice it.

“We named her Lily,” I said.

“Lily.” He tried it out. “Was she gonna be big like me?”

“Big for sure. And bossy. She’d have had your mom’s red hair and her
temper, and she’d have bossed you around something awful.”

He smiled and I hurt. It was the only picture I carried of Lily, and it
was in my head. Just like her mother.

He ate another Cheerio. “I know about her.”

“How, bud?”

“She told me.” He shrugged, like I’d asked how he knew it was Sunday.
“She didn’t know her name, though. I found it. In the box with Mommy’s
glasses.” He picked a Cheerio out of the couch cushion and ate that too.
“Does she get a birthday?”

“I don’t know, Eli.”

I said it, and I was surprised at how true it was.

He leaned into my side, the way he does when he’s done with a subject,
and said into my sleeve, “She can hear you now, Daddy.”

“Hear me how?”

“’Cause you said it out loud.” He reached into the box. “You have to say
it out loud.”

Stephanie got back around noon with four bags of groceries and a full
report on parking downtown.

“Okay, so the meters on Main are broken again, which, fine, whatever,
but then this guy in a Silverado literally parks across two spots, on
purpose, I’m not even kidding, and I almost—” She stopped with the milk
halfway into the fridge and looked at me. “Are you okay? No offense, but
you look like you got hit by a truck.”

“Allergies,” I said.

“In October?”

“I’m allergic to October.”

“Mm-hm.” She didn’t buy it, but she let it go. She’s good at that. She
went and sat on the floor next to Eli. “Okay, what are we drawing?”

“Doug.”

She leaned over to look. He’d given her pet betta fish about forty more
teeth than it actually had. “Oh my God. He’s gonna be so flattered. Look
at you, you’re terrifying.”

-–

Steph didn’t ask me again if I was okay. Not out loud. But that evening
she stood in the doorway with her overnight bag and didn’t leave.

“You sure? Because I can literally sleep on the floor, I don’t care. I
slept in a tent at a music festival for three days in the rain. I’m
built different.”

“We’re fine, Steph. Go home to your parents’ place.”

“Okay. Okay.” She still didn’t move. “Oh, and the fridge makes this
noise at night like it’s dying? It’s not. Probably. Gus won’t replace
it, obviously, because Gus would rather die himself. Just ignore it.”

“Will do.”

“Text me if anything happens. Like, anything.”

“Go home, Steph.”

She left. Eli fell asleep on the couch around nine. I’d tucked the Power
Rangers blanket under his chin, and I lay on the floor beside him and
listened to the building. The pipes knocked. Somebody’s TV came through
the wall. A truck downshifted out on Main. I named every sound in my
head, one at a time, like telling beads.

Around midnight, the refrigerator kicked on.

It was an old Frigidaire, older than Stephanie, I was sure. It hummed
with a tune you could feel in your fillings. I heard it start, and I
heard it settle, and then, underneath it, I heard a little voice say,
“Daddy?”

It didn’t come from the couch. Eli was asleep with his mouth open and
both fists knotted in the blanket. It came from the kitchen, from
somewhere behind the fridge. The same small voice I’d heard under the
house, the voice of somebody who hadn’t used it much.

“Thank you for telling him.”

I didn’t answer. I was scared, sure, but mostly I didn’t know who I’d be
answering: Lily, or something that had learned to sound like her the way
it learned my father’s cough.

I got up, walked into the kitchen, and pulled the plug out of the wall.

The hum died. The kitchen ticked. I stood there in the dark holding the
cord until my hand cramped, and nobody said anything else.

In the morning, Stephanie let herself in with two coffees. She handed me
one, opened the fridge, and stood there.

“Why is my milk warm?”

“Long story.”

She looked at me, then at the plug lying on the floor. “Why is my fridge
unplugged? Is this—okay. Is this a long story I don’t want to know?
Because I can totally not know. I’m great at not knowing.”

“It’s that kind.”

“Cool. Love that.” She poured the milk down the sink and plugged the
fridge back in, with a look that said she’d be asking about it later.
“I’m gonna go get more milk.”

-–

By Monday night, I had a rule of my own.

No hums.

I turned Stephanie’s thermostat to OFF and taped over it. Mind you, it
was thirty-four degrees outside.

“Are we camping?” Eli asked from the couch, in his coat and hat.

“Inside camping.”

“Is that a real thing?”

“It is now.”

He thought that was the funniest thing he’d ever heard. I moved the food
to a cooler on the fire escape and unplugged the fridge, the microwave,
the TV, the bathroom fan, and the little pump that kept Stephanie’s
betta fish alive. I moved the fish into a mixing bowl. Doug took it
pretty well.

That night we ate cold cereal on the floor in our coats, by flashlight,
and Eli asked if we could do Lily’s birthday.

“Tonight?”

“You don’t know her birthday,” he said, like it was the easiest thing in
the world. “So it can be tonight.”

I couldn’t argue with that. There was a honey bun in the cooler from the
gas station, and Stephanie had birthday candles in her junk drawer, the
skinny striped kind. I stuck one in the middle of the honey bun and lit
it with a kitchen match.

“How old is she?”

I did the math, and then I wished I hadn’t. “She’d be two.”

He sang to her. The whole song, loud and off-key, the way his mother
used to sing in the car. I came in on the last line. He blew out the
candle, broke the honey bun in half, and set her half on a paper towel
by the couch, and neither of us said a word about it.

In the morning it was still there. I left it.

The streetlight outside the window hums. The coolers at the Dollar
General hum. So does the ice machine at the gas station. The highway, a
mile off, makes one long low note all night that I had never noticed in
my life. I’d lie there at two in the morning and hear the whole town
humming at my son, and I’d think, I can’t unplug the highway.

Tuesday morning, I went down to the hardware store for batteries.

It takes up the whole ground floor under Stephanie’s apartment, and it
looks like nobody’s dusted since it opened: nails by the pound, mason
jars, a bucket under a water-stained ceiling tile right next to the
counter. The business license taped up behind the counter said GHASSAN
[REDACTED]. The name tag on the man under it said GUS. He was fifty or
so, heavyset, with a gray mustache and a string of worry beads he kept
clicking through his fingers. He watched me set twelve packs of D
batteries and four camping lanterns on the counter, and he didn’t ring
up any of it.

“You,” he said. “Second floor.”

“That’s me. I’m Jeff.” I held out my hand. “Stephanie’s letting us stay
a while. Me and my boy.”

He looked at my hand and kept clicking the beads. “Stephanie lease say
one person. Only Stephanie.”

“It’s just through the holidays. She’s staying at her folks’, and I’m
covering her rent in the meantime.”

“Through the holidays.” He said it like he was pricing it. “You know
what I can do? I call the sheriff. I tell him, there is man living in my
building, not on my lease. Friday, you are out.”

I didn’t mention that the sheriff already had my number. I let out a
nervous chuckle instead.

“Okay. Or?”

“Or.” He finally rang up the lanterns. “Four hundred. Cash. Every first
of the month, on top of her rent. Then you are guest.”

“Do guests get a mint on the pillow?”

“Guest don’t get evicted.” He bagged the batteries. “I don’t care what
is your trouble. I don’t care why you are here. I care for my building.”

“Speaking of your building,” I said. “The thermostat upstairs. I shut it
off, and the heat keeps coming on.”

“Because it does nothing.” He shrugged. “Nobody’s does. Whole building,
I wire straight to the furnace. Sixty-eight, all day, all night. Before,
people turn it up to seventy-five, I pay. Pipes freeze in January, I
pay. Now nobody touch it.”

“What if I need it off?”

“Then you need different building.” He pushed the bag across the
counter. “And tell your friend she still owe me for the window.”

“What window?”

“She know what window.”

I took my batteries upstairs.

I was getting Eli a juice box out of the cooler when the register behind
the couch started to tick, then to breathe, then to hum.

Eli was on the floor, coloring. He stopped.

Stephanie had told me about this, but believing somebody and watching it
happen are two different animals. He just went still, the crayon resting
on the paper, and tilted his head to the left, the way a dog does when
it hears its name from another room. His eyes were on the register.

“Eli.”

Nothing.

I said his name again and started across the room, and halfway across
it, the air changed. It got heavier. Warmer. It smelled like the cab of
my father’s truck: Winstons, cracked vinyl, the pine tree air freshener
he never once replaced. I hadn’t smelled it in three years. My knees
almost went.

“Selah,” Eli said, clear as anything.

I didn’t know the word. Then he said it again, softer, like he was
calling to a friend across a classroom, and I understood it was a name.

I knelt down and took his face in both hands. His cheeks were warm. He
looked right through me at the register.

His hand never stopped moving. He’d been drawing the whole time, and
while I knelt there, I watched him. He drew a house, a regular kid
house: a square, a triangle roof, a chimney with a curl of smoke. Then
he drew another house under it, below the grass line, the same shape but
upside down, its roof pointing down into the dirt. In the strip between
the two, between the floor of one and the ceiling of the other, he drew
a man lying flat on his back with his arms out in a crucifix, like
somebody had laid him there.

Downstairs, the furnace shut off. The register ticked and went quiet.

Eli blinked. He looked down at his drawing, then up at me. “Why are you
on the floor?”

“What’d you draw, bud?”

“A house.” He tapped the top one with his crayon.

“What about this one?” I put my finger on the upside-down house under
the grass.

He frowned at it. “I didn’t draw that.”

“Who’s Selah?”

“Who?”

I let it go. He asked for two juice boxes, and I gave him two. He set
one beside his paper, unopened, next to the man lying flat in the dirt.
Then he climbed into my lap with the other one, which he hadn’t done
since he was four, and stayed there till it was gone.

The smell of my father’s truck hung around for an hour. After it faded,
I found a fresh burn on the arm of Stephanie’s couch, small and round,
the size of a cigarette end. The fabric around it was still warm.

Nobody smokes in that apartment. Stephanie doesn’t. I don’t. Gus would
hand me my own ass if I started.

My father was a filthy smoker and a filthy liar. He cracked the window
only an inch, and we could smell it on him even when we tried to ignore
it.

That night, Eli wanted to sleep in his coat.

“You’re going to get hot, bud.”

“I’m cold.” He pulled the Power Rangers blanket up to his nose. Then,
from under it: “Did Papa smoke?”

I was sitting on the floor with my back against the couch. I didn’t turn
around. “Yeah. He did.”

“In his truck?”

“In his truck.”

“With the window down.”

“Just a crack,” I said. “So nobody would smell it on him.” Everybody did
anyway.

He was quiet so long I thought he’d gone to sleep. Then he said, “He
says sorry about Stephanie’s couch.”

I sat there until his breathing went slow. Then I got up and ripped the
tape off the thermostat, since apparently it didn’t do a damn thing, and
stood in front of the register for a while, listening to it not hum.
Then I sat back down and didn’t sleep.

That was the first week. The next few went about the same.

My company gave me family leave, which is a nice way of saying they
didn’t know what else to do with me. Eli’s teacher emailed worksheets,
and we did them at Stephanie’s kitchen table by lantern light, adding
apples and coloring in vowels. Stephanie came by most days and talked
the whole time she was there, and she started leaving sandwiches in the
cooler with my name on them in Sharpie, because I kept forgetting to
eat. On the first of the month, I paid Gus his four hundred in cash. He
counted it twice, right in front of me, and never once said good
morning.

I covered Stephanie’s rent and her bills, plus a little extra, since she
was the one sleeping in her childhood bedroom at her parents’ house so
we’d have somewhere to go. She fought me on the extra every single time.

“You overpaid me again,” she said one afternoon, holding up her phone.

“No, I didn’t.”

“You literally did. I can do math. I’m in nursing school.”

“Then you know what groceries cost.”

She stared at me. “That’s not—okay, that’s actually a good point. Thank
you. I hate it.”

The heat came on whenever it wanted to. Some days nothing happened. Some
days Eli went still in the middle of a sentence and finished it in a
voice that wasn’t quite his, and came back hungry, and didn’t remember
any of it. I stopped telling Stephanie. She stopped asking.

I don’t think I’ve slept a full night since October.

-–

Torres called the Tuesday before Thanksgiving.

“How’s the boy?” was the first thing he said.

“He’s okay.”

“He with you?”

“Stephanie took him to the store.”

“Huh.” He let that sit just long enough for me to hear it.

“You sound like a man who hasn’t slept in a month.”

“I haven’t.”

“Yeah,” he said. “Me neither.”

“Everything we bagged is at the county station,” he said. “The
ultrasound, too. It’s locked up. Nobody’s looking at it but me.”

“Okay.”

“I tried the Okafors. Both numbers are dead. I mailed a letter to their
last address, down in Georgia, a couple weeks back.” A pause. “Came back
yesterday. Return to sender.”

“So nobody knows anything.”

“Somebody knows something. People don’t leave a house like that and
forget to forward their mail.” Another pause, longer. I could hear him
deciding how to put the next part. “County engineer came out. He’s
calling your foundation a collapse hazard. They want to fill the whole
thing with gravel Monday morning. Tunnel, rooms, all of it.”

“What happens to our stuff down there?”

“It gets buried.”

I thought about my wife’s sweater under a dump truck’s worth of crushed
rock. My father’s cardigan. Eli’s little hoodie with his name in front
of it.

“I want our things back,” I said.

He was quiet long enough that I thought the call had dropped. “That
house is sealed,” he said finally. “Nobody goes in without the county
signing off. That’s procedure.”

Then, after a while: “I’m on duty Thursday. Nobody at the county’s going
to be looking at that house on Thanksgiving.”

“Is that procedure too?”

“No,” he said. “But after Monday, there’s no getting anything out.”

I didn’t tell him about the burn on the couch, or the drawing. I’d
folded that up and put it in my back pocket.

That afternoon, Eli came and stood by my chair with his hands in his
coat pockets.

“Daddy, where’s my blue hoodie?”

“It’s at the old house, bud.”

“It’s on my chair,” he said.

I knew which chair. I’d seen it there, laid over the back with the
sleeves on the armrests and a card with his name on it in front.

“I know,” I said.

He nodded and went back to his coloring. I sat there with Torres still
in my ear. It gets buried.

-–

On Wednesday, the day before Thanksgiving, I went looking into the
house.

Stephanie took Eli to the park, on foot.

“No bathrooms with hand dryers,” I told her at the door.

“Like… at Target?”

“Like anywhere. No gas stations. No cars. Nothing that hums.”

“Nothing that—okay. Okay. You’re being so weird right now, but okay.”

“Promise me, Steph.”

“I promise, I promise. Oh my God.” She held out her hand to Eli. “Come
on, bud. Your dad’s being a weirdo.”

The courthouse is three blocks from Stephanie’s. St. Anne’s sits on the
second block. I crossed to the other side of the street before I got to
it.

The courthouse had closed at noon for the holiday, so I walked on across
the square to the library. They keep the county’s old deed books and
newspapers on microfilm in a back room called Local History, which
turned out to be two volunteers, a coffee maker, and a laminated sign
that said PLEASE DO NOT REWIND OTHER PATRONS’ FILM.

The volunteers were Doris and Kaylee. Doris was about seventy, with a
cardigan buttoned up to her throat that made me look away for a second.
She’d been the county recorder for thirty-one years and had retired, she
said, “strictly on paper.” Kaylee was maybe seventeen, in a hoodie, with
her phone in one hand.

I told them the address.

“The old Hollis place,” Doris said, before I’d finished saying it.

Kaylee didn’t look up from her phone. “She does that.”

“I do not do that.”

“You did it to the chicken farm guy.”

“That was the Mabry place. Everybody knows the Mabry place.” Doris was
already pulling a binder off the shelf. She flipped pages without
looking at them. “Hollis, 1911. Then a bank, then an estate. Then
Dunleavy, Pruitt, Okafor.” She laid the printout on the table like a
hand of cards. “My mother wouldn’t drive past that house. She’d go clear
around by the river road.”

“Why?”

Doris thought about it. “She said it was always suppertime out there.”

“That’s so creepy,” Kaylee said.

“It’s local color.”

“It’s literally a ghost story, Doris.”

“What else do people say about it?” I asked.

That was a mistake. Doris sat down like she’d been waiting thirty-one
years for somebody to ask.

“Well. The one everybody’s mother told them was that Ezra killed his
family. Took a hatchet to Ruth and the boys and buried them under the
kitchen floor.”

“Did anybody ever actually check?” Kaylee said.

“Honey, nobody checks. The truth’s never the better story.” Doris held
up a second finger. “Then there’s the Dunleavy version. Their kids used
to swear he dug himself a hole under the house and bricked himself up in
the walls, and nobody ever found him because he’s still in there.”

“Sounds like a contractor I used to work for,” I said.

Kaylee snorted. Doris didn’t.

Doris held up a third finger. “And my cousin Darlene swears up and down
she saw a man in a farmer’s cap standing on that porch in 1987. Says he
waved at her. She thinks he never left. She thinks he’s still out there,
living in it.”

“That would make him, like, a hundred and fifty.”

“A hundred and three. He was born in ’84.”

“That’s not the point, Doris.”

“Then there’s the Facebook group,” Kaylee said, to me now, like she’d
finally found something interesting about me. “Haunted [Redacted]
County. One lady on there thinks the house is a portal. Somebody else
says Satanists. One guy thinks it’s the government.”

“The government,” Doris said.

“He thinks everything is the government.”

“Which one do y’all believe?” I said.

They looked at each other. Doris straightened her cardigan.

“I believe my mother drove the long way around for forty years,” she
said, “and she wasn’t a woman who scared easy.”

Okafor, 2007. Pruitt, 1989. Dunleavy, 1961.

The same names as the jars.

Kaylee set me up at a microfilm reader the size of a washing machine and
showed me how to thread it, which did wonders for my pride. She did it
with a calm and ease that gave me hope for the generation coming up.
Some old crafts may never die after all.

“It eats film if you look at it wrong,” she said. “So, like, don’t.”

I sat back there for three hours. Here’s what stuck.

October 1918: Thomas and Samuel Hollis, sons of Ezra and Ruth Hollis,
killed in France nine days apart. There was a photograph of the two of
them in uniform. They’d lied about their ages, like a lot of boys did
back in the day.

February 1919: Ruth Hollis, influenza.

March 1919, a few lines on page four. Ezra Hollis hadn’t been seen in
town for two weeks. A neighbor who drove out to check on him found the
house locked and the table set for four, supper still on the plates. The
neighbor said that all winter, Hollis had set the table for his whole
family every night, and that you could hear him talking into the
stovepipe when the wind was up. The sheriff searched the farm and the
outbuildings, dragged the pond, and found nothing.

They never did find him. I looked. There’s no obituary and no grave. He
freezes in March 1919.

I almost left. Kaylee leaned in the doorway, still on her phone.

“You want the newer stuff, too? It’s all on the computer after, like,
2000.”

“He doesn’t want the newer stuff,” Doris called from the other room.
“Nothing happened after 2000.”

“Everything happened after 2000.”

I said sure, because I’m the kind of person who reads the whole thing.

I typed in Okafor, the last name on Doris’s list, and cross-referenced
it with the address. I got an old real estate listing, a tax notice, and
an obituary from 2019.

Selah Grace Okafor, age 7, died at home.

That was most of it. Beloved daughter. Loved to draw. In lieu of
flowers, gifts to the children’s library. Above it was a school picture
of a little girl with her hair up in two puffs and a front tooth
missing, grinning like the photographer had just told her a joke. She
had a pink barrette in her hair, shaped like a butterfly.

I said her name out loud once, under my breath, the way Eli had.

The microfilm reader hummed a tune. I knew that noise all too well.

-–

Stephanie and Eli got back right after I did. His cheeks were red from
the cold. He went straight to the bathroom, and Stephanie waited until
the door shut, and then it all came out at once.

“Okay, so. He made a friend? Kind of? We were there two hours, and he
spent most of it over by that big green electrical box at the edge of
the playground, the one with the warning sticker, and I kept going over
there like, Eli, bud, come on, swings, and he’d come for like a minute
and then go right back. Just sitting in the grass. Talking.”

I could hear it in my head, the low, steady hum those boxes make. I’d
told her no hums. I hadn’t thought to say transformers.

“Talking to who?”

“That’s what I asked! And he said a girl. He said she used to live in
his room, and she misses her mom. And I looked everywhere, and there was
not a kid within, like, a hundred yards of him. So I figured, okay,
imaginary friend, cute, whatever.” She pulled her hat off and twisted it
in both hands. “Then he said she had a butterfly in her hair.”

The bathroom door opened. Eli came out drying his hands on his coat.

“Hey, bud,” I said. “Who were you playing with at the park?”

He shrugged. “Nobody.”

“Stephanie says you were talking to a girl.”

He looked at Stephanie, then at me, with the face kids make when the
grown-ups are being weird. “I was playing by myself.” Then he went to
find his crayons.

For once, Stephanie’s mouth gaped open without a word following.

-–

Stephanie’s family does Thanksgiving at her parents’ house, a
split-level out on the county road with a flagpole in the yard and a
deer made of white Christmas lights. They’d invited us before I could
think of a way to say no. Her mom, Linda, met us at the door. She hugged
Eli, then hugged me, then held on to my arms and looked me over like a
nurse checking for a fever.

“So you’re Jeff,” she said. “Steph won’t tell us a thing. Where are
y’all from? What happened at your house? Was it a gas leak? The Wilsons
had a gas leak.”

“Mom,” Stephanie said.

“I’m just asking.”

“It’s a long story, ma’am,” I said. “Mostly it’s an old house with a lot
of opinions.”

“Old houses,” Linda said, and took our coats.

The house was full. Stephanie’s dad, Frank, came up out of his recliner
and shook my hand like we’d known each other twenty years. “Get you a
beer? Lions are already down ten. Every year it’s a tragedy.” Her
brother Josh was there with his two, Tanner and Maddie, eight and five,
who took one look at Eli and dragged him off toward the basement stairs.

“The big TV’s down there,” Stephanie said. Then she caught my face and
called after them, “Hey—upstairs, guys. Take the Switch.”

Frank was looking at me now. “Steph says you’re a mason.”

“Yes sir.”

“You do that new plant out off 54?”

“Most of the north wall.”

“No kidding.” He clapped me on the shoulder. “Hell of a building. Josh,
you hear that? He built the plant.”

Before dinner, I went down the hall to wash up. On my way back, I heard
them in the kitchen.

“—just look at him, Steph. That man hasn’t slept in a week.”

“He hasn’t, Mom.”

“So what happened? Is he in some kind of trouble? Is the boy okay?
Where’s the mom?”

“Mom. That’s not mine to tell.”

“He’s living in your apartment,” Frank said. It was the first edge I’d
heard in his voice all day. “That makes it a little bit our business.”

“Dad.”

“I’m only asking.”

“And I’m only not answering.”

“Your brother’s setting Carol’s plate again,” her mom said, quieter.

“Let him,” Frank said. “It’s Thanksgiving.”

Somebody opened the oven. I went back to the living room before anybody
came out.

Ray was in the corner chair by the window, seventy or so, in a sport
coat that had fit him better a few years back. He was Frank’s older
brother. You could see it in the jaw. He was watching the game without
watching it. I sat on the end of the couch nearest him because it was
the only seat left.

“You’re the boy’s dad,” he said.

“Yes sir.”

He nodded at my hand. I’d been turning my wedding ring with my thumb
without noticing. “How long?”

It took me a second to understand what he was asking. “Two years.”

He turned his own ring once around his finger, the same way. “Carol. Six
years in February. I sold the house after and moved in here with Frank.”
He tipped his head toward the recliner. “He keeps telling people it’s
temporary.”

I didn’t tell him I was sorry. I’d had two years of people telling me.

“She lost her ring the spring before she passed,” he said. “Took it off
to do the dishes and set it down someplace, and that was that. I tore
our old house apart looking. Twice. Everything I didn’t sell is in boxes
out in Frank’s garage now, and I’ve been through those, too.” He looked
out the window at the garage. “Linda says I ought to let it go.”

“Have you?”

“No.” He went back to the game.

I thought about a table full of jars under my house, one of them with
our name on it, and I didn’t say anything else.

At dinner, her uncle Ray had set a place for Carol at the corner of the
table: a plate, a fork, a folded napkin, an empty chair. Nobody said a
word about it. Somebody passed him the rolls.

Torres was picking me up at two, so I didn’t sit. I stood behind Eli’s
chair with my hands on his shoulders and kept my eyes on him while Frank
thanked God for the food and the family and “the company of new
friends.”

Eli had his head down and his eyes squeezed shut, the way his mother
taught him.

Out front, Torres tapped his horn once, polite.

I crouched down by his chair. “I’ve got to go get our stuff, bud. I’ll
be back before pie.”

“From the house?”

“From the house.”

He looked under the table for a while. “Can you get my hoodie?”

“I’ll get it.”

“Papa says it’s cold down there,” he said. “He says bring a coat.”
