Original Post ~ Part List

Content Warning: >!Child Harm!<

I think we all expected more fanfare when trying to open the grand set
of double doors that my thread trailed into. It was clear by its
grandeur that the barrier acted as a threshold from our plane into
whatever one the triad of witches had created. Surely entering into
their cosmic vanity project would have come equipped with some sort of
welcoming ceremony.

When we pushed, however, the doors heaved open without issue, slow and
stiff at first, then coming alive and carrying the weight away on their
own. Their waking hinges yawned with a soft groan that echoed out into
the space lying beyond, and I swallowed when I saw how vast it was.

No longer were we in the tight floral halls. The smell of moist, dank
earth wafted over us like a crashing wave, accented by the slightest
tinge of rot. We were outside, it seemed, though you wouldn’t be remiss
for thinking otherwise. Like the halls, darkness and the air around us
seemed married to one another, snuffing our flashlights before they
could travel more than a couple yards.

Immediately from the other side of the door, a patio of cracked bricks
caked with moss and lichen stretched out before us then descended
downward through a grand set of stairs. A railing adorned with small,
sculpted statues followed alongside it, but each was crumbled and
weathered away beyond interpretation—far more decrepit than anything
even in the Red Manor. Did time move differently here in this pocket of
reality woven from souls? If so, how much longer had this place been
laying in ruin than the home it was carved from? If there ever had been
a welcome ceremony, we were several decades late.

Our flashlights revealed that the steps down led to an intricately
pattered road of brick leading away into the dark that was just as
battered as the patio, but any other tells of what the land here was
like were concealed by the ebony curtain. I suppose that in itself was a
tell. Whatever had happened to this place, it had caused the light that
graced it to go out.

I’d be inclined to believe that light never had graced this place to
begin with (both literally and figuratively), and it was simply the way
it was meant to be, but there were signs that stated otherwise. We
couldn’t see directly in front of us, but speckling the horizon, there
was strange patches of dull light that graced the wastelands, punching
through the sky like spotlights to focus on points of interest. Their
source was nowhere to be seen up in the black sky, just dull luminary
pools on the ground like lamps framing artefacts in a dim museum.

The one nearest to us, maybe a few miles off in the distance, was lower
in elevation, informing us that we were on a hill or mountain of some
kind. In its beam lived inorganic terrain—a city composed of stacked
stone monuments made of white marble and trimmed with gold, the way
Roman temples were depicted in paintings or even something as grand as
the gardens of Babylon. From so far away and beneath the dim golden
light, you could almost be convinced that the city was beautiful, but
there were clear breaks in the grandeur that showed ruin: towers that
appeared crumbed and shattered into rubble.

Further beyond and speckling all sides in varying distances from one
another were more light patches, their beams illuminating nothing but
dense forest. From the way the light danced through their branches, it
was easy to see that most of them were dead or in rot. Almost all of
them. Strangely, there were a few spots that looked weirdly alive
despite the surrounding death; One patch was divided near perfectly down
the middle by a line, one side lush and healthy, the other brittle and
naked with gnarled black bramble.

No matter the condition of it all, one thing remained the same. It was
dead silent. No wind, no animals, no voices. Just a rotting, dark husk
of a place.

The only other point of note, positioned on a mountain directly opposite
of us, was a tower. Not far up the hill from the city, a spire rose from
a breathtaking castle that’s top barely crested into a circle of light
before being swallowed by the darkness below. Compared to everything
else, it seemed to stand untouched by time.

Its location across the valley, looming above the city, made it clear
that the place was meant to be a centerpiece, even before the sun left
the land. A crown atop creation to display who presided over it all.
There was no question of who those persons might be. The tower seemed to
almost eagerly stare at us like the slit of a serpent’s eye against the
rounded shape of the yellow pool.

I thought I would feel fear or have my breath taken away by the sight,
but instead I found myself completely shocked. Overwhelmed by the sheer
scale of what awaited us. I had known and been reminded several times
that the witches were building a land of their own, but for some reason
the weight of what that entailed hadn’t hit me until I was fully staring
over its vast expanse.

Slowly, though, as I took in what it had become, that shock wore off in
favor of rage. All of those lives lost, all the sacrifices of innocents
committed to build this place—it had all been for nothing. No, Less than
nothing.

There was no ‘personal heaven’ anymore; now this was hell. A city in the
valley implied some sort of civilization here at some point, but the
lack of noise meant that they had long passed, surely suffering on their
way out from whatever apocalypse had ravaged the land. The children used
to make this place didn’t get to live among it like the witches had
promised; instead, their souls were bound to wander the loom for all
eternity, never finding rest from their pain. Hell, not even the vain
and vile goals of this place’s creators—if you could even stomach
counting them getting their way as a ‘victory’—had been fulfilled.
Surely they were suffering just as much as the others; why else would
they go through such lengths just to find us?

And then there was that. All of that suffering, and it still wasn’t
enough. There was so much of it that it leaked through the seams,
bursting into the real world to ruin our lives too. To take Mindy and
Casey and Mrs. Thatcher then torment us for endless, sleepless days and
nights.

It was a frustration so strong that it propelled me onward down the path
with a different vigor than I’d come in with. I couldn’t believe that I
had almost come in here with the intent to give the sisters what they
wanted. Now I felt closer to how my friends did—to what Kait had said. I
wanted to storm our way over to that tower and climb to its top where I
knew we’d find the witches. I wanted to rip their lives from their
precious loom the way they had done to so many others.

I turned back to see if my friends had the same vigor, and was certain
they did. In the faint afterglow of their flashlights, the looks on all
of their faces was one of grit. This had gone on for long enough, and it
was time to end this.

Once they’d arrived, I had gotten an earful from Lacey, Carly and Bryce
just as equal as the one Kait had given me, but given that I was still
in a sorry, sullen state upon their arrival, they cooled off fast.
Besides, if this new course of action was going to work for us, there
was no time to waste bickering, and that went for my stubbornness too.
The witches would soon realize that I was no longer here to turn myself
in, and when that happened, I was sure they’d have a fresh beast to send
our way; most likely the grand finale they had planned for if I hadn’t
shown.

We had mastered the art of holding a choke point against the monsters in
a cramped, fortified basement. I didn’t want to count our odds against
such a foe in this open valley where vision was a luxury we couldn’t
afford.

Luckily, even if we failed and something new tried to break loose, our
plan so far had worked. Back down the halls and on the other side of the
red door, the sheriff had rallied his deputies to stand guard, and Carly
and Bryce had actually pulled through, getting a news station to come
out and investigate the mysterious manor. There was no telling what on
earth anyone would make of it, or if the station would even run the odd
story that they might assume was some kind of hoax, but so long as it
was in the publics eye somewhere where it couldn’t be erased, I didn’t
care. The red door was known now, and though I hated to thrust its
responsibilities onto someone else, there would soon be many lines of
defense to hold against it.

I think this is what we were all thinking as we watched the double doors
slowly drift themselves shut behind us, ending in a prominent yet soft
echoing thud as they met in the middle. I wasn’t even sure if we would
be able to open them again from this side, but by now, I think we had
all found a way to accept that this would most likely be a one-way trip.
We would rather die together than live knowing that lives were traded
for the other’s sakes.

“You’re sure the sheriff won’t follow us in if we don’t come back?” I
asked in a low tone. Even the soft utterance felt like I was sounding an
alarm out into the inert valley, and I hoped the echo of the doors that
was still ringing would mask it.

Lacey nodded then jabbed, “It took a lot of convincing, no thanks to
your ‘clever’ little set up, but he understood. If we don’t come back,
he knows that guarding the door is life and death for everyone outside.”

“What about the camera crew?”

“No telling what they’ll make of this,” Carly shrugged, “I can’t believe
we actually got them to come out, but now that they’re here, I’m sure
they’ll report on it, even if they think its all just a hoax.”

“Just getting it into public eye is the important thing,” Kait said, “So
long as this can’t be buried by any force—supernatural or
otherwise—that’s all we need.”

There was a long beat of silence as we all glanced back to the doors
again. The building they were attached to looked like some kind of grand
cathedral, though, the building was just as tattered as everything else
in the valley. Bryce finished running his beam over its surface before
he skimmed back out into the vast expanse. Then, he thought twice about
the beacon he was creating for anything that might be out there, and he
aimed it once more to the ground.

“So this place is it? This is what they killed all of those children
for?” he spoke.

I scanned around for Lucy, who’s form had faded not long after my
friends had shown up, but she was nowhere to be found. She either
couldn’t follow us in here, or didn’t currently want to. With a sigh, I
nodded, “It seems that way.”

“I hope it was worth it,” Kait scoffed, running her hand over one of the
destroyed head busts on the railing, “really looks like they got their
money’s worth.”

“Probably did at one point,” Carly said, casting her gaze over the light
speckled valley, “Before it fell apart, it looks like they really had
gone all out.”

“Yeah, and I can’t help but notice the only thing still intact is the
castle above it all,” Lacey scowled, eyes fixing on the tower, “I
suppose there’s no mystery as to where we need to head.”

I nodded, “I’d wager we’ll find them there, too.”

Bryce shook his head, “You would think with everything we’ve been
through this wouldn’t be so weird to me, but… fuck, how is any of this
possible? This is like something straight out of a dream.”

“Or a nightmare,” Kait corrected.

“What do you think is out there?” Bryce continued, “The monsters they
send—you don’t think they roam freely?”

I thought on the question, but then shook my head, “It doesn’t seem
likely. Them only showing when the clock chimed made it seem more like a
spell of theirs. I’m sure there’s a method to the madness.”

That answer seemed to reassure everyone, but there was still a good
couple miles of hiking downhill to get to the first stretch of light
lingering over the city, and within the darkness between, there could be
anything hiding there.

Lacey heaved the shotgun in her hands and took the lead, pushing past
me. While she must have felt the same frustration I did from this place,
she was still boiling within about the way the witches had used her
brother’s corpse, and she wasn’t going to let any demon, no matter how
horrifying, stop her from making them pay. I was quick to join her side.

And just like that, we were off on our quest, following the once ornate
brick road whose serpentine body had become a jumble of debris and
tripping hazards. From the darkness, the tall lurking figures of twisted
cast iron lamp posts watched us, their vacant glass eyes curiously
wondering who the new intruders were.

I suddenly felt like I was in the show that had so firmly stuck in my
mind as a child. The one that I had never seen the ending to before my
father’s recliner permanently branded me a waste of space across the
back of my hand. Young kids in a fantasy land traveling into the unknown
to stop an evil witch. Only this wasn’t a show, and this place was no
fantasy. This was a real, hellish nightmare, and unlike the Saturday
morning cartoons that dictated the heroes must always win, there were no
such stipulations for us.

Our dread kept mounting the longer we pressed on down the road, but no
threats ever revealed themselves to us, which in turn only fueled that
anxiety more. This land hadn’t been turned into a total wasteland by its
collapse, it was somehow much sadder: it was completely abandoned. All
around us were signs of a once intelligent civilization—benches, signs
written in odd, flowy script, small houses in the distance that the
fingers of our light could just barely reach—and yet a soul never turned
up, friendly or otherwise.

The first living thing we came across nearly made us jump out of our
skin, and I respected Lacey greatly for the restraint of her trigger
finger. We had been walking for nearly an hour when suddenly, our lights
caught a glimpse of two glowing orbs hanging nearly eight feet off the
ground ahead. We froze, and Lacey raised her weapon, but as we stalked
closer to pull it into our light and see what threat we were up against,
our fear turned to confusion.

The creature did not look like the beasts we fought, but it was also
nothing that was known to us. It appeared to be some sort of cross
between an elk and horse, with thick fur that was a robin egg blue and a
fluffy ringed tail that curved up and over its back like a scorpion. Its
face was thin, long and uncanny, and as it turned fully to face us, I
saw something open on its forehead to reveal what I could only assume to
be a third eye, glittering and shining in the dark like a diamond.

The deer thing stared at us, probably just as confused by us as we were
by it, then without making a noise, it pranced off somewhere into the
fields beside us, and we never saw it again.

The grotesque gnarled arms of the dead foliage began to get thicker as
we neared the edge of the city, but thankfully, it wasn’t so dense that
it could be called a forest yet. If we had seen something resembling a
deer, and this place was made to function as its own world, that implied
the existence of a predator, and while I stood by my earlier statement
to Bryce about the monsters from the red door, that didn’t mean there
weren’t any threats at all.

The city had looked beautiful from a distance, even in its ruin, but now
that we were up close, the fear that we had managed to slowly shed
during our walk came rolling down the hills after us to catch back up.
The light seemed to emanate from a single point above the cities center,
and it was barely bright enough to read in, which cast all the
white-stone walls and golden trim pillars in ominous silhouettes.

My museum comparison from earlier suddenly felt awfully accurate; like I
was walking through an exhibit for this extinct place long after the
gallery had closed. I looked up to the dark sky to see if I could find
the sun the glow appeared from, but it was just a dark abyss.

We were all hyper aware of the weight of our steps as they traveled into
the streets away from us before bouncing back. The ruin and layer of
moss covering everything once again confirmed we were alone, but with
the buildings of the city lacking any doors or panes in their windows,
it was hard not to imagine bodies within possibly watching our every
move.

The road we remained on carried us through the heart of the city,
showing off several more statues and once glorious buildings that must
have been meant to impress anyone passing through. Some were of
creatures that I didn’t recognize, others were clearly human women, most
likely busts of the ‘benevolent’ goddesses who made this place. I
wondered if those who lived here were awed by the sculptures every day,
otherwise I didn’t know who they could be for. After all, who was
visiting this place from the outside?

“As much as I hate everything this place stands for,” Kaitlynn began, “I
would be fascinated to know what it was supposed to look like.”

“Clearly they built it with humility,” Bryce mocked, shining his
flashlight on the grand fountain settled in the heart of a square we’d
just entered. Three statues—one of each sister, completely nude and with
regal features—stood on a pedestal, their arms holding cornucopias that
they tilted toward the surrounding pool, clearly meant to spout water as
if they were gracing the land with some sort of bounty. Now, the spouts
were dry, and the water in the basin had collected a film of settled
dust and grime so thick across its surface that it almost looked solid.

The smallest pattering noise from beside us jolted us to attention, and
all at once we whirled and aimed our flashlights. In a doorway only a
few yards away, something pale peeked around the corner, human in shape,
but not in physicality. It was small, with long gnarled hair the color
of ash and skin as pale as snow. Its form was gaunt and starved, bony
hands holding the corner to peer at us, and its face, much like the deer
thing we saw earlier, was a uncanny level of thin.

 Even beneath its gangly, tattered exterior, the odd starry iridescent
orbs in its eye sockets sparkled beautifully in our lights, and there
were hints of an elegance that the creature might have once held. It
wore no clothes and shivered though the air was not cold, and by the
expression it wore on its face, I suddenly got a rush of familiarity at
the sight. The thing looked at us the same way that Lucy often did. This
thing was a child.

None of us moved, afraid that we might startle the child, or that it
might become aggressive should we start too suddenly. We stood that way
eyeing one another until, in the after glow of our beams, another pale
thing appeared. This one was much bigger, some kind of adult, and it
made a small, feral huffing noise before snatching the arm of the child
and dragging it back into the dark.

“My God…” Lacey began, “Were… those the denizens of this place?”

I cautiously took a step closer to the building that they disappeared
into, casting my light through the nearest window and peering inside.
Beyond was a home that I could only guess was once beautiful, but now,
any and all furniture within had been turned over, smashed, or reduced
to parts and scattered about the cold, hard stone. Behind it all,
huddled at a corner in the back, the child was held tight by the adult,
both of them huddled in with a group of four more.

Their eyes squinted in the glow of my flashlight, and they froze
helplessly with trembling fear as I studied them. My mind couldn’t help
but connect them to the deer creature from earlier. They seemed just as
wild and feral with their naked and timidness. If they were the
civilization that once ruled this place, it must have taken countless
generations for them to be reduced to this. That only begged the
question again: How long had this place been left to rot?

That’s when I realized something. Stupidly, I checked the sky as if to
find the suns position before remembering that wasn’t possible. Turning
to my friends, I asked, “Have any of you heard the hour strike since we
got in here?”

I could see of their brains set to work trying to recall if they had.
The last hour went off only about 15 minutes before we’d entered, and
we’d been traveling for well over an hour already. The clock should have
struck by now.

“No,” Lacey returned, letting the implications hang in the air.

Luckily, it seemed time traveled slower here, rather than the other way
around. On the off chance we did make it back out, what a cruel fate it
would have been to return to a world decades in the future.

Just then, another sound erupted through the streets. It crashed off the
walls of the labyrinthine corridors then whirled around the circle of
the square, rattling us each to the core. We’d heard countless beast
cries by this point and shouldn’t have been afraid, but this particular
one gave me chills because it was a reoccurring one.

It was the spider, and this time, we were fully on her turf.

I had hoped that even thought it didn’t kill her on the spot during our
last encounter, the shotgun blast to the skull still would have killed
the woman later. Clearly that wasn’t the case, and that also meant that
any attempt at fighting her right now would only end poorly, no matter
how experienced we were. If a slug at point blank couldn’t smite the
demon, then our axe swings and machetes weren’t going to do much either.

Running was another option, but with the way her wailing was
reverberating off the walls of the silent city, we had no idea where the
spider would appear from, and any direction we went ran the risk of
running straight into her. That left our best option to attempt to hide.

The group seemed to unanimously agree on this without a word as we ran
from the hut before us and dove through the door of the space next door.
Flashing our beams around the room, we found that thankfully this den
was not occupied by the feral denizens of the city, and there were
plenty of debris piles for us to take cover behind. We did so, then
turned back out toward the square, dowsing our lights and becoming one
with the shadows beneath the city’s dull lamp.

A moment later, the gangly pale form of the spider scuttled into view,
clawing her way onto the rim of the fountain and using all fours to
scurry across its edge. She was so much faster in this place than in the
loom back home, and I worried that her senses might somehow be
heightened in a similar way. She obviously knew we were here—why else
would she have arrived—but was she able to sense our exact location?

It didn’t seem like she could. The spider clambered over the fountain
then pounced to the ground, and as she did I noticed the earth around
her hands glowing vibrantly with a silvery light. From the cracked stone
streets, her fingers tangled threads just like when she was weaving
through the loom of the past, though I suppose given that this world was
composed entirely of them, she had a lot more tangibility.

She strummed the fine strings beneath her feet, her dark locks pooling
in the dirt as she nearly pressed her nose into them, like a dog
sniffing up a scent. I could almost hear the muscles in Lacey—who had
ended up crouched beside me—tense in preparation.

For a moment, I thought we were immediately had when the spider’s head
snapped up in our direction. Through her greasy black veil, it was
impossible to tell where her eyes lingered, but with the whole city a
ghost town, there were only a few targets for them to find. I think we
were all about to jump up at the same time as she darted across the
square toward us, but thankfully, we had the good sense to hold our
ground. She was heading next door.

It turned out her eyes had found one of those other targets. In the
abode next door, I heard a violent thud, then the scuffle of debris
getting tossed around. There were strange gasping noises that were
quickly overpowered by the spider’s own screams, then she reappeared.
The empty stone window framed the perfect screen for us to watch the
horror unfold as the woman dragged the child we’d just seen moments ago
into the open.

It turned out that, timid as they were, the adults still had fire within
them. A sharp gasping noise like a cat’s hiss erupted from next door,
and one of the pale humanoids burst forward rushing at the monster that
had its baby by the ankle. We all waited with held breath to see what
was about to happen, knowing how durable this demon was, but we never
could have guessed the outcome.

As casually as someone shooing away a fly, the spider lifted one of her
arms and flicked her hand through the air. The hissing sound from the
denizen died on their lips as their body sagged and elongated, their
flesh bursting and ripping with random seams and unspooling into…
threads.

By the time they reached their target, there was nothing left of their
emaciated form. They’d become nothing but a messy pile of silver threads
like a bale of hay, and as it met the ground, the whole thing was pulled
under, like water leaking into the earth. With a wave of her hand, the
Spider had reduced a living being into nothing but the threads they had
been stitched from.

What lay in store for the child pinned beneath her was even worse.
Instead of being unraveled out of existence, their fate was far more
gruesome. We watched from our spots, paralyzed with repulsion as the
child silently struggled against the spider’s grip, its tiny weak legs
kicking uselessly against the eldritch woman. The long locks almost
concealed the sight as she dangled her head parallel with her victim,
but there was just enough visible for us to see her raise a single hand
and descend it down into the poor kid’s face.

It was over before we could even think of intervening. There was a
squishy crackle of flesh and a panicked gasping as tiny limbs kicked
faster and harder, then, all at once, stopped moving. Only for a moment,
though. With her hand now somehow buried completely in the Child’s
skull, the spider violently wrenched it upward, causing the body to
contort and jitter. Its limbs began to elongate, then do strange things
like curl or inflate. Their skin seemed to bubble and morph like it was
made of latex, but then it changed altogether.

Parts of it mutated. The hands turned dark and sprouted claws before
morphing into some kind of talons. Their body grew bigger like an
adults, then became barreled and rounded like the torso of an animal.
Where its face used to be, I could see light flickering, as if the
spider’s hand was striking a flint against the back of their skull. I
was mortified, and for a long spell I had no idea what I was witnessing.
Then, I noticed the strange sounds coming from the body like recordings
on a speaker, and it hit me.

My first guess had been that the spider was flickering the child’s body
through different forms of life that existed here—reshaping it into
something new. The way it was digging its arm around made it appear like
it was looking for something, though, the same way it could rifle
through the layers of the past back home. It was scouring the child’s
memories. Flipping through the threads of its being to find out what it
knew.

The information she was looking for was obvious. She knew we were here,
and she was trying to see if this child was a witness.

I grabbed Lacey’s arm and leaned so close my lips grazed her ear, “It’s
looking for us.”

She pulled away confused, but the urgency in my voice helped her connect
the dots of the scene, and she nodded, looking back toward the others.
As she heaved her shotgun, I nodded to the door. Judging by Bryce’s
fervent head shake, it was clear they didn’t understand, but there was
no time to get the information to them. The spider was distracted right
now and facing away, but if it eventually found the gold it was digging
for, we’d be cornered in this small house, and we would have to watch
the same scene unfold again from a first person perspective.

In the meager light that glowed in from the window, we somehow crossed
the space without kicking any debris, and the four of us reached the
opening then slip through. In the streets, the horrible squelching
sounds coming from the child’s ever shifting body were even clearer, and
I prayed that she couldn’t do the same to us if we were caught. The lot
of us sidled along the building’s edge with great care, and we were
finally to the corner when the spider made a sharp grunt and sat up
straight.

On the ground, the child had stopped moving, landing on a tall form that
looked startlingly human. No—it was human. A human wearing the same
clothes as me. A memory from moments ago.

We started to run before the spider’s head had even snapped fully in our
direction. Erupting with anger, the sound of shrieks mixed with the
rapid patter of her bare hands across the ground echoed from behind us,
and I chanced a look over my shoulder. The spider had just pounced from
the fountain and landed only fifteen feet away, but somehow even with
how dire the distance was, it wasn’t my focus.

Behind her, I could see the body that had transformed into my visage
start to move independently. It scraped itself off the ground, and once
it was in a full sit, I could see that it wasn’t the perfect copy I
might have thought. Its features were disproportionately off, and it
retained several parts that didn’t belong on a human. It’s slack jaw
sported fangs like a hound, and its eyes were strangely glowing like the
beam of my flashlight.

With a frantic panting that I could hear even over the screams of the
spider, my doppelganger stumbled to all fours, then snapping its head
toward the building it had been dragged from, it sprinted with a
disturbingly hungry speed through the window. I focused ahead after
that.

KOW!!!

The blast of the shotgun beside me nearly made my eardrums explode
within the silence. Even though there was screaming and stomping and
panting already clogging the air, the sheer blast of the cannon would
reach much further, informing the entire lost kingdom that unwelcome
guests had officially arrived.

The shot that Lacey had directed toward the spider didn’t hit its mark
at center mass as we ran, but by some miracle, the slug found a way to
clip the side of the creature’s arm. Indestructible as ever, the bolt
that should have removed the limb did little to stop it, but it
certainly made the woman stumble and lose distance, which had been the
intention.

We skidded around the corner of a street into a crossroads, and suddenly
I felt like I was back in the halls for the first time again—just as
disorienting and dense. My eyes skimmed around for Lucy, praying that
maybe the young spirit might have appeared to help guide us, but I still
didn’t even know if she could enter this place. Instead, we would have
to settle for a different guide.

The motionless form of a palace built high above everything in this
layered cake of a city stared down at us, it’s edges lit by the glow
from above. Without needing to confer, we all pressed onward, taking any
streets that sloped upward and hitching turns along the way to throw the
spider from our trail. A high point would allow us to see out over the
entire city and gather our bearings; it was our best bet.

Behind us, the spider tailed as close and persistent as our shadows.
Living up to her nickname, she didn’t just move across the ground; I
could see her hands digging into the threads that composed the wall,
using them to scale after us and cut through shortcuts that we couldn’t.
Lacey had to fire two more times to keep her at bay, but we had limited
ammo now, and with Lacey unable to get a clear shot, they weren’t doing
much but buying microseconds.

One last crack ripped the sky as Lacey finally took the risk to dead
stop, whirl around, then take aim. With impressive accuracy, the slug
struck the side of the woman’s face, ripping free a chunk of her black
locks that scattered away into air behind her. In the gap in the
curtain, I could see an eye for the first time, bloodshot and dark, a
wild fire behind them.

The blast was disorienting enough to make the woman yield a few extra
steps, just enough for us to make it around the next corner and break
the line of sight. We took an immediate turn to gain even more cover,
then flew up a set of stairs and under a grand looking archway before
the palace at the top of the hill.

The place we’d found ourselves in was a garden of some kind. Like
everything else, it must have been quite a sight in its prime, but now
it was nearly a jungle with how wild and unruly it had become. Bramble
and bushes flooded the paths, but like the wilds surrounding the city,
they were a patchwork of lush, flourishing flora, and gnarled, brittle
branches.

We were just scouring for a path through when something dawned on us.
The screams of the spider were still present, but they were a good
distance away now, and not drawing any closer. Against my better
judgement, I halted my running, then crossed to the edge of the garden
where, through metal bars of an ornate fence, I glanced back down at the
streets.

There in the middle of the road, the pale woman angrily thrashed and
spat guttural, incomprehensible curses at me. Clearly her desire to
obtain us hadn’t changed, but for some strange reason, she wouldn’t move
any closer. My friends joined my side to investigate just in time to see
the spider howl one last time, then clamber away back into the streets
of the city.

Kait put it best, “That can’t be a good sign.”

I turned back out to the garden to see what sort of threat might be out
here to scare her away, but there was nothing but leaves and rotting
planters. None of it so much as even swayed since there was no breeze to
dance to.

“Come on,” I said, heaving my axe and taking the lead, “Let’s move
through here fast, just to be safe.”

The tower on the horizon had gotten away from us, so we reoriented our
path and moved through the gardens, looking for an exit to take us in
that direction. Everyone was on high alert looking through the
overgrowth, jumping at statues speckled throughout or gnarled branches
that reached like hands.

The chase aside, we were all on edge thanks to the scene we’d just
witnessed, and though it probably wasn’t going to help with nerves,
there was a lot we needed to unpack. With no time to stop, we did so
while moving.

“That child… did you all see what she did to it?”

Carly nodded, “And that adult, she just… obliterated them.”

“You don’t think she can do that to us, can she?” Kait asked.

“I’ve never seen her do anything like that,” I noted, “I thought she
could only move around the threads.”

“Here she must have more control over them,” Bryce offered, “This whole
place would be made of the stuff, right?”

I furrowed my brow, something about the spider feeling off to me, “Yeah,
but still, no other monsters we’ve run into have been able to touch the
threads like that, let alone bend them around. She fully changed that
kid—sculpted them into something else like—”

The revelation hit me before I could finish the sentence, and I froze in
my tracks. There might not have been wind, but I felt a cold breeze
ripple through me. How could I have not connected the dots sooner? Maybe
it was because of the spider’s grotesque appearance, or how animalistic
she was like the other beasts, but I somehow hadn’t lined up the
evidence.

I was right; no other monster could manipulate threads, let alone weave
them into something new. The only people with that power were the
witches who had made this place, and while at one point they had been
regal and beautiful, untold centuries trapped in this hell would make
anyone lose their grace. One of those witches also had a feature that
lined perfecting with the spider. The long, raven black hair that flowed
like a curtain.

“Oh my God,” I began slowly, “That’s not just a monster. That woman is
one of the witches. It’s Natellie.”

The group paused at the revelation like I did, the implications setting
in fast. If just that feral beast of a person had so much control over
this place and was immune to our bullets, what chance did we stand at
coming in here and trying to take them down?

We didn’t have long to sink into our despair. Just then, from a doorway
behind us that peered into a garden mausoleum, a voice spoke, crackly
and weathered.

“You met my sister…” it rasped, “I’m afraid time was less kind to her
than even me.”

For a moment, I considered not even regarding whatever lay in the
darkness of the building. The second it spoke, I wanted to corral my
friends forward and out of the garden without looking back. Once the
words registered on me, however, I dared to raise my flashlight into the
space, casting light on the culprit.

There, hanging from the far wall, tangled in bushes and ivy, a woman
hung by her hands and legs. The light couldn’t reach enough to fully
reveal her features, but there were two so vibrant they were illuminated
all on their own. Curly red locks that were gnarled into near knots and
stained brown with dirt, and two bloodshot, pale eyes, their irises so
icy blue I swore they were glowing as they peered at us.

Her lips that were once painted a perfect glossy red were now only the
shade of brown dry blood from her cracked lips. I thought I could hear
them crackle as they turned up in a smile. No matter how worn and
weathered she now looked, I could place the face instantly from that
smile, even all these years later.

“Hello, Jessie…” hummed Adeline, “It’s so lovely to finally see you
again.”
