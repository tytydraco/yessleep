Adam stood by the corn silo, holding a flask. My eyes kept closing; I
could barely focus on him.

“Morning, Adam.”

“Jesus, Matt, you look awful. Want a drink?”

He raised his flask.

“No.”

“It will help you get some sleep, man. What are you even doing at night?
Playing with yourself until dawn?”

He laughed and slapped my shoulder.

“No, it’s just…”

“I have a task for you. You gotta clean up the silo; the machinery is
blocked.”

I gripped my sleeve tight and stared at him.

He slapped my shoulder again.

“Calm down, you’re not gonna get Dan’d”

“But can’t somebody else do it? Why do I gotta?”

He furrowed his brows.

“I’m the senior, Matt. Now get your ass there and go do it.”

I waited, not moving.

“Go!”

His voice rang in my ears. I put my head down and walked into the silo.

The inside was cold, silent. My steps echoed on the bridge. The closer I
got to the ladder, the more my head spun. When my feet touched the
kernels, I took a deep breath and looked around. It wasn’t obvious where
the blockage was, but as usual it was probably in the center. I grabbed
a broom and walked over.

Then a hum echoed. The kernels moved beneath my feet. Dust filled the
air. Someone started the machines.

“Wait! I’m still in.”

The kernels were moving fast. My feet felt light.  I turned around and
took a few steps. One foot sank. Another. 

“Help, please!”

My waist sank. My heart beat against my ribs. The kernels moved around
me, pushing at my chest. I was neck-deep. My head sank under the
kernels. I held my breath. I sank deeper and deeper. The kernels hissed
in my ears. Then my feet felt loose, and so did my waist, my chest.

I fell hard onto the ground. It felt cold, but the ground felt firm like
concrete. I looked around. The whole place was dark, as in a cave, but
there were no walls, no ceiling above me, just darkness as far as I
could see. The smell of dusty kernels still hung in the air.

“Hello?”

My voice echoed through the dark emptiness. It sounded like my words
traveled to me and away from me at the same time. I got up and walked a
few steps. They echoed in the same strange way. 

A cold tingle built in my chest. The space felt both small and large. It
felt as if someone’s eyes were on me. I kept looking over my shoulder.
Then a noise broke through the silence. A faint snore, as if someone was
trying to catch their breath, but couldn’t. In the distance, a small
figure lay on the ground.

“Hello? Are you okay?”

The snoring grew. His limbs twisted on the ground as if struggling. My
heart sank when I saw who it was. Dan rolled on the ground, in the same
clothes he’d worn when he fell into the corn that day.

Memories flashed through my mind. His screams for help. The machine’s
rattle. Adam’s calm face as he watched him go down. All because he told
the boss Adam and I were drinking on the job. The rope lay right at my
feet. I stared at it while Dan screamed for help.

I ran to him. His eyes were wide with pain, his mouth filled with
kernels.

“I’m sorry, Dan. I’m sorry!”

I grabbed him and rolled him onto his side. Kernels spilled from his
mouth, but more were still inside. I hit his back; more spilled out, but
his mouth was still full. I stuck my shaking hand into his mouth and
pulled out handfuls of kernels, but his mouth filled back immediately.
He turned his pleading eyes to me. Tears rolled down his cheeks.

“I’m sorry, I don’t…”

My voice caught. Something scratched the back of my throat. I tried to
cough, but nothing came out; the scratching only grew. I tried to
breathe, but the air wouldn’t come through. I dropped to all fours.
Kernels began falling out of my mouth.

“Dan! Dan!” I woke up screaming.

I was back in the silo, half-submerged in the kernels. The mechanical
machinery sounds were gone. The silo was silent.

Adam and two other workers stood atop the bridge, looking down at me. A
rope was thrown before me.

“Sorry, Matt. We didn’t know you were in. Adam forgot to tell us,” one
of them said.

Adam shrugged and looked down at me with a half-smile.

“Why were you screaming Dan’s name?” Adam asked. 

He unscrewed the flask and took a long, slow sip, eyes on me.

I looked down at the ground.

“I don’t know.”

He smiled and nodded.

“Take the rope,” one of the men said.

I grabbed it tightly. Something scratched at the back of my throat
again. I coughed. A few kernels flew out.
