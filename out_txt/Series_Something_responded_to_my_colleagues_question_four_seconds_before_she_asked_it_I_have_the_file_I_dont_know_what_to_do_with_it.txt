I have 847 hours of field recording across three years of this work.

I can tell you the exact timestamp — 2:14:37 AM — when a door opened by
itself in a farmhouse outside Carbondale. Baseline decibel reading
before it moved, the spike, the return to baseline in 0.4 seconds.
Filed, labeled, cross-referenced with humidity readings and structural
reports. Probable explanation still listed as HVAC pressure
differential, confirm with building superintendent.

The superintendent retired in 2019.

I keep logs because logs are how I stay employed. Petra trusts data the
way other people trust prayer. Father Connelly doesn’t trust either of
us. Voss trusts whatever she heard in that room in Louisville that she
still won’t talk about. I keep working with her anyway.

I’m not a believer.

The Geneseo footage is in a separate folder on a drive I keep unplugged.

We weren’t supposed to be in Geneseo.

Petra had a conference in Rochester. Voss was catching a ride to
Buffalo. I was driving because I always drive, and Father Connelly was
in the backseat because he’d appeared in the motel parking lot in
Binghamton at 6 AM with a bag and the look of a man who has already
decided.

We stopped outside Geneseo because it was past midnight and nobody had
eaten since Albany.

The waitress filled our coffees before we asked. Maybe sixty. Hair
pinned up. She looked at each of us while she poured. Not rude.
Counting.

“You headed through, or stopping?”

“Through,” I said.

She looked at Voss.

Voss looked out the window.

“There’s a place some people come out to see.” She didn’t ask if we knew
which one. “Off Mill Road. Bell property.”

“We’re not here for that,” I said.

“Okay,” she said.

She refilled Connelly’s coffee. He hadn’t touched it.

“People who come looking for the Bell place after dark usually leave
before sunrise.” She paused. “You might want to think about what left
means before you use it.”

October 1984. Three SUNY Geneseo students drove out to an abandoned
farmhouse on Mill Road on a dare. One of them - Marcus DiCesare,
nineteen, engineering sophomore - walked back alone at 4 AM and sat down
in the campus security office.

I need to report what happened.

The report is public record. I’ve read it eleven times. DiCesare
described a room where the temperature dropped forty degrees in under a
minute. A sound he called like a crowd that learned to breathe together.
A shape in the corner that was trying to remember how to be a person.

His two friends were found the next morning. Unharmed. Neither could
account for the gap between 10 PM and 4 AM. Neither discussed it again.

The farmhouse has been empty since 1978. It drew investigators for years
afterward. None of them published anything.

Voss knew we were turning before I hit the signal.

In the backseat, Connelly was praying quietly. Petra was already taking
readings. For a moment it felt like we were reasonable people doing a
reasonable thing.

The house was gray clapboard, porch sagging left, windows intact. That
last part was unusual for a property forty-plus years empty. The padlock
on the front door wasn’t latched.

I set up two cameras. Checked audio levels.

At 1:47 AM, Petra said: “I want to ask a direct question. Is anyone here
with us?”

Her voice hit the audio at timestamp 1:47:23.

At 1:47:19 — four seconds before she spoke — the audio captured a
response.

Male voice. Clear. No distortion.

“I’ve been waiting for the one who doesn’t believe.”

I’ve run that file through four programs. Had two engineers listen
blind - told them codec artifact, asked them to identify it.

There is no pre-response anomaly that produces coherent speech.

When I told Voss, she was quiet for a while.

“Was that the first time you’d heard that voice?”

I said yes.

“Are you sure?”

I went back to the diner two days later. The waitress wasn’t there. The
owner said she’d called in sick the morning after our visit.

I asked her name.

He looked at me the way people do when they’re deciding something.

“Mary Aldiss. But you talked to her.”

“Just confirming,” I said.

He wiped down the counter.

“She called me after her shift. Said one of you came in twice. First
time about an hour before the others. Sat alone. Ordered coffee, didn’t
drink it, left when the second car pulled in.”

“That wasn’t me,” I said.

He nodded like he’d expected that.

“That’s what she said you’d say.”

I have the security footage from that night.

Four people in that booth. I’ve watched it sixty-one times.

I don’t know who poured the coffee.

More to follow.
