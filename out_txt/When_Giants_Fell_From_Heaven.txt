The first man is born approximately fifteen hundred feet above the
ground, and he lives for twelve seconds.

I know this because I count them.

There is some disagreement, even now, over whether what happens to him
constitutes a birth. My friend Freddy, who is helping me type this
account, argues that a person must first emerge from something to be
born. I maintain that perhaps a beginning is simply a beginning,
regardless of whether there is a womb involved. Neither of us knows what
to call a man who comes into existence already falling toward his
demise.

For this account, I will call it a birth.

I should also clarify that the man is not the first. He is merely the
first I see, and the first whose life I have the opportunity to measure
more thoroughly. I have come to think of them as the first man and the
second, though neither was either.

The others come afterward.

But I should perhaps rewind a little.

My name is Esther. I am an Afen, which is what my people call ourselves.
Most humans know us as Denens, although a surprising number of you have
never heard of us at all.

Some of you, however, may remember the young man named Freddy.

Over the past couple of months, he published two accounts here about his
employment with a company called Town and the rather unfortunate
circumstances under which he came to know me and my people. I appear in
both accounts, though I suspect you’ll remember me as the elderly woman
with the burned face and the sewing needle.

I assure you my life is much more than either of those things.

Nevertheless, Freddy’s descriptions were accurate enough. I stand 2
baksom and a kip, or about five and two-fifths inches by your
measurements. My hair is gray, and the left side of my face bears the
consequences of an encounter with a chemical that should never have
existed. I carry a sewing needle because, well, at my size, it makes a
perfectly fine cane.

I would prefer not to discuss the circumstances surrounding my injuries.
Freddy has already written about them, and I see little reason to make
myself miserable repeating what he has adequately described.

Instead, I would like to tell you about something that happened
recently.

Firstly, I no longer live in the United States.

Shortly after the events of Freddy’s second account, I relocated to
Nämori, a town of perhaps four thousand Afenas in a mountain valley that
appears on no map. A small number of humans live along the valley road.
They know what we are, and they have kept the secret for longer than any
of us have been alive. In exchange, they receive our rice and an
arrangement with their local doctor.

I live in the oldest part of town.

Here, the streets are my streets. The buildings are constructed for
people of my stature, and the people who maintain them are generally of
my stature as well.

I cannot adequately describe how peculiar it feels to walk into a shop
without first having to consider whether its owner might attempt to
murder me.

Perhaps that is a subject for another account. For now, I should explain
how this one is being written.

Freddy is helping me type, although he remains in America. We
communicate over a video call, and he has agreed to reproduce my words
as faithfully as possible. Shared documents are a wonder. He
occasionally interrupts to ask whether a particular sentence is
necessary. I have informed him that, as he is not the author, per se,
his literary objections are of some importance.

He has also suggested that I explain how an Afen can operate a human
keyboard.

The answer is that we generally do not.

We have our own computers, naturally. It would be rather inconvenient if
an entire civilization were obliged to jump between keys simply to
compose a letter. The laptop I am using, however, belongs to a human
acquaintance, and I have not yet persuaded him to purchase something
more appropriate.

I am capable of typing on it myself. It simply requires a great deal of
walking back and forth.

In fact, the previous sentence took me nearly three minutes to produce,
partly because I fell over, and partly because the space bar is farther
from the letters than I believe strictly necessary.

Freddy has returned to the call.

We can continue.

I am standing outside the bakery when the first man appears.

It is a pleasant afternoon. The air smells of bread and damp earth, and
somewhere beyond the northern houses a Chuck is making that awful sound
they make when somebody has denied them fresh meat. I have a parcel
tucked beneath my arm. Inside it are two rolls, still warm, which I
intend to eat when I return home.

I am thinking about supper when the screaming begins.

At first, I assume it is coming from the human village at the mouth of
the valley. Although the village lies a mile off, human voices carry
across the valley with ease, as one could imagine. A human shouting a
mile off can sound as though he is standing directly behind you.

But the sound is not coming from the human village. It is coming from
above.

I look up.

There is a man in the sky.

He is naked. His arms are extended, swinging in circles, his legs kick
uselessly beneath him, and his mouth is open in an expression of such
complete terror that I recognize it even from the ground.

He has no history that I can see. There is no building from which he
might have jumped, and no aircraft from which he might have fallen.

There is only the blue afternoon, and then there is him.

I begin counting. One. Two. Three.

I do not know why I count. I suppose I need something to occupy my mind
while I watch him fall. I have never been particularly good at knowing
what to do in emergencies.

At seven, he passes behind the roof of the bakery. At nine, I hear
someone shout.

The sound arrives at second twelve. A terrible concussion. It is
followed by a tremor that travels through the street and into the soles
of my aching feet. The bakery windows shudder. One of the rolls slips
from beneath my arm.

I have witnessed Afenas as well as humans die before. I have attended
funerals. I understand the mechanics of mortality as well as any person
can reasonably claim to understand them.

But I have never watched a person live their entire life in twelve
seconds.

And I have never seen the sky give birth.

Until now.

For several seconds, I do nothing.

I stand outside the bakery with one roll under my arm and the other
lying in the street, where it has begun to collect dirt.

I remember being rather upset about this.

It was a good roll. The baker had only just taken it from the oven, and
I had paid eight whole xu for it, which I considered an outrageous price
for something consisting primarily of flour and water. I considered
retrieving it, but another earthquake passed through the pavement, and I
decided that perhaps my supper was not the most pressing matter at hand.

Someone begins screaming.

Not the man, of course. He is quite finished with that.

The screaming comes from somewhere farther down the street, beyond the
bakery and the row of narrow houses adjoining it. I hear breaking glass,
followed by a tremendous metallic clatter. A woman emerges from the shop
opposite me, holding a small child against her chest.

She looks up.

“Miru cos!” she shouts, pressing the child’s face against her shoulder.

I have no idea whether the child understands her. The woman hurries back
inside, and I am left standing alone with my remaining roll.

I consider following her. Instead, I look toward the northern end of the
street.

A cloud of dust has begun rising above the rooftops. It is thick, making
it ever more difficult to breathe, and it moves slowly between the
buildings, obscuring the afternoon sunlight. Somewhere beyond it, a bell
begins ringing.

The sound is that of the emergency alarm.

I have heard it only once before, during a fire in the neighboring
district. It is an unpleasant thing, consisting of high screams repeated
at intervals, and I have always thought it sounds rather like someone
attempting to play a horn while being strangled.

Nevertheless, it serves its purpose well.

Doors open along the street, and people emerge from shops and
apartments, some carrying children, others still wearing their work
clothes. A man leans over the railing of a second-floor balcony and
shouts a question I cannot understand.

I begin walking north.

I have never considered myself particularly brave. Indeed, I have spent
much of my life avoiding situations in which bravery might be required
of me. But I am curious, and curiosity has occasionally led me into
circumstances from which good sense might otherwise have spared me.

This is one of those occasions.

The closer I come to the intersection, the more apparent the damage
becomes. Several windows have shattered. A wooden sign hangs from its
mounting, and pieces of broken tile litter the pavement. The air smells
of blood.

A man in a blue warden uniform comes running toward me.

“Go back!” he shouts.

I stop. “What’s happened?”

He looks at me. I realize, a moment too late, that this is a rather
foolish question.

“There’s a human in the street!”

“Well, yes,” I tell him. “I saw him fall.”

The man opens his mouth, closes it, and then looks over his shoulder.
“Then you know why you shouldn’t be here.”

“I would like to know whether anyone has been hurt.”

“There were people in the building,” he says.

I look past him. At the far end of the street, where a row of houses
once stood… something enormous lies among the wreckage.

At first, I can make out only a hand. It rests palm upward across what
remains of a roof, its fingers extending into the street below. One has
crushed a balcony beneath it. Another lies across the entrance of a
shop.

I have seen human hands before, naturally. Freddy possesses two of them.
But I have never seen one quite like this.

The warden takes hold of my arm. “Please,” he says. “Go home.”

I look at him, then again at the ruined building.

“How many were injured?”

He does not answer immediately. But eventually, he says, “I don’t know.”

I do not press him further. There are questions one ought not ask a man
who has just watched a building full of people collapse.

He releases my arm and hurries toward a group of people gathering near
the intersection. I walk slightly to get a better vantage point, and
from here, I can see more of the human.

His arm extends across the remains of two buildings, disappearing
beneath a considerable pile of masonry. His hand is open, and there is
something strangely ordinary about it. His nails are neatly trimmed, and
there is a small scar across one knuckle, almost as if it were a
birthmark.

A second alarm begins sounding.

This one comes from the human village.

It is a much deeper noise than our town bells. The sound rolls over the
rooftops, causing several people around me to cover their ears.

There is something I should explain about the humans of the valley.
There are thirty of them who know about us, and only three have vehicles
large enough to carry a body. None of them were ever meant to enter our
streets. Nor can they move freely among our buildings without causing
considerable damage.

This means that when a human falls into Nämori, removing him is not as
simple as calling an ambulance.

In fact, it is considerably more difficult than that.

I learn later that the first man’s body requires nearly three hours to
remove. By then, of course, there are others.

But I am getting ahead of myself.

Three minutes after the first impact, the next one appears.

I do not see their arrival.

I hear their impact.

A distant scream is followed by a sound like a large piece of furniture
being dropped from an upstairs window.

It is farther away this time, perhaps somewhere beyond the eastern
district.

The ground trembles beneath my feet. Several people look toward the
sound, and yet nobody speaks.

For a moment, I think the noise might have come from a damaged building
finally collapsing. Then someone points upward.

There is a woman in the sky. Her hair streams above her head, and her
arms are held tightly against her chest. Unlike the first man, she does
not appear to be struggling.

She is falling almost perfectly straight. I watch her descend behind the
distant rooftops. A few seconds later, another impact rolls across the
valley.

And then another. And another.

I cannot tell you how many people fell during those first few minutes.

Some landed in the river, others struck buildings so completely that
their remains could not immediately be distinguished from the wreckage.
At least one that I am aware of fell into the wooded hills beyond the
valley and was not discovered until the following morning.

What I can say is that the screaming escalated into near pandemonium.

Sometimes it came from above, other times from the streets.

I begin walking home. I no longer care about the bread. I have lived
long enough to recognize when the world is becoming a place in which one
ought not linger outdoors.

The streets are crowded now. People hurry past me carrying parcels,
children, pets, and whatever possessions they have managed to collect. A
man struggles to pull a cart loaded with furniture. Another stands
outside his shop, attempting to fasten the shutters with hands that will
not stop shaking.

I pass the bakery again.

The woman who sold me my rolls is closing the door. She recognizes me.

“Esther!” I hear someone call from behind me.

“Are you going home?”

“That is the intention.”

She is hesitant.

“Be careful,” she says after a while, then she holds out a paper bag.

“Here.”

I look inside. There is a roll. I look back at her, a little confused.

“You’ve already paid,” she says. 

I have always liked that woman. I thank her and continue walking.

For several minutes, nothing happens. The alarms continue, and somewhere
in the distance I hear the rumble of human emergency vehicles, but the
sky above my street remains mercifully empty.

I begin to think that the worst has passed. This is a bad thought, and I
know it.

I have many bad thoughts throughout this account, and I shall endeavor
to identify them as they occur.

As I approach the entrance to my apartment building, I hear something
strike the pavement behind me. It is not a tremendous impact, and there
is no earthquake this time.

I turn around. A hand is in the street. It is attached to the arm of a
rather small human being. He has managed—miraculously, and without
killing me—to land directly behind me.

He lies on his stomach, his face pressed against the ground. He, like
the others, is entirely naked.

For a moment, I assume he is dead, having watched all the others die
upon impact.

But then he moves. It is scarcely more than a twitch of his fingers, but
I see it plainly. I stand there, looking upon this giant.

He lifts his head, and, quite unexpectedly, begins to weep.

I do not approach, not immediately. I would like to say this was
caution, but after what I have endured, it was a paralysis. I am an ant,
and he is a weeping mountain laid about the road I must use to reach my
front door. There is no etiquette for such a bizarre situation. 

His hand rests about a yard from my feet, and I can feel the heat
radiating from it. 

I think, well, another foolish thought: that because he is crying, he
cannot be dangerous. But I have known many dangerous people who wept
freely, and some often. 

Nevertheless, I take a step closer. “Sir,” I say.

He does not react. His breath is coming in shuddering waves, each one
stirring up dust around me and making my skirt flutter about my legs.
This is not the weeping of the bereaved. His left leg lies at an angle
that no leg, human or Afen, ought to lie. 

“Sir!”

His eye rolls toward me. It is the size of a platter; it is wet and red,
and it does not find me so much as stumble across me. Once he finds me,
it focuses, and he goes rigid.

“Ah!”

The sound is a gale. I am nearly knocked off my feet, and I drive my
needle into a crack in the pavement to hold on.

“Please do not shout,” I say.

“There’s a, there’s a person.” His voice is hoarse and cracking, and it
shakes the roof tiles of my home. “You’re so small. Why are you so
small? What are you?”

“I am an Afen. My name is Esther.”

“I don’t.” He drags in a breath. “I don’t know what that is.”

“No. I did not expect you would.”

“My leg.” His face twists. “My leg is, it’s, I fell, I know the leg is
broken because I fell, but why does it hurt like this? It’s already
broken! Why does it keep going and going?”

I have no answer that will be of any use to him. I, like most people,
have grown accustomed. 

“I do not know why it must,” I say. “But it will ease. Not soon, but it
will.”

“Make it stop.” His hand twitches against the road, and I take a long
step backward. “Please, please, can you make it stop, can you do
something?”

“I cannot. I am sorry.”

He cries, and I let him. There is nothing else I could usefully do.

Then something unexpected happens. I begin to speak to him the way one
speaks to a child. I do not decide to do it. My voice drops, and I hear
myself say, “There, there, it’s all right, just breathe,” in the
sing-song tone I used on my sister’s boys. His eye swings toward me.

“I’m not,” he says. “I’m not a… I’m not a child. Am I?”

I stop. “No,” I say. “No. Forgive me.”

I have seen the grown man he is. His voice is a man’s voice. He has the
vocabulary of an adult, and he is lying in the street in terror. 

“You are not a child,” I tell him, in my ordinary voice. 

The weeping eases a little. He hiccups, and I find it unbearably sad.

“Where am I?” he asks.

“A town called Nämori.”

“I don’t know those words.”

“You do not need to know them yet.”

“Who-who am I?”

I open my mouth. Then I find that I am staring up at a man who has asked
the most difficult question one could ask. 

“I do not know,” I admit. “But I should like to find out with you.”

He looks at me for a long time, and I do not rush him. A shout goes up
from the end of the street.

He flinches so hard the road jumps, and a pane of glass falls out of a
window above us and shatters beside my foot. He makes an awful sound and
begins dragging himself away from it with both hands scraping the
pavement raw.

“Stop it, you will hurt yourself.”

“They’re coming, I can hear them, they’re coming.”

“I know they are.”

“What do they do?” His breath is ragged. “I don’t know what people do. I
don’t know what anybody does.”

I turn, and four town wardens are running up the street, and behind
them, much farther back, a large human vehicle is forcing its way
between two buildings and scraping the plaster from both.

“Lie still,” I tell him. “Do not move the leg. Do not speak unless they
speak to you. Can you manage that?”

“I-I think so.”

“Good, then let’s try.”

I take my needle out of the crack in the pavement and walk out to meet
them.

The front man is the same one who took my arm earlier.

“Madam, I told you to go home.”

“I am home,” I say. “That is my door.”

“There is a human in the street.”

“There is. And he is alive.”

The wardens stop, all four of them, in fact. I have never seen something
like that before.

“That is not possible,” says the one at the back.

“He landed behind me. He has a broken leg and no clothes, and he is
frightened out of his mind. I should like it very much if nobody did
anything hasty.”

“Madam.” The first man wets his lips. “The others, every one of the
others. We have not found one who…”

“I understand,” I say. ” But he is alive, that much is true.”

“Esther?” His voice is almost too sad for its own good.

“I am here,” I say, without turning.

The youngest of the wardens takes off his cap. “It talks,” he whispers.

“He,” I say. “He talks. And he understands a good deal more than he has
any reason to. I would take it as a kindness if you please would not
call him it.”

It takes nearly an hour to arrange his departure.

A woman from the human village office arrives with a clipboard. There is
an argument, which I do not follow, about whose jurisdiction a man falls
under who has fallen from nowhere. Eventually somebody points out that
he is bleeding on a public road.

Throughout all of this, he lies still. His hand lies flat on the
pavement beside me, palm up.

When the few human medics finally arrive, they are hauling a stretcher.
One of them is a woman who, upon seeing him, says, “Oh, honey.”

He turns his head toward her, and I see it happen. For the first time
since he arrived, someone of his own size is looking at him, kindly. His
whole face comes apart.

“There’s more of you,” he says. “There’s more of me.”

“There are,” she says. “There’s a lot of us. Come on, let’s get you
somewhere warm.”

“The little one,” he says. “Esther. She has to come with me, please.”

The woman looks down at me, a long way down. “She’ll come,” says the
woman. “I promise. I’ll carry her myself.”

And she does. I sit on the heel of a stranger’s glove, holding my needle
in both hands, with a roll in a paper bag between my knees, and I watch
the street slide away. 

The sky does not stop until nearly midnight.

There were twenty-three of them in all. Not one landed in the same place
twice. And not one, besides him, lived through the fall. The
investigators, such as they were, could not account for it. The valley’s
one doctor measured him, scanned him, and photographed the break from
every angle, and arrived at the only conclusion available, which was
that a man who falls fifteen hundred feet ought to have died.

None of it reached the news. The humans who know about us took the
bodies out by night, one at a time. By the end of the week, the sky
above Nämori was as blue as it had ever been, and nobody mentioned it.

Freddy is asking me to say something here. He wants to know whether I
mean to include how it ended, whether the rain stopped, or whether
anyone ever learned what made it start. From what I was able to gather,
the rain stopped, but no source was ever identified, and may never be
identifiable.

They put him in a warehouse on the edge of the human village. It is a
very large building, the sort that was once used for storing machinery.
The village has taken it over and divided it into something resembling a
ward. There is a bed in it that is not quite long enough for him, and a
bank of fluorescent lamps that hum loudly, which I do not care for, and
which he does not mention, because he does not yet know that lamps are
not supposed to do that.

His leg is set within two days, and for the first week, he sleeps almost
constantly, and when he wakes he is frightened. Usually frightened of
waking itself.

I visit every morning. There is a small platform near his bed, installed
by a thoughtful nurse, that raises me to the height of his pillow. I sit
on it with my needle across my knees, and he turns his head, and I say,
“Good morning.”

“Esther,” he says, as if he had been afraid I would not come.

I have been wrong about a great many things in my life. I was wrong
about my sister’s husband, for instance. He is a very nice man past the
exterior. But for years… I was wrong about whether I was a person worth
being kind to.

But I have not once been wrong about whether he is a person.

He asks me everything. Not in the way he did the first day, in panic.
But out of curiosity. What is a Tuesday? What is rain? What is the thing
in my chest that gets tight when I’m sad? I answer what I can. When I
cannot, I say so.

On the ninth day, I bring him a book.

It is an ordinary picture book. It is large print from the human school.
I have to read it to him from the platform, which means reading a page
that is twice my height.

When I am finished, he is quiet for a moment.

“Show me the writing,” he says. “The little marks. You made sounds when
you looked at them.”

So we begin.

It takes a few weeks. I will say, he learns more slowly than he did
everything else, and more thoroughly as well.

B.

Ba.

Bakery.

“That’s your word,” he says, the day he reads it. We are sitting with
the paper bag the baker gave me. He has it spread across his knee, and
he has traced the printed name with a finger so large that it covers
four of its letters at once.

I look at him for some time, this enormously beautiful creature with a
scar through one eyebrow—where one of the nurses’ trays caught him—who
came into the world already falling towards his death, and has taken, I
think, every bad thing this world has ever given him and shoved it
somewhere I cannot find.

“Esther.”

“Yes?”

“The ones who fell.” He is looking at the bag. “The ones before me. The
ones you counted. Did they, were they also…”

“I believe so.”

“And none of them got to…” He gestures with his hand at the room.
“This.”

“No.”

He goes quiet. I believe he is thinking.

“I’m sorry,” he says after a long while. “I don’t know who to say it
to.”

“Say it to me,” I tell him. “I will see that it is passed along.”

He nods, and I watch his shoulders come down.

Freddy asked me later whether I still thought a beginning was simply a
beginning. I said that it was, but that it never hurts to have someone
there when it happens.

I should mention that the door of the ward has a lock, and that it is on
the outside. The nurse says it is for safety; I have not asked whose.

I also no longer walk to the bakery without looking up. The sky has been
empty for weeks, and yet I look anyway.

The first man lived for twelve seconds. The second man has lived for
weeks.

I know this because I count them.
