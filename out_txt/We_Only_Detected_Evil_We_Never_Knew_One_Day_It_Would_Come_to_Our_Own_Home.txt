My name is David Brown, and my life has always revolved around V-Me —
the name of my agency. Couples come to us all the time, terrified of
their own past. They’re convinced that some dark thing they went through
will somehow reach the child they haven’t even had yet. Most of the
time, nothing comes of it. But there’s one case I’ll never forget — the
case of multi-millionaire Robert Kaufman and his wife, Emily.

It was around 11:30 at night. I was just about to leave the office when
my secretary called and said a couple needed to see me urgently. I told
her to turn them away — it was late, and I was exhausted. She hesitated,
then said, “Sir, it’s the Kaufman family. The real estate tycoons.” I
couldn’t say no to a name like that. They wanted me at their bungalow,
at midnight.

I got there around 12. The place was massive, and dead silent. I gave my
name to the guard, drove in, parked, and walked up to the door. Robert
and Emily were already waiting for me. They told me that earlier that
day, a checkup had confirmed Emily was pregnant. We should have been
celebrating. Instead, their faces were pale with fear.

Nearly two years ago, Emily and I had gone on our honeymoon. That’s
where we met another couple — Charles Brown and his wife, Stephanie.
Charles ran a fishing business, and Stephanie was about six months
pregnant at the time. He and I got along fine, but Emily and Stephanie
became something closer — inseparable, almost overnight.

They’d spend hours talking. Stephanie always said how Charles’s business
kept him away, how she worried he wouldn’t have time for the baby
either. Emily used to reassure her — a man with a business that size
still makes time, if he wants to. By the time our vacation ended,
Charles didn’t want us to leave. Stephanie needed Emily, he said.

We went back to our separate lives, but the friendship didn’t fade.
Every night, Stephanie and Emily were on the phone for hours — pregnancy
updates, small talk, promises that Emily would be the first person to
meet the baby.

Then the miscarriage happened. Complications during delivery.

The baby didn’t survive, and the doctors told Stephanie she would never
be able to carry a child again.

She broke. For weeks she wouldn’t answer Emily’s calls, and when she
finally did, something in her voice had changed. One night she called
crying and said, “You’ll have a baby. I never will.” After that, the
resentment was impossible to miss.

The last message she ever sent Emily read: “I can never be a mother. But
you will be. And one day — watch — my child will be born from your
body.” A few days later, we heard Charles and Stephanie had died in a
car accident, together. We were devastated. Emily and I moved from New
York to Texas, and over time, we let it go.

Today, the doctor confirmed Emily’s pregnancy, and for a few hours we
were happy again. Then, that same night, sleeping beside me, Emily woke
up screaming. She was sitting bolt upright, staring into the dark like
she wasn’t fully in the room with me.

“She was here,” she said, her voice shaking. “Stephanie. She was
standing right by the bed.”

I turned on the light.

The room was empty. But the window — the one I’d locked myself before
bed — was wide open. Wet mud was smeared across the sill, and the
curtains, as if someone had just climbed in from outside.

Emily gripped her stomach, her fingers trembling. “She said it, David.
She said it would happen.”

A smell filled the room — wet earth and something rotting beneath it,
the kind of smell that clings to old graveyards.

I picked up my phone and called my lab team right then, in the middle of
the night. “We need the test. Tonight. Right now.”
