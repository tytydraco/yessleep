The first time someone got hurt laughing at one of my jokes, I thought
I’d finally figured out how to be funny.

I’d been doing stand-up for almost seven years, which is a long time to
spend being moderately good at something that doesn’t pay. I worked in
insurance claims during the day, lived in a one-bedroom apartment above
a Chinese restaurant, and spent most of my evenings performing for
people who hadn’t necessarily come to see comedy.

I wasn’t terrible. That was part of the problem. Terrible comedians
eventually give up, or at least develop the sense to keep their day jobs
and stop spending money on gas. I could usually make a small room laugh.
Every few months, I’d have a set good enough to convince me I was
getting somewhere, and then I’d spend the next several weeks performing
for six people in the back of a bar.

The night it started, I was at The Lantern, a narrow brick storefront
with a bar at one end and a little platform at the other. Frank, the
owner, had given me fifteen minutes because another comic had canceled.
There were maybe twenty-five people in the audience, most of them
sitting at small tables with their coats hanging over the backs of their
chairs.

I’d spent that afternoon rewriting a joke I’d been working on since
winter. The premise had always seemed promising, but I could never get
the ending right. I’d tried shortening the setup, changing the order of
things, and replacing the punchline altogether. Nothing had made much
difference. That afternoon, I moved one sentence from the beginning to
the end, and for reasons I couldn’t explain, the whole thing suddenly
seemed to fit.

I tried it about halfway through my set.

The first laugh came from a middle-aged man near the front, wearing a
blue button-down shirt. He bent forward over his drink, and his wife
started laughing at the sight of him. Within a few seconds, most of the
room had joined in.

It was a better response than I’d ever gotten from another joke. I
smiled, enjoying the moment, and glanced toward the back of the room
where Frank was standing behind the bar. When I looked forward again,
the man in blue was still doubled over.

I gave him a little more time. Sometimes an audience member gets caught
up in a laugh and keeps going after everyone else has settled down.
Usually, you can move on and they’ll recover in a moment.

His wife rubbed his back while he laughed into the crook of his arm. He
tried to straighten up, made it halfway, and bent forward again with a
sound like a cough. The people at the neighboring table were watching
him now, a few still smiling.

I asked whether he was all right. He managed to lift one hand, thumb
extended, and I took that as permission to continue. I’d barely started
my next joke when his chair scraped across the floor.

He’d stood up beside the table, one hand gripping its edge. His face was
a deep red, and he was making short, breathless sounds with his mouth
wide open. His wife got to her feet and reached for him, but before she
could take his arm, his knees gave way.

He struck the floor hard enough that I heard it from the stage.

Frank came out from behind the bar while somebody called 911. I stepped
down to help, although there wasn’t much I could do. The man’s wife was
kneeling beside him, saying his name over and over, and I remember
standing there with the microphone still in my hand until Frank took it
from me.

The man regained consciousness before the ambulance arrived. He was pale
and confused, but he knew where he was. His wife helped him sit up, and
he apologized to everyone around him as though he’d knocked over a
drink.

The paramedics took him to the hospital anyway. Frank ended the show,
and I went home without getting paid.

I slept badly that night. I kept remembering the man’s face just before
he fell, the way he’d been trying to breathe while his body continued
laughing.

The next afternoon, Frank called to say the man’s wife had contacted
him. Her husband had been discharged after several hours of observation.
The doctors thought he’d fainted because of a sudden drop in blood
pressure, possibly triggered by laughing too hard. He was supposed to
follow up with his physician, but nothing they’d found suggested a
serious problem.

“She wanted me to tell you he enjoyed the show,” Frank said.

I was relieved enough to laugh at that.

For a while, I considered dropping the joke. Then I performed it at
another club the following weekend and got the biggest laugh of the
night. Nobody fainted, and the audience was still applauding when I
walked offstage. I kept using it.

Over the next four months, things started going better.

Not overnight, and not entirely because of that joke. I’d been improving
for years, slowly enough that it was difficult to notice. I was finally
getting comfortable with silence, learning when to cut material that
wasn’t working, and finding ways to talk to an audience without sounding
as though I’d memorized every word.

I started posting clips online. A couple got shared around, and I picked
up some paid opening spots. Eventually, I booked a weekend at Lyle
House, a proper comedy club with a raised stage, decent lighting, and a
room that seated a little over a hundred people.

I still worked in insurance claims. I still had to ask my supervisor for
Friday afternoons off. But I was earning enough from comedy to cover my
expenses, and for the first time I could imagine it becoming my actual
job.

The joke had become my closer. I trusted it when a room was difficult,
and I liked knowing that no matter how the rest of the set went, I had
something reliable waiting at the end.

On my second night at Lyle House, a woman in the third row began
laughing before I’d even finished the punchline.

She was sitting beside a man who looked to be her boyfriend or husband.
Her dark hair was tied back, and she’d spent most of the show laughing
quietly with one hand over her mouth. When the joke landed, she dropped
her phone and bent forward, laughing so hard that the people around her
turned to look.

I let the reaction run for a while, enjoying the sound of a full room.
The woman was still laughing when the people around her began settling
down, and her companion leaned over to say something to her.

She grabbed his wrist and began pulling at his arm. He was still
smiling, apparently trying to make some comment about the joke, but she
was shaking her head. By the time I asked if everything was okay, she
had tears streaming down her face and was struggling to get a word out
between bursts of laughter.

The man beside her finally seemed to understand that something was
wrong. He stood and helped her out of her chair, supporting most of her
weight as a server came over to assist them.

I stepped away from the microphone while they guided her into the lobby.
The audience watched in uncomfortable silence, and a few minutes passed
before the manager returned to tell me she’d recovered enough to leave.
Her boyfriend was taking her home, and she didn’t want an ambulance.

I finished the show with a different joke. It got a decent response,
although I don’t remember much about delivering it.

Afterward, I asked the manager whether he thought she was all right.

“She was walking when they left,” he said. “I think she just got herself
worked up.”

I mentioned the man who’d fainted at The Lantern. The manager said
people sometimes passed out from laughing, coughing, or standing up too
quickly, and that I shouldn’t assume the incidents were connected.

I’d performed the joke dozens of times by then, in front of hundreds of
people. Those were the only two incidents I knew about, but I left it
out of my next few sets anyway.

About a week later, Frank called while I was at work. A woman had
contacted The Lantern looking for me. She’d seen my name on an old
poster and thought I might still perform there. Frank had given her my
business email, but he wanted to warn me because she’d sounded upset.

Her message arrived that evening.

Her name was Claire. She’d been at Lyle House with her boyfriend, and
she wanted to know whether I’d noticed anything unusual about the
audience that night.

She explained that she’d been having episodes of uncontrollable laughter
since the show. The first happened in her kitchen the following morning.
She’d been making coffee when she suddenly started laughing, hard enough
that she had to sit on the floor. It lasted several minutes.

There had been more episodes since then, including one while she was
driving. She’d managed to pull into a parking lot, but she was
frightened that it might happen again somewhere she couldn’t stop
safely.

She’d seen a doctor, who had referred her to a neurologist. In the
meantime, she was trying to understand what had happened.

I read the email a few times before answering. I told her I was sorry
she’d had such a frightening experience and asked whether there was
anything I could do. I also mentioned that someone had fainted during an
earlier performance, although the hospital had attributed that incident
to a drop in blood pressure.

Her reply came the next morning.

So you knew this had happened before?

I explained that the man had recovered and that there’d been no reason
to believe my performance had caused anything beyond an unusually strong
laugh.

Claire wrote back that afternoon.

I understand you didn’t know what would happen to me. I’m asking whether
you’re still telling that joke.

I told her I’d stopped performing it.

She thanked me and said she hoped I’d keep it that way until someone
could figure out what was happening.

I found myself getting angry whenever I thought about the exchange. I
knew that wasn’t fair to Claire, who had every reason to be frightened,
but I couldn’t understand what she expected me to do. People laughed at
comedy shows. Occasionally, people fainted. She was having a medical
problem that might have had nothing to do with me.

I started writing a reply to her last message a couple of times, mostly
to explain that I couldn’t be responsible for every medical problem
someone experienced after one of my shows. I never sent either version.
Even reading them back, I sounded like an asshole.

I didn’t have any way of knowing whether her symptoms would have started
if she’d stayed home that night. But whenever I considered putting the
joke back in my set, I remembered her trying to get her boyfriend’s
attention while he was still laughing.

Three weeks later, Frank offered me a headlining show at The Lantern.

He’d recently started selling tickets for Saturday nights instead of
relying on walk-in customers, and he wanted to try giving local
comedians a full set. The room could hold about sixty people if he
rearranged the tables, and he could afford to pay me two hundred
dollars.

It wasn’t the sort of opportunity that would change my life, but it was
the first time anyone had asked me to headline a show under my own name.

Frank made a poster and started advertising the date online. I spent the
next two weeks putting together forty-five minutes of material, most of
it drawn from sets I’d been performing for years. The difficulty was
finding an ending. I’d become so accustomed to closing with the joke
from The Lantern that everything else felt anticlimactic.

I tried an older bit at one open mic and something new at another. Both
did reasonably well, but neither got the kind of response I wanted for
my first headlining show.

A few days before the performance, I watched an old recording of myself
telling the joke. It was from a small club where the audience had
laughed hard enough that I needed to pause before continuing. Nothing
unusual happened. People laughed, applauded, and settled down while I
moved on to the next bit.

I watched it twice, remembering how many times I’d performed the joke
without anyone getting hurt. The man at The Lantern had been discharged
with a plausible medical explanation, and Claire was still waiting to
find out what was wrong with her. I couldn’t prove the incidents were
unrelated, but I couldn’t prove they were connected either.

By Friday evening, I’d decided to use it.

The Lantern was full when I arrived on Saturday.

Frank had borrowed chairs from the restaurant next door, and a few
people were standing along the wall near the bar. My mother had driven
in to see me, along with two friends from work and several people I
hadn’t spoken to since high school.

She was sitting near the back when I came out to start the show. I could
see her smiling as I adjusted the microphone, and I remember being
embarrassed by how pleased I was to have her there.

The first half went well. I had a few rough spots, but the room was warm
and the audience stayed with me. Some of the newer material got better
responses than I’d expected, which made me wonder whether I’d worried
too much about the ending.

I’d put the joke last on my set list. I didn’t need the notes anymore,
but I kept glancing at the folded paper on the stool beside my water.

As I approached the end of the set, I considered skipping it. The show
had gone well enough already, and I could have finished with something
else. Then I remembered how the room had sounded the first time I’d
performed it at The Lantern, before the man in blue collapsed. For those
few seconds, I’d felt as though everything I’d spent seven years working
toward had finally come together.

I introduced the joke and began the setup. A few people laughed at the
opening, and I let them finish before continuing. I knew exactly how
long to pause before the final sentence.

The response was immediate. Laughter filled the room, louder than
anything I’d heard that evening, and I could see people leaning forward
over their tables. Frank was laughing behind the bar. I was still
smiling when a glass broke near the front.

A man had knocked his drink onto the floor. He was doubled over,
gripping the edge of his table, while the woman beside him tried to get
his attention. She touched his shoulder, then began shaking his arm when
he didn’t respond.

I asked whether he needed help. Several people laughed at the question,
apparently assuming it was part of the act, but the woman was having
trouble answering. Every time she tried to speak, another burst of
laughter interrupted her.

Near the bar, a man was bracing himself against the wall, laughing so
hard that he could barely stand. His friends were watching him, amused
at first, until one of them began doing the same thing. People who’d
already settled down started laughing again when they saw their
neighbors struggling.

The man near the front slid out of his chair and landed on his side. His
companion dropped to her knees beside him, but her own laughter kept
interrupting her attempts to help. She tried to roll him onto his back
and lost her grip, then sat beside him, struggling for breath.

I called for Frank, but he was leaning over the bar with both hands
gripping the counter. His face was red, and he was trying to draw air
through his open mouth.

I jumped down from the stage. Someone had called 911, but the woman
holding the phone couldn’t speak clearly enough to give the dispatcher
the address. A man at the next table took it from her and managed to say
the name of the bar before he started laughing too.

I knelt beside the man who’d fallen. He was conscious, but his breathing
had become shallow and irregular. He caught my wrist when I leaned over
him, and I tried to get him to lie flat. Around us, chairs were being
knocked aside as people struggled toward the exit. A few had made it
outside, but others were supporting friends who could barely walk.

My mother was standing near the back wall. She wasn’t laughing, and when
I called for her to get outside, she started toward me instead.

I tried to reach her, but a man stumbled against me, laughing so hard
that his knees buckled. I caught him under the arms before he hit the
floor, and he clung to my shirt, making a strained, wheezing sound. His
chest jerked against mine each time he tried to breathe.

While I was supporting him, something caught in my throat. I swallowed,
thinking I was about to cough, but the sensation returned as a pressure
in my chest.

The punchline came into my head.

I’d written it, revised it, and performed it so many times that I knew
every pause and every inflection. Repeating it night after night had
made it almost meaningless to me. Now I could remember the words
perfectly, and I was struggling not to laugh.

My mother was calling my name. I could see her trying to get past the
tables, but when I opened my mouth to answer, a short laugh escaped. I
covered my mouth and tried to breathe through my nose.

The man I’d been supporting slipped out of my arms. I bent to help him,
but my stomach tightened so violently that I nearly fell on top of him.

I tried to straighten up and call for my mother again. Instead, I
laughed hard enough that my knees weakened, and I had to grab the back
of a chair.

I could hear people choking around me. Someone was lying motionless near
the stage, and my mother was still trying to reach me through the crowd.
I wanted her to stay away, but every attempt to speak brought on another
fit of laughter.

I remember looking at her across the room while I struggled to stay on
my feet. After that, my memory becomes difficult to piece together.

I woke up in the hospital the following morning with a sore throat and
aching ribs.

My mother arrived a little later. She looked exhausted, but she was
unhurt. She told me several people had been admitted to intensive care
and that one man had died before the paramedics could get him out of the
building. She didn’t know whether it was the man I’d been trying to
help.

According to her, I’d still been laughing when the paramedics carried me
outside. They put me on a stretcher and tried to calm me down, but I
couldn’t seem to hear them. At one point, I’d laughed so hard that I
vomited, and one of the paramedics had to turn my head to keep me from
choking.

I don’t remember any of that.

The police came to speak with me that afternoon. They asked what I’d
seen, whether anyone had been drinking excessively, and whether I’d
noticed anything unusual about the ventilation or the drinks being
served. When they asked whether anything similar had happened at one of
my shows before, I told them about the man who’d fainted at The Lantern
and the woman at Lyle House.

They took down everything I could remember, including Claire’s name and
the dates of our emails.

Frank closed The Lantern temporarily. He called me once after I got
home, mostly to ask how I was doing. We talked for a few minutes about
the people who’d been hurt, and he said he didn’t know whether he’d ever
reopen the place.

I apologized again before we hung up, although I wasn’t sure what I
expected Frank to say.

I haven’t performed since that night.

For the first few weeks, I couldn’t imagine getting back onstage. I
avoided comedy altogether, including shows I’d previously enjoyed
watching. Hearing an audience laugh made me uncomfortable, and I found
myself turning off the television whenever anything remotely funny came
on.

I’d spent months practicing the joke, though, and the words came back to
me at odd moments throughout the day. Sometimes I’d be washing dishes or
sitting at my desk at work when I’d remember part of the setup. I could
usually distract myself before reaching the punchline.

About a month after the show, I was standing in the freezer aisle at the
grocery store when I remembered the final sentence.

The laugh came so suddenly that I had to grip the handle of a freezer
door to keep my balance. A woman pushing a shopping cart smiled at me as
she passed, probably assuming I’d remembered something funny. I turned
toward the freezer to hide my face while my shoulders shook.

It lasted less than a minute. By the time it passed, my stomach hurt and
my eyes were watering. I left my groceries in the cart and went home.

I’ve had three more episodes since then. None has lasted as long as the
first, and I’ve been able to stop myself from making much noise. My
doctor has referred me to a neurologist, but the appointment isn’t until
next month.

I haven’t contacted Claire. I think about doing it sometimes, especially
when I’m having a bad day, but I don’t know what I’d say to her.

I know what happened at The Lantern. I remember the people on the floor
and the look on my mother’s face. Someone died that night, and I’ve had
plenty of time to think about my decision to tell the joke again.

None of that makes it any less funny when the punchline comes into my
head.

Sometimes I catch myself going through the setup deliberately, just to
see how close I can get before I have to stop.

I haven’t made it all the way through again.
