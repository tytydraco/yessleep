I know how this sounds. I know you’ll read this and think it’s fiction,
or that I’m having some kind of episode. But I need to write this down
while I still can. While my memory still holds together. While I can
still tell what’s mine and what’s bleeding through from somewhere else.

If you find this, if you read this, maybe you’ll understand. Or maybe
you’ll just remember one line. Because I’m forgetting the rest.

Prologue

The ink is almost gone. Or maybe it’s the words running out — it’s
getting harder to find ones that won’t crumble the moment I write them
down. My fingers tremble, the letters crawl, as if they want to escape
the page. But I have to leave something behind. At least a scrap of
truth, before my memory turns into a sieve full of holes.

The world still pretended to be normal back then — well, as normal as a
world can be where politics is endless snarling and local wars flare up
like dry bonfires from a stray spark. Politicians screamed at each other
from the screens, as if the strength of their shouting decided whether
the sun would rise. And it rose. And set. And rose again. And the city
blocks kept burning. We shrugged: “Well, somebody’s shooting somewhere
again.” We got used to it the way you get used to the sound of rain
outside your window — first it keeps you awake, then you stop hearing it
at all. Only this rain was made of ash, and the sound was made of
sirens.

I see that corridor — long, white, sterile, as if scrubbed to a
crystalline emptiness. It smells of alcohol and of fear that everyone
carefully hides behind calm faces. Someone in the queue nervously flips
through a magazine, someone sighs as if already tired of hope itself.
And I stand there thinking: let it work. Let this be the miracle they
keep talking about on every channel. Let the pain go, the way water goes
into sand.

A vaccine. A miracle. That’s what they called it. A cure for cancer — as
if someone had finally found a lockpick for the most stubborn door in
the world. At first it really did cure. Everyone. Or we wanted so badly
to believe it — the way you want to believe in a fairy tale when there’s
nothing around but cold and damp. On the television — tears of joy,
embraces, happy faces, as if the world had finally been fixed with a
single injection. I remember that room. A quiet voice: “Done.” Cold in
the vein — as if an icy ant had run through my blood. And then —
silence. The kind I hadn’t heard for so many years, because the pain was
always whispering something, screaming, howling inside me. And suddenly
— it went quiet.

Everyone rejoiced. We thought we’d won. And then… then the silence
became a different kind. Not the good, healing kind, but heavy, as if
the air had thickened and was pressing down on my shoulders. First there
were rumors — light as down, carried off by the wind, vanishing. Then
articles that were deleted faster than you could read them. Then —
silence where there used to be screaming. Something had broken. Or
mutated. Who helped it along — there’s no way to know now. And no one
left to ask.

Sometimes I think I’m a mistake of nature. A rare glitch in a flawless
algorithm. The vaccine was supposed to either cure, or… and I just
stayed. Like a pebble at the bottom of a rushing river: the water tears
past, boiling, foaming, and I lie there counting the years until they
start blurring into one gray smear. Five hundred years? More? Less? The
numbers crumble like dry leaves in the wind. Sometimes it seems to me
I’ve lived enough lives to fill a whole street of old men. And sometimes
— that it was all yesterday, and I’m still standing in that queue,
waiting for someone to tell me: “Done.”

Very rarely — in literally a handful of cases — the vaccine produced a
different side effect. Longevity. Not just extra years, but years that
break the mind, because there are too many of them. One injection — and
time seemed to stop sticking to me. It slid past like water off a greasy
surface, and I remained. How many years have gone by — I couldn’t say
anymore. Memory is an old rug now: worn through to holes in places,
darkened in others, and the pattern is almost impossible to make out.

For everyone else, the vaccine became a door. Only they didn’t know
where it led. At first it was small things: a person looks through you
as if you’re not a person but a shadow on the wall. Then the small
things grew, like cracks in glass, until they shattered everything.
People stopped recognizing their own family. Stopped recognizing
themselves. Their movements became jerky, sharp, like puppets on strings
— as if someone were tugging invisible threads without knowing the
rhythm. Their voices sounded as if it wasn’t them speaking. As if
something alien had moved in, like an uninvited guest who sits down in
your seat and now looks out through your eyes. They weren’t sick — they
were empty. As if someone had pulled out the core and left only the
shell, which walks, and searches, and reaches…

I see my grandson’s face. He’s small, laughing, reaching his arms toward
me. The sound of his laugh — thin, clear, like the ring of a crystal
bell. And the name… the name slipped out like a coin through my fingers.
I hear the laughter, but I can’t catch the word. That’s the most
terrible thing — not forgetting, but knowing you’ve forgotten and being
unable to bring it back.

And then the strikes began. Not all at once. First one — distant,
someone else’s, like a far-off roll of thunder that doesn’t even
frighten you at first. Then the answer. And another. And another. The
earth flinched, as if someone were hitting it in the back with a heavy
fist. I was standing… where? I don’t remember. On the balcony? At the
window? I only remember the horizon burning, as if someone had set the
edge of the sky on fire. And the sky… it was changing. Becoming alien.

The world cracked. Like an old cup squeezed too hard in a hand — and now
something else is coming through the fractures. Shadows moved wrong — as
if they lagged a fraction of a second behind, like an echo that arrives
too late. Creatures knew things they couldn’t have known, as if they’d
dreamed dreams that didn’t belong to this world. The air grew thick as
tar, sticky, heavy — you wanted to shake it off your shoulders like a
cobweb you’d walked into. Smells turned strange, alien: ozone mixed with
something ancient, as if from another time. Once I saw a sky with violet
veins. The stars circled not in a ring but in a spiral, as if someone
had twisted the sky like water down a drain. I stood and watched, and my
fingers trembled. Not from the cold. From understanding: we had opened a
door that should never have been opened. And now it doesn’t close.

Sometimes I forget my grandson’s name. Sometimes — my own. But I
remember the main thing: the world didn’t break in a single day. It
broke slowly. To the sound of talk about greatness. To promises of
salvation. To a quiet needle in a vein.

And after those strikes the world didn’t change all at once — it seemed
afraid to show its new face whole, and revealed it in pieces, gradually,
like a man trying on someone else’s mask who still can’t bring himself
to turn and face you. It changed the way water changes when you drip ink
into it: first only a faint cloudiness, a barely visible ripple, and
then suddenly you realize — the old clarity is never coming back.

At first they were small things, which I blamed on exhaustion, on nerves
drawn tight as a string ready to snap at any rustle. But then the small
things stopped being small.

I remember walking once across a bridge that had half collapsed. The
wind hummed in the steel beams like in empty bottles, and the sky was
gray, as if it had been washed until the color came out. And suddenly
the air in front of me seemed to thicken — not like fog, but like
setting syrup. It shimmered, rippled, and in that trembling veil I
glimpsed the outlines of something else: not ruins, not rubble, but… a
city. Tall, strange, with towers like needles and streets that ran not
along the ground but somehow through the air. For a moment it all lay
over my world like a transparent film, and I saw both the rusted railing
of the bridge and those towers at once. Then the veil burst with a quiet
crack, like the sound of tearing cloth, and there was only cold wind and
the smell of burning. I stood and stared at the place where the overlap
had just been, and my fingers trembled not from cold but from
understanding: I had seen what should not be here. And the worst part
was that I was starting to get used to that feeling — the way you get
used to a creak in the house that frightens you at first and then
becomes part of the silence.

Then I began noticing tracks. Not human, not animal — as if someone had
passed without touching the ground. The prints were shallow but strange:
their edges were too even, as if they’d been cut out rather than left by
a foot. And they led where there was no road. I followed them, not
knowing why, until they dissolved into dust like a mark on water. And
nearby, on a wall, there was a spiral — not chalk, not paint, but as if
burned into the brick. It wasn’t like a sign or a drawing, but when you
looked at it, alien images surfaced in your head: snow-covered forests,
narrow lanes, the smell of smoke and iron. Alien images, yet somehow
familiar, like memories of dreams you had as a child and long ago
forgot.

Once I met a man who spoke as if he knew this new world better than the
old one. He wore a battered jacket, but there was a strange composure in
his movements, as if he were always listening for something I couldn’t
hear.

“Everything’s shifting here,” he said, looking not at me but somewhere
through me. “The boundaries are wearing away. What used to be separate
now overlaps. And the further it goes, the stronger you feel it.”

He spoke calmly, matter-of-factly, the way people talk about the
weather, and that made it even more frightening.

“You’ll see,” he added. “First it’ll be shadows. Then sounds. Then
smells. And then you’ll start noticing how things don’t match what
they’re supposed to be. And the hardest part is not going mad from the
fact that you’re starting to accept it as normal.”

He left, and I stayed standing there, breathing air that suddenly
smelled not of burning and dust but of the sea — salt, cold, alive. And
that smell lingered a long time, though there wasn’t a drop of water
anywhere around.

Since then I’ve made a habit of watching the mismatches. The shadows
that fell in the wrong direction. The sounds that came from the wrong
place. The smells that didn’t belong. Once, in an abandoned depot, I
found a strange pattern on the floor — not drawn, but as if it had
surfaced out of the very texture of the concrete. It resembled a map,
but with no names, no scale, with lines converging at points that made
no sense. I ran a finger along them, and a faint chill stayed on my
skin, as if I’d touched ice. And when I raised my head, a figure
flickered in the far corner — not human, too elongated, with long,
unnaturally bent limbs. It moved without a sound, and a trail of
distorted air followed it, like the shimmer above a heated stone. I
didn’t scream. I didn’t even move. I just stood and watched it disappear
into the wall — not through it, but as if for a moment the wall had
stopped being a wall.

The world now is like an old photograph placed under glass together with
another one, and the layers have overlapped: something alien shows
through the familiar landscape, and the longer you look, the harder it
is to tell which layer is real. Sometimes it seems to me the vaccine
wasn’t just a medicine and wasn’t just a curse. It was a point where
reality cracked. And through that crack came a draft from other places —
alien, ancient, unfamiliar. And that draft stirs the hair on the back of
your neck and whispers: “You’re standing at a junction. And the roads
lead in every direction at once.”

I don’t know how much longer I’ll be able to hold in my head what is
mine and what came from outside. Memory fails me more and more, and
alien images weave into my recollections like threads of another color
into old cloth. Sometimes I catch myself remembering places I’ve never
been and faces I’ve never seen, and they seem just as real as the face
of my grandson — the one whose name I can no longer recall.

My fingers tremble, the ink is running out, the words grow heavier, as
if every letter pulls downward. But I have to write it down. While I can
still tell my own memories from what seeps through the cracks. While I
still remember that once the world was simple, comprehensible, cruel,
but mine. And now it has become an enormous crossroads where roads from
a thousand places meet, and no one knows where the next turn will lead.

Maybe someone will find these pages and understand. Or at least feel it
— how the air trembles at the junctions, how alien shadows fall in the
wrong direction, how words sound with an unfamiliar accent, as if spoken
on the far bank of a river that flows through time itself. Or maybe
they’ll just remember one line. One. Because the rest I’ve already
forgotten. Or it mixed with what came from outside, and now I can’t
separate mine from theirs.
