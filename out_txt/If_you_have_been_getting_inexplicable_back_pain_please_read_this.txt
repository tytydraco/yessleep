​

I’m not a doctor. I’m just a guy who spent three months getting chewed
up by the market here. If you live in this city, you know the drill. You
scroll through listings at 2:00 AM, wading through obvious wire scams,
$1,800 “micro-studios” that are literally just converted walk-in
closets, and landlords demanding twelve months of rent upfront. It’s
exhausting.

So when I found a bright, spacious one-bedroom right in downtown Toronto
for $1,350 a month, I didn’t ask questions. I was too relieved to care
why it was still available, or why the previous tenant had left so
suddenly. The landlord handed me the keys with a strange, nervous haste,
but I just took them and smiled. I thought I had won the jackpot.

That was two weeks ago.

Almost immediately after moving in, the back pain started. At first, it
was just a strange, deep ache right at the base of my spine. I brushed
it off. I searched online and found countless forum posts saying it’s
completely normal to get backaches when you switch to a new mattress
while your body adjusts to it. So, I figured I just needed to ride it
out.

I let the first few days pass like that, but the pain only got worse. It
got to the point where, the moment I woke up, I literally couldn’t walk
for the first hour. I just lay there in bed, practically paralyzed,
staring at the ceiling and waiting for the agony to dull enough so I
could slide onto the floor.

I finally caved and went to see a doctor. After taking some scans and
running a couple of tests, he walked back into the room, looking
confused. He asked me straight up: “Is there any chance you’re a
powerlifter? Or have you been hauling around heavy sacks of concrete
lately? Maybe working construction?”

I looked at him like he was out of his mind. I told him no—I’ve never
even stepped foot inside a gym in my whole life, and I work a standard
IT desk job, so construction was entirely out of the picture.

He tapped my scans, pointing at the compressed discs, and told me that,
regardless, the reason for the pain was that my spine was under severe
physical stress from carrying an immense amount of static weight. I
insisted there was no way that was possible. He just shook his head,
gave me a prescription, and told me to come back and see him in a week
to check on how it was progressing.

I carried on with my normal routine and went back home, but the pain
just kept getting worse and worse every single night. I kept blaming the
mattress. In my head, that was still the only rational explanation.

To test it out, I went over to a friend’s apartment and crashed on his
couch for the night. When I woke up the next morning, I had almost zero
back pain. Nothing at all. I felt completely refreshed for the first
time in weeks.

That was all the proof I needed. It basically confirmed my assumption
that the bed in my new place was the whole problem.

So, I went home.

Once I got back to my place, a thought hit me: what if I just lie on the
bed for a couple of hours right now and see if the pain starts coming
back?

So that’s what I did. I climbed into bed, laid down, watched a couple of
movies, doom-scrolled on my phone for a few hours, and… nothing. There
was literally no pain at all.

That was the moment the whole mattress theory started to crumble. It
seemed like whatever was causing my back pain was only happening while I
was fast asleep at night.

I decided to just test it out a bit longer to see if the pain would keep
going. And when I tell you the next couple of days were absolute hell,
I’m not exaggerating. It got so bad that I had to call off work entirely
and just stay home. It was completely messing up my life. Every morning
I woke up, I wasn’t just sore—I was paralyzed for hours at a time, stuck
flat on my back, completely unable to move.

So, I set up a camera.

I figured it was the only logical next step. I wanted to see what was
actually happening to my body while I slept at night. Was I contorting
myself into some bizarre, unnatural position? Was I somehow twisting my
lower spine against the headboard? Was I sleepwalking and doing
something stupid in the middle of the night without realizing it? I
spent half an hour rationalizing it, coming up with every ridiculous
theory I could think of just to make sense of the damage.

I propped my phone up on the dresser facing the bed, set it to record,
and went to sleep.

As usual, the next morning was absolute agony. I woke up to a sharp,
crushing pressure that felt worse than anything I’d experienced yet—an
unbearable weight grinding straight into my lumbar spine. It was getting
worse every single night.

I lay there paralyzed for nearly two hours, staring at the ceiling,
waiting for the stiffness to ease just enough so I could reach the
dresser. When I finally dragged myself up and grabbed the phone, my
hands were shaking uncontrollably.

I opened the video file, skipped past the first three hours of absolute
nothingness—just dead silence and me sleeping on my stomach—and started
scrubbing through the midnight footage.

Around 2:53 a.m., something swung my door slightly open.

A long, pale, white hand reached through. It opened the door ever so
carefully, moving with a sickening, effortless precision that didn’t
make a single sound.

Then it stepped inside.

It was a tall, pale, white figure—easily ten or eleven feet high—with a
body so impossibly skinny and slender that its joints looked paper-thin.
Its arms hung long and limp past its knees. On its face, two massive,
wide red eyes stared straight ahead, set above a jagged, fixed smile
that never left its face.

It walked slowly toward the side of my bed, completely silent, towering
all the way up to the ceiling.

Then it climbed onto the mattress.

Because of its sheer height, it didn’t just sit—its entire ten- or
eleven-foot body crumbled and folded in on itself like a collapsing
frame. In the dead quiet of the recording, the microphone picked up
every single movement. Every time its joints bent, you could hear this
horrible, sickening snap—almost as if thick, dry bones were breaking
under tension. It straddled me, sitting squarely on my torso, facing me,
its face hovering just inches from mine as it stared down into my eyes
with that unmoving grin.

Its entire, unnatural weight pressed flat down onto my chest and
stomach, forcing my lumbar spine to bow violently against the mattress
beneath it.

And after three hours of it sitting on my body like that, not moving at
all, not even a change in the expression of its face, it left. The time
was exactly 5:53 a.m. when that happened. The noise when it finally
moved was terrifying—as it lifted itself off me, the microphone caught
this loud, heavy suction sound, like someone pulling a vacuum seal
straight off your flesh, or wet plaster peeling violently off a wall.
The sheer release of pressure was sickening to watch. It left exactly
the way it came.

The next few days, the exact same thing happened. It comes inside my
room at 2:53 a.m. every single night, and then goes back outside at 5:53
a.m.

I don’t know what to do. I don’t know how I can get rid of it. But the
only thing I know is that I won’t be able to live with this back pain
anymore. This has gotten to a point where I don’t even know how long I
can go on like this.

So if any of you guys have ever had this problem, or if you’ve been
dealing with the same entity, please message me. Please tell me what I
can do. What are my choices? I’m begging you. Please, I need help.
