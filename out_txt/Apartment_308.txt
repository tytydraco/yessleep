​

I moved into Cedar Grove Apartments in October, about five months after
my divorce was finalized. I was thirty-four years old, and at the time I
was pretty convinced my life was over.

I don’t mean that in the dramatic way people sometimes say it after a
breakup. I genuinely couldn’t picture a future for myself anymore. My
wife and I had been together for eleven years and married for seven.
Then one night she sat across from me at our kitchen table and told me
she didn’t love me anymore. There wasn’t an affair, at least not that I
ever knew about. There wasn’t some huge fight or betrayal that I could
point to and blame everything on. She just didn’t want to be married to
me anymore.

Five months later, she had the house and I had apartment 307 in Building
C at Cedar Grove.

There was absolutely nothing remarkable about Cedar Grove. It was one of
those apartment complexes that seemed to exist in every town in America.

Several identical three-story brick buildings from the 1970s surrounded
by cracked parking lots, dumpsters and patches of grass. Beige metal
doors, aluminum railings, concrete balconies, brown carpet in the
hallways. There was a laundry room downstairs where one washer was
always broken and a row of mailboxes by the entrance. It wasn’t creepy.
It wasn’t particularly run down. It was just old and boring.

My apartment had one bedroom, a small kitchen and a sliding glass door
overlooking the parking lot. For the first few weeks, I barely unpacked.
I slept on a mattress on the floor even though the bed frame was sitting
in pieces against the wall. Half my clothes stayed in boxes. I ate
frozen dinners over the kitchen sink because buying a table seemed
pointless.

Everything seemed pointless.

I went to work because I had bills. I showered because people would
notice if I didn’t. I ate because eventually hunger became uncomfortable
enough that I had to do something about it. Then I’d come home, lock the
door and sit in front of the television until it was late enough to
sleep.

Weekends were the worst. Nobody tells you how long a Saturday can feel
when nobody is waiting for you.

Sometimes I’d sit in my car after work for half an hour because I
couldn’t think of a reason to go upstairs. Eventually I’d remember there
wasn’t anywhere else to go, so I’d climb the steps to 307 and spend
another night alone. Friends called for a while. I stopped answering.
Eventually they called less. My parents worried about me, but I got
pretty good at sounding normal on the phone. “I’m doing fine.”

That became my answer to everything.

I wasn’t fine.

There were nights when I’d lie awake until three or four in the morning
staring at the ceiling, wondering how I was supposed to do this for
another forty or fifty years. I wasn’t making plans to hurt myself. I
just felt completely detached from the idea of having a future. If
someone had told me I wouldn’t wake up the next morning, I’m not sure I
would have cared.

I met Sally about three weeks after I moved in. It had been a bad day.
Nothing had actually happened, which somehow made it worse. I went to
work, sat at my desk for eight hours, drove home and spent twenty
minutes sitting in my car staring at the windows of Building C. I
remember thinking there wasn’t a single person in that entire building
who cared whether I came upstairs or not. Eventually I did.

When I reached the third floor, the door across from mine was open a few
inches. I’d never seen whoever lived there. I knew someone did because
every once in a while I’d noticed a warm yellow light underneath the
door at night. As I put my key into 307, I heard a woman’s voice behind
me.

“Long day?”

I turned around. An elderly woman was standing just inside apartment
308. She looked to be in her late seventies, small and slightly stooped,
with short, curly white hair and large glasses.

She wore a pale blue cardigan over a flowered blouse and had one hand
resting against the doorframe.

“Something like that,” I said.

She studied me for a second. “You look like you could use some coffee.”

Normally I would have made an excuse. I hadn’t spoken to anyone
voluntarily in weeks. Instead I looked at my own apartment. Dark. Quiet.
Empty. Then I looked back at her.

“Sure.”

She opened the door wider.

“I’m Sally.”

“Chris.”

“Nice to meet you, Chris.”

I stepped inside. That was the first time I entered apartment 308.

Sally’s apartment looked like someone had decorated it around 1985 and
then decided that was good enough forever. There was a green sofa with
wooden arms, brown curtains over the sliding glass door, fake wood
paneling along one wall and an old television on a wooden stand. Framed
photographs covered almost every available surface. Most of them were of
the same man.

“That’s Arthur,” Sally told me when she noticed me looking. “My
husband.”

Arthur had died years earlier. Sally didn’t say much about it, only that
it had been sudden. They had never had children, and she’d been alone
for a long time.

“So it’s just you?” I asked.

“Just me.” She smiled. “Looks like we’re in the same club.”

I smiled too. That was how it started.

After that, I started knocking on 308 every once in a while after work.
At first maybe once a week. Then twice. Eventually I was there most
evenings. Sally made terrible coffee. Somehow it was always both weak
and bitter. I never had the heart to tell her.

We played cards. We watched television. Sometimes we sat by the sliding
glass door and watched headlights move through the parking lot below.
Mostly, though, we talked. She knew about the divorce. Eventually she
knew everything.

What I liked about Sally was that she never tried to fix me. Everyone
else had advice. Start dating again. Go to the gym. Find a hobby. Get
out of the apartment. Meet people. You’re only thirty-four. You’ve got
your whole life ahead of you. Sally never said any of that.

One night I finally told her something I hadn’t said out loud to
anybody. “I don’t think I’m ever going to be happy again.”

We were sitting in her living room while snow fell outside. Sally didn’t
immediately tell me I was wrong. She just nodded. “I remember thinking
that.”

“After Arthur?”

“Yes.”

“How long did it last?”

She thought about it. “A long time.”

It wasn’t comforting, but it was honest. I think that’s what I needed.

“I don’t know what I’m doing anymore,” I told her. “I go to work. I come
home. I sleep. Then I do it again. That’s my life.”

Sally reached over and put her hand on mine. Her fingers were
surprisingly cold. “You’re still here.”

I gave this bitter little laugh. “For whatever that’s worth.”

She looked at me. “It can be worth quite a lot.”

I cried that night. I hadn’t planned to. It just happened. Sally didn’t
tell me everything would be okay. She didn’t give me some speech about
moving on. She just sat there with me until I was done.

Something started changing after that. Not all at once. I didn’t
suddenly wake up happy one morning. I still missed my wife. I still
hated coming home to an empty apartment. There were still mornings when
getting out of bed felt almost impossible. But now I had Sally across
the hall.

Sometimes something stupid would happen at work and I’d catch myself
thinking that I couldn’t wait to tell her about it. That sounds like
nothing. At the time, it was everything.

I assembled my bed frame. I finally unpacked the boxes. I started
cooking actual meals again. I bought a plant for the living room. It
died within two weeks, and Sally thought that was hilarious. I bought
another one mostly to prove to her that I could keep something alive.
That one survived.

There were things about Sally that seemed strange, although I didn’t
think much about them at the time. She never came into my apartment. I’d
invite her sometimes, especially once I started cooking again, but she’d
always politely decline.

“I’m comfortable here,” she’d say.

I never saw her downstairs. Never saw her by the mailboxes. Never saw
her in the parking lot or on the stairs. Actually, I never saw Sally
outside apartment 308 at all. If her door was open, she was standing
just inside it. Whenever I left after visiting her, she’d walk me to the
doorway, say goodnight and stop at the threshold.

By January, I was doing better. Not great. Not even good, really. But
better. I started answering my friends again. I went out to dinner with
my brother one weekend. I visited my parents without them having to ask
me five times first. I stopped drinking alone so much.

I started thinking about things beyond tomorrow. Nothing ambitious.
Maybe I’d take a trip somewhere in the summer. Maybe I’d finally replace
my old car. Maybe I’d get a better apartment someday. For the first time
since the divorce, the future didn’t feel completely empty.

Without realizing it, I also stopped knocking on Sally’s door every
night. One evening when I did visit, she mentioned it.

“You don’t come over as much anymore.”

I immediately felt guilty. “I’m sorry.”

Sally shook her head. “No.” She smiled. “That’s good.”

“You’re getting sick of me?”

“Never.”

Then her expression changed. She looked sad. But there was something
else there too. Relief, maybe.

“You’re doing better, Chris.”

“I guess.”

“You are.”

Something about the way she said it made me uncomfortable. It sounded
strangely final.

I looked around and realized her apartment was freezing. It was the
middle of January and the place felt almost as cold as the hallway.

“Your heat isn’t working.”

Sally looked around like she hadn’t noticed. “I suppose it isn’t.”

“I’ll call maintenance tomorrow.”

Her eyes immediately went to mine. “No.”

The answer came so quickly that it startled me. “Why not?”

For a moment she didn’t answer. Then she smiled. “Don’t worry about me.”

I laughed a little. “Somebody has to.”

Sally looked at me for a long time after I said that. Then she looked
away. Something about her seemed different that night. She looked tired.
Faded. That’s the only word I’ve ever been able to think of. Faded. Like
an old photograph that’s been sitting in sunlight for years.

When I got up to leave, Sally walked me to the door.

“Chris?”

“Yeah?”

“I’m glad you moved here.”

I smiled. “I’m glad you were here.”

Her face changed when I said that. For a second, I thought she might
cry.

“Goodnight, Chris.”

“Goodnight, Sally.”

She smiled. I walked across the hallway and went inside 307. That was
the last time I ever saw her.

The next morning, I called the rental office about her heat. The
maintenance guy was named Frank. He’d worked at Cedar Grove for more
than thirty years. I told him the heat wasn’t working in 308.

There was a pause.

“Building C?”

“Yeah.”

Another pause.

“What’s your apartment?”

“307.”

Frank didn’t say anything for several seconds. Then he asked, “Who told
you there’s no heat in 308?”

“The woman who lives there.”

“What woman?”

“My neighbor. Sally.”

Silence.

“Sally Price.”

Frank went completely quiet. Finally he said, “I’ll be up in a few
minutes.”

About ten minutes later he came upstairs carrying a ring of keys. He
looked uncomfortable.

“You’re sure you said 308?”

I pointed across the hallway. “That one.”

Frank stared at the door. Then he looked at me. “Nobody lives there.”

I actually laughed. “What?”

“308 is vacant.”

“No, it isn’t.”

“It is.”

“I was in there last night.”

Frank didn’t respond. I could feel something cold spreading through my
stomach.

“Open it.”

He hesitated.

“Frank, open the door.”

He unlocked 308. The door swung inward.

I don’t think I’ll ever forget what I saw.

Nothing.

The apartment was empty. Not recently empty. It looked like nobody had
lived there in years.

The green sofa was gone. The television was gone. The brown curtains
were gone. There were no photographs. No coffee table. No cards. No
coffee cups. The fake wood paneling was still there, but it was warped
and peeling away from the wall. Dust covered the faded carpet. Dead
insects filled the track beneath the sliding glass door.

I stood in the doorway staring. For some reason, the first thing I
looked for was Sally’s chair. There was nothing there.

“No.”

Frank looked at me.

“No. This isn’t right.”

He didn’t say anything.

“I was sitting right there last night.” I pointed toward the empty space
beside the sliding glass door. “She was sitting there.”

Frank’s face had gone pale. “Who?”

“Sally.”

He stared at me. Then he asked me to come downstairs with him.

In the maintenance office, Frank dug through an old filing cabinet until
he found a folder. Sally and Arthur Price had been among the first
tenants in Building C when Cedar Grove opened in the 1970s. Arthur died
years later. Sally stayed in apartment 308 by herself.

Then Frank told me Sally had died in 2008.

I told him that was impossible.

He didn’t argue with me. He just took an old photocopy from the folder
and put it on the desk. It was Sally’s driver’s license.

Short curly white hair. Those big glasses. That same gentle smile.

I stared at the picture for a long time. Then I went upstairs.

I called out of work and sat inside 307 with every light on. I kept
expecting to hear Sally’s door open across the hall. It never did. I
never saw her again.

For the first few days, that terrified me. Then, strangely, it started
to make me sad. I kept thinking about the last thing she’d said to me.

“You’re doing better, Chris.”

She had sounded relieved. Maybe Sally knew it was time to go.

I don’t know what happened to her after that night. I don’t know why she
was there, or why she chose to open her door to me. I just know that
when I was at the lowest point of my life, Sally was there.

And now I’m doing better.

I still have bad days. I’m still alone. But I see my family. I talk to
my friends. I make plans. I have reasons to get up in the morning.

I’ve never knocked on 308 again. I don’t want Sally to come back. I hope
she finally got to leave. I hope Arthur was waiting for her.

And I hope somehow she knows I’m okay.

Still here.

For whatever that’s worth.

Sally was right.

It can be worth quite a lot.
