Chapter 1

My wife and I slipped into another argument. By now, they came as
naturally as nightfall, predictable, unavoidable, and always leaving
something broken. Our voices climbed from sharp words to screams that
rattled the walls until silence swallowed us whole. A chair splintered
beneath my hands. Glass glittered across the floor where a picture frame
had died. The house seemed to breathe with us, feeding on every ounce of
rage we poured into it.

But the furniture wasn’t the only thing we shattered.

We weren’t always like this. We were high school sweethearts, then
college best friends, and eventually husband and wife. We used to move
through life like two peas in a pod, finishing each other’s sentences
and believing nothing could ever come between us.

Now we were still two peas in a pod, only we’d been left to rot.
Resentment spread through us like mold beneath old floorboards, slow and
unnoticed until there was nothing healthy left to save. I could still
see the woman I fell in love with, but she looked like a stranger
wearing my wife’s face. And when I caught my reflection in the broken
glass, I wasn’t sure the man staring back was me anymore.

Sometimes I wonder if our marriage is dying. Other times, I wonder if
something inside this house is making sure it does.

We went to bed. Not together, of course. We hadn’t shared a bed in two
weeks.

She claimed the master bedroom upstairs. I claimed the long navy-blue
sectional in the living room, a three-piece couch with six throw pillows
that offered more comfort than the silence between us ever could.

There wasn’t a blanket.

The last of them had been ripped apart during one of our fights and
thrown into the trash like everything else we’d once tried to keep
together. Funny how anger always knew exactly what to destroy.

The house settled around me with slow, aching creaks. Every pop in the
walls sounded like footsteps. Every groan in the ceiling felt like a
warning. Sleep never came easily anymore. The darkness didn’t feel
empty, it felt occupied, as if something lingered just beyond the edge
of my vision, patiently waiting for me to close my eyes.

Just another ordinary night.

I woke up the next morning shivering so hard my teeth ached.

For a moment, I couldn’t remember where I was. The ceiling above me came
into focus, followed by the familiar stiffness in my back from another
night on the couch. Then I felt the cold, not the kind that nips at your
skin, but the kind that seeps into your bones and settles there.

As my eyes adjusted, I spotted the crack running across the living room
window.

Right. The vase.

She’d thrown it at my head the night before. I managed to duck just in
time, and it exploded through the glass instead. Lucky me. The window
wasn’t nearly as fortunate.

I dragged myself to the hall closet and found a roll of duct tape. It
wasn’t going to fix the window, but maybe it would convince the icy wind
to stay outside. The tape peeled away with a slow, sticky groan as I
pressed it over the fractured glass, the crack spidering beneath it like
frozen lightning.

It was late December in Wisconsin, only days before Christmas. By
nightfall, the temperature dipped to nearly ten degrees, and every draft
felt like a knife. Once, this season smelled of pine needles, cinnamon,
and fresh cookies. Once, this house glowed with warm lights and louder
laughter.

Now it smelled of stale air, broken promises, and a cold that no furnace
could chase away.

The worst part wasn’t the winter outside. It was how easily the house
had learned to feel just as lifeless.

I put the duct tape back in the hall closet and closed the door with
more care than it deserved.

A moment later, I heard the bedroom door upstairs creak open.

In a house like ours, every sound traveled. Built in 1919, the old
Victorian still wore its original maple floors, and they announced every
footstep like a confession. The wood groaned beneath her weight as she
crossed the second floor. Slow. Deliberate. Familiar.

I didn’t have to look up to know it was her.

She descended the staircase one measured step at a time, each creak
echoing through the house until she stepped into the living room.

Our eyes met.

For a heartbeat, the world stood still. There were a thousand things
either of us could have said. An apology. An accusation. Anything.

Instead, we stared.

Then she looked away.

So did I.

Just another morning in a house where silence had become our first
language, and somehow, it always felt like something else was listening.

I sat down on my bed.

At least, that’s what I called it now. The navy-blue couch had become my
bed for the past week, a place where I slept, stared at the ceiling, and
wondered how two people who once couldn’t stand being apart had somehow
learned to live worlds away under the same roof.

She walked over to the coffee table and picked up the water bottle.

The same one I had thrown against the wall the night before.

The plastic was dented from the impact, a small reminder of how quickly
we could turn ordinary moments into something ugly. She twisted the cap
open, took a drink, and then, without a word, sat down beside me.

Beside me.

It was such a simple thing. Something we used to do without thinking.
Something that once felt natural.

But now, her sitting inches away felt unfamiliar. Almost dangerous.

I stared at the space between us, waiting for the next explosion. The
next accusation. The next thing thrown across the room.

But nothing came.

And that was when it hit me.

I couldn’t remember the last time we’d been this close without something
breaking.

Without one of us yelling.

Without something, an object, a wall, or a piece of ourselves, taking
the damage.

Two people who used to hold each other through everything had somehow
become two strangers sitting on the same couch, afraid of what would
happen if we reached out.

“Josh, I can’t keep doing this.”

The words hit harder than anything ever thrown in this house.

For a moment, I forgot how to breathe. My chest tightened, my heart
pounding against my ribs like it was trying to escape before it had to
hear what came next.

“What do you mean, Abby?” I somehow managed to force the words out.

She looked down at her hands, avoiding my eyes.

“The fighting… the violence… the excuses… us.”

Her voice cracked on the last word.

Then came the tears.

Small. Quiet. Fragile.

They slipped from her blue eyes and traced paths down her red, swollen
cheeks. Seeing her cry used to be something I would do anything to stop.
I used to be the person who wiped away those tears.

Now, I was the reason they existed.

“I know,” I whispered.

The words felt heavier than they should have. Like admitting them gave
them power.

I looked around the room, the broken furniture, the damaged walls, the
pieces of a life we once loved scattered everywhere.

“What have we become?” I asked.

The silence that followed felt endless.

Then Abby looked at me.

Not with anger. Not with hatred. With sadness.

“Animals,” she said.

And somehow, that hurt more than any fight we’d ever had. Because she
was right.

Somewhere along the way, we stopped being two people who loved each
other and became creatures trapped in a cage of our own making, hurting,
lashing out, and forgetting that we were supposed to be on the same
side.

“I made a phone call last night,” she said.

The way she said it made the room feel colder.

“I don’t know what else to do.”

My stomach dropped.

There are certain words that can change the shape of a moment forever.
Words that make your heart stop before your mind can catch up.

“What do you mean?” I asked.

I tried to hide the fear in my voice, but I knew she heard it. My mind
raced through every possibility. Was it a divorce attorney? Had she
already made the decision to end us? Was this the beginning of the final
chapter of something I wasn’t ready to lose?

“Who did you call?”

She looked at me for a long moment.

“A professional,” she finally said. “A couples therapist.”

I blinked.

For some reason, I had expected something worse.

Something final.

But somehow, those words hurt in their own way.

“I know it sounds stupid,” she whispered, “but I’m lost, Josh. I don’t
know what we are anymore.”

The room went quiet.

Not the familiar silence we had perfected over the last few weeks. This
was different. This was the kind of silence where the truth finally had
room to breathe.

I looked at the woman sitting beside me, the woman I promised forever
to. The woman I once couldn’t imagine a single day without.

Slowly, carefully, I reached for her hand.

I hesitated before touching her, almost like I was afraid she might
disappear.

And then I realized something that scared me more than any fight we’d
ever had.

I couldn’t remember the last time I had touched my wife without anger
between us.

No arguments.

No yelling.

No broken things.

Just us.

Her hand in mine felt familiar.

But somehow, it also felt like I was meeting a stranger.

“We have an appointment today at 2pm.”

Her words pulled me out of my thoughts.

For a moment, I didn’t understand what she meant. Then reality came
rushing back, the therapist, the phone call, the fragile thread we were
both desperately holding onto.

My eyes drifted toward the clock hanging on the living room wall.

Or what was left of it.

It dangled crookedly from a single string, a souvenir from one of our
arguments a few nights ago. Another casualty of the war we had somehow
started inside our own home.

The hands pointed to 12:16pm.

My stomach dropped.

“Fuck,” I muttered under my breath.

I hadn’t realized how much time had passed.

Funny how time worked in this house. The days blurred together. The
fights. The silence. The regret afterward. It all became one endless
loop, like we were trapped inside a nightmare neither of us could wake
up from.

But today was different.

Today, we weren’t fighting.

Today, we were going to sit across from a stranger and admit what we had
become.

I looked at Abby sitting beside me, her hand still in mine.

Two hours until someone asked us the question we had been avoiding for
months.

Can this be saved?

Or is this just the beginning of the end?
