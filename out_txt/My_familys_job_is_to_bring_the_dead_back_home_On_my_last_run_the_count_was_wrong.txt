There are people buried here who were never allowed to leave.

We’ll never find most of them. No marked graves. You either get lucky
with modern construction or know where to look: railroad cuts, abandoned
mines, rural farmland. Usually men who came over for Gold Mountain and
found a country that wanted their backs instead of their brains.

When they inevitably died, it was as far away as possible from anything
resembling home or their ancestors. In the early twentieth century,
there were secret societies that did something about it. Dug them up.
Cleaned the bones. Packed them in crates. Shipped them back across the
ocean to return to their proper resting place. But that time is long
gone.

They didn’t stop the work because it was finished. They stopped because
the only people who knew how to do it died out. The ones who knew how to
keep the souls in the box.

My grandfather did it the old way. Now I do.

I’m not here to explain the whole method. In part because I don’t fully
understand it, but mostly because you shouldn’t have access to it. I
will provide the rules, because they are what matter. Because something
happened in my last run. Because I want a record in the event no one
hears from me again.

The rules passed down to me are simple:

-   You only travel at night. You must get behind a doorway with salt
    and a name over it before the sun comes up. Whatever happens, you
    cannot let the sun catch you on the road with them.
-   The bell rings the whole time you travel. Hang it from the mirror to
    keep it moving. Take it with you when you stop. As long as you can
    hear it, you’re the one in charge. If it goes quiet, you’ve lost.
-   Never let them get wet. Rain, river, burst pipe, doesn’t matter.
    Water wakes up the thing inside them that remembers being a person.
    The thing that is angry.
-   Paper stays on their faces at all times. My grandfather called it
    the seal. If one comes loose, you stop, and you fix it, immediately.
-   You count the bodies at every stop. You’re handed a number before
    you begin. That number is what you pick up. That number is what you
    deliver. It must never change. If the count is ever wrong, you’re in
    serious trouble.

And the last one, which he made me repeat back to him in the hospital,
his hand around my wrist hard enough to bruise.

-   You must never look at their faces.

I’ve done four runs alone since my grandfather died. Went into the
hospital in March. Never came out. A series of strokes where he couldn’t
talk. We only communicated via a whiteboard. He made me promise to
cremate him. No body to move. I tell you this because it matters later.

The count last week was three. Out of eastern Oregon. We were heading
for a port my family’s used for decades. Three ticks in the ledger. Now
in my handwriting, not his.

I couldn’t see the man’s face when he let me into the grange hall.
Normal for these kinds of interactions. We counted the bodies in the dim
light of dusk. Behind the salted door with a name on it I didn’t
recognize. Three crates, three seals, three still shapes inside. After
dark, we loaded them in order. Oldest death at the front, newest at the
back. I hung the bell. Counted them again before I turned the engine
over.

Three.

I want to be clear that I counted three.

The first two hours were boring. That’s most of the work, honestly. A
man alone in a truck on a dark road. The bell rings, a distraction from
the bodies behind. I had cracked the window to get some cool air. Keep
me awake. The bell only rings when you’re moving. I was good at this.
Moving. Staying awake.

I pulled out the ledger at the first stop, despite knowing the count.
Rules. 

Three tick marks stared back at me in the dim light of gas station
overheads. 

I got out, bell in hand. Ringing as I pumped. To the back of the truck
for my count.

Four.

The lot was empty. I was tired. So I counted again. Slowly. Placing a
hand on each crate as I went. One. Two. Three. Four— a shape not in a
crate. 

Lying along the bed of the truck behind the others. On its back. Paper
seal on its face like the others. Resting in a position no one had put
it in.

Here’s what you need to understand about the rules. They are not advice.
They do not bend. 

The count was wrong. The echo of my grandfather whispered to me, “You’re
already in trouble.”

So I did the only thing the rules allow. I lowered the canvas. Got back
in the cab. Hung the bell. Drove. Because the one iron rule, under all
the others, was that you finish the run. No matter what. You never
abandon them on the road.

The tempo of the bell changed about forty minutes later.

It didn’t stop. I want to be precise. It slowed.

The same ebbs and flows of the truck that rang it cleanly for two hours
were now offbeat. The ring itself was a dull, half-swallowed sound.

I shook the mirror with my hand to force it. Rang clean for a second and
then went back to that wet, muffled non-ring.

This is when I understood that something behind me was deciding whether
I was in charge.

Then the rain started.

I understand we don’t get to choose the weather. A cruelty of the whole
trade. I had maybe ninety minutes of dark left. The port was still an
hour out, and the rain came down the way it does in that part of the
country in spring. Sudden and total.

Never let them get wet. 

The canvas wasn’t a sealed box. I could feel the water finding its way
in the way it always does. I had to stop. There was no version where I
didn’t stop. 

There’s a turnout I know, an old scale station from when this was a
logging road. Had a tin roof over the weigh pad. I got the truck under
it with the back end mostly sheltered. 

My hands shook on the wheel as I sat there. The bell stopped ringing.
The rain hammered the roof. 

I knew. Knew in the way your body does before your mind agrees. One of
the seals had come loose.

You fix it. Immediately.

I took the flashlight. Went around the back in the rain. Lifted the
canvas.

The smell smacked me in the face. Not rot. Old ground, cold mineral, the
smell of being buried for a hundred years.

Three crates. And the fourth shape on the floor behind them. On its
back, the paper peeling up at the corner. Lifting in the wind whipping
through the structure. About to leave entirely.

I keep the spare seals in a tin in my jacket. My grandfather taught me
to wet my thumb and press the new corner down. Never, not for a second,
look higher than the paper.

I knelt in the bed of the truck. Thumb on the corner of the seal.
Looking at the paper. Doing it right. 

The wind took the loose corner just as I reached it. Lifted the whole
seal off the face. Drifting away into the dark. For the length of a
breath, there was nothing between me and the face. I broke the only rule
that matters.

I looked.

My grandfather.

Not a man who resembled him. Not a trick of the light. My grandfather.
The small sharp face I had watched go slack in a hospital. The man we
cremated. The man with no body to move. Lying in the bed of my truck
with his eyes open. Looking back at me.

He smiled. He had not smiled at me in years. Even before the strokes.

And he said, in the voice I’d thought I’d never hear again, the thing he
used to say to me when I was small and afraid in the back of his truck.
A private thing. A thing that is mine and his and no one else’s. One
that I’ve never told anyone. That I will not tell you. 

He said it gently, the way he always did. And then he said one more
thing, which was: “you forgot to count, the first time. At the grange.
You took his word for it.”

I had not. I counted three. I’m sure I counted three.

I got a second seal out of the tin. Put it on his face. 

I want that on the record. It’s the only thing I did right that whole
night. I did not run. I did not leave him uncovered in the rain. I
sealed the face that was my grandfather’s face. Lowered the canvas. Got
back in the cab. Drove.

The last hour, the bell wouldn’t ring. Rain pelted the windshield. A
count of four that should’ve been three. I got the truck behind a
threshold, a real one. The port-house door with the salt and the old
name over it. Eleven minutes before the sun came up.

I counted them again after sunrise, which you’re allowed to do once
they’re behind the threshold.

Three.

Three crates. Three shapes. Three seals. The fourth gone.

No paper. No smell. Nothing. 

For a moment, I let myself believe I’d imagined the whole thing. The
rain. The smile. The private words.

Then I opened the crates to do the handover. The family was coming for
them. The third crate, the newest death, was empty.

Not disturbed by the journey. Not taken. Like it was never filled. The
cloth lining smoothed. Empty. 

The man I was hired to bring home. One I personally counted at the
grange, was gone.

Something rode home in his place that night. Wearing a face it knew
would stop me from doing my job. When I sealed that face anyway and got
it behind a threshold, it gave the box back empty. Took the count back
down to three and left.

The family is coming for bones that I do not have.

And I have another run after that. It’s appeared in the ledger. Two men
out of Nevada. A number already in the ledger: two.

I keep going back to look at it. I’ve looked at it nine or ten times
today.

It’s not my handwriting. It’s his.

The way he made his twos, with a long tail he always put on them, you
could almost read it as a three.
