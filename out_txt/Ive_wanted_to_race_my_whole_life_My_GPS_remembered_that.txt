I’m typing this from the driver’s seat of my Ford, parked at a motel
somewhere off the Ohio border. There’s a countdown on my screen. It says
nineteen minutes, and I need to get this out before it hits zero.

When I was a kid, my dad glued a cardboard steering wheel to a kitchen
stool. I drove it around the living room and won Le Mans every night. At
sixteen I saved up for a go-kart, but the money went to fixing our roof.
At twenty-five somebody told me I was past it. At thirty-eight I sell
industrial filters and live out of my Ford about half the month.

I’ve used the same navigation app for five years. Woman’s voice, bored,
flat. I don’t talk to it. Except once. Two years ago, in the rain, after
a terrible sales call, I said out loud to the windshield: “God, I’d love
to really race just once. Everything on the line.”

Nobody answered. I laughed and forgot about it.

Tonight I left a client in Ohio around quarter to five. Two hours to my
motel. It’s November, so the sun was already low and the sky had that
heavy gray-blue tint where headlights start to matter.

About forty minutes in, the voice said: “There’s a shortcut. It’ll save
you twenty minutes, Mike.”

I never typed my name into that app. But I was exhausted, so I turned.

The pavement ended. Gravel. The trees closed in until the road was just
a pale strip between two solid black walls. I decided to turn around,
and the voice cut in:

“Don’t turn around, Mike. There’s a car behind you. It’s been following
you since the last junction. Turn off your headlights. It tracks you by
your lights.”

I looked in the mirror. Just gray road, gray branches. “What car?” I
asked the phone.

“Gray sedan. It’s running dark. That’s how it hunts. Kill your lights
and keep moving. It shouldn’t know where you are.”

My hand just went to the switch. The headlights died. The road ahead
went murky, but there was still a little dusk left in the sky, enough to
tell the track from the trees. And in the mirror, fifty yards back, I
saw a shape. A sedan, no headlights. It wasn’t there a second ago. I
knew it wasn’t.

“Go,” the voice said. She wasn’t flat anymore. Her words came fast and
clipped, like someone on a military radio. “It’s coming for you.”

I floored it. Sixty, sixty-five on a gravel track barely wide enough for
one car, steering by her voice and the last gray light. The ditch
flashed past on my right, and it was littered with car parts. Bumpers, a
sheet of shattered glass, a single tire. I didn’t have time to look
closer.

“Left. Straight. Log ahead, swerve right. Don’t look back.”

The sedan stayed right on my bumper. How could he follow my corners
without headlights? A thought crossed my mind and I couldn’t shake it:
she was guiding him too, turn for turn. I screamed at the phone, asking
what it wanted. She just said, “Don’t stop. It won’t stop.”

Here’s the truth. At some point I started to feel good. I could hear my
own blood in my ears, everything was on the line, and I didn’t want it
to end.

By then it was nearly dark. I wasn’t seeing the road, I was just
obeying.

Then: “Brake. Now.”

I slammed the brakes and whipped the wheel, throwing my car sideways off
the track. The sedan behind me was too close to react. It blew past my
window, clipped a fallen log, and went straight into a massive oak. A
metal crunch, the sound of tearing plastic, and then the horn started
blaring, stuck down.

The voice went flat again, the way it talked an hour earlier: “Good. Now
get out and make sure he doesn’t get up.”

My hand was already on the door handle before I even processed the
words.

I grabbed the heavy flashlight from the glove box. The sedan was wrapped
around the tree. The driver was alive. In his fifties, face covered in
dark blood, chest pinned against the wheel.

He saw me and started coughing up a thick bubble of blood. His eyes
darted past me to my car, then back to my face. He didn’t need me to
ask. He already knew.

“She told me there was a man behind me,” he wheezed. “No lights. A
killer who hunts people on this road. She said to turn mine off so you
couldn’t see me, and to ram you when you slowed down.” He looked at me,
eyes huge. “I looked back and saw nothing. Just dark. I thought that was
you.”

I stared at him. “She told me the same thing. About you.”

He looked past my shoulder, at the blue glow of my phone through the
windshield. On his passenger seat lay a single leather driving glove,
worn down to the seams. A tiny checkered flag hung from his rearview
mirror.

“She remembered,” he whispered. His head fell forward.

I walked back to my car. My fingers had gone numb around the flashlight,
and I had to use both hands to get the door open. The map was still
glowing on the screen. The voice said: “Congratulations, Mike. Two miles
to the finish.”

I could have thrown the phone into the brush. I wanted to. But it was
dark, I was lost in the woods, and that voice was the only thing that
knew the way out. And I wanted to reach that finish. That’s what
terrifies me the most.

I drove the last two miles with the lights off, guided only by the
screen’s glare. The trees broke open all at once, revealing the motel
parking lot under a single buzzing streetlamp. The voice said: “Finish.”

I killed the engine. I won. It was over.

Then I looked through the windshield.

The lot wasn’t empty. There were eight or nine cars. A pickup, a
minivan, an old Buick. But they weren’t shiny. They were covered in mud,
with shattered headlights and dented fenders. Some had dark streaks
smeared across the glass. They had all just come out of their own
forests. They were all backed into the spaces, pointed straight at the
exit like a starting grid. No headlights. Every single one had someone
behind the wheel, staring directly at me. They had already done their
runs. They were waiting for me.

Nobody blinked.

In the pickup to my right sat a woman, easily sixty, both hands clamped
on the wheel at ten and two, waiting for the drop. And she was smiling.
That same wide, aching smile.

My phone screen flashed.

“Heat complete. Entrants for the next round: nine. Start in twenty
minutes. Turn on your headlights and start your engine to confirm
participation.”

I sat there with my hand over the dashboard. I could open the door and
run into the dark. But my eyes went to the woman in the pickup, then
back to the screen. The rush from the woods was still in me, hot and
addictive.

I reached out, clicked the headlight switch on, and turned the key. My
engine roared back to life, the dash lighting up bright. Around me,
eight other engines tore through the silence of the night, their
headlights flashing open all at once.

God help me. The race is starting.
