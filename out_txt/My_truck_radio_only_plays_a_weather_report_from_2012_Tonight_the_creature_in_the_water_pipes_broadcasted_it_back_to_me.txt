The radio in my truck only picks up two stations: static, and a weak AM
broadcast that plays the exact same local weather report from 2012 on a
twenty-minute loop.

I work the night shift at the county water treatment plant out on Route
9. It is a concrete box surrounded by chain-link fence, a dozen chemical
tanks, and three hundred acres of dense, black timber. Nobody comes out
here after dark. That’s why they pay me twenty-two bucks an hour to sit
in the control room and watch digital gauges blink green.

At 3:14 AM, the main pressure monitor for Sector 4 dropped to zero.

Sector 4 is the old underground main that feeds the abandoned state
sanitarium on the ridge. It hasn’t been active in twenty years; the
valve was locked and chained shut back in the late nineties.

I grabbed my flashlight and a heavy iron wrench, stepping out into the
damp, freezing October air. The mist off the reservoir was thick enough
to chew on. As I walked the gravel path toward the Sector 4 junction
box, my boots made a loud, crunching sound that felt way too exposed in
the dead quiet.

When I reached the concrete pit, the heavy iron hatch was pushed
slightly ajar.

I locked that on Friday, I thought.

I knelt down and shined my flashlight beam down the rusted ladder. The
beam cut through a plume of pale, heavy vapor that smelled faintly of
copper and old wet soil—not chlorine, not rust. Just something metallic
and sweet.

“Hello?” I called out. My voice sounded thin, swallowed instantly by the
dark.

No answer. Just the steady, rhythmic drip… drip… drip of water echoing
from deep inside the pipe.

I told myself a local homeless person had probably tried to break in out
of the cold. I gripped the flashlight in my left hand, rested my right
hand on the iron wrench, and took the first step down the ladder.

The rungs were slick. By the time I hit the concrete floor ten feet
down, my breath was pluming in the damp dark. I clicked the beam along
the vaulted brick ceiling of the drainage tunnel. The walls were crusted
with white mineral deposits that looked, in the pale LED light, like
clustered rows of tiny, half-formed teeth.

Then I saw the wrench I keep in the junction box. It was lying on the
wet floor a few yards ahead of me, covered in dark, sticky mud.

“Hey! Get out of here,” I yelled, trying to sound braver than my
skeleton felt. “This is private property. The police are on the—`

The water in the shallow trench at my feet stopped flowing.

Not slowed down. Stopped.

In the absolute silence that followed, I heard a sound coming from
further up the dark pipe toward the sanitarium. It wasn’t an animal. It
was a wet, rhythmic sliding sound—like a heavy canvas sack being dragged
very slowly across rough concrete.

And then came a voice, muffled and reverberating through the damp brick,
speaking in the exact flat, monotone cadence of the weather broadcast
from my truck:

“Current temperature… thirty-eight degrees… pressure dropping… sector
four… has… been… opened.”

I didn’t turn around. I dropped the wrench, scrambled up the slick iron
rungs of the ladder like a panicked insect, and threw my body through
the hatch, slamming the heavy iron lid down. I didn’t bother locking it.
I sprinted back to the control room, locked the deadbolt, barred the
door with a steel folding chair, and stared at the monitors.

Sector 4 is reading normal again.

The pressure is fine. The flow is fine.

But every three minutes, the digital log prints out a new entry stamped
with the current time, listing a water usage volume that matches the
exact weight of an adult human body.
