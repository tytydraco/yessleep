​

I’m writing this because I don’t think I’ll get another chance to say it
somewhere people can read it. I’m using a fake name. If you know me,
please don’t tell anyone yet.

I grew up in a house where love had rules. My parents weren’t bad in a
way you could easily point out no police were ever called. It was
quieter than that. Long cold silences. Being told I was a disappointment
for crying. Being told I was “too much” for needing anything at all. By
the time I was fifteen, I knew how to hide inside my own house just to
feel safe.

Then there was Eli.

Eli sat two seats behind me in tenth grade. He was quiet and polite. He
always seemed to notice things other people missed the days my eyes were
red, the days I flinched when a teacher raised their voice. He never
asked questions. He just started leaving small things on my desk. A
pencil when mine broke. A note that said, “You looked tired today. I
hope it gets better.” Nothing big. Just someone paying attention, which
was more than I’d had in years.

I fell for him before I even understood what falling meant. By December,
I liked him more than I had ever liked anything even more than my own
family. In February, I told him. My hands were shaking so hard I almost
couldn’t get the words out.

He said yes before I even finished talking.

The first few months were the best of my life. I know how that sounds
now, but I need you to understand it so the rest of this makes sense. He
remembered everything I told him. He noticed when I was sad before I
even said a word. He’d wait outside my classes just to walk with me for
two minutes between periods. No one had ever paid that much attention to
me before. I mistook it for the safest thing I’d ever felt.

The changes were so small at first that I didn’t even notice them.

He started asking who I had talked to that day. Not in an angry way —
just “curious,” or so I told myself. Then he started showing up in
places I hadn’t told him I’d be. He said he “just had a feeling.”
Slowly, my other friendships faded away — not because he told me to end
them, but because he’d get so quiet and hurt whenever I mentioned
spending time with someone else. It became easier to just stop seeing
them.

By junior year, he was checking my phone “to keep me safe.” He said the
world was full of people who wanted to hurt me, and he just needed to
make sure I was okay. When I pushed back, even a little, his face would
go still — like something behind his eyes had turned calm and far away.
I didn’t have a word for it yet.

I told myself this was what love looked like. It was the only kind of
strong attention I had ever gotten, and my whole life had taught me to
mistake intensity for care.

Here is the part that’s hard to explain, because even now I don’t fully
understand what I found.

In our last year of school, a boy named Marcus asked me to help him with
homework after class. That’s it. That’s the whole thing. I said yes
because he sat near me and seemed nice, and it only took fifteen
minutes.

Marcus changed schools two weeks later. His family said it was because
of his father’s job. I believed that, for a while.

Then I found Eli’s journal.

I wasn’t looking for it. It fell out of his bag in my room. I only meant
to put it back. But it had fallen open to a page with Marcus’s full name
on it, along with his class schedule and his usual walk home. One line
was underlined twice: He won’t be a problem anymore.

I confronted him. I expected him to say I had it wrong. Instead, he just
looked at me with that same still, calm face and said, “I didn’t hurt
him. I just made sure he understood some things weren’t his to touch.”

I should have run that night. I know that now. But I had nowhere to run
to. My house wasn’t safe either. As far as I could tell, he was the only
person on earth who had ever chosen me on purpose. So I stayed. I told
myself I could handle it, the way I had spent my whole life handling
things that should never have needed handling.

In the last few months, I stopped being able to tell what was real. He
started saying things like, “No one else will ever look after you the
way I do,” and, “Your parents don’t want you. I’m the only one who
does.” The scary part wasn’t that he was lying. The scary part was that
he wasn’t, not really. No one else had ever looked after me. He knew
exactly where that wound was, because I had shown it to him myself, back
when I thought it was safe to.

Three weeks ago, I told him I wanted space. Just space, not even a real
goodbye. I watched his face fill with grief and anger at the exact same
time, like two waves hitting the same rock.

He didn’t yell. He never yelled. He just said, very softly, “You don’t
actually want that. You just don’t know what’s out there. I do. I’ve
always known what’s out there, and I’ve kept it away from you. If you
leave, I can’t promise I’ll be able to keep doing that.”

I asked him what he meant.

He said, “It means I would rather you be safe and gone than out there
and hurt by someone who isn’t me.”

I didn’t understand what he meant until four nights ago.

I’m not going to describe what happened four nights ago. I don’t think I
could even if I tried, and I don’t think you’d want me to. I’ll just say
I’m alive, and I don’t fully understand why. The last thing I remember
him saying, very gently, almost tenderly, was, “I never wanted it to end
like this. I just needed you to be somewhere I knew you’d always be
safe. I’m sorry it has to be this kind of safe instead.”

I woke up in a hospital two days later. My parents were in the waiting
room. They didn’t come see me until the second day. When they did, my
mother’s first words were, “Do you know how embarrassing this is for
us?”

Eli is missing. No one has found him. Not the police, not his family, no
one.

But three nights ago, I woke up and my bedroom window which I know I had
locked — was open. There was a folded note on the sill. It said: I’m
still watching. I still love you more than anyone ever will. That part
was never a lie.

I don’t feel safe telling my parents. I don’t feel safe telling the
police, not really, not after everything. I don’t know who’s actually
looking for him, or if anyone believes he’s still out there, or if I’m
the only one who knows he never really left.

I used to think the scariest part of my life was going home after
school.

I was wrong. The scariest thing is knowing that somewhere out there is a
boy who loved me so much he would rather I be gone than out of his reach
and as far as I can tell, he still isn’t finished loving me yet.
