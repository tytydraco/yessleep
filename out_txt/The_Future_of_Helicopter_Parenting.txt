“We made this decision as a family. And, frankly, it was an easy one.”

Could this be the defining story of my career? I’d been a journalist for
thirty years, and an aspiring one long before that.

But this scoop was truly jaw-dropping. Beyond anything I’d worked on
before.

The couple before me looked unremarkable. A short, bald husband who
worked as a social media manager for a suite of healthcare brands. An
even shorter, not particularly striking woman who’d given up her career
in retail to raise their son.

“It honestly came down to what’s best for Billy.” The male subject
rubbed his wife’s shoulder in what seemed like an attempt to remind her
of the conviction that had led them here. “Social media’s just a toxic
environment for a youngster and we’re confident we won’t be the last
parents to do this. I recognize the irony in what I’m saying,
considering my job.”

He wasn’t a natural speaker but had started to find his voice over the
past twenty minutes of Q&A. His wife was clearly the more eloquent one,
but talking about her son was causing her a degree of emotional
vulnerability that made for a great interview but poor transcript.

“You’re right. Reports suggest you aren’t even the first parents to make
this choice. But I suspect after this interview airs you’ll give this
parenting approach a degree of exposure that will invite more families
to consider it.”

“And that’s the main reason we’ve agreed to this interview.” The husband
was doing most of the talking now. These two didn’t give the impression
that he made all household decisions. His worried expression and
deferential nature suggested his wife was the true leader, especially
around matters regarding their son. “Enough is enough. Cyberbullying is
the greatest threat to children and it’s obvious those in power have no
interest in putting an end to it. So we’ve had to step in.”

I was hoping one of the parents would say something like this. Because
this story was about far more than powerful tech companies and the
governments that are lobbied to protect them.

“Do you feel that a child who interacts more with AI than other humans
has a greater chance of living a happy life?”

The couple looked at each other. He encouraged his wife with a
reassuring nod. You could see him mouth ‘go on.’

The mother looked at me and exhaled deeply before answering.

“Look, I never really thought I’d be a mother. Not because I didn’t want
kids. It just seemed unlikely. Life’s more expensive than ever. Finding
someone who’s willing to sacrifice everything for their children is
really, really hard. You talk to people who have kids… and they’re not
happy. They love their children, but the kids clearly aren’t happy.”

This was going very well. I could see a Pulitzer in my future should the
next portion of this interview go as planned.

I remained silent. Knowing when to speak was a skill I’d only picked up
in the past ten years of my career. It’s what separates good journalists
from great ones.

“Billy isn’t a happy child. He’s extremely sensitive. Middle school’s
tough for most kids, but it’s worse for late developers like him.” She
looked at her husband again and was met with another nod of reassurance.
“It’s our responsibility to ensure he makes it through.”

“Do you mind elaborating on that?” I sensed a tad of encouragement was
needed.

“Billy isn’t the only kid that isn’t happy. He can’t escape the bullies
when he’s home. It almost feels like it’s worse online than in the
classroom. Maybe that’s just because it’s only visible to us at home…
but his mental health is terrible. And it started with chatrooms and
video games. It’s clear that any interaction with other children is
negative. So our decision protects him.”

This was the moment. I needed a soundbite here. One that required no
context.

“And what is your decision exactly?” I was met with a sigh. “If you
could spell it out for someone in the plainest English possible.”

“We’re not allowing our son to use social media or talk to any other
humans online. When using his phone or computer, he’ll only be allowed
to talk with his assistant.”

“You mentioned the assistant’s name earlier?”

The female subject looked at her husband with a confused expression. He
whispered into her ear and she mouthed, ‘oh, right.’

“Jarvis. Billy likes Iron Man and he’s been using the assistant
regularly since he was five.”

“So your decision is to control your son’s online experience?”

A nod from both parents was followed by what looked like relief on the
mother’s face. As though she’d been liberated from the vast majority of
worries for her son.

“Yes.” She wiped a tear from her eye. “We’ve been given an amazing gift.

“We can customize everything that happens to him. Jarvis will talk to
him how we want and show only the parts of life we want him to see.”

‘Ok, go in for the kill.’

It was the first time my segment producer, Greg, had said anything for a
while into my earpiece.

“What would you say to those who are confused or shocked by your
decision?”

Another look between the parents. Nothing was said, only raised
eyebrows. To a seasoned reporter, this was evidence that they’d
considered this question shortly after agreeing to the interview. The
mother turned back to me.

“Humans are far more dangerous than AI. Billy’s assistant will never try
to harm him by attacking his character, making him feel small or going
so far as to encourage him to take his own life. It’s people that are
the problem.

“And,” she was starting to get emotional, “if people are the problem,
then we have to remove people from Billy’s life.”
