Izzy, I thought.

The writing moved.

It reached my eyes last.

I saw it come. It gathered at the inner corners, a small bright dark,

They were completely and utterly black.

The reflection went on smiling. I stood in front of it and, very slowly,

The whisper had been a breath at the edge of hearing. Now it had a body.

It came up out of the floor first. I felt it in the soles of my feet,
through the cold tiles, a slow dull pulse that I took for my own heart.

gathering weight as it climbed, until it filled the little room from
corner to corner. It was a voice. It was deep, deeper than any voice I
had ever heard, and it was old, with the grain of stone in it and the
long drag of rivers over gravel. It was almost a growl. It sounded as
though the mountain had decided to speak, and had not yet decided to be
kind.

It was singing.

I want to be exact about this, because it matters. It was not a
language. I have been all my life among people who speak six or seven of
them, and I have the ear of a man who sits in meetings where they are
interchanged, and I knew at once that this was none of them. It had no
roots I could trace. It had sounds I wasn’t sure a human mouth could
make, a hard click at the back of the throat, a long hum like a struck
bell, a rasp like a blade drawn over bone. Nobody had ever spoken it.
Nobody, I understood, had ever learned it. It had simply been there,
before the first mouths, and it was only now being let out.

And I understood every word.

That was the dreadful thing. It went into me as water goes into sand.
There was no translation, no grasping, no lag between sound and sense.
The song poured into my head and was already meaning, as plain and
certain as the knowledge of my own name.

It sang of blood. It sang of the first red tide, the dark pulse in the
dark before light, the slow hammer of a heart in the earth. It sang of
fire, and that was the heat in the stove and the heat in my skin and the
white roar at the root of the world where the stone was liquid. It sang
of ice, the long patient grinding of it, white upon white, ten thousand
years of weight carrying down the valley and leaving the valley changed.
And it sang of lust, though that is a poor word for it. It was hunger,
plain and enormous, the wanting of a thing for what it is not yet. It
sang of bodies and heat and bringing together, of the great slow clasp
of the cold and the warm. It beat the rhythm out on the walls of the
cabin like a fist on a drum, blood and fire and ice and want, blood and
fire and ice and want, and under my ribs the script beat with it, and my
heart fell into step, and I could no more have stopped it than I could
have stopped the tide.

I stood with my hands on the sink and my black eyes in the mirror and

The reflection was still smiling. It was swaying slightly, to the beat,
the way a man will in a crowd that has begun to sing.

The song came to a place where it turned, and I felt the turn like a

Come.

It did not say it in words. It said it in the thing that lay behind the
words, a pull, a long steady drawing, like the tide on the shingle. It
wanted me to go up. It wanted me to go to the top of the ice, to the
head of the glacier, where the white river began, where the snow fell in
the first dark and lay down and did not melt. It wanted me to go to the
place where the water ran out from under the world, clear and black and
older than anything that had ever drunk it.

Drink.

It showed me the water. It showed me it as a taste in my mouth, cold
beyond cold, clean as the first morning, and the thirst that rose in me
then was so sharp and so total that I swayed and had to grip the
porcelain. I wanted that water more than I had ever wanted anything. I
wanted it more than breath, more than sleep, more than the sound of her
voice.

The birth of the world, said the song, and it was not a figure of
speech. Drink where it was born.

I understood that it was an invitation. I understood too, quite calmly,
that it was not a request.

The steam had thinned. Through the open door of the bathroom I could see

I took my hands from the sink.

I did not go in the night. I can’t say whether it forbade me, or whether

The cold came in at the glass. It did not reach me.

At some point I got up and went to the bathroom, and stood in the

The writing was red.

It had been black when I last looked, a fine dark lace. Now it was lit

The song had not come back.

great face bent very close.

It was waiting for me.

I understood this slowly, the way you understand that someone has asked

I opened my mouth.

“I am the hollow laughter from the empty hall.”

I took off the sunglasses and put them in my breast pocket. The light
was thin and white. It did not hurt.

The first cab in the line was a grey saloon with a licence clipped to
the sun visor. I knew it before I saw the name. The driver was looking
at the windscreen, and he did not get out.

I walked up to the passenger window. It came down a few inches.

“Jorgé,” I said.

He turned his head. He looked at me for a long moment, at the scarf and
the cap and the careful, ordinary face, and I watched something go
across his own, a small frown, as of a man trying to place a tune.

“You,” he said. “I know you.”

“Briefly.”

“The money.” He almost smiled. “Fifty. You gave me a fifty.”

“I did,” I said. “And I should have waited for the change.”

He shrugged, with the whole of his upper body, a gesture I could not
have made a fortnight before. The boot clunked as he pulled the lever,
and rose four inches, and stuck.

“We arrive, you go,” he said, but the words were gentle now, and he was
looking at the holdall.

I lifted the boot lid with one hand and set the suitcase in with the
other, without effort. It went in like a thing that wanted to. I shut
the lid, and it closed under my palm with a soft, neat click. I kept the
holdall. I opened the back door and slid in with it across my knees, and
the seat was warm.

Jorgé did not start the car. He sat with both hands on the wheel, and
his nostrils flared a little, and he turned his head very slightly
towards the back.

“Is nice,” he said. “What is that? Like flowers.”

“A gift,” I said.

“For your wife?”

I looked out of the window at the rain. A young man in a hi-vis jacket
went past with a kitbag on one shoulder, and a woman beside him laughed
at something he said, and turned her face up to the drizzle.

“Yes,” I said.

He nodded slowly. He reached over without being asked and turned the
radio down, and then off. The cab filled up with a quiet that I knew. I
could feel the grey mosaic lying cool and exact along my ribs under the
shirt. I could feel the stillness in the place where the voice had been,
wide and level, and, a long way down in it, a small slow shifting, like
something settling in its sleep.

“Where to?” said Jorgé.

I gave him the address. I said it clearly, in the careful voice I had
worn for twenty years to meetings and board rooms and the occasional
funeral. It had never sounded so well.

He pulled out. The rank slid away behind us, and the terminal, and the
long wet apron with its small grey aeroplane. I put my head back on the
seat. Through the glass the road unrolled in front of us, shining and
empty, running to the city, and the flat, and the hallway, and the coat
hooks beside the door.

In the mirror, Jorgé’s eyes found mine and moved away. He wound his
window down an inch, and then, after a moment, wound it up again.

“Is good smell,” he said, to himself. “Is very good.”

I closed my eyes. The wheels sang on the wet road, and I thought, quite
clearly, in a voice that was not mine, and was not hers, and was somehow
both:

So many still left to find.
