​

I’m a traveling researcher in the sweltering savanna of East Africa. I
work with an organization called FCRM. Feline conservation, research and
medicine. I’ve been at this company for about a decade, and it’s the
perfect career for me. I majored in biology in college, and my parents
sent me to America to get a proper education. After earning my masters
in wildlife biology and zoology, I returned to my home in Kampala,
Uganda. I used the money I received from signing on with FRCM to support
my parents, buying them a seaside condo on the Swahili coast and
ensuring them a comfortable retirement. I will always be grateful for
their constant support and faith in me, even after recent events. My
most recent expedition has shaken me to my core, and I’ve begun to lose
hope in my own abilities.

Six months ago, I received a grant from the foundation to fund my
project on developing vaccines against Babesiosis, a tick inflicted
bloodborne disease that attacked the wild lion populations. Unlike Lyme
disease, we still didn’t have much knowledge on Babesiosis. All we knew
was that it was rapidly spreading as more immigration brought more dogs,
and with them, fleas and ticks. It was becoming increasingly difficult
to predict, nearly impossible to treat. The lions affected would show
signs of lethargy, pacing around and panting with dehydration. Then,
because of the spreading CDV from local stray dog populations, the
perfect storm would cause the lions to succumb to infection. While I
could not simply stop people from losing dogs on the savanna, I could
try and lighten the fatality rates in the large cats. With a population
of approximately twenty-thousand, they could not afford to be wiped to
extinction by an unlucky tick bite.

It was easy to recruit team members, many of my coworkers and veterinary
partners with FCRM were eager to develop a vaccine. It took only a few
weeks to sign on a dozen researchers- four scientists, five
veterinarians, and three feline researchers, including myself. Only a
month after launching the project, we were already set out to collect
samples of local prides.

We took our small train of game viewers-three of them- out into the
heart of the Chalbi desert, North of Kenya. We had rifles, narcotic
ammunition, and standard bullets in case of trouble, but none of us were
hoping for that outcome. I sat in the passenger seat of the lead truck,
leaning out of the side, hollering in excitement along with the others.
Hot, dry wind warmed my face, the tires kicking up clouds of sand that
powdered our skin. I shielded my eyes from the unforgiving sun and its
brilliant rays, looking out onto the cracked dunes of the savanna.
Baobab trees jutted from the earth, resilient despite the hellish
climate. A few of the scientists complained about the heat, pulling
their shirt collars from their glistening skin and waving paper against
their faces: a fruitless attempt to generate some sort of breeze.

The vehicles swallowed up the rocky distance, occasionally jumping and
taking to the air briefly.

“Jackline!” A woman’s voice, strong and deep, called me from my
thoughts. I snapped my head back into the buggy, looking at her with a
grin still pressed on my face. Sofia, one of the researchers I worked
most closely with. Her hair was cut short in locs. “Coming up on lion
territory.” She said, waving me to the bed of the truck where she
crouched. I climbed over to join her, earning an annoyed glare from the
driver as I obstructed his view momentarily. I followed her gaze to a
pile of scat. The vehicle slowed over particularly rocky terrain.

“Good, good! Looks to have fur as well.” I nodded, giving her an
appreciative pat on the shoulder. Perhaps we may find evidence of lice.
She leaned forward, telling the driver to stop while we collected a
sample. The two trucks behind us slowed to a halt, a few of them
stepping out to stretch their legs or find a flask of water in their
packs. I retrieved a specimen container from the cooler on the truck
bed, slipping on two pairs of gloves. Sofia helped to collect the
sample, and I observed it for a moment, holding it up to the sun’s
light. Since the disease was not parasitic, it would be harder to
detect. Intestinal worms could typically be seen in feces, removed and
examined or exterminated. Tick carried diseases were blood born, not
gastrointestinal. With this virus, we would have to hope for trace
evidence of blood. It would be more accurate to collect blood from an
affected individual, but a good researcher never passed up on an
opportunity to find something new. It went into the cooler, to be later
dissected and put under a microscope.

We got back into the safari trucks, hydrating and talking amongst
ourselves. The sun was rising at its peak, and wandering far into the
territory was unwise when we lacked energy. Lions sleep nearly twenty
hours a day, and we were familiar with this pride. We knew they were
driven by curiosity, and the lead male would eventually approach to
investigate us.

It was around an hour later when the soft rumble was heard. A scientist,
previously napping with his cap over his eyes, jolted awake, all ears.
Sometimes I forget this would be frightening to an average individual,
being approached by something weighing 180kg on average. We cautiously
sat up, watching as a large male with a thick, matted mane emerged from
a dry bush. His mouth was parted open, panting. Large teeth poked from
his gums like overturned icebergs.

He was not frightened as he approached, mane swayed as he walked,
looking over us with mild interest. Sofia grabbed my forearm tightly,
looking like a kid at the top of the steps who had just seen Santa
leaving presents under the tree. I whispered an excited “I know”,
searching through a duffelbag for a rifle. We would sedate the beast,
prioritizing its safety before collecting a blood sample, and examining
him for latched on ticks. It was difficult to tell from a distance if he
had any bites. An average male lion, old enough to lead a pride, had a
pelt marred by a map of scars. He was no exception- old cuts scattered
his pelt like constellations. He grunted, laying down in a patch of
nearby brush, watching us cautiously.

I picked up the duffelbag containing our darts and carried it to the
rear truck bed, setting it down with a soft thud. I gestured for our
marksman, an Egyptian man named Khufu, to take aim.

He loaded the rifle with the red bristled darts, muttering something in
the language of Egyptian I hardly recognized- perhaps a quick prayer or
good wish towards the lion. He fired a silent shot towards the male’s
shoulder, earning a distressed growl. He rose to his paws, rumbling as
he quickly cantered off. I whistled for the other men to get back into
their vehicles and follow us, Sofia hopping back into the truck bed
alongside me as we tailed the lion. He was beginning to slow, but we
followed him through the stretch of clearing for nearly twenty minutes,
before he staggered to a halt and toppled to his side.

Luckily, he had led us far enough away from the territory that his
lionesses would not be immediately alert. At the peak temperature of the
day, they were most likely lounging with cubs under a shady tree
somewhere. We carefully extracted our equipment from its storage in the
trucks, all hands on deck as we set up the portable station we would
need to assess his condition. Sofia knelt down by the male, her hand
carding through its mane as she whispered soothing words. His eyes were
half lidded, glassy as he rumbled incoherently. I smiled at the scene.
She was a good woman, someone who put the animal before the research. A
gangly veterinarian in a white coat with medical grade gloves opened a
sample kit, preparing a needle and several vials. A shorter, stocky
veterinarian I assumed was European patted the lion’s flank, scanning
for suspicious bites or mounds among the battle scars.

While I enjoyed field work, I typically did not handle the animal
outright. I would instruct and lead the expeditions, but the others were
more specialized for certain tasks. A good leader knows when to delegate
tasks, and I stood aside to let them perform the skills they trained in
for years.

We collected several vials of blood, snapping them into an airtight
container, then into the cooler for good measure. I joined the European
woman at the lions side, following her efficient hands as they examined
the unconscious beast.

“I know this male. We call him Jelani. He’s been at the head of this
pride for three years.” I said, my voice low, careful not to rouse the
slumbering lion.

She glanced at me with radiant green eyes, clucking her tongue in
acknowledgment. “He’s.. hmm.. Seven or eight years old. A bit on the
elderly side, impressive considering his size.” She said, her voice
carrying a hint of wonder. All of my team were enchanted by the felines,
and their passion was the most valuable trait I admired.

“Many scars, too. He’s fought to be the top dog.” I remarked with a nod,
eyeing the gash across his flank: three deep strikes, healed but still a
dark pink. Most likely earned when he took over this pride, three years
ago.

“The top dog.” She repeated with a small smile, her hand pausing at a
particular patch of raised skin on Jelani’s hind leg. It was red, with
concentric circles of purple and black in its center. I sucked in my
teeth with a grimace.

“Looks like an old bite. Is it infected?” I asked. She did not react for
a moment, her finger prodding at the bump. It flexed and oozed something
viscous and black. A stagnant odor like sweet rot emanated from the
wound.

She frowned, withdrawing her hand with a small gasp. “I haven’t seen
this before. It looks like the fluids of a mambas venom.” I shuddered at
the thought, feeling a pang of sympathy for the unlucky position the
lion had ended up in. A tick bite, stacked with a mamba’s unforgiving
venom? Unlikely, but out in the desert, everything was out to kill.

“Should we have him lifted out of here? I don’t want to disrupt the
pride order..” She muttered to herself, perhaps a rhetorical question.

“There is a secondary male. It’s a coalition pride. Led by two brothers.
The pride will be okay temporarily.” I answered regardless, unable to
look away from the reeking bile with a wrinkled nose.

We called for the local animal hospital and quarantine facility, and
dosed Jelani once more to keep him sedated while we waited. Within an
hour, he was loaded onto the floating platform of a helicopter, and
carried the distance to the facility. We packed up our things and set
out on the journey back, thanking the pilot and crew for their timely
arrival. The sky was darkening to dusk, and despite my experience,
untamed Africa at night was a trap of scorpions and vicious creatures.
The mission was successful, I would have both blood and feces specimens
to test, and we had rescued Jelani from a possibly fatal injury. I
suspected it was some evolving strain of Babesiosis, but I did not
mention it yet. There was no point to frighten the others by projecting
my nerves and predictions on a hunch.

The transportation went successfully, and Jelani was given the night to
rest and recover from the brief trauma of sedation. I went to my
temporary housing, located near the facility. It was a slightly more
luxurious barracks, without being quite as private as an apartment. Each
researcher had a small room to sleep in, located in one larger sort of
warehouse with a large kitchen and even larger laboratory. I couldn’t
sleep that night. I sat at a desk with a dimly lit lamp, browsing my
laptop. I had compiled years of notes, but I had still hardly made a
dent in the research of this particular virus strain. All I knew were
possible symptoms, and how it was constantly evolving. It went from a
simple bloodborne disease, to a respiratory infection, and in the most
recently observed case of a lioness last month, eventual organ failure.
I couldn’t track its progression fast enough- couldn’t predict which
system it would attack. In articles published about other animals; like
elephants or gazelle, it was clear cut. A bite that became a rash, then
an infection it either recovered from, or succumbed to after several
years of slow spreading within the body. In the cat’s, it seemed to
mutate far more.

I stared at the screen until the blue light burned my eyes and painted
pictures in my retina. Jelani’s blood script results had come in, and I
couldn’t wait to overview it with the team in the morning. The
anticipation was making me itch.

I entered the various login information prompts- passing by a dozen fail
safes and security walls before I was able to access the report. Once I
finally came to the particular file, my heart fell to the soles of my
feet. Nothing about this report was normal.

Unless you work in zoology or are familiar with feline blood typing, the
data is very hard to translate. I will simplify it by saying the typical
blood levels. An average healthy male, well hydrated and fed, should
have a healthy range of red blood cells of 8.9g/dl and 14.5g/dl, similar
to that of a young child. Much like humans, anything drastically lower
than this may indicate anemia or other underlying health issues. Jelani
had a hemoglobin level of 2.0g/dl. Anything under 6.0 is almost always
fatal, and implies severe blood loss or internal drainage.

He should not be alive. While a Black Mambas venom can be deadly, no
natural venom should cause his blood levels to decrease so drastically.
This threw our hypothesis of a coincidental mamba+tick bite out of the
window. He would’ve had to be bitten by a damn vampire.

My research carried me to the early hours of the morning, spiraling from
panic to outright disbelief. I hardly greeted Sofia when she arrived in
the kitchen, offering me coffee. I had to get to the facility, to check
on his condition, granted he was stable. I called in before, just to
make sure, and was relieved to find out he had received an IV and was
under monitoring, behaving normally despite the anomaly in his blood.

I took one of our research trucks down to the facility, my fingers
frantic on the dashboard. I couldn’t keep still, not when the most
exciting thing in my career was happening at this very moment.

I parked by the side entrance, scanning my badge for clearance and
marching into the lab, dodging scientists holding beakers and specimen
jars. Luckily, I had become familiar enough with the laboratory within
the past six months to navigate toward the quarantine unit. The process
was much more rigorous and tedious than I would have ever expected. They
treated me like a terrorist, smuggling something illegal. I had to strip
my clothes and wash off in a decontamination shower before being allowed
entry into the room he was being kept in. The veterinarian on
observation explained to me this lion was highly immunocompromised, and
they could not risk the valuable research with a seemingly harmless
speck of bacteria entering his system. I leaned forward, squinting
against the glass enclosure to make out the jagged shape of Jelani, a
black silhouette in the dark enclosure.

“Why is it so damn dark?” I asked, my eyes still not adjusting to make
out significant details. The vet, whose name badge read Ashka, sighed
deeply, looking timid and uncertain.

“He does not like the light.”

I furrowed my brows at this answer. It wasn’t satisfying, it raises more
questions than it answers. She sensed the puzzled look on my face, and
gestured for me to follow her toward her desk, where several monitors
displayed various vitals.

“Look. Watch his EKG.” Ashka said, before flipping a switch on a board,
illuminating the enclosure with a sterile, white glow. I kept the view
of him in my peripheral while I watched the numbers. They shot up
skyward, 50bpm to 300bpm. I tore my eyes away from the startling data,
certain it must have malfunctioned. That was well past heart attack
levels for an apex predator as large as a lion. It is hard to describe
what I saw next.

Jelani was now visible, but he was not how he should be. His mane was
matted further, sticky with a thick black sludge oozing from his
wrenched open jaw. His tongue lolled out in a crooked roar, although it
sounded more like the croak of a death rattle. His hips jutted sickly
from his spine, bones shifting beneath skin thinner than tissue paper.
The bite on his foreleg was swollen, beating like a heart. I gasped in
horror at the sight, and Ashka did, too.

She shot up from her seat at the desk, knocking over a cup of writing
utensils in her attempt to flick the light back off. My feet stayed
firmly planted on the ground. I felt like I was a matador, staring down
a raging bull. The room was swallowed by darkness once more, and his
rattle of agony faded into a quiet murmur, lower than the beeping of the
monitors. The heart rate dropped back to a steady resting pulse of
50bpm.

I looked at Ashka, her face a pale sheet.

“What’s wrong with him?”

She was about to answer, when a crashing sound stole her breath from her
lips. The glass splintered into fractured spiderwebs.

Jelani had charged directly into the glass, over and over again.
