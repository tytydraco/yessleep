Part 2

The following morning, my parents decided I needed to go back to school.
They couldn’t afford to take another day off work to stay home with me.
I understood that, at least as much as a child my age could, but that
didn’t make the prospect of returning any less terrifying.

I hated the idea. HATED it. I nearly cried when they told me, but I knew
there wasn’t much I could do. Both Mom and Dad promised that they would
keep their phones nearby in case anything happened. Their assurance did
little to help how I felt.

Reluctantly, I got dressed and climbed into Mom’s car. I spent the
entire drive to school worrying about my spine. I waited for it to start
hurting. Was it going to burst from my skin? Was I going to transform
into something? What was going to happen?

At every stop along the way, I would straighten my back and subtly feel
along my sides, checking to make sure everything was where it was
supposed to be. But my back remained perfectly normal.

Once the car came to a stop in the school parking lot, I hesitantly
stepped out and waved goodbye to Mom. Her car soon vanished into the
chaotic stream of morning traffic, leaving me standing alone outside the
school building. Taking a deep breath, I turned and headed inside.

As I walked to class, I hoped and prayed that nobody remembered. The
last thing I wanted to deal with was being made fun of by a bunch of
kids who didn’t understand what I was dealing with. Unfortunately for
me, gossip travels fast among children. 

Some kids started following me on my way to Mrs. Garland’s class, making
a point of staying just close enough for me to hear their comments.

“Careful,” one boy said. “Get too close and you might turn to ice.”

One girl began shivering dramatically, rubbing her arms as she pretended
to be freezing. “Yeah, gotta keep your distance from the freak.”

I put my head down to hide the tears beginning to form.

“Aww,” another girl said, pretending to sniffle. “Is the freak gonna
cry?”

I did my best to ignore them, but they persisted with their
name-calling. Some were creative. Others weren’t. Most just called me
weird or made snide comments. Children have a way of knowing which
buttons to push, and I wasn’t exactly the best at hiding my feelings.

Rather than go to class, I took a detour and found refuge in the girls’
bathroom. Thankfully, nobody followed me inside. I locked myself in one
of the stalls and sat on the toilet, not even bothering to stop the
tears from falling.

I tried to hold myself together, but the more I thought about their
comments, the more I couldn’t bear the thought of walking into class.
Not with everyone staring at me at least. I waited for the morning bell
to ring before leaving the girls’ bathroom. Once the hallways cleared, I
headed toward Mrs. Garland’s class.

Disparaging looks aside, nobody uttered a single word about me when I
walked in, much to my relief. The rest of the school day passed
relatively uneventfully, at least until Mrs. Garland called me up to
solve a math problem. Math was far from my best subject, but this was
one I could handle. I pushed my chair back and stood.

POP. POP. POP.

My back. Was it my back?

It sounded like someone cracking their knuckles at the exact moment I
stood up. Had I been sitting awkwardly at my desk for too long? That had
to be it. Whatever the case, my concern lasted no more than mere
seconds. 

I headed toward the board and plucked a colored marker from the small
container beside it. As I started writing out the answer, the noises
returned.

POP…POP.

I paused, staring at the numbers I had written. Was the marker making
that noise? I examined it and gave it a little shake. Nothing out of the
ordinary.

CRAAAAAACK.

I concentrated on the problem in front of me, but the increasingly
chaotic racket made it impossible to focus. Behind me, the noise
swelled, seemingly coming from every direction at once. Students
exchanged confused looks with one another. 

“Kayla?” Mrs. Garland called, but her voice was quickly drowned out by
another chorus of cracks.

CRACK. CRACK. CRACK.

The marker slipped from my fingers and clattered against the floor. I
reached behind me, clutching my lower back as an unfamiliar soreness
spread through my spine, like someone had pressed a soldering iron
against it.

Mrs. Garland rose from her desk and approached me at the board, asking
what was wrong. But I couldn’t make out her words over the laughter of
my classmates. Apparently, they found my bizarre behavior hysterical.

“Kayla, do you need to go see the nurse?”

“Yeah, does the freak need to go to the nurse again?” Someone in the
back jeered. The laughter continued, much to my dismay.

“No!” I shouted. “I don’t want to go there. Please, don’t take me to the
nurse.”

“Quiet down!” Mrs. Garland ordered. “That’s quite enough. This isn’t
something to laugh about.”

In spite of her warning, the laughter persisted. I could see my
classmates’ mouths moving, their faces contorted with amusement, but I
could no longer hear them. Their laughter, their voices, even
Mrs. Garland’s attempts to quiet them had all faded into the background.
All I could hear were the popping noises.

POP. POP. POP.

The classroom began to spin around me as I hunched over. They echoed
inside my skull, growing louder with each successive crack.

POP. POP. POP.

I couldn’t take it anymore. I spun around to face the class and shouted
at the top of my lungs. “SHUT UP!”

In a sudden, uncontrollable movement, my body arched backward, forcing
my head and shoulders toward the floor.

POP. POP. POP. POP. POP. POP.

The sounds resembled a bunch of balloons being popped all at once. The
classroom fell eerily silent. Every single person was looking at me. It
wasn’t until I tried to make sense of my position that I understood why.

The way my torso had folded defied logic. My spine had lost its
structural integrity entirely, allowing my body to become grotesquely
malleable. My head lay flat against the floor, my upside-down
perspective revealing the ceiling above me and the petrified faces of my
classmates as they stared down.

The laughter had stopped. Nobody was making fun of me anymore. They were
screaming.

I tried to straighten myself, but my body refused to cooperate. I
couldn’t get my spine to move back into place. Tears began trickling up
my face, falling toward my forehead as I stared helplessly at the
ceiling.

The students fled the room in a frenzy, their panicked cries filling the
hallway, as Mrs. Garland shouted for someone to get help. Beneath all of
it, I could hear my spine. It sounded like it was going to snap.

CRAAAAAAAAACK.

The last thing I remember before blacking out was the sight of the
classroom floor beneath me. Seeing it from behind my own feet was
surreal and profoundly unsettling. That was the last time I ever set
foot in a classroom. After that incident, I wasn’t allowed to return.

My parents spoke to several doctors and specialists at the hospital. All
of them insisted that I was a perfectly healthy girl. They couldn’t find
anything wrong with my spine. No breaks. No cracks. Not even a hairline
fracture.

“How can a little girl suffer from severe hypothermia and then nearly
break her spine?” I remember hearing my dad shout at one of the doctors.
“Those kids! The teacher! They saw it with their own eyes! How is any of
this possible?”

But nobody knew. Nobody, except me.

The doctors decided to keep me overnight for observation. I remember
feeling a tremendous sense of relief when my parents told me. I know
that probably sounds strange, but considering everything that had
happened, I couldn’t have been happier. I wouldn’t be in my bedroom or
have to worry about the Man coming to visit me in the middle of the
night.

My parents insisted on staying with me, and after some additional
testing, the doctors advised me to get some rest. I couldn’t wait. The
previous days had been hell. I was exhausted. I wanted nothing more than
a good night’s sleep.

Before bed, Dad brought me a small package of animal crackers from the
vending machine. “Hey, I’m sorry about how today went.”

I shrugged and kept reading one of the books Mom had brought me. “It’s
okay.”

“No, it’s not.” Dad pulled a chair up beside my bed, next to Mom. “I
dealt with bullies too growing up. Kids are mean, but that’s not an
excuse for them to act that way toward you.”

“What happened at school?” Mom asked. “We want to help.”

Memories of what had happened earlier that day flooded my mind. I
recoiled from her touch. “I don’t want to talk about it.”

“Kayla, we need to understand,” Dad said. “But we can’t unless you talk
to us.”

I closed my book. “There’s a man in my closet. He’s the one who did
this.”

“Sweetheart,” Mom said, “there’s nobody in your closet.”

“Yes, there is. I saw him.”

“I know you think you did, but—”

“I saw him!” I exclaimed. “He’s the one doing this!”

“We believe you,” Dad assured me. “We believe that something is
happening to you.”

“They’re not nightmares!” I groaned, throwing my arms up in frustration.
“You’re not listening! I’m not making this up!”

“We’re not saying you’re making it up,” Mom said, though her voice
betrayed the strain. “We’re saying that we haven’t seen anything. We
don’t know what you’re seeing.”

“Because he doesn’t let you!” I yelled, my voice breaking on the final
word.

Dad leaned forward, resting his elbows on his knees. “Who doesn’t let
us?”

“The Man! He’s in my closet! He comes out at night and asks me
questions. And when I answer them, bad things happen!”

“What kind of questions?” Mom asked.

“He asked me what was strong enough to smash ships but afraid of the
sun. Then he asked me what was comfortable in my mouth and in my shoe.
And last night,” I lowered my voice as I recalled the final question.
“He asked me what cracks when it’s weary, and tingles when it’s scared.”

Dad was taken aback by what I was able to recite from memory. “And you
answered these questions?”

“Yes. I did.”

“What did you say?”

“I didn’t want to answer. He made me!” I insisted, opening my mouth as
if that would somehow help them understand. “Ice. A tongue. And a
spine.”

Dad leaned back, his shoulders sinking. “Okay,” he said. “I think I
understand what’s going on.”

“You do?”

He paused as he searched for the right words. “You’ve been through a
lot, Kayla. Sometimes, when we’re overwhelmed, things can start to feel
connected even when they aren’t.”

“No!” I blurted. “This is exactly what I’m talking about! You never
listen!”

Mom reached for my hand. “Kayla—”

“HE’S REAL!” I roared, my voice ringing through the room.

Dad fidgeted in his chair nervously before scooting it closer toward my
bed. “We’re going to figure this out, Kayla. I promise.”

“I don’t want to talk about it anymore.” I turned my back to them,
drawing my knees toward my chest.

Neither Mom nor Dad asked me about the Man again. The room settled into
a tense silence, broken only by the occasional nurse coming in to check
on me throughout the night. They asked me questions and checked my
vitals before moving on to their next patient. 

Mom was the first to fall asleep. Dad remained awake, periodically
standing to stretch his legs after sitting for so long. Every now and
again he would ask how I was feeling. I would tell him that I was fine.
I didn’t want him to be more worried than he already was. Even though I
was fatigued, I found myself watching the two of them, grateful they
were there. 

I tried to sleep, too. But no matter how comfortable I made myself, I
couldn’t shake the restlessness that had taken over me. Every time I
closed my eyes, I found myself opening them again to peer toward the
doorway. There was nothing there. 

Every so often, I could hear the far-off squeak of a cart rolling down
the hallway, accompanied by the subdued voices of nurses talking at
their station. Ordinary hospital noises. It should have been comforting.
But I couldn’t relax.

Sometime later, I heard footsteps. THUD. THUD. THUD. They were coming
from the foot of my bed. I assumed it was one of the nurses making their
rounds. After all, it had been the soundtrack to the entire evening up
to that point.

THUD. THUD. THUD. They clomped against the tile floor with the cadence
of someone who knew exactly where they were going, each footstep
separated by precisely the same interval, like a metronome. They weren’t
getting louder. They were getting closer.

A dreadful feeling settled in my stomach, telling me not to look. But
the footsteps stopped. Right next to me. I nearly jumped out of my skin
when I saw who it was. Dad.

Thinking I was still dreaming, I stared at him, waiting for him to say
something. I wanted to believe I was mistaken, but one look at his face
was enough to convince me otherwise. It was my father’s, but all the
warmth was gone. He looked down at me, his features arranging into
something that no longer looked human. His eyes cast an eerily familiar
glow.

In the space behind him stood the Man. He towered over my father, his
soot-black silhouette pressed against the ceiling, making the room
appear far too small to hold him. He was engulfed by the shadows,
leaving only his head and spindly hands visible. They hung just above
the floor.

“Mom?” I called out. “Mom? I need you.”

But Mom remained asleep in her chair. Undisturbed by what I was
experiencing. 

“Kayla…” Dad said. “Everything’s going to be all right.”

Before I could respond, hands began crawling out of Dad’s mouth. The
same fingers that had tormented me on previous nights were now pulling
at his lips, stretching them until his mouth hung wide open. More
fingers surfaced, one after another, their knuckles grinding against his
teeth. 

His jaw buckled beneath the strain. “You’re certain to be wearing your
best outfit or something special when you come to stay. What am I?”

That wasn’t his voice.

“Leave Dad alone!” I screamed.

“Ah, ah, ah, Kayla.” Dad grinned maliciously. “That’s no way to talk to
your father.”

The fingers strained against the walls of my esophagus as they rose
toward the surface. My mouth was pried open. 

My lips began to move, and with an almost breathless whisper, I
answered. “A coffin.”

Everything returned to normal like someone had pressed rewind on a
television. The hands retreated into my throat and Dad’s. Afterward, he
stepped backward from my bedside, returned to his seat beside Mom, and
closed his eyes. Within seconds, he was asleep again, just as he had
been before the Man arrived.

My sense of reality had been warped. Was the Man able to possess people?
My family? Hospital workers? My eyes darted around the room in paranoia.
I couldn’t trust anyone. Not after what had happened.

“Where are you?!” I screamed, searching the room. “He’s here! He’s here!
I saw him! He’s here!”

My outburst startled Mom awake. She rushed to my bedside, looking around
in confusion. “Who’s here, Kayla? There’s nobody here besides Dad and
me.”

“The Man!” I declared, retreating farther into my bed. “He had Dad!
He…he…” I struggled to catch my breath, pointing toward Dad as he began
stirring in his chair. “He was trying to hurt me!”

“No one’s going to hurt you.” Mom reached to embrace me, but I pulled
away.

“Get away!” I commanded. “Get away from me!”

“Kayla,” Dad said, rubbing his eyes and rising from his seat. “It was
just another nightmare.”

“STOP SAYING THAT! DON’T LET HIM NEAR ME! HE’S A LIAR!”

“Please, sweetheart,” Mom pleaded, raising her hands in an attempt to
calm me down. “It’s okay. You’re safe now. We’re right here.”

“Make him go away!” I sobbed. “Make him go away!”

A hurt look washed over Dad’s face. “I’m not going anywhere. I’m here
for you. I’m staying here until we figure out what’s wrong.”

“She’s having an episode. We need to do something,” Mom said, trying to
mask her distress.

“I know. I’m going to get help,” Dad said before stepping into the
hallway to call for assistance.

Meanwhile, Mom remained beside my bed. I watched her cautiously, my eyes
never leaving her.

“Mom?” I asked.

“Yes, Kayla?”

“Are you really my Mom?”

“Kayla, I—”

“Are you?”

“Of course I am,” she said, fighting back tears. “I’ll always be your
Mom.”

“I don’t believe you.” I pounded my fists against the mattress. “I don’t
believe you!”

“Please, take a breath.”

“STOP TELLING ME WHAT TO DO!”

Just then, the door swung open, and two hospital workers hurried inside.
One was a nurse I recognized from earlier that evening. The other was a
doctor carrying a clipboard.

“What’s happening?” the doctor asked my parents.

“She…she woke up screaming,” Dad explained. 

“She’s convinced that she saw somebody in here. A man.”

“He was here!” I interrupted, pointing at where Dad had been standing.
“He was standing right behind you! Right there!”

The doctor glanced toward the vacant space I had indicated before
turning back to my parents. “Has she been like this all evening?”

“She was upset earlier,” Mom clarified. “But she seemed to be doing
better. We thought she was finally getting some rest.”

“I saw him!” I continued pointing at the same spot. “You have to believe
me! He’s still here! I’m not safe here!”

“Kayla, I need you to take a breath for me,” the doctor said,
approaching my bedside.

“Don’t come any closer!”

“Okay.” He stopped advancing. “That’s fine. I’m not going to hurt you.”

“Mom…Dad…make him leave!”

The doctor exchanged a concerned glance with the nurse. I studied their
faces, searching for any sign of the Man’s yellow eyes. Neither of them
had them.

“Can you tell me what you saw?” the doctor asked.

“He had Dad’s face, but it wasn’t Dad. He had these hands, and they were
coming out of his mouth. They made him talk.”

“Made him talk?” 

“Yeah.” My voice faltered. “He made me answer another one of his
questions.”

The doctor looked toward my parents again. Dad held Mom while she sobbed
into his chest. I probably looked just as frightened and confused as I
felt. As they felt.

“She’s been through a tremendous amount tonight. We’re going to give her
something to help her settle down.” The doctor told my parents,
motioning for the nurse to prepare the medication.

“No!” I shouted. “I’m not going to sleep!”

“Kayla,” Dad said, still holding Mom in his arms. “Nobody’s going to
make you do anything. We just want you to feel better.”

“You don’t understand! He comes when I sleep!”

“Don’t make me go to sleep!” I begged, pulling away from the nurse as
she reached for my arm. “Please, don’t do this.”

The nurse lightly gripped my arm. “It’s all okay. We’re just going to
give you something to help you relax.”

She held my arm steady and connected a syringe to my IV. Mom and Dad
watched her administer the medicine.

I looked at my parents. Then the nurse. Then the doctor. A cool
sensation traveled up my arm, reminding me of the brain freezes I’d get
whenever I ate ice cream.

I couldn’t stop looking at everyone. My eyelids grew heavy, each blink
lasting a little longer than the last.

“Please don’t let him get me,” I said aloud to no one in particular.

The medication began to take effect, making the room spin like something
out of a carnival funhouse. Above me, the lights became little more than
hazy white circles. If my parents and the medical staff were talking, it
was becoming harder to hear them. Their voices sounded like they were
coming from the opposite end of a deep cavern.

I tried to keep my eyes open. Every time they began to close, I forced
them back apart, terrified of what might happen if I let myself fall
asleep.

“Please don’t let him get me.”

The last thing I remember was watching the blurry silhouettes of my
parents standing beside my bed before everything went black.
