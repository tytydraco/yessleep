I wasn’t always an only child. Growing up I was the youngest of three. I
barely remember them. They had names once, but they’re lost. Their faces
are smudged and blurred in my mind, like a water-damaged picture. They
disappeared during a family trip and I thought I’d left the thing that
took them behind.

Forgive me if I make any mistakes, I’m still getting used to typing with
one hand. Never realised how many letters are on the left side of a
keyboard. You’d think it would be 50-50. Maybe it is, but it sure feels
like there are a lot more over there.

Sorry, getting distracted. My hometown. It’s called…West-something.
Feels like it’s on the tip of my tongue. Maybe it’ll come back to me.
Maybe it won’t. That’s kind of what I want to talk about. If I can.

The incident with my brothers was maybe five years ago. Its difficult to
say exactly when it happened because no one else remembers it. Things
had returned to normal, or rather were the same as they had always been
as far as anyone knew. I’d gotten a job stocking shelves at the local
branch of some supermarket chain, no idea which one. I’d been there
maybe three weeks, picking up shifts at weekends and after school. I
hadn’t been trained on tills yet, which I was grateful for, but my
manager kept telling me it was coming.

There was this girl I worked with, she went to my school but we’d never
talked before I started at the shop. She had this sort of gothy-artsy
thing, black hair cut short and dip dyed a really vibrant red. Her name
began with a D. She had a piercing too, but I’m not sure where. Her lip
or nose, maybe her eyebrow but I don’t think so. Her eyes were a little
too close together but in a cute way and when she laughed her top lip
would curl and reveal too much gum. Sorry again, I know it’s stupid. She
was really sweet and kind and I just don’t want to forget her, so I’m
trying to remember everything I can while I can.

The other night we were both working. It was late and they’d turned off
the lights in the refrigerated bays. Not the overheads, the shop was
still open, just the ones that lit the actual products.

“Hey, Brainrot!” D said as she skidded into the aisle where I was
restocking various yoghurts. She would always call me brainrot because I
spent every break browsing short videos on my phone which, according to
her, would rot my brain.

“What’s up Dynamite?” I said. Can’t say why I called her Dynamite. Was
her name Dinah?

“Have you seen Paul? He was supposed to give me a lift.” She said it so
casually but it made the hairs on the back of my neck stand up. No one
named Paul worked there. The name hadn’t been mentioned once since I’d
started. I hadn’t been down to work that day. My manager had called and
asked if I could come in, said there’d been some error with the rota and
they were short a body. I didn’t know what to say without sounding
crazy, since my thoughts had gone to a pretty crazy place.

“It’ll be OK.” I managed after an uncomfortable amount of time. D just
burst out laughing.

“Christ, man, he’s probably stoned again, not dead.” With that she
walked away and left me holding yoghurt drinks that promised their
trademarked good bacteria would improve your gut health. I tried
stocking the shelves again, hoping the monotonous activity would flush
the worry from my mind. But all I could think about was Paul.

Hours passed and the name resounded with every step on the vinyl tiling.
The rhythm of stacking and stocking and scanning all moved to that one
syllable until I couldn’t take it. I lurched into the break room after
D, the words vomiting out of my mouth and down my chin.

“Did you find out about Paul?” They came out too fast, too muddled.
Instead of an answer I was met by blank stares from D and my other
coworkers.

“Who’s Paul?” D said at last, breaking the silence and the remains of my
sanity with it. All I could do was nod. Everything became distant after
that. Someone guided me to a chair and handed me a glass of water. Then
I was alone in the break room with D, no with my manager. He was saying
something to me, but I couldn’t make it out.

“You’ve all forgotten Paul.” He said again and again. I gasped, a first
breath after plunging into an icy pool. The world drew back in and the
words he’d been repeating came clearly. “Is there someone I can call?”

“I’m fine. Sorry, I’ll get back to work.” I made to rise but his hands
were in front of me. Not touching but urging me to stay.

“Look it’s fine. I shouldn’t have called you in tonight. An ambulance is
on the way, they’re going to check you over.”

I lurched to my feet, this time ignoring his protestations. “I’ll be
fine. I’m just going to head home.” He called after me but his words
were an indistinct blur. As I left via the back door I caught sight of
something in the corner of my eye. Something half-remembered. It looked
like a large sheet of black plastic held taut in the air to obscure
whatever lay behind it. It was gone when I turned my focus on it.

I didn’t live far from the supermarket, just a thirty minute walk from
the town centre to the more residential area. The air had just started
to turn cold and I welcomed each icy breath that struck at my ability to
concentrate.

The house was quiet of course. One of the perks of living alone, I
suppose, that you never have to worry about waking anyone when you get
back in the middle of the night. I went to the kitchen to grab something
to eat and the sink was full of pots. I could have sworn I’d washed up
yesterday, but there were multiple plates in there. I opened my fridge
and grabbed the roast dinner that had been left for me and stuck in the
microwave. It hummed and spun slowly and as I stared at it the
incongruities began to hiss and sizzle in my mind.

The thing about this creature, whatver it is, is that when it feeds it’s
like it eats the memories of a person. Everyone else just forgets they
exist. But the remnants of their life remain. When my brothers
disappeared I returned to a home set up for three boys. But my parents
couldn’t process it. I slept alone in the smallest bedroom with a bunk
bed while a larger bedroom sat furnished but unoccupied. It happened to
me, even though I remember them. I kept my brother’s journal and some
pictures of the three of us beside my bed for months after they were
taken. Until, one day, I found the journal was empty and wondered why
I’d even got it. Pictures that I know they’re in just seem to feature
me, badly framed off to one side. Remnants. A negative space, by which
absence can inform you that something was there.

It had taken my parents, just as it had my siblings. As it had taken
Paul. And I was less resistant to it than I had been. There were three
dirty plates in the sink. I don’t know if my parents had a guest over,
or a relative. I don’t know if I had another sibling that has been
entirely taken from me.

I moved about my house from room to room, looking for the echoes of
their lives before they disappeared to me. A yoga mat, a boxed set of
world war two documentaries, low sodium ketchup, a bible with an Old
receipt as a bookmark. There were so many things that hinted at a life
lived. I wanted to cry but I couldn’t. I just felt empty.

I slept in the master bedroom that I’d left empty since moving here,
surrounded by objects I didnt own. I prayed that I would be taken too,
into that black hole of memory itself.

An old radio alarm woke me at 6:45 am and I had no notion as to why I’d
surrounded myself with rubbish. I got up, got dressed and headed to
school. It was on the far side of town, about forty minutes away. I
walked there along the same paths I always did, through town past the
empty lot and on to the school.

I saw D as I was heading to registration and waved at her. She seemed a
little confused, probably because of my outburst at work, but she waved
back. It was only as I sat in registration that I remembered my parents.
Or remembered that I couldn’t remember them. It sent me into a stupor.
Things happened around me but I couldn’t focus on it. I couldn’t focus
on anything. If I focused, I thought about my parents.

I saw D again as the school day wound to a close. She was hiding behind
the old science block, surreptitiously smoking a cigarette while others
nearby stole hits from a vape.

“Sorry about last night.” I said, heading towards her. She looked at me
and cocked her head.

“What about last night?” She said. I couldn’t tell if she was playing
coy.

“You know, at work?” I prompted. I needed to know if she actually
remembered.

“Think you’re confused, Brainrot. I don’t have a job.” She took a long
inhale and I knew she wasn’t being flippant. In my mind I quickly
retraced my steps since leaving last night. Nothing stuck out at me
except…except the empty lot. The empty lot that had been a supermarket
the night before. I needed to escape it, needed some way to stop it.
Mostly, though, I needed someone I could talk to about it.

“Why do you call me Brainrot?” I asked, teasing at one of those
vestigial memories the creature left that would eventually wither and
die.

“Because-” She frowned. “You’re always on your phone looking at shorts
during lunch.”

“We take lunch at different times.”

“Someone must have told me.” She shook off my response but I could see
that the holes were there for her. I pressed.

“So, you never saw me watching shorts?”

“Christ, this is the weirdest interrogation. So what if I have or not,
freak?” her voice had raised and other kids were looking at us now. I
leaned towards her so I could speak without being overheard.

“Something’s happening. I need to talk to someone.” I tried to keep my
voice calm, but judging by her face it didn’t work. It had gone deathly
quiet.

“I suggest a therapist.” She tossed her cigarette to the floor and
pushed past me. As I turned to follow I could tell it had happened
again. I didn’t know what it was in that moment, only that all was not
the same as it was.

Then I saw it. The black box. A thin opaque film that lay over my eyes
to block the creature from my site and mine alone. D screamed as she saw
the thing itself. I reached for her, but in my panic I did so with my
left arm. Perhaps it’s for the best that I did because there couldn’t
have been more than a hair’s breadth between my arm and that covering
and I shudder to think what may have happened had I made contact with
it.

D’s scream ended all at once, as though someone had pressed mute on it.
There was a sickeningly wet sound and blood spewed up from behind the
bar. I winced back in anticipation of being hit by those globules that
had once been my friend. But it never came.

The black bar was gone, but the blood remained, for now at least. There
was a solid line in the spatter, as though it had been unable to cross
that barrier that kept the creature from my sight even when it seemed
ready to fly over it.

As to the rest of the blood, there was an outline in it. A negative
space image of the thing that had killed my entire family. Looking at it
made my head throb, my mind desperate to escape the confines of the
skull that trapped it in such close proximity to even an approximation
of such horror. When at last I blinked it was nightfall and I was alone
in a muddy field. At first I thought I had run in a state of delirium
until at last coming to my senses. But as I began to walk again I
started to suspect that was not the case. I don’t think I moved at all;
rather everything around me had been removed.

I walked for at least an hour before I reached a road, never
encountering any sign of human existence before it. Not a fence, or a
shed, not even a piece of rubbish. It had eaten my town and everyone in
it. My friends, teachers, bullies: all wiped from the earth. My house,
my school, the hospital where I was born, the supermarket where I
worked.

It’s me it wants. I don’t know why, or what for, but I know now that it
is eating everything around me until I am untethered. Until there is no
record of my existence upon this earth, no one who can say they know me.
I persist, but soon there will be no proof of it. One day this post will
no doubt disappear too, when it finally is done tormenting me.
