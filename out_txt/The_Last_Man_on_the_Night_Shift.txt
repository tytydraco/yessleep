I lied about how long I sat in my truck. That was the first thing I
changed when the investigator asked me what happened at Hartwell
Packing, and it’s the one thing I still think about whenever somebody
calls me brave. They say I went back into a burning factory and helped
two women get out. I did. My coat was ruined, my lungs were full of
smoke, and I spent a night in the hospital. None of that changes the
fact that I’d sat in the parking lot with the heater running, trying to
decide whether to go home. I’d known the emergency exit was chained shut
since the night before. I knew people were working on the other side. I
didn’t know a man named Ben was going to die, but I’d been warned enough
times to do something. I chose not to, until I saw Nora at a window and
finally got out of my truck. The smoke came after.

It was November 1996. I was forty-one and taking whatever maintenance
work I could get around western Pennsylvania. I’d repaired washing
machines for years, then moved on to bigger equipment when the money got
better. We weren’t starving, but we had a mortgage, an old truck that
needed brakes, and a daughter who was sixteen. My wife worked part-time
at a grocery store. When a man called and offered me two hundred dollars
a night to inspect machinery at a meatpacking plant that was closing, I
said yes. His name was Martin. He said the job would take four nights. I
asked if the building was still operating. He said a small crew was
finishing the last orders and the rest of the equipment would be taken
away before the property was sold.

Hartwell Packing stood beyond a freight yard and a row of empty
warehouses. It was a big old building with loading docks at the back,
dirty upper windows, and a brick chimney nobody seemed to use anymore.
Martin met me Monday evening in an office near the loading bay. He was
in his late fifties, neatly dressed, with a watch he looked at every few
minutes. He gave me keys to the maintenance rooms and showed me which
motors and electrical panels needed checking. Before he left, he pointed
to a corridor with two heavy doors at the far end. A chain ran through
the handles. He said the east wing had been closed since 1982, after a
refrigeration accident killed three workers. He told me not to go in. I
asked whether the power was disconnected. He said it had been shut off
years ago.

I spent Monday checking a conveyor drive, replacing a switch and trying
to loosen bolts that hadn’t moved in years. A few men were loading
frozen boxes onto a truck. There was also a security man called Alan, a
large fellow with a gray mustache who asked whether Martin had told me
to stay away from the east wing. I said yes. He asked again, just to
make sure. I remember that because he didn’t seem concerned about any of
the other rooms I was working in. I went home around two in the morning
and didn’t think much more about it.

Tuesday night I heard voices behind the chained doors. First there was
the sound of equipment running, then somebody laughed and a man told her
to get back to work. I stood there listening for a minute. The padlock
was new, and there were fresh marks on the handles where the chain had
rubbed away the paint. Somebody was opening those doors regularly. I
called Martin from the office. He said a temporary crew was clearing old
stock from the storage rooms. I reminded him he’d said the whole wing
was shut down. He told me the processing lines were shut down, not every
room, and asked whether I’d finished my motor inspections. I hadn’t. He
said to get on with them.

An hour later I found a new power cable running into the east wing.
Whoever installed it had done a decent job, but the old fire alarm
circuit had been taken out of service. There was a bypass switch where
the connection should have been. If a fire started in the east wing, the
main alarm might not sound. I wrote down the panel number. Alan came in
while I was checking it. I showed him what I’d found and told him it
needed fixing if people were working in there. He said Martin already
knew. I asked how long it had been that way. Alan told me everybody
would be out by the weekend, so I should stop worrying. I said a fire
wouldn’t wait until the weekend. He didn’t like that. He stood in the
doorway until I packed up my tools.

I drew the circuit in my notebook because I wanted to be certain. A new
supply could be fitted for temporary work, but that didn’t explain the
alarm. Someone had deliberately taken the two alarm wires out and capped
them. I could have put them back if I’d known what detectors were still
attached at the other end. I didn’t, and joining wires blindly in an old
building is a good way to make trouble. I wrote NEEDS TEST BEFORE
OCCUPANCY in block letters, with the date beside it. It was the sort of
note that should have forced me to stop the job. Instead I told myself
it belonged on Martin’s desk. I left a copy of the panel number on the
maintenance office clipboard. When I checked the clipboard Thursday,
that page was gone.

The next night a woman came into the maintenance room wearing a white
plastic apron over a dark sweater. She had a red mark on her nose from a
face mask. She asked if I could open the emergency door beside the east
loading bay. I asked what was wrong with it. She glanced behind her
before saying it wouldn’t open from inside. Alan appeared a moment later
and told her to get back to her station. She said she’d been looking for
the bathroom. He watched her walk away, then told me she complained
about everything. Her name was Nora. I didn’t say anything else to him.
Before going home I went around the outside of the building and found
the exit. A chain held the door handle to a steel post. The door would
only open a couple of inches, even if someone pushed the bar from
inside. I knocked and a man answered. I said I was maintenance and
wanted to check the door. He asked if Alan had sent me. When I said no,
he went quiet. Then he told me to ask Alan why it was locked.

Alan said the chain stopped people stealing company property. I told him
that wasn’t a reason to block an exit. He said the crew had another way
out and reminded me I wasn’t a fire inspector. I could discuss it with
Martin on Thursday. I was tired, and I wanted to get paid, so I wrote
another note in my book and left. That is the part people don’t like
hearing when I tell them the truth. Nobody needed to convince me the
chain was dangerous. I’d pulled on the door myself. I went home anyway.

Martin called Thursday afternoon. He was friendly this time. He said the
crew would finish its last shipment that night, the doors would be
unlocked, and I could inspect the old electrical equipment after
midnight. He offered an extra hundred dollars if I completed the final
forms. I said I wouldn’t sign anything I hadn’t seen. He said that was
fair. When I arrived he handed me coffee and a folder of inspection
sheets. I brought up the exit again. He said the chain would come off
once the shipment was done. I told him it should come off now. He said
people were removing valuable stock and he wasn’t having any more
arguments about theft. I should’ve walked out then and called the fire
department. Instead I took the coffee and went to work.

Alan unlocked the double doors just after midnight. The east wing wasn’t
a storage room. It was a working meat line. About a dozen people were
packing meat in plastic bags and cardboard boxes. One man was scraping
old date labels off frozen packages while a woman stuck new labels on.
The room smelled of cleaning fluid and meat that had been stored too
long. The overhead lights were yellow, and stacks of packing material
stood close to the conveyor motors. Alan saw me looking at the labels
and said the crew was sorting stock for disposal. I asked why they were
printing new dates if it was going to be thrown away. He said to do the
electrical inspection and stay out of it.

The wiring was newer than I’d expected. The old equipment was running
through a temporary supply, but the fire alarm still wasn’t connected. I
checked the wires twice in case I’d missed something. I hadn’t. Nora
approached while Alan was checking the loading dock. She said Martin was
selling old meat under new dates and that some of the workers hadn’t
been paid in two weeks. She’d thought they were sorting stock for
disposal when she started. Once they were told to put new labels on the
boxes, some people quit. Others stayed because they needed the pay.
Martin said nobody would receive the rest of their wages until the last
truck was loaded. When I asked why they didn’t report him, she said one
of the men had tried, and things had got worse for everybody.

Nora didn’t start by telling me everything. First she asked whether the
new power feed was safe. I said it had been connected properly, as far
as I could tell, but that the alarm wasn’t working. She asked if a fire
in that room would set off a warning anywhere else. I told her not
unless somebody pulled a manual alarm in the main section. She looked at
the boxes beside the conveyor, then asked where the nearest working
alarm was. I showed her the corridor on my drawing. She studied the
lines for a moment. I could see she was deciding how much she could
trust me. I asked how long she’d been there. Six weeks, she said. She’d
taken a bus from a nearby town for the first few shifts, until Alan
began insisting that the crew arrive together in a company van. When I
asked why, she said he wanted to know exactly who was on site and who
wasn’t. That answer stayed with me.

That man was Ben. He was twenty-five or twenty-six, thin, with dark hair
and small cuts across his fingers. He’d found papers from the accident
in 1982 while clearing an old office. The papers said workers had
complained about the same locked emergency door before the refrigeration
pipe failed. The official report said the door had jammed from damaged
hinges. Ben had copied some records to give to a newspaper. Martin found
out and ordered Alan to search the office and keep the crew inside until
he got the copies back. Ben told me he’d seen my name on a report in the
old office. He’d been made to carry a box of papers for Alan and noticed
it. He wouldn’t touch the folder again while Alan was nearby, but he
told me where it was. Then he went back to packing boxes. Nora said he
had a little boy at home and bills from a recent hospital visit. He’d
asked to leave early and been told he wouldn’t get paid if he walked
out.

Ben came back while I was packing up my meter. He asked if the safety
notes I’d written could be copied. I said they could, but I wanted to
know why. He told me his father had worked in the plant years before and
was still angry about the way the 1982 accident had been handled. He’d
grown up hearing that the company blamed workers for mistakes the
managers had made. When he found the old papers, he recognized one of
the names and started asking questions. That’s when Alan began searching
lockers. Ben had hidden his copies somewhere outside the east wing, but
he wouldn’t tell me where. He said if Martin found them before the final
shipment left, it wouldn’t be only his job he’d lose. He was shaking
slightly, though he tried to hide it by keeping his hands busy with the
plastic bags. He asked whether I’d ever been punished for refusing to
sign something at work. I told him once, a long time ago. He said he
hoped I still knew how to say no.

The office at the end of the hall was unlocked. I found the folder
marked FINAL INSPECTION in the top drawer of a filing cabinet. Most
pages were ordinary forms. Then I found a typed report about a fire in
the east wing. The date was Friday, November 15, 1996. It was still
Thursday night. The report blamed a faulty conveyor motor, said the wing
had been cleared of employees, and claimed two workers had entered a
restricted area without permission. It also said the disconnected alarm
was part of an approved shutdown. My name was at the bottom, with a
signature that looked close enough to fool someone who didn’t know me. I
hadn’t signed it. I read the date again and checked my watch, as if that
would help. Under the report were lists of machines the company claimed
had been removed years earlier. I’d just seen several of them running.
There were insurance papers too, with amounts written beside the
equipment. Whoever prepared the file expected the old wing and its
records to be destroyed, with a maintenance man to blame if anybody
asked questions.

There were mistakes in the report that made it worse to read. Whoever
typed it had left blanks for the time the first alarm sounded and the
number of firefighters who arrived. The names of the two dead workers
were blank too. But my name was filled in. It said I’d confirmed the
electrical system was safe, and a typed line underneath claimed I’d
ordered the temporary alarm turned off. I’d done neither. There was even
a sentence saying the loading door had been checked from inside that
evening. I thought of Nora asking about the exit and Ben speaking
through the steel door the night before. I tried to imagine a reasonable
reason for someone to have written all this in advance. I couldn’t. I
took the top sheet but left the folder as I’d found it. I wanted to be
able to prove where it came from. The trouble was, I’d gone into an
office I wasn’t supposed to be in. I was already thinking about how
Martin might use that against me.

I folded the report and put it in my pocket. Then I heard Martin and
Alan outside. Alan said the last truck was almost loaded but Ben still
wouldn’t hand over the copied papers. Martin said to search the
remaining boxes. Alan asked about the workers. Martin said they could
leave when the records were found. He also told Alan to finish the
shutdown before the morning crew arrived. Neither man sounded especially
worried. That bothered me almost as much as the report. I waited until
they moved away, then went back to the processing room. One of the old
conveyor motors sounded rough, and there were cardboard boxes pushed up
close to it. I could smell something sharp beneath the cleaning fluid,
but I couldn’t tell where it came from. I told Nora I was going to find
Martin. She asked me not to leave without opening the doors. I said I
wouldn’t.

Martin was in his office with his coat on. I laid the report on his desk
and asked why my signature was on a statement about a fire that hadn’t
happened. He didn’t look surprised. He said it was only a draft for the
shutdown. I asked why a draft named two people who were supposed to die.
He told me I was misunderstanding it. Then he opened a drawer and took
out an envelope with money inside. He said he knew I had a family and
bills. The shipment was nearly finished. If I went home, I’d be paid for
the full week and nobody would make trouble for me. I told him I wanted
the workers out, the exits unlocked, and the fire alarm fixed. He said
the crew would be released when the work was done. I told him I wasn’t
signing any forms. He said that was my choice. Then he told me to leave
his property. I didn’t take the money, but I did leave. Alan watched me
collect my toolbox and stood by the outside door until I walked through
it.

I started my truck. The heater blew warm air over my hands. There was a
police station about fifteen minutes away. I had the report, my notes
about the alarm, and enough to explain why the county should send
somebody to the plant. I could have gone there. Instead I sat thinking
about Martin’s threat, about whether he’d accuse me of stealing the
report, and about how badly I wanted to finish the job and forget the
whole place. I thought maybe the fire report was a plan they’d never
carry out. The crew could be out in an hour. I remember looking toward
the east wing and seeing Nora at a dirty upstairs window. She pressed
her palm to the glass. A shadow moved behind her, and she stepped away.
I turned off the engine, but I still didn’t get out immediately. That
was the time I changed in my statement. I don’t know the exact number of
minutes, and I’ve stopped trying to make it sound shorter.

While I sat in the truck, a driver stopped beside me to ask how to reach
the highway. I gave him directions. That’s the part that still makes my
stomach turn. I could speak perfectly well. I could have asked him to
call the police from the next gas station, or told him what was
happening in the east wing. Instead I talked about the roadworks by the
freight yard. The driver thanked me and left. I watched his tail lights
go. I pulled the folded report out of my pocket and read the date again.
The next morning. In a few hours it would be the date on the page. I
remember looking at the upper windows of the factory and trying to make
sense of why I was still sitting there. I told myself I needed to be
certain before making an accusation. But I was certain enough. I just
didn’t want to pay the price of being right.

When I finally got out, I went through the maintenance entrance. I took
the heavy bolt cutters from the workshop and started toward the east
loading bay. Halfway down the corridor I smelled burning plastic. Then
somebody shouted from behind the wall. I ran outside to the emergency
door. Smoke was coming out beneath it. The chain had been wrapped around
the handle twice and pulled tight against the post. I worked the jaws of
the cutters around a link, but they slipped. Somebody hit the door from
the inside. I shouted for them to move back. Ben answered. He said four
people were still in there. Most of the crew had been let out after the
last truck left, but Alan had kept four behind while he searched for the
papers. When the smoke started, they’d tried to move a storage rack away
from the exit. It tipped and blocked the door. I told Ben I was going to
cut the chain. It took three tries. Finally a link snapped and I pulled
the chain free. The door moved a few inches, then struck something
solid. Ben said his leg was caught under the rack. I pushed my shoulder
into the door, but I couldn’t force it farther. He was coughing hard. I
told him to stay low. He said two women were trying to reach the main
corridor and another man had gone toward the storage rooms. I told him I
would come through the other entrance. He asked me to hurry.

I ran inside to the newer part of the building and pulled the manual
fire alarm. It worked there, at least. From the maintenance office I
called the fire department. I gave the dispatcher the address, told her
a fire had started in the east wing and said there were four people
still inside. She asked whether the doors were open. I said the
emergency exit was blocked from within and the main doors were chained.
I heard myself saying it and wondered how many days I’d known about both
of those problems. She told me help was coming. I went back to the east
corridor with the cutters. Alan had locked the double doors after
showing me out. I cut that chain as well. The doors opened only partway.
Stacks of empty boxes had been shoved against them from inside, probably
by people trying to find somewhere to put them when the smoke began. I
pushed until I could squeeze through.

The room was filling with smoke. Flames had reached the packing material
above the old conveyor, and some of the overhead lights were flickering.
I couldn’t see more than a few steps ahead. I shouted and heard Nora
answer somewhere to my left. She was crouched beside a table, trying to
drag another woman toward the doors. The woman was barely conscious and
had lost one of her boots. When we lifted her, her free foot kept
catching on the floor. I took her beneath the shoulders while Nora held
her legs. We stumbled through the smoke, banging against boxes I
couldn’t see until I’d hit them. At the narrow gap in the doors the
woman woke enough to struggle. She kept reaching back for her missing
boot. I told her to forget it. Nora was coughing too hard to say much,
so I pulled the woman through sideways while Nora pushed from behind. We
got her into the clearer air of the corridor and lowered her against the
wall. Nora grabbed my sleeve and said Ben was still near the emergency
door. I told her to get the woman outside and show the firefighters
where we’d been. She shook her head, wanting to go back herself, but she
could hardly stand. I promised I’d look for him.

I went back into the processing room. The smoke was lower now and I had
to feel my way along the tables. I followed the outside wall toward the
loading bay until I heard Ben coughing. He was on the floor beside the
emergency door, trapped under the storage rack. One leg was pinned at an
angle that made me look away for a second. He had a piece of cloth over
his mouth. I tried lifting the rack from one end. It barely moved. I
found a steel pipe beside the wall and used it as a lever. For a moment
the frame rose an inch or two, but the pipe bent and slipped. Ben cried
out. I put the pipe down. There was an old fire extinguisher on the
wall. I pulled the pin and aimed it toward the flames nearest us. It
gave one weak burst and died. Ben saw what I was doing and shook his
head. He asked whether Nora and the other woman had got out. I said yes.
He closed his eyes, then asked about the fourth worker. I told him I’d
sent the firefighters the location of the storage rooms and they were on
their way. I hadn’t seen that man, and I wasn’t going to pretend
otherwise.

I tried the rack again with both hands, burning my fingers through the
gloves, and it still wouldn’t lift. Ben stopped me. He said there was no
point breaking my back and asked me to find something stronger. I told
him I would. He grabbed my sleeve as I stood. ‘Don’t leave me,’ he said.
I promised I’d come back. He reached into his pocket and held out a
folded photograph. I didn’t understand why he was giving it to me then.
He pushed it into my hand, so I tucked it inside my coat. I said I was
going to get help and started toward the corridor. I hadn’t gone twenty
feet when part of the ceiling came down between us. Hot material struck
the floor, and smoke rushed into the space. I shouted his name. I heard
a cough, maybe a word, but couldn’t make it out. I tried going around
the fallen section and found flames climbing the wall. I got down on my
knees and looked for a gap. Somebody caught the back of my coat. It was
a firefighter, coming in through the corridor. Two more went past with a
hose. I told them exactly where Ben was trapped. They kept going toward
the loading bay. The first man pulled me out. I fought him for a few
seconds, not because I thought I could save Ben by myself but because
I’d just promised I would return.

Outside, somebody gave me oxygen. Nora was sitting on the ground beside
the other woman, who’d been placed on a stretcher. I asked whether
anyone had reached Ben. A firefighter said they were still working their
way to him. I watched men going in and out until someone finally cut the
power and the building went dark. Ben was brought out later. He was
dead. The fourth worker was found near a storage room and survived,
though he spent weeks in the hospital. Four people had been trapped
after Alan locked the doors: Nora, the woman she helped, Ben and the man
near the storage room. Three survived. Ben didn’t. The newspapers later
said I’d helped rescue two women. They were right. They didn’t mention
the two doors I’d known were locked before the fire.

At the hospital a nurse cut the sleeve of my coat away from a burn on my
forearm. The doctor checked my breathing and kept me on oxygen for a
while. A deputy came in to ask what I’d seen. I took the folded fire
report from my trousers and gave it to him. I told him where I’d found
it and showed him the notes I’d written about the alarm. I did not
mention sitting in my truck. I said Martin had sent me away and I’d
returned when I realized there was trouble. The deputy wrote that down.
He asked whether I knew the emergency exit had been chained before the
fire. I said I’d raised a safety concern with management. That was true,
as far as it went. He didn’t ask how long I’d known, and I didn’t offer
the answer.

The investigation went on for months. Inspectors found traces of
flammable cleaning liquid among the packing material near the old
conveyor, though no report I saw proved who had lit it. The rough motor
wiring had made the fire spread faster, but that alone didn’t explain
how it started. Alan disappeared before police could question him. They
found his car at a bus station two days later. Martin said he knew
nothing about the fire. The report I’d taken from the office told a
different story. It had been written before the fire, with blanks left
for the time and the names of the victims, but it already blamed a
maintenance man and declared the east wing empty. Other documents showed
false equipment losses prepared for an insurance claim and records of
meat sold with altered dates. The old complaints Ben had found showed
that the exit had been chained during the 1982 accident as well. Martin
was charged, and the case took years. I testified. Nora testified.
Workers spoke about missing pay and the locked doors. I can’t tell you
how every charge ended because I didn’t attend every hearing. I know the
company was finished and the building was torn down.

One investigator came to my house with photographs of the electrical
panels. I showed him the new cable and the bypassed alarm, and he asked
when I’d first seen them. I said Tuesday. He asked when I’d discovered
the emergency exit was locked. I said Wednesday. He wrote it down. Then
he asked why I’d waited until Thursday night to call for help. I told
him Martin had promised to open the doors and reconnect the alarm. That
was true too. But it made my choice sound simpler than it was. I’d
accepted his promise because the alternative meant an argument, the loss
of a job, and maybe a trip to the police. The investigator asked what
happened after Martin told me to go home. I said I had gone back when I
smelled smoke. I didn’t tell him about the truck driver who’d asked
directions, or the minutes I spent looking at the windows while I tried
to talk myself out of getting involved. Nobody knew that part.

I considered correcting my statement later. Once I drove to the
investigator’s office and parked across the street. I had the notebook
with me. People were going in and out of the building, ordinary people
on ordinary errands, and I couldn’t make myself cross the road. I drove
home instead. My wife asked where I’d been. I told her I was running
errands. She asked where the groceries were. I’d forgotten them. I went
back out and bought them. That was how it became easier to live with the
lie than to explain it. People thanked me for going into the factory.
Nora wrote me a letter. One firefighter said I had done something good.
I believed them about the two women, but none of them knew what I’d
failed to do before the fire.

I found Ben’s photograph while emptying my ruined coat at home. It
showed a little boy beside a red bicycle. He had a wide smile, missing
front teeth and one hand resting on the seat. On the back, in a child’s
uneven writing, were the words, Daddy, come home soon. I recognized the
boy from what Nora had told me. I called Ben’s mother after the funeral
and said I had something that belonged to him. She told me his wife and
son were staying with relatives and didn’t want to speak to anybody
connected to the factory. She said she’d pass on my message. Nobody
called back. I kept the photograph in my bedside drawer. Every few years
I’d come across it while looking for something else, and I’d sit with it
for a while before putting it away again.

Life went on. My daughter grew up, married, and had children of her own.
My wife died. I retired and moved somewhere smaller. I did ordinary
things. Fixed a leaking tap, watched television, went fishing when my
knees felt up to it. I didn’t spend every day thinking about Hartwell.
There were months when I hardly remembered the place. Then I’d hear a
sound that reminded me of a conveyor belt or smell hot plastic near a
workshop, and I’d be back in that corridor for a minute. I don’t think
guilt works the way people describe it. It isn’t always there making
noise. Sometimes it just waits until you have nothing else to think
about.

Last spring I found my old folder from the trial in a cabinet in the
garage. There were copies of the alarm notes, the fake fire report and a
letter Nora had sent after the case ended. She’d included records Ben
copied from the 1982 accident. Workers had complained about the locked
emergency exit back then, too. Those complaints were marked resolved
even though nothing had been changed. Alan had signed one of the forms.
Martin had signed another. Nora wrote that Ben wanted the families of
the men who died in 1982 to see those papers. She also said he’d asked
to leave early the afternoon before the fire because his little boy had
a doctor’s appointment the next morning. Martin told him he’d lose his
pay if he left before the shipment was done. I knew Ben needed money. I
hadn’t known he was trying to get home in time for his son.

I read Nora’s letter again and checked it against the records. Ben’s
father had worked in the plant years before, and one of the complaints
from 1982 carried his signature. Ben had grown up hearing how the
company blamed the dead workers. When he found the records, he’d made
copies because he wanted their families to know the door had been
locked. His son was ill, and he’d asked to leave early that last
afternoon to help at home. Martin told him he’d lose his pay. He stayed.
There wasn’t a clever answer to any of that. He had warned me about the
report even though it put him at more risk, and I’d spent that same
night working out whether helping him would be too much trouble.

I looked at the photograph again. I had carried it home from the factory
but never managed to return it. That was one thing I could still do. I
made calls and eventually found Ben’s son. He was grown, with a family
of his own, and he worked in construction about two hours away. When I
explained who I was, he said he knew my name. His mother had kept
newspaper clippings about the fire, including one with my picture. She’d
always told him I was the man who tried to save his father. I said I had
a photograph Ben had given me on the night of the fire. He asked if it
was the one beside a red bicycle, with white handles. I looked. It was.
He’d been looking for that picture for years.

We met at a diner near the highway the following Saturday. He was
already there when I arrived. I could see a little of Ben in his face
when he stood to shake my hand. We ordered coffee. I handed him the
envelope, and he took the picture out carefully. He smiled when he saw
the bicycle, then turned it over. For a while neither of us said much.
He told me his father had bought the bicycle secondhand and fixed it up
in their garage. The picture was taken the day he learned to ride
without training wheels. He said he’d forgotten about the writing on the
back. Then he asked what his father had been like at work. I told him
Ben was quiet, careful around Alan, and brave enough to warn somebody
he’d barely met. I told him Ben had been trying to help the other
workers get out when the fire started. All of that was true.

He asked whether his father had suffered. I told him Ben had been
trapped by a fallen rack and that I’d tried to move it. I didn’t pretend
it had been quick. I told him I’d spoken to Ben and tried to get help,
but the fire spread before the crews could reach him. He sat with that
for a moment. Then he thanked me for staying with his father as long as
I could. I thought about the truck again. I could have told him
everything right there. He deserved to know the exits had been locked
before the fire, that I knew it, and that I’d waited. Instead I told him
about Ben asking whether Nora had got out. He seemed glad to hear his
father had been thinking of the others. We talked about our families for
a while after that.

I could see he was doing his best to ask questions without making me
uncomfortable. That made it harder. He wanted to know whether the men
who locked the doors had ever apologized to his family. I said I didn’t
know. He said his mother had followed the case for years and finally
stopped because she couldn’t stand hearing the company talk about costs
and lost property. He’d been too young to attend most of the hearings.
He said the newspaper articles gave him a list of facts, but not much
idea what his father had been doing in those last minutes. I told him
Ben had asked about Nora and the other women. He looked down at the
photograph for a long time, then said that sounded like his dad. I
wished I could tell him something that would make it easier. There
wasn’t anything.

Before he left he asked if we might talk again someday. I said yes,
although I wasn’t certain I’d be able to do it. He said he wanted his
children to know a little about their grandfather. I told him the truth
about one thing. I said Ben had tried to do the right thing when others
around him were making it difficult. His son smiled and said his mother
had always told him that. I watched him put the old photograph in a
folder so it wouldn’t bend. He checked the seat belt twice before
pulling out of the lot, and for some reason that stayed with me. It was
such a small, careful thing. Ben would have been in his mid-fifties by
then if he’d lived. I tried to picture him as a grandfather. I couldn’t
get past the young man on the floor of the loading bay.

Outside the diner he shook my hand and said his mother had always told
him Ben was the kind of man who wouldn’t leave somebody behind. He hoped
he would have done the same. I said I thought he would. He thanked me
again for coming, then drove away with the photograph on the passenger
seat. I stayed in the parking lot. The place was busy, and a truck
pulled in beside me while I sat there. Its heater fan was loud enough
that I could hear it through my closed window. I thought about November
1996 and the minutes I’d spent trying to talk myself into going home. I
started the engine and turned the heater off.

I don’t know whether keeping the worst part to myself protects Ben’s son
or only makes things easier for me. That question hasn’t become any
simpler with age. What I do know is that Ben barely knew me, and when I
told him I’d come back, he believed me. I meant to keep that promise. I
just should’ve started keeping it a lot earlier.
