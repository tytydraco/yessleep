I know corpses don’t smile, but… I didn’t want to find out. It’s been
many years, and I can’t help but feel that somehow it was all my fault.
If I had just… left that book alone, maybe it wouldn’t have happened.
Maybe she wouldn’t have come back.
My mother had always been a sickly woman, ever since I was a little
girl. For as long as I could remember, she would break down in fits of
coughing at the slightest exposure to dust or pollen. Her skin was so
light and thin you could practically see her bones through it, and the
medications gave her such a tremor she couldn’t even hold a pen. I
didn’t know what it was at the time, but later learned that it was
cancer, and she had battled it for some years. It was no surprise to
anyone when she passed away, though knowing it was coming did nothing to
curb the pain. My father wept for weeks. I was eleven.
It took years, but we eventually moved on. I didn’t talk about it much
to my friends. I hated the pitying looks it got me, so at a certain
point I just stopped telling people. I told them my parents were
separated instead. 
The loss of my mother truly crushed my dad. Before her death, he was
always anxious about her health, but he at least seemed happy most of
the time. We’d go on day trips and he’d pick me up from school to get
ice cream on Wednesdays; but after she was gone, he just… crumbled. He
spent all his time in the basement, working on some project. It felt
like looking at me just made him sad – perhaps because I reminded him so
much of her.
Once I reached high school, he sent me to a special private school for
gifted and advanced students that had an emphasis on STEM. I think he
wanted to busy me so that he would see me less, and it worked. The
school also had a specialty program that channeled students into
translation and an interpretation, and I was particularly a fan of
language studies, so my friends and I were well versed in foreign
languages. I studied French primarily, and my friends were advanced in
Korean, Spanish, German, and Italian. Because of this, I guess I didn’t
entirely mind being so busy – I was having fun with it, even if it meant
I saw my dad less.
One night, my friends and I had a sleepover. It was October, so we were
doing all the traditional Halloween sleepover things – watching scary
movies, telling ghost stories, playing around in the dark and unexplored
parts of the house. It was the first time I learned about the closet in
the basement though. I don’t really ever remember it being there before.
Perhaps I should have thought a little harder before going in there… but
what was I to think? It was just a closet. That I had never noticed.
We found a book. It was buried in the boxes filled with old pictures and
moldy papers, though the pictures were odd. There seemed to be
children’s drawings and portraits, and they looked familiar but… I don’t
remember drawing anything quite like that, and none of the portraits
were of people I knew. Some of them looked like me or my dad, but we
don’t have green eyes. The papers seemed to be old legal papers, birth
certificates, a deed to the house, and I started to get the feeling that
we shouldn’t be rummaging through this. Once we found the book, though,
my friends were satisfied. Still, the smiles in the portraits unnerved
me.
The book was small, leather bound, with remnants of gold lettering on
the cover. Josie was convinced it was Greek, but I didn’t recognize the
words despite the little I had studied on my own. On the inside cover,
written in French, was a short phrase – or perhaps you could call it a
warning: “To receive, you must first give.”
The book was clearly a prop from some movie, or an elaborate Halloween
decoration of some sort, so the girls and I were excited to play around
with it, although something about it made me feel… uneasy.
We took it up to my room, grabbed some candles, and put out the lights.
Flipping through the book, most of the writing was done in black ink,
written in a mix of languages, although none of them seemed to be
English and the parts we could read between us were archaic. After
leafing through the book, we found a passage in French whose apparent
title was written in some odd squiggly script none of us recognized. It
was hard to make out most of it from how old it was, but I managed to
read the majority of it. It appeared to be some sort of prayer that, if
granted, would bring someone home… or something. The more we read of
this book, the more uncomfortable I got. I tried to close it, to tell
them we’d done enough and we should put it away, but my friends wanted
to have a pretend seance with the book. I didn’t like it, but they were
so insistent I felt like I couldn’t say no. 
So, we sat in a circle and brought our hands together, and I read the
prayer from the book. I can’t tell you why, but I… I thought of my
mother. As I read the prayer, I felt a deep loss, a longing to be held
in her arms again, one more time. The candles flickered, and went out.
The girls screamed, then laughed. We put the book away. I had trouble
sleeping that night.
The next morning, we heard a knock on my bedroom door. I opened it up,
and stopped. My friends groggily greeted her as though they expected
her. I simply stared. She smiled, and told us breakfast would be ready
soon. They all hurried downstairs, dragging me along, but I couldn’t
speak. How could she be here? What was going on? Where did she come
from? Was this… Was this because of last night? Why did everyone just
think this was normal? She had been gone for 3 years. She couldn’t have
just… come back.
I recognized the breakfast she had cooked. It was what she had always
cooked for me on special occasions, when she was feeling well enough.
Scrambled eggs, sausage, and french toast with whipped cream in the
shape of a smiling face on it. My friends all dug in, but I just stared
at it. I looked up, and she was looking at me. Her skin looked warm and
healthy. She had a full head of hair. She stood upright, and walked with
confidence and strength. This was not the woman I remembered. And as my
friends ate hungrily, as I stared at her, bewildered, she smiled, and
raised a single finger to her lips, for just a moment.
I ate quickly, and hurried off to see if my dad had seen her, and if he
was as shocked as I was. I checked his normal spot in the living room,
then the bedroom, then I checked the basement in case he was working on
something down there… he was nowhere to be found. I went back and asked
mom where dad was. She asked what I meant. I said I had a question for
him. She smiled pityingly, and asked me if I remembered that he was
gone. Gone as of three years ago. I felt a horrible pit in my stomach. A
phrase came back to me, and I felt sick. “To receive, you must first
give.”
The rest of the day was a blur. My friends eventually went home, and I
was left alone with… her. She set to work cleaning the house, and I
offered to help. I didn’t know what else to do. I was so anxious, I had
dreamed of having my mother back for years, but this just felt wrong. As
I was dusting the living room, I took a moment to look over our family
photos – everything from the last 3 years was gone, and replaced with
photos of me and her. But something about the photos unnerved me.
Perhaps it was because her smile just stretched a little too wide. As I
thought about it, I realized, since the start of the day, she hadn’t
stopped smiling. But that could have been my imagination, although I
don’t ever remember her eyes being green.
That night, I woke up at around 2 am. I could hear her cooking in the
kitchen. I’m not sure what she was cooking at that time of day. I went
downstairs to ask her what was going on, but when I did, her smiling
face simply asked me if I wanted some. Something in those green eyes
wasn’t quite right. I went back to bed.
The next day, I came back down and something felt weird about the living
room. It hit me, our sofa was gone. I asked her where it had gone. She
asked me, “what sofa?” Over the following weeks, furniture, heirlooms,
paintings, everything slowly trickled out of the house, until you would
swear our house was ready to be sold. But she never sold it. She kept
only enough for us… or at least, for me to survive.
I lived with this for a few years. I didn’t have much choice, really.
Every time I tried to bring it up to my friends or to the staff at
school, they gave me confused looks and asked me what was wrong – but I
could never explain. Just that something was. Once I turned 18, I
resolved to move out. I tried to talk to Mother about it, but she
wouldn’t have it. Every time I tried to bring it up, it’s as if she
knew, and she would change the subject. One day, I thought I would try
to just… go. But when I went to the door… my shoes were gone. And so
were my keys. I went to go back upstairs, and Mother was standing behind
me. She simply said, “This is your home, sweetheart.”
I gave up. I stopped trying to leave. My shoes came back, as did my
keys. My friends would text me constantly asking where I was and if I
wanted to hang out, but it was hopeless. I would never leave this house
again, I thought. I was confined, cared for, but a prisoner nonetheless.
A prisoner to someone – or something – that I wished was my mother. The
days turned into weeks, and I buried myself in whatever could distract
me from the scraping noises that echoed through the house at three a.m.
I didn’t want to know what she was doing. It was doing. Whatever.
Eventually, I told my best friend the truth about it. About all of it.
How I had a father, how we said the prayer that night, and how things
all changed after that. She didn’t believe me at first, but I begged her
and she conceded – I think more out of concern for my mental well being
than any true belief. She helped me convince my mother to let me go to a
party one night, and I didn’t come home. I stayed at my friend’s place.
We made up some story about how my mother was abusive, and I needed to
get out, so her parents would let me stay. My friend insisted I see a
therapist, but quietly respected it when I told her I just needed a new
life.
I never went back to that house.
A year later, I finally got an apartment of my own. I had a well paying
job, and a strong group of friends. Life seemed to be turning around,
even if I had no family to speak of. I even started to date someone,
though I told her both my parents were dead. I almost forgot about it
all.
That’s when I got the first letter. I never opened it. As soon as I saw
who it was from, I burned it. I nearly set the fire alarm off in my
hurry, and my roommates scolded me for it. I didn’t care. The next day,
another letter came. This one I didn’t burn, but I did cut it up as
small as I could and tried to flush it down the drain. I had to get some
drain cleaner to get it all down. The next day, another letter.
Eventually, I bought a professional shredding machine, and it became a
daily ritual to destroy the newest envelope. It got so bad that I
convinced my roommates we should find a new apartment. But even after we
moved, the letters wouldn’t stop coming. Every day, there was a new
one. 
To this day, I haven’t opened a single letter. The only letter I opened
about her was a black envelope – the invitation to her funeral. But like
I said, I didn’t go. I didn’t want to find out what would happen if I
did. I didn’t want to see those green eyes ever again. I just wish the
letters would stop coming.
