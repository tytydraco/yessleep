I pulled into a gas station on some side road. There had been no other
buildings for miles so I counted myself lucky for finding this haven.
God how I was wrong.

After pumping enough fuel for the rest of the journey, I strolled over
to the shop. It looked dark inside, though I could see lights on. I was
going to get a chocolate bar for my kid. Since work had kept me away all
weekend, I wanted to buy him something just to see his face light up.
When I drove in there were thinning rays of light, but darkness now
shrouded the station, almost like someone had draped a massive curtain
over the roof.

The door had one of those tinny bells which rattles around your skull
long after you’ve heard it. The lights glowing from the ceiling
struggled to illuminate anything, instead just emitting a low buzz.
Inside was instead lit by the lights from the gas station’s roof, though
the end of the shop was still in shadow. Aisles stretched along before
disappearing about 10 metres away. The darkness of the shop felt
different to the one outside but I couldn’t yet put my finger on why. I
walked across the display window toward the counter, pausing at the edge
of an aisle to pick up a chocolate bar—a “family size” Dairy Milk bar;
that should make my son happy. I tucked it into my coat and carried on
to the counter. It sat unmanned. There was a small bell to ring if you
needed assistance, similar to the tinny one above the door, but I
couldn’t bring myself to press it; I didn’t want to have another sound
rattling my skull, but I also felt… uneasy—like someone was watching me
from the darkness. Instead, I crept over to the self-service machine
that was beside the counter, only a few steps closer to the wall of
black. I quickly paid for pump 3 and my chocolate and turned around to
go.

I hurried toward the door, glancing down each aisle as I went. I
couldn’t make eye contact with the darkness, I was worried I may notice
something that stared back and instead looked at the shelves. Stock was
nearly falling off the edges here, which I felt strange for such an
isolated location. I pulled on the door. It was locked. How could it be?
I saw no one around and I heard no one walk behind me. I yanked on the
door again. My heart tightened and the air flew from my lungs. The top
half of the door was glass and had iron bars running down. I looked
along the shopfront and every window was barred. Were they always like
that? Dashing back to the counter, I snatched a chair. The metal scraped
against the floor, ripping through the silence of the shop. “If that
seemed loud, just wait for the glass to mash, you’ll think the world’s
ending,” I thought to myself. Holding two of the legs, I swung the chair
forward like I was chopping a tree. A kkrang split my ears and glass
rained against my boots. I grabbed an iron bar with one hand. It didn’t
budge. I tried slamming the chair into it to no avail. Behind me, deep
in the darkness, I heard something clatter onto the ground. It bounced a
couple of times and echoed a couple of times more. From the sound that
pinged down the aisle, I knew what it was. A key.

Down the second aisle—beyond the crisps, the biscuits and the cakes,
beyond the sweets and the island of healthy snacks—I could see a key
glinting at the edge of the shadow. Outside, my car stood by pump 3
walled in by darkness. The darkness out there had a slight grey shade to
it, but the one inside was utterly and entirely washed with black. All I
needed was the door key and I could drive away from this place. I began
to walk down the aisle.

I felt as if I was walking down a tunnel, not knowing if a train was
going to charge towards me at any moment. My echoing footsteps were
absorbed the closer I got to the darkness; they became muffled before
fading away completely. The buzz of the lights also disappeared. It was
a struggle to trudge through the heavy, thick air and it required me to
flail my arms like I was wading through water. My hand swiped a packet
of crisps off the shelf and it crashed to the floor but made no sound as
it landed. The key was only a few paces away, sitting a metre from the
wall of darkness. I knelt and stretched out one arm. The key felt stuck
to the floor. I reached out another arm and used my fingernails to get
leverage. The weight of the key made my nails feel as if they were
peeling off but I managed to pull it onto my palm and started my way to
the door.

I took a few steps and looked back—the shadow was closer now; for every
time I moved forward, it closed the gap. It smelled acrid and burned the
back of my throat. I broke out in a sprint trying to outpace the
darkness; as it chased me, I felt a hot wind that made my entire body
sweat. I reached the door and fumbled around the keyhole. Darkness now
engulfed me, gripping every part of my body as I slotted the key into
the lock. Acrid air stuffed into my lungs and I momentarily lost my
grip. I jerked myself forward, twisting the key and ramming my shoulder
into the door. Cold air washed over me as I fell onto the tarmac. The
darkness out here felt different, it felt natural. I ran over to my car
and looked back at the shop window. The shadow had retreated, like a
spider waiting for a fly to enter its web. I dropped into the driver’s
seat. There was a snap along the left side of my body, but I felt no
pain. I froze, before realising it was my son’s chocolate; I think I
would have preferred to break a rib than have to hand him a broken bar.
I don’t know what I interacted with that day, but I am sure it happened.
I can still feel the hot embrace every morning before my body fully
wakes up.
