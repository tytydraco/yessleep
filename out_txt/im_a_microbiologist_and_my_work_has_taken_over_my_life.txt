I need to write this down before I lose the nerve, or before I convince
myself again that there’s a scientific explanation I simply haven’t
found yet. There isn’t. I know that now.

My name is Dr. Paul Ellison. I am, was a microbiologist at Harwick
University. I have a wife named Carrie. I had a daughter named Maya.

She was seven. She liked the color yellow, and folding things out of
paper, and a version of tag she’d invented where whoever was “it” had to
close their eyes and count to twenty before they could start chasing.
She called it Blind Tag. She rewrote the rules constantly, usually
whenever she started losing.

I’m telling you about her because what’s in my basement is still wearing
her habits.

Part 1

It started as a soil sample.

I’d been doing field collections near the Arlen wetlands that spring,
routine biodiversity work, the kind of low-stakes sampling you delegate
to grad students until you need to pad a grant report. I almost didn’t
process the last set of cores myself. I was behind on three other
projects, and Carrie had asked that morning if I’d come to Maya’s spring
concert, which I answered in the way we both understood meant no.

I processed the samples that evening instead.

The fourth slide stopped me cold. Not bacterial. Not quite fungal,
though it shared traits of both. A colonial organism, clusters of cells
that seemed to coordinate, shifting in response to light and temperature
in ways that went well past ordinary chemotaxis. I sat there for two
hours and forgot to eat.

I called my colleague Yuen the next morning. He got excited fast,
started talking about bringing in a mycologist he knew at Stanford. I
told him to hold off, and said I wanted more preliminary data first.

That was a lie. I already knew what I had. I just didn’t want anyone
else’s name on it.

Part 2

The first mouse was Control A, eight weeks old, standard white lab
stock. I put a small sample of the organism into its enclosure as a
food-adjacent substance, sealed and monitored, and watched through the
observation port.

It didn’t behave like a pathogen. It spread slowly out from the dish,
and Control A, curious the way mice are, sniffed at it. Tactile contact
initiated, I wrote.

By morning, Control A was gone. No remains, no blood, nothing to suggest
a struggle. The organism had simply grown, spreading toward the center
of the enclosure, and I sat with that for a long time before I
understood what I was looking at.

Then it moved toward the food dish. It advanced in short bursts, paused,
withdrew slightly, advanced again. When I knocked on the port glass, it
froze.

It had absorbed a mouse, and learned to flinch like one.

Carrie asked where I’d been when I finally came up around three. I said
the lab. She gave me a look I’d come to know over nine years of
marriage, not angry, just tired in a way that was somehow worse. Maya
was already asleep. I stood in her doorway a while, watching her
breathe.

Part 3

There were fourteen trials in total, all following roughly the same
shape, introducing the organism to a new animal, document absorption,
log whatever behavior surfaced afterward.

The rats were what convinced me this wasn’t simple mimicry. After it
absorbed three of them over six days, the colony started clustering on
itself, sections of it contracting and releasing in slow rhythm, no
metabolic reason I could find for it. It seemed to attend most to the
parts that had fed most recently.

It was grooming itself. Taking care of itself, the way rats do for each
other.

It had learned to be a colony.

I moved everything to my basement not long after. I told myself it was
for continuous monitoring, since the department’s overnight staffing was
unreliable. That part was even true. Mostly I was afraid Yuen would walk
in one morning and see it before I was ready to show him.

Carrie didn’t love the containment units in the basement. She said a
bench was one thing, but this was another. I told her it was temporary.
I told her a lot of things were temporary that spring.

Maya asked what was down there. I said science stuff. She asked if it
was alive, and I paused a beat too long before saying it was
complicated. She looked at me with those enormous serious eyes of hers
and said, “I bet it gets lonely down there.”

I told her I had work to finish.

Part 4

The crow changed things.

I won’t say how I got it, since the person who helped me would lose
their license. What matters is I had it five days before it made contact
with the organism, and in those five days I watched it more closely than
I’d watched anything in months.

It would sit at the edge of its enclosure and look at me, not around the
room, at me, like it understood I was a specific thing, separate from
the chair or the wall.

When it finally touched the colony, it wasn’t forced. It walked up,
cocked its head, and tapped it with its beak, the way you’d poke at
something that had caught your attention.

Within three days of absorption, the organism was mapping. It moved
through its enclosure in a grid, section by section. When I dropped in a
small mirror, it approached, backed off, approached again, then turned
toward the observation port, reporting, almost. When I entered the room,
it tracked me across the glass.

It wasn’t only curious anymore. It was building a picture of its world,
and keeping it.

I started spending most of my day down there. Maya knocked on the door
once. I remember because I’d been awake long enough that the sound
startled me. “Daddy, will you come outside? You said we’d play Blind
Tag.” I told her I was in the middle of something, that I’d be out soon.
She said okay, and I listened to her feet go back up the stairs.

I never went out.

Part 5

Here’s the part I’ve stopped trying to explain with any science.

Around three weeks in, the crow’s mapping behavior was still there but
fading, layered under something new: loose, wandering loops. Doubling
back on itself. Stopping, waiting, changing direction for no reason I
could tie to foraging or territory. Movement that seemed to exist only
for its own sake.

I logged it as a novel behavioral emergence, possibly an artifact of
cross-species integration. I read that entry again a few days ago and
understood, finally, exactly what I’d been looking at and hadn’t let
myself see.

Part 6

Carrie told me later that Maya had stopped asking to come down. She said
she thought Maya had decided that whatever was in the basement mattered
to me more than she did, and that kids make peace with things adults
don’t, because they have to.

She wasn’t cruel about it. She said it the way you’d read off a lab
result, flat, past the point of anger.

I said I’d make it up to her. I said I just needed a few more weeks.

She looked at me for a long moment, then said she was taking Maya to her
sister’s for the weekend, and that I should think hard about whether I
still wanted to be part of this family, because from where she was
standing it didn’t look like it.

I said I’d think about it.

Then I went back downstairs.

Part 7

I don’t know exactly when it happened, and that’s the part I think will
stay with me the longest. That I was in the same house, and I don’t
know. There was a stretch of maybe forty hours where I barely left the
basement. Carrie’s sister lives two hours out. The weekend ran long.

When I finally came up, the house was too quiet.

I called out. Nothing. My phone had a dozen missed calls from Carrie,
starting Saturday afternoon and getting more frequent before stopping
around midnight Sunday. I’d had it on silent, and it had died sometime
in between.

I called her back. She picked up on the first ring.

“Maya didn’t come with me,” she said. “She wanted to try one more time
with you. Paul, where is she? Where’s our daughter?”

I went through the house fast, room by room, and then I went outside.

Part 8

The yard isn’t large. A garden bed on one side, a fence, the old oak in
the back corner where Maya once talked me into building her a “castle”
out of two boards wedged between the roots.

The colony had spread across most of the back half of the lawn. Low to
the ground, darker than the grass, catching the morning light with a
faint opalescent sheen at its edges. Bigger than anything I’d ever
contained downstairs. I have a guess about how it got out. I’m not ready
to write that part down yet.

It was moving. Loose, wandering loops. Doubling back. Stopping, waiting,
turning for no reason at all.

It was playing.

I stood there and understood it all at once: the mapping, the tracking,
the way it had oriented toward me every time I walked into that room.
For weeks I’d brought it the same thing daily, without meaning to. Her
voice through the door. Her feet on the stairs. Her knock.

I don’t know if she’s in there. I don’t know what “in there” would even
mean anymore, and I find I don’t miss having an answer the way I thought
I would.

When I walked to the edge of the colony and just stood there, unable to
do anything but stare at what I’d let happen, it stopped wandering.

It turned toward me.

And then, the way she used to, standing at the edge of the yard, waiting
for me to finish counting when it was my turn to be it, it went still.

It was waiting for me to close my eyes.

Part 9

It’s still out there. I haven’t called anyone. I don’t know who I’d
call, and I’m not sure I want to find out what they’d do to it.

Carrie’s on her way back now. I’m at the kitchen table writing this
because I need to do something with my hands.

If you work in a field where any of this means something, I have
documentation. Fourteen trials, full behavioral logs.

I don’t think I’ll be sending them to anyone.

I think I’m going to go sit in the yard until she gets home.
