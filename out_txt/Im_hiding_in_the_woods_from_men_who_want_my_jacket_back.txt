The impact almost bit my tongue in half. I hit the canvas tarp of the
flatbed truck hard, rolled over the wooden crates, and slammed into the
steel cab just as the truck swung onto I-95. My ribs are definitely
cracked, my left knee is twice its normal size, and I’m bleeding through
my jeans.

I’m typing this right now with a thumb that’s practically numb, sitting
on the floor of a rotting timber deer-blind a few hundred yards deep
into the woods. I managed to crawl up here fifteen minutes ago. I’m
using a single, blinking bar of weak cellular data that I can only catch
by holding my phone against the highest wooden beam of this roof. I
don’t know who else to tell, but I need to put this out there before the
signal drops or the black SUV finds where I hid.

If you ever go to the flea market on 4th Street in Philadelphia, don’t
buy anything from the old guy selling military surplus from the back of
a green van.

Three days ago, my friends invited me to a weekend hunting trip up in
the mountains. I didn’t own any proper rugged gear, so I went to that
market and bought a heavy, faded black canvas field jacket from that van
for forty bucks. It had a thick brass zipper, an old grease stain on the
left cuff, and a faded white stenciled serial number inside the collar:
V-1784. It felt warm, and I thought it was perfect for the woods.

This afternoon, the world went completely insane.

I was crossing the pedestrian bridge over the rail yard near Vine
Street, heading to meet the guys for the trip, when I noticed two men
walking thirty paces behind me. They wore identical matte-black
windbreakers, heavy boots, and earpieces. When I stopped to tie my shoe,
they stopped. When I looked back, the taller one brought his hand to his
collar, pointed directly at me, and said something into his microphone.

I didn’t think. I broke into a run.

They didn’t yell “stop.” They didn’t pull badges. They just sprinted
after me with terrifying speed. As I reached the end of the bridge, a
dark Ford Explorer with tinted windows blew through a red light, tires
screeching, blocking the exit ramp right in front of me. Two more guys
stepped out of the doors.

I was trapped on the overpass. The tall guy from behind was closing the
gap, his hand reaching inside his jacket for something heavy and
metallic.

I looked over the concrete railing. Below, a massive commercial flatbed
truck was hauling a load of covered cargo toward the interstate,
rumbling right underneath the bridge. I didn’t hesitate. I vaulted over
the concrete barrier and dropped.

The air tore out of my lungs when I hit the heavy canvas tarp. I
survived the fall, but my car keys and my wallet flew out of my pockets,
clattering into the dark grid of the highway below. I have nothing but
my phone, which I instinctively clutched in my hand.

I managed to crawl into a gap between the crates, shivering in the cold
wind, as the truck accelerated down the highway. I thought I escaped,
but looking back through the crates, I saw the Ford Explorer swerve onto
the highway entrance ramp behind us, its tires smoking as it began
tailgating the truck. They knew I was on it.

Soon, the truck slowed down as it approached the heavy lighting of a
toll plaza.

The bright halogen lamps flooded the area, and through the gap in the
wooden crates, I saw that same dark Ford Explorer pull up beside the
truck in the adjacent lane. The passenger window rolled down. The man
inside was staring directly at the truck bed, his eyes locked on the
space between the crates where I was hiding.

As the two vehicles moved side by side under the glare, the man leaned
slightly forward, and the overhead light hit his shoulder.

The material of his jacket was identical to mine. The same heavy, faded
black canvas. The same unique brass zipper. But what made my heart drop
into my stomach was his sleeve. Stenciled in bright, faded white ink
across his left arm was a serial number: V-1780.

The flatbed truck’s air brakes hissed as it pulled into the final toll
lane. Through the crates, I saw the Ford Explorer already idling by the
shoulder fifty yards ahead, waiting. Two men stepped out, their hands
resting on their holsters, staring at the back of this truck.

I had to jump again.

I rolled off the passenger side right into the dark drainage ditch
before the truck hit the main lights of the toll booth. I dragged my bad
leg into the wood line and managed to scramble up into this old
abandoned blind before my knee completely gave out.

I tried to take the jacket off ten minutes ago because I realized it has
to be the reason they are tracking me. There must be a localized
transponder sewn into the lining, or the fabric itself is treated with
something. But the temperature out here has plummeted. I’m already
soaking wet from the ditch, and when I took it off for just five
minutes, my fingers turned blue and I couldn’t stop violent,
uncontrollable shaking. Without it, I will die of hypothermia before the
sun comes up.

But if I keep it on, I’m a walking beacon. I can hear dirt bikes or ATVs
revving out by the highway shoulder now. They are starting to map the
perimeter of the woods.

I don’t know what kind of tracking technology is hidden in a heavy
canvas seam. If I stay here, I’m cornered. If I run deeper into the
freezing dark, I won’t make it till dawn.

Funny how fast it turns around. I bought this jacket to hunt. Now I’m
the one being hunted.

The engine sounds are getting closer. I can see flashlights cutting
through the trees below my wooden perch. My phone battery is low. I’m
using a sharp wooden splinter from this rotting blind to frantically
hack away at the thick collar lining, trying to feel for a hard plastic
lump or wire to rip out before I have to crawl down and drag myself into
the dark. I’m hitting post before I turn my screen brightness to zero.
Wish me luck.
