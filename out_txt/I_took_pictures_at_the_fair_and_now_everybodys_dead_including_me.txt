I’m not sure where I am right now, so please bear with me. I know it
sounds crazy, but after what happened I’m afraid I might be in some kind
of afterlife. I thought if I described what took place somebody might be
able to make sense of it before it’s too late. I’m an amateur street
photographer who likes to shoot on film. I guess you’d call me old
school. I use a 35mm Leica M3 camera that came out back in 1954, long
before anything digital was around, and I sleep on the couch because
I’ve turned my bedroom into a darkroom so I can develop my pictures. I
know that if I used a smartphone or a digital camera it would save me
the trouble of having a darkroom and I could see the results right away.
But using an old camera makes me feel like I’m shooting alongside great
street photographers like Henri Cartier-Bresson, Helen Levitt and Garry
Winogrand. I’m nowhere near as good as they were, of course, but their
work inspires me and the fact that I’m shooting the streets just like
they did makes me feel like I’m one of them. 

 Which brings me to what happened last Sunday when I saw the fair was in
town, and decided it would be fun to go take pictures. The fairgrounds
were already jammed with visitors by the time I got there. A rock band
was playing on a bandstand and people were riding the Ferris wheel and
the roller coaster and all the other rides. I could hear kids screaming
at the top of their lungs because they were so happy. They were
screaming so loud they were drowning out the rock band. Their parents
looked pretty happy too. There were long lines for fast food snacks like
hot dogs and hamburgers, and I saw plenty of kids walking around eating
cotton candy and candied apples. Everybody looked like they were having
a good time, which is why I was there. I wanted to capture that fun and
bring it to life on film. To freeze a smile forever, I guess. I took out
my camera and started shooting. I focused on the fairgoers as I wandered
through the fairgrounds, and I was glad that nobody minded me taking
their pictures. It gave me the freedom I needed to compose my shots for
the best possible results. By the time I was done I had shot three rolls
of 36-exposure film. 

 When I got home I couldn’t wait to see what I’d shot so I went into the
darkroom and started developing the negatives. It can take a while to
process 36 negatives, and once I had a contact sheet I would still have
to decide which ones I wanted to enlarge to create 5x7 or 8x10 prints
that I could add to my photo albums. But something went wrong somewhere
along the line, because when I looked at the contact sheet the images
didn’t look anything like what I’d seen. It wasn’t just that the joy I’d
seen was missing. The people had changed too. But 35mm negatives are
pretty small, and my eyesight isn’t the best, so I decided to enlarge a
few of the negatives so I could see what was really going on. And when I
did that, when I saw the images as they materialized in the developer
tray, I shuddered as a chill ran down my spine. Because all the people
I’d shot at the fair were dead. Men, women, parents, children, security
guards, musicians, clowns, everybody. They were dead. All of them. Their
bodies littered the fairgrounds or lay slumped on the rides and picnic
tables. My hands trembled as I held the prints. It was as I’d
photographed a nightmare, and I wanted to destroy the negatives and run
out of the darkroom. Instead, I decided to enlarge all the images to see
if they were all the same. But they were all the same. And all I saw was
a county fair filled with corpses. I tried to breathe but it was as if
the photos were suffocating me. How could this have happened? What had I
seen? What was I seeing now?

I decided that I needed to go back to the fairground and see if what I
was seeing in the photographs was what actually happened. But when I got
there I was astonished to see that the bodies were gone and the
fairgrounds were in ruins and littered with garbage. The roar of heavy
machinery filled the air. I could see workers wearing hardhats and
safety vests using bulldozers and excavators to tear down walls and
clear away the rubble. Dump trucks filled with debris ringed the
property. The roller coaster and the Ferris wheel had been dismantled,
and the sections lay in jagged, rusty stacks like some gigantic modern
art sculpture.

I waved to a grizzled worker who looked like a foreman and he waited
while I approached him.

“Where’s the fair?” I said.

The foreman took off his hardhat and wiped his brow, then looked at me
as if I was an idiot and said, “What fair?”

“I was just here, I took pictures,” I said.

“Not here you didn’t,” he said. “Last fair we had here was twenty years
ago, and the company went bust.” He cocked his head at the wreckage.
“Stuff’s been getting rusty ever since. “Gonna put up condos soon as we
get it all cleared out.”

“I don’t understand…” I said, as the ground shifted beneath my feet.

“Yeah, I can see that,” the foreman said, with a mocking smile.

Then he walked away and went back to work. A worker came up to him. He
cocked his head at me and I heard him say, “What was that about?”

The foreman shook his head.

“Said he was here today taking pictures at the fair.”

The worker laughed out loud.

“Takes all kinds, don’t it?” he said, as they went back to work.

My mind reeled as I looked around the fairgrounds. Where was the fair?
Where were the bodies? I could barely control the car as I drove home. I
left it in the driveway instead of putting it in the garage and went
inside. 

It was then that I realized that I had to see if the same thing would
happen with any other pictures I took. Maybe it was just the camera, I
thought, or maybe something happened at the fair. Either way, I couldn’t
explain it. I reloaded the camera with a fresh roll of film, set it on a
tripod and took a series of self-portraits using a cable to release the
shutter. But my heart stopped when the images appeared in the tray and I
saw that I was dead too.
