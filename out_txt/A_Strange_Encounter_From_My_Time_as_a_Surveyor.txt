We did the vast majority of our work during the day, mostly because it
was dark out there when the sun set. No artificial light for miles -
unless you counted our shoddy diesel-generator camp lights that were
unreliable at the best of times, and what little natural light we
would’ve gotten was perpetually strangled out by a near-constant cloud
cover. Even with the benefit of daylight, the snow fucked up our
visibility something fierce and one wrong move could lead to a series of
wrong moves that could then either lead to you sliding on your ass down
a sheer cliff or falling into some godforsaken ice pit to freeze to
death.

Well, at least the pay was good.

We were surveyors, working out of █████ at the time on a corporate
contract. The company, mind you, was American, but they themselves had
some sort of contract with the government out here to build a research
station in the mountains. We had the actual building plans, but we
weren’t really privy to what the actual purpose was. If I had to guess,
it was probably weather monitoring, maybe geological data. I worked on
the preliminary stages of a couple similar facilities before - albeit in
less isolated spots, and we did… mostly the same thing there. Map out
the site, take some altitude readings, gauge the general structural
soundness of the surrounding area, and send that data off to our
engineers. I could go into the specifics of it all, the software we
worked with, how to calibrate an altimeter, or how to properly utilize a
laser rangefinder to determine the distance between two distant points -
but I won’t bore you with that. Being bored with the specifics was my
job.

Ordinarily we’d use satellite imaging to simplify a lot of this stuff,
but █████’s national security was pretty tight and considering we were
technically international, that whole thing was no bueno. It wasn’t like
they were full-on isolationist black-out-the-map, but… not far off. Took
a lot of clearance for us to even get in here without too many
restrictions. A few of our team actually had to stay back home thanks to
minor infractions or nebulous red flags. You’d think that they wouldn’t
care quite so much about this kind of secrecy, considering their borders
expanded vastly in either direction - the product of countless subsumed
nations, eventually coalescing into a whole either by force or
desperation - and most of that land was completely empty save for snow
and rocks.

Well, it was no skin off my back either way. Just meant we had to do
more by hand.

For the most part, save for the occasional supply drop, we were on our
own. There were no roads - even the eventual facility was to be served
by helipad primarily - and the nearest city was maybe a hundred miles of
barren tundra away. The only people outside of our direct team that we
came into contact with were a number of indigenous ████ people, who
maintained a largely traditional, disconnected lifestyle explicitly
protected by the government. I’d quietly thought this was odd, as they
historically certainly didn’t care to protect the cultures of those who
at one time lived independently in places that would later cease to
exist, dissolved and assimilated and integrated into their great
economic machine. Maybe their ways were preserved as a form of cultural
pride, or maybe it was penance for some great tragedy - an unspeakable
evil inflicted upon them in times long past, having since been forgotten
and normalized by the march of time.

Well, the ████ were a strange people anyway. Skin and hair both as pale
as the moon - with eyes that didn’t stare, but lingered slightly too
long on whatever they were looking at. Curious and hungry. We didn’t
interact with them terribly often, but their villages dotted the
foothills and plateaus, and occasional contact was an inevitability.
They weren’t territorial or aggressive, mind you - for the most part
they just watched, their children particularly taking interest in our
strange equipment and puffy polyester jackets, their eyes watching in
awe from underneath the thick, flowing cloth that swaddled them as
though they were witnessing some great secret.

Among our number in the camp was a guide. This man was of their tribe,
but had left in young adulthood to pursue a life among the glittering
towers of the distant cities. He was in his middle ages now, but still
knew how to navigate the mountains like the back of his hand, those same
strange eyes working to great effect in discerning which identical
patches of snow contained either solid footing or a lethal drop beneath.
He was indispensable, and in the few instances we did have to
communicate with the native population, he would facilitate contact and
serve as a translator - the majority of them had no knowledge of the
English language, after all. He was a quiet man, though he was not
unfriendly, and often shared in food and drink with the rest of us after
a long day trudging through the snow.

On occasion, should the weather unexpectedly grow in intensity when we
were far from the camp, he would negotiate with the locals and have it
arranged for us to stay the night in one of their villages. As they
frequently traveled from village to village themselves, stopping and
resting as they traversed the wastes, each of their villages had a small
building to house wayward travelers. They were well-insulated with furs,
and the wooden walls were carved with strange, beautiful patterns.
Silent as ever, they provided us with food and drink. Largely dried
meats and an unfamiliar - yet pleasant - liquor that tasted sweet. Each
morning, our guide spoke to them in their language once more, and each
morning we left without issue.

It was one of these nights that I thought to ask him about his culture,
the culture of the strange mountain tribe that had silently watched us
for months now. Ordinarily, he did not like to discuss such things, and
I did not like to ask - but the liquor had seemingly loosened both of
our tongues that night. He told me two things. First, he explained that
they believed in a sort of magic. Not the kind you read about in fantasy
novels or the kind that a teenager would try to invoke in their house by
saying shit into a mirror - but an ancient magic, an evil magic that
flowed through all things of flesh and leaf and stone and could awaken
at any time. I asked what this actually meant in practical terms, and he
told me that it was the reason for their passive observance. They
believed they were particularly susceptible to its influence, and they
were always on alert for that which did not make sense - a sign of the
terrible thing.

Then, paradoxically, he told me something that - at least to me -
certainly did not make sense. He said that time did not matter to them,
and that their history, their folklore, were actively shaped and
remodeled by experiences in the present. To that, I said that most
people did that, because that’s just how culture works.

He shook his head. It was more than that. He gave an example, using one
of our colleagues - a safety guy named James who, at the time, was still
back at the base camp. He said that, if James were to walk into camp and
begin interacting with the locals, it would be as though James had
always been there. He would have an invented history, an active
integration into their stories, their childhoods, and maybe their
ancient past. Each member of the tribe would seamlessly integrate this
into their own history, a collective rewrite using knowledge of what
existed in the current time to supplement the past. Likewise, if one of
their villages were to be destroyed in a great avalanche, perhaps they
would rewrite history so that it never existed at all. Stricken from the
record, along with its denizens. Maybe they would weave a tale about how
the village was destroyed by an agent of the “evil magic”, a nameless
figure they would later contextualize as the next stranger to visit
town, who would be met with fear and violence. This, he claimed, was a
way of fooling the magic, which also did not care about time. False
histories would trick it, and keep it from interfering with their lives.

This just left me with more questions than answers. I asked him if he
still believed the same, and he laughed, telling me that “Time’s arrow
flies a strange path.” I think I honestly understood less than I did
before, because I didn’t know what that meant. Either way, I then asked
another question. I asked him, considering he had spoken to the
tribesmen, what role they had given us in their story. He said that I
would have to ask them myself, as they did not say - beyond the apparent
fact that they did not ascribe us the role of an adversary. We stepped
outside of the hut and he drew a few small symbols in the snow. Those
symbols, as he informed me, meant something along the lines of “Who
speaks the outer language?”, and that I may be able to find someone who
is able to speak English who could tell me.

I was still somewhat intoxicated, so maybe I was just ballsy at the
time, but I was curious enough now that I felt like I had to know. It
was dark out, suffocatingly so, but the village was aglow with the flame
of a large bonfire at its center, which a few of the strange tribesmen
lingered near still, drinking and watching the children run about in
circles. I approached them, and they stopped - all their eyes now
curiously examining me with that same bizarre look. I crouched down in
the snow and traced the same symbols the best I could, and their eyes
turned towards each other now, speaking softly in an odd tongue I’d only
heard from a distance before. A woman, the one closest to me, pointed to
a hut around the edge of the village, the windows alight with the
flicker of a fire still burning inside. I realized I didn’t have a way
to say “thank you”, so I hoped a brief nod would suffice.

I brushed aside the furs draped over the hut’s entrance and saw an
elderly man inside, crouched by the hearth, bundled in cloth marked with
more strange lettering. Whether he was worn by the elements, or just by
age, he looked impossibly old. Wrinkles creased like the waves and
trenches of a great ocean, framed by a beard white as the snow outside.
He turned to me, his head rotating slowly and with great effort. To my
surprise, the man smiled warmly - the first glimmer of emotion I had
seen from these people - welcoming me to sit in near-perfect English. I
did so, taking a seat on a fur laid out on the opposite end of the
hearth. I told him his English was very good, and asked him where he
learned it.

At this remark, a strange emotion - a mixture of nostalgia and
confusion - crossed his face, subduing his smile and settling his
rippling flesh for a moment. He told me that I had taught him English,
back when he was a young man. Well, that was obviously not correct,
because I didn’t know this guy and I’d never been here before. Never
even been close - this was my second time in Europe period and the first
time was a week in Italy for a wedding. He was also easily pushing 80
while I was 32, so that was off the table. I supposed, in the moment,
that this was my “story”, that someone had come to the village, taught
him English, and left. Now, I was filling that role.

I decided I would play along, and I pushed a few more inquiries towards
the old man. Why did I teach him English? Why was I in the village?
Maybe I was being an asshole, “culturally insensitive”, if you will, but
this was slightly amusing and I thought that if I kept asking more
specific questions, eventually he would trip up. To my surprise, he did
not. He told me that I came to the mountains as a traveler, seeking
refuge from the oppressive night. I stayed with his tribe for a time,
and taught them my own tongue as a means of payment - though none was
necessary. Though most of his peers were now long-gone, the
proliferation of English having largely disappeared with it, he had
never forgotten the language - as I had told him a story in English
once, before I vanished back into the darkness from whence I came, and
he did not wish to forget it.

I asked him what the story was, and his expression changed again, his
smile fading into a more downcast, imperceptible emotion. Something I’d
never seen before. He told me that he couldn’t tell me the story,
because I had made him promise to never tell it again. I asked why, and
he just said that, when I told him the story, I did not like the ending.
Neither did he. It was a terrible ending, and one that both of us wished
to forget. He smiled again, sadly this time. He was happy that I had
forgotten, but he never had. I apologized, which in hindsight was insane
because I had never told this guy a story in my life, but he told me
that there was no need. Someone needed to know the ending, and he was
just happy to see me again after all these years.

I sat with the old man a while longer, listening as he recounted various
other stories from “my” time at the camp - all unfamiliar and
impossible. None of them were the story. Eventually, the hearthfire
began to die down, and I bid the old man farewell. As I pushed aside the
furs that sealed out the frigid mountain air, he said something very
strange. He told me that he wished I brought my dog along with me this
time, because it was a very friendly and beautiful dog that danced and
played with the children in the snow and he very much would’ve liked to
see him again, much as he was hoping to see me again before he passed. I
didn’t know what to say to this, so I just apologized again. I told him
that the dog was old, and could not make the trip. He understood, and I
went back to my hut, where I slept and left the next day. A month later,
we left █████ altogether, and I never saw the old man again.

The thing is, I never had a dog. Not as a kid, not as a 32-year old
surveyor. Not until 8 years later, when my wife would bring home a Husky
pup from an animal rescue. A pup that grew into a very friendly and
beautiful dog that very much liked children, and very much liked to
dance and roll in the snow on cold winter nights, when the moon was high
in the sky and the snow was gently falling, a thousand glimmering flakes
matting his fur.

Strange coincidence, that’s all.
