My jaw is locked completely shut, and the skin across my cheeks is
hardening into smooth, cold plastic. I know why. I looked directly into
the painted eyes of a display mannequin in an abandoned retail stockroom
because I wanted a better thumbnail for my channel. I am typing this
with one finger on my bedroom floor, and every time I swallow, I feel
rigid resin scraping against my throat. My face burns and tightens as it
freezes into a solid shell.

I went to the Mercer Street store because the TikTok comments demanded
it. I had seven videos of abandoned retail, sixty thousand followers,
and the algorithm was starving. I walked in with my ring light and my
gimbal, completely confident that nothing in an empty building could
hurt me. I told myself it was just an empty shell of a building. I told
myself the mannequins were just fiberglass and dust.

The mannequin was in the back stockroom, facing the door. That is not
where they keep them, but I did not think about that until later. I
filmed her. I stepped closer. I looked directly into her painted eyes
because I wanted a dramatic angle for the camera. Four seconds. That is
all it took to seal it. I felt a sudden drop in temperature, a freezing
pressure against my cheek from a hand made of dry ice. When I pulled
back, the skin was already bruising, a perfect ring of white frostbite
spreading from my jawline.

My roommate David does not believe in ghosts or curses or anything he
cannot touch. But when he saw the fingerprints on my cheek yesterday
morning, he stopped eating breakfast and went to his room. He has not
come out since. I know what actual frostbite feels like. I have been
there. This is different. The marks are a direct transfer. The plastic
is bleeding into my skin, or my skin is bleeding into it.

The spread started at midnight. First my teeth locked together. The pain
was real and it never stopped. Then my jaw went rigid. Then my
collarbone hardened into solid resin. I can still move my arms, but they
creak with the stiff, grinding friction of old plastic wheels. At 2:03
AM, David knocked on my door. He had heard the sound of me trying to
talk, the low grinding noise my locked jaw made when I tried to call his
name. He looked at me through the crack and his eyes went wide. He said
others had come through this apartment before. They all went to the
Mercer store. They all came back with smiles that did not reach their
eyes. He did not tell me where they are now.

The footsteps outside are getting louder. I can hear her now. It is the
sharp click of plastic heels on hardwood, followed by the drag of
something heavy. She is outside my door. She has been standing there for
the last seven minutes. The metal doorknob is frosting over. My vision
is starting to go white at the edges. My only fear is what I will become
after she is done. David told me before he went silent that every person
who goes to that store eventually finds themselves standing in a window
somewhere downtown. Posed. Facing the street. Waiting for the next
person who looks too long.

I can feel her fingers on the other side of the door now, plastic
pressing against wood. My hands are almost useless. My typing is down to
one finger and the cursor is jumping. The cold is spreading up my neck,
locking my vocal cords into rigid tubes of polymer. 2:14 AM. She is
inside the room. Her face is right next to mine and her painted eyes are
open. My eyelids just snapped open to match hers, and I can feel my arms
lifting to pose. I am going to stand in the window downtown, and I am
going to smile until my plastic face cracks.
