I’ll be honest, I didn’t notice them that much when I first moved here.
Far more concerned with getting in on time, what my boss thought of me,
you know, stupid work stuff.

But they’re everywhere. I see them scampering across the lines and
ducking underfoot as they dart for a crack in the station’s wall. The
cost of living is bad enough for us let alone those little critters.

They’re always squabbling over the measliest crumb or rummaging through
the often-toppled bins. God I’d hate to be one of the late-night
cleaners.

It was even a little funny seeing one drag away a full pizza slice or
watching this particularly large one square up to someone’s yapping dog.
You’d almost admire their nerve.

Those squeaks and shrieks in the evening, sometimes they get to me. I
know they’re only small but there are so many. I even checked under my
bed one time, when I swore I heard scratching.

They’re like a wave, some unseen sea of rodents scurrying in the depths
of our city, picking it clean.

Their own kind isn’t even off the menu. Gaunt little corpses dragged
away into alcoves, picked clean before you’ve even had time to look
away.

I know they’re starving, man some of us are, but they’re taking risks,
getting just a little too bold. They don’t even flinch when around
people, they just wander through the crowds, eyeing up their next meal.

He was just a bum, some homeless guy off his rocks on whatever
substances were in circulation, a heap on the cold tiles. He’d been
sleeping down there for some time, probably passed out after shooting
up.

Getting off the train, the human wave seemed to break around him, some
giving disgusted looks, others completely unaware. I was curious, I
don’t think I will be next time.

It was hard to see through the swaths of commuters, and I was already
cutting it short for my next train, but I stopped. There was movement
beneath his tattered clothes, then a small ball of brown shot up, its
face stained with a deep red.

I looked up and caught his eye. Just one, wide and glassy, the light
behind it slowly fading. But he saw me.

He was maybe half a man by that point, still sputtering, but helpless.
They’d gnawed through part of his face, most of the right side and a
portion of his shoulder. His tongue and jaw hanging limply as he spat
and mouthed some sort of helpless plea.

Most of his lower left leg was exposed through his ripped jeans. I’d
initially mistaken the bulging shapes beneath the skin for muscle, but
they were moving.

Glistening polished white reflected the fluorescent overhead lights as
another mouthful of tiny teeth returned to work.

A train screeched to a halt behind me, snapping me out of it. The doors
opened and another wave of commuters flooded the already cramped
platform. I didn’t have the luxury of time, turning and fighting through
the crowd for a seat.  

I knew they’d be back once we’d dispersed, who wouldn’t want the free
meal.

The next morning there wasn’t even a skeleton. Ripped fragments of
clothing and a dark smear disappeared between the tracks, leading off
into the deeper tunnel system.

I can only hope he went out on his own terms.

Packs scurried those platforms, hoovering up whatever scarps were on
offer or taking advantage of whatever vulnerable soul walked the broken
tiles.

By that point the bins were overflowing and I recall hearing a guy at
the ticket office say something about missing staff.

There were lots of temporary workers, people who didn’t speak that much
English, but even they were in and out in days.

I don’t blame them; the rumours were starting to seem more believable.

Something big, big enough to cause some service workers to down tools
and vehemently refuse to go in there at any cost.

Finally, our government, the saints they portrayed themselves as, closed
off the stations for a day. An investigation maybe, but the signs said
maintenance, though maintenance never required forearms.

One day turned into two, then a week. Men in plastic yellow suits
wandered in and out, spraying some kind of gas, supposedly in an attempt
to clean them out.

Then, all of a sudden, they simply opened the stations back up, and not
another word was spoken.

Obviously, most people who valued their mortality found other means of
transport, but not everyone is so lucky.

I try not to go down there, especially when there’s low foot traffic,
but that’s not it. I’ve seen more of them recently.

Putting out my bins, there they were picking through my neighbour’s
garbage, not even raising their heads when I approached and in broad
daylight.

Small gangs diving in and out of the storm drains, dragging who knows
what. Even behind the restaurant across the street, I could hear the
owner shouting and swatting them away.

I tried that too, waved a stick at one that just stood in the middle of
the walkway, on my commute home. It should have run, shouldn’t it? I’m
no giant, but it didn’t seem phased.

They’re not contained and nobody seems to care or even acknowledge the
problem. No new stories, government notices, even the people in my block
refuse to talk about it.

I’m not blind. I see them every day now. But I suppose we’re all
programmed to avoid the things we’d rather not see.

There was a guy on the corner outside my flat. Used to see him most
mornings, begging for whatever spare change people could manage. Haven’t
seen him in a few days.

Saw something coming out of the storm drain yesterday, though. If I’d
have squinted harder, it could have been him. All skin and bone, picking
through the rubbish for even a morsel.

But I didn’t stop.

Why would I?

Nothing new.
