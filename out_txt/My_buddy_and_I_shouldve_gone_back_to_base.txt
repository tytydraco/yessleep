That’s the part I keep coming back to.

We’d already spent most of our day off wandering around Eastern State
Penitentiary in Philadelphia. We’d been on orders for the better part of
a week, and this was one of the few days where neither of us had
anywhere we needed to be. We could’ve gotten dinner. Gone back to the
hotel. Had a few beers and called it a night.

Instead, we were sitting in a car in a parking lot outside of Eastern
State when my buddy pulled out his phone.

“You know there’s another place like an hour and a half from here?”

I looked over at him.

“Another what?”

“Old building. Supposed to be haunted as shit.”

That should’ve been the end of the conversation.

Instead, I asked, “Where?”

He turned his phone toward me.

Rolling Hills Sanatorium. New Amsterdam, Pennsylvania.

I’d heard of that place before, although I couldn’t remember where.
Probably from one of those paranormal shows I used to watch. You know
the kind; some dudes screaming at thin air when there’s an unexpected
bump. The use of a radio scanner to hear ghosts. Night vision, that
hurts your eyes after a while. Silly practical effects. Spooky
soundtrack. The location was certainly interesting enough. It was an
enormous red-brick hospital surrounded by woods, with long wings
radiating outward from a central building.

According to the first couple things we found online, Rolling Hills had
opened in 1874 and operated for more than a century before finally
closing in 1985.

That was enough for us.

We punched the address into my GPS and headed west.

The drive wasn’t anything special at first. We bullshitted about work,
complained about traffic, and vibed to my playlist. Normally I’d have
been a little embarrassed by my bizarre collection of classic rock,
oldies, movie soundtracks and jazz, but apparently my buddy’s taste was
just as fucked as mine.

Eventually, the highways got smaller.

The scenery changed.

Pennsylvania is beautiful once you get away from the cities. Thick woods
covered the hills around us. Old houses appeared between the trees.
Every now and then we’d pass through some little borough that looked
like it had been sitting there since the nineteenth century.

Then, seemingly out of nowhere, we hit New Amsterdam.

I had expected a small town. But New Amsterdam was a city.

Not a particularly large city, mind you, but a good-sized city
nonetheless. Dense neighborhoods stretched away from the highway. There
were old brick commercial buildings, apartment blocks, churches, and
giant municipal buildings. Railroad tracks cut straight through the
place, complete with an old passenger station near downtown.

It reminded me of some of the old mill cities back home in
Massachusetts.

Fitchburg. Gardner. Worcester. Lowell. Places like that.

Except those cities made sense to me.

You could look at the old mills and immediately understand why thousands
of people had settled there. Even after the industry disappeared, the
bones remained.

New Amsterdam had the bones. I just couldn’t figure out what had built
them.

There wasn’t a major university that I could find. No military base. No
amusement park. No giant shopping district. No obvious modern industry
supported a city of its size.

There wasn’t even much tourism.

It was just there.

“This place is bigger than I thought,” my buddy said.

“Yeah.”

I looked at the brick buildings passing outside my window.

“What the hell does everyone do here?”

He shrugged.

At the time, it was nothing more than an observation.

We weren’t there to see New Amsterdam.

We were looking for Rolling Hills.

That should’ve been easy.

The place had once housed thousands of people. From what little we’d
read, the main hospital complex was enormous. There were multiple wards,
administrative buildings, workshops, employee housing, farmland and
something like six miles of underground tunnels.

You don’t exactly hide something like that.

Except New Amsterdam seemed strangely uninterested in telling us where
it was.

There weren’t any billboards advertising ghost tours.

No signs directing tourists toward the old hospital.

No museum that I could see.

Not even one of those little brown historical signs on the side of the
road.

That struck me as strange.

I’m from Massachusetts. I’ve spent enough time around Salem to know what
happens when a city realizes people will spend money to look at
something creepy. If Rolling Hills had even half the reputation the
internet gave it, I expected somebody in New Amsterdam to be making
money from it.

Instead, if I hadn’t already known Rolling Hills was there, I could’ve
driven straight through the city without ever realizing it existed.

My GPS eventually told me to turn right.

We left the busier part of New Amsterdam behind and started climbing
into a residential neighborhood on the outskirts of the city.

The houses gradually spread farther apart.

Then came the trees.

Another turn.

Then another.

My GPS told me we were two minutes away.

“See anything?” I asked.

“Nope.”

One minute.

Still nothing.

Then the GPS told me to turn onto what looked like an old service road.

I almost drove past it.

The pavement was cracked and uneven, with weeds growing through sections
of it. Trees crowded both sides, their branches hanging over the road.
An old utility pole leaned toward the woods nearby.

My buddy stared down at his phone.

“This can’t be right.”

“Google says it is.”

“That’s reassuring.”

I turned onto the road.

“You think Jason is gonna jump out at us?” I joked as we drove down the
road. It climbed gently uphill through the woods.

There were signs that something substantial had once existed back there.
Sections of old fencing appeared between the trees. Utility poles
followed the road. At one point we passed the remains of a concrete
foundation almost completely swallowed by brush.

But there was no hospital.

Then my GPS announced:

You have arrived.

I slowed to a stop.

Nothing.

Trees to the left.

Trees to the right.

More cracked pavement ahead of us.

“Well,” I said. “That’s fucking helpful.”

My buddy laughed and started screwing around with Google Maps, trying to
figure out whether we’d entered the wrong address.

I kept driving.

Maybe fifty yards farther down the road, we found the gate.

Unlike almost everything else we’d passed, it wasn’t falling apart.

A chain-linked fence stretched across the road. It disappeared into the
trees on either side. Barbed wire ran at the top of it.

A sign was bolted to the loosely chained and padlocked gate. It read:

PRIVATE PROPERTY

NO TRESPASSING

I stopped a few yards away.

“Guess that’s it.”

My buddy leaned toward the windshield.

“Can you see anything?”

Other than the gate, barbed wire fence and trees, I couldn’t see shit.

Whatever remained of Rolling Hills was somewhere beyond the fence.

There was another sign attached to the gate. Smaller. White. No company
logo or government seal. Just a telephone number and a warning that the
property was under video surveillance.

Then my buddy pointed.

“Camera.”

I followed his finger.

Mounted high on a utility pole beside the gate was a modern security
camera.

Its lens was pointed directly down the service road.

Directly at us.

I stared at it for a second.

Then shrugged.

“Let’s head back.” My buddy said.

There was nothing particularly strange about the whole situation.
Abandoned buildings are dangerous. Property owners don’t want idiots
like us wandering around and falling through floors. And neither of us
had any intention of trespassing.

So I backed up, turned around, and we tried to find our way back to
town.

There had to be one.

We went back the way we came.

Eventually we came to where the service road met the turnoff from the
neighborhood. But it was blocked by cones and a road block.

Shit.

So we went back, and found another service road, but that led back to
another fence.

This one had concrete barriers.

We tried another.

Another route took us toward a government medical facility on what
appeared to have once been part of the greater Rolling Hills property.

Blocked again.

By then this had stopped being fun.

Not scary.

Just annoying.

We’d driven an hour and a half to look at an abandoned hospital, and
apparently the closest we were going to get was staring at trees from
behind a fence.

Eventually we spotted an employee walking toward one of the buildings.

My buddy looked at me.

“You wanna ask?”

“Fuck it.”

I pulled onto the side of the road.

I approached the guy and gave him a wave.

“Hey, sorry to bother you sir.”

He stopped.

“What’s up?”

“We’re looking for Rolling Hills.”

Something about his expression changed.

Not dramatically.

He didn’t freeze. He didn’t suddenly start acting like a character in a
horror movie.

He just looked at me differently.

“Rolling Hills, huh?”

“Yeah. The old hospital. We’re sightseeing.”

He glanced past me toward my car.

My buddy was still sitting inside.

Then he looked back at me.

“That’s private property.”

“Yeah, we figured that out. Is there anywhere you’re allowed to see it
from?”

“No.”

“Nothing?”

“This is private property, buddy.”

There wasn’t any hostility in his voice.

If anything, he sounded uncomfortable.

I thanked him and walked back to the car.

“What’d he say?” my buddy asked.

“Private property.”

“No shit.”

“Apparently that’s the official slogan of New Amsterdam.”

He laughed.

I put the car in gear.

At that point, we both agreed we’d wasted enough time.

We’d seen Eastern State.

We’d taken a nice drive through Pennsylvania.

Rolling Hills was a bust.

Whatever.

There was still one important item left on our itinerary.

Beer.

We drove through the parking lot to the facility and out onto a road. On
our way back to town, we found a bar and decided to grab something to
eat before making the drive back.

That was where things started getting weird.

Not at Rolling Hills.

Not on the service road.

Not when we found the cameras.

At the bar.

Because apparently two strangers asking questions about Rolling Hills
were considerably more interesting to the people of New Amsterdam than
I’d expected.

And once they found out that we came from Massachusetts, they became
very interested

The bar was maybe ten minutes from Rolling Hills. Though, calling it a
bar probably doesn’t do it justice. It was one of those modern breweries
that seemed to have popped up in every old industrial town in America
sometime during the last fifteen years. Exposed brick. Black steel
beams. Edison bulbs hanging from the ceiling. A row of enormous silver
brewing tanks stood behind glass at the far end of the room.

There were board games stacked on shelves near the entrance. Jenga.
Cards Against Humanity. Monopoly. And a couple others I’d never heard
of.

The place smelled like hops, fried food, and fresh pretzels.

Exactly what we needed.

We grabbed two seats at the bar.

My buddy ordered an IPA called Rainbow Farts or something. I picked
something dark off the menu without really knowing what it was, then we
split one of those giant Bavarian pretzels with beer cheese.

For the first twenty minutes, Rolling Hills didn’t come up.

We talked about work.

Bitched about stupid shit. Like traffic and that one jerkoff at drill
who stuck his nose in everyone’s business.

We even made fun of each other’s beer.

Normal shit.

Eventually the bartender came back around.

“You guys passing through?”

I looked down at myself.

We were both in civilian clothes, but apparently we stuck out like a
sore thumb.

“Sort of,” I said. “We’re from Mass.”

“Boston?”

“Yeah.”

“What brings you all the way out here?”

“Work.”

My buddy took a drink.

“We’re Army.” He said.

That seemed to satisfy him. I kicked him under the bar table. He winced
and shot me a look.

“Fort Indiantown Gap?”

“Dix.”

The bartender nodded with a whistle and started wiping down the counter.

“How’d you end up way out here in New Amsterdam?”

I laughed.

“Bad decisions.”

My buddy pointed at me.

“Her idea.”

“Fuck you. You found the place.”

The bartender looked between us.

“What place?”

“Rolling Hills.”

His hand stopped. But only for a second.

Then he continued wiping the counter.

“Rolling Hills, huh?”

“The old asylum.”

A guy sitting two stools down looked over.

He’d been watching a baseball game on one of the televisions above the
bar. Older guy. Maybe late fifties. Gray beard. Ball cap. Half-finished
beer sitting in front of him.

“You went up there?” He asked.

“We tried,” I said.

That got another man’s attention farther down the bar.

The bartender put the rag down.

“Today?”

“Yeah, like twenty minutes ago.” My buddy added.

The guy with the beard turned on his stool.

“You get inside?”

I laughed.

“No. That place is locked down tighter than Fort Knox!”

Nobody laughed.

There wasn’t an awkward silence or anything dramatic. It was subtler
than that. The bartender looked at the guy with the beard. The guy with
the beard looked at us.

My buddy noticed it too. He nudged my knee with his, as if to say ‘this
is weird.’

“We weren’t trespassing or anything–” he trailed off.

“Good,” the older guy said.

He turned back toward the television. It was the Phillies versus the
Astros. I looked at my buddy. He gave me the slightest shrug.

The bartender poured another beer for someone at the other end of the
counter.

“How’d you hear about Rolling Hills?” He asked conversationally.

“I’ve heard about it before,” I said. “Paranormal shows or something.”

“Which one?” Asked the bartender.

I thought about it. But the name was on the tip of my tongue.

“No idea.” I said, after a long pause.

That bothered me more than it should have. I could picture the episode;
green night vision, guys walking around dark hallways. One of them, the
guy with a gothic t-shirt and crazy hair, losing his shit because
something touched his shoulder. The usual ghost-hunting stuff.

But I couldn’t remember the name of the show.

My buddy pulled out his phone.

“I found it when we were leaving Eastern State.”

The older guy looked back again.

“Eastern State?”

“Yeah.”

“The old prison in Philly?”

“Unless there’s another one.”

He stared at us for another second.

“So you were in Philadelphia, looked up Rolling Hills, and drove here?”

“When you put it like that, it sounds stupid.”

The bearded man smiled.

“Long way to drive to look at an empty building.” He said.

“An hour and a half isn’t that bad.” I said, remembering the ride we
took getting down here.

“No,” he said. “Suppose it isn’t.”

The bartender set my beer down in front of me.

I hadn’t ordered another one.

I looked at it.

He nodded toward the older guy.

“His.”

“Appreciate it.”

The man raised his glass. I raised mine back.

“Eight years in the army. How long have you two been in?” He said.

“I’ve been in six and a half.” My buddy said. The bearded man looked at
me.

“Ten.” I said.

That broke whatever weird tension had settled over us.

For a while, anyway.

We started talking.

His name was Tom. He’d lived in New Amsterdam for most of his life.
After his stint in the army he worked for the city for thirty-something
years before retiring. The bartender had grown up there too. Once they
realized we weren’t idiots planning to cut through the fence with bolt
cutters, everyone relaxed.

Mostly.

“You should’ve been here ten years ago,” Tom said.

“For what?” I asked.

“This place.” He gestured vaguely around us. “New Amsterdam.”

“What about it?” My buddy asked.

“It used to be packed.” The bartender said.

I looked around the brewery.

There were maybe ten people inside.

“Seriously?”

“Every October. Couldn’t even park downtown.” The bearded man chuckled.

The bartender laughed. “He’s not exaggerating.” He said.

“Halloween?” I asked.

“Oh yeah, ghost tours, block party. All sorts of people would come.
They’d come from everywhere.”

Tom pointed toward the wall behind us.

I turned around.

There were old photographs hanging between advertisements for trivia
night.

I’d barely noticed them when we walked in.

One showed the street outside absolutely packed with people. Orange
lights were strung between buildings. People wore costumes. Vendors had
tents set up along the sidewalks. A banner stretched across the street.
I couldn’t make out what it said from where I was sitting. Another
photograph showed a group of people posing in front of a huge brick
building.

I knew it was Rolling Hills.

Unlike the pictures we’d seen online, the building wasn’t abandoned. Not
completely. Floodlights illuminated the facade. There were people
everywhere.

“Holy shit,” my buddy said.

“That was here?”

“Yep.”

“What happened?”

Neither of them answered immediately.

Tom took a drink.

“Things change.”

The bartender suddenly became very interested in arranging glasses. I
looked back at the photograph.

“What, people just stopped coming?”

“Pretty much.”

“Why?”

Tom looked at me.

“Covid changed things. Tours stopped. Amd the hospital closed–”

“Yeah, in 1985.”

That got his attention.

Not much.

Just enough.

“You’ve been reading.”

“A little bit.”

“Then you know enough.”

I laughed.

“I know it was an asylum and apparently haunted as shit.”

Tom didn’t laugh.

“That’s about all you need.”

My buddy looked over at me. There it was again. That same feeling I’d
gotten talking to the employee outside the medical facility. There was
no hostility. Nor was it fear exactly. It was more like we’d brought up
something impolite. I pointed toward the photograph.

“So they actually let people inside?”

“Used to.”

“Did you ever go?” My buddy asked.

Tom smiled at that.

“Everybody went.”

The bartender shook his head.

“Not everybody.”

“Everybody worth knowing.”

“You’re full of shit, Tom.”

“Probably.”

I laughed.

“What was it like?”

Tom stared at his beer. For the first time since we’d started talking,
he seemed to actually think about his answer.

“Big.”

“I gathered that.”

“No.”

He looked at me.

“Big.”

Something about the way he said it made the hair on my arms stand up.

My buddy leaned forward.

“You ever go in the tunnels?”

The bartender stopped arranging glasses. Tom looked at him.

“Who told you about the tunnels?”

“It’s online.”

“Okay.”

“Six miles, right?”

Tom rubbed his thumb along the side of his glass.

“Something like that.”

“What are they for?”

“Service tunnels.”

“Can you still get into them?”

The bartender finally cut in.

“Not anymore.”

My buddy looked at him.

“Its private property.” He said.

I laughed.

“There it is.”

“What?”

“That’s what everyone keeps telling us.”

The bartender smiled, but it looked forced.

“Probably because you’re not supposed to go there.”

“Fair enough.”

Tom finished his beer.

He put some cash on the bar and stood.

For a moment, I thought he was leaving.

Instead he walked over to the photographs.

He pointed at the one showing the Halloween festival.

“That was 2015.”

I stepped closer.

Now I could read the banner.

ROLLING HILLS HAUNT WEEKEND

October 23–25, 2015.

“What happened in 2016?” I asked.

Tom looked at the picture for a long time.

Behind us, someone dropped a glass.

It shattered. A couple people gasped. The bartender shouted something
sarcastic. Normal bar noise swallowed the moment. Tom shoved his hands
into his pockets.

“Bad year.”

“How bad?”

He looked toward the windows. The sun was getting low outside.

“You boys staying around here tonight?”

“Nope, we gotta go back to base.” I said.

“Good.”

That answer irritated me. Not because he’d said anything rude. Because
everyone in this town seemed determined to answer every question with
another reason not to ask it.

“What happened?” I asked again.

Tom sighed.

“Kid went missing.”

That shut me up.

My buddy lowered his beer.

“Did they find him?”

Tom shook his head.

“No.”

“Jesus.”

“The whole town looked. Cops. Fire department. Volunteers. People came
in from three counties.”

He glanced back at the photograph.

“They found somebody.”

I waited.

“Just wasn’t the kid.”

“Who?”

Tom picked up his jacket.

For several seconds he didn’t answer.

Then he smiled.

Not a happy smile.

The kind you give somebody when you’ve decided the conversation is over.

“You boys enjoy your beer.”

And that was it.

He walked out.

I watched him through the window until he disappeared down the sidewalk.

My buddy leaned toward me.

“What the fuck was that?”

“No idea.”

The bartender appeared in front of us.

“Another?”

I looked at the half-full beer Tom had bought me.

“I’m good.”

My buddy shook his head.

The bartender nodded.

Then, before walking away, he looked directly at me.

“He’s right, though.”

“About what?”

“You guys should head back before it gets dark.”

I smiled.

“Jason comes out after sunset?”

He didn’t smile back.

“No.”

He set down our checks without us saying that we were closing out our
tabs. Then he walked away.

I looked at my buddy. He shrugged. And that should’ve been it. We
finished the pretzel. Finished our beers. Paid, and went back to the
hotel.

The next morning, my buddy slept in. It was Sunday. That meant we’d take
our flight home tomorrow.

One more day before our orders ended.

And I couldn’t stop thinking about what Tom had said.

I tried scrolling through anything else for a while. News. Messages.
Instagram.

Every few minutes, I found myself back on Google.

Rolling Hills.

They found somebody.

But who?

I searched Rolling Hills again.

Then I searched for the missing child.

Then 2016.

Then the tunnels.

Then old maps.

I don’t know how long I sat there on my phone.

Long enough for my gas station coffee to go cold.

Eventually I found something I hadn’t seen the day before.

An old service road.

Different side of the property.

No gate visible on Street View.

Probably nothing.

I’m sure everyone reading this is going to tell me to leave it alone.

You’re probably right.

But I drove an hour and a half yesterday and never even saw the damn
building.

I’ve got one more day in the area.

Fuck it.

I’m going back.
