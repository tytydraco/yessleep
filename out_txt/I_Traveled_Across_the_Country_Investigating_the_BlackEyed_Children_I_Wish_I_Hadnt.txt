I had always been a seeker, drawn to the kind of stories that left
others either spellbound or shaking their heads in disbelief. After more
than a decade of chasing leads across the country, I had built a career
out of finding the uncanny and peeling back its layers. But the thrill
that used to come with unraveling mysteries had slowly dulled over the
years. Stories that once felt electrifying had begun to feel hollow.
Another headline about haunted houses, another exposé on supposed alien
sightings. It was all starting to blend together, losing its magic.

My office was a clutter of old case files, yellowing newspapers, and the
stale scent of burnt coffee. Each morning, I sat behind my desk, staring
at an inbox filled with pitches and PR stunts masquerading as tips,
nothing that could light the fire I remembered from the early days of my
career. Sometimes, I would catch a glimpse of myself in the reflection
of my computer screen, eyes tired, a weariness etched into my face that
no amount of caffeine could shake. I felt like a shadow of the reporter
I used to be, chasing echoes rather than real stories.

It was on a Tuesday afternoon, during one of those particularly restless
moments, that the email arrived. It came without a subject line, the
sender listed simply as “Anonymous.” Normally, I would have dismissed it
as spam, but something made me click. The message was brief, almost
cryptic: “Black-eyed children. They’re real. People need to know the
truth.” Attached were a handful of addresses, spread across several
states, and a link to an online forum where people shared their
encounters, accounts that read like something out of a fever dream.

I leaned back in my chair, a slow smile forming. The black-eyed children
were a story I knew only in passing, a whisper among the urban legends
that haunted small-town America. Children with eyes as dark as pitch,
who appeared on doorsteps at night, asking in cold, mechanical voices to
be let inside. There were hundreds of stories, all with the same eerie
details: the way people felt an inexplicable sense of dread, how the
children’s voices seemed devoid of emotion, how their presence lingered
long after they’d vanished. It was the kind of story that could be
easily dismissed, and yet there was something about it that wormed its
way under my skin.

The idea of setting out on the road again, of chasing something unknown,
stirred something in me that had been dormant for far too long. I
remembered the long stretches of highway, the thrill of the unknown
waiting beyond each bend, the quiet nights in strange towns where every
shadow seemed to hold a secret. It was those moments that made me feel
truly alive. There was a realness in these stories, something raw,
unsettling, and distinctly human in the fear that each account conveyed.
Maybe this was the story that could bring back the passion I had lost. A
story that wasn’t about clicks or ratings, but about confronting the
things that lurked at the edges of human understanding.

The next morning, I packed a bag with the essentials… a battered
notebook, a digital recorder, and a camera. I also packed extra clothes,
some snacks, and a first aid kit, knowing this trip would be long,
spanning multiple states. I took a moment to stare at the corkboard
hanging over my desk, the one I’d filled with reminders of past
investigations… old photos, cryptic notes, a map with pins scattered
across it. With a deep breath, I pulled down everything, clearing the
board for something new. Something real. Something that might, finally,
bring back the sense of purpose I’d lost.

The road stretched ahead of me like a promise. I tried to keep the
skepticism intact, I’d learned the hard way that jumping headfirst into
belief could lead to embarrassing dead-ends. But even as I kept my guard
up, curiosity tugged at me. I couldn’t help but wonder: what if there
was something to all of this? What if, behind the urban legend, there
was a sliver of truth? And what if that truth was something truly
unsettling, something that could change the way people saw the world?

The first destination was a small town in Ohio, where a man named
Richard claimed he had met the black-eyed children on his porch one
summer night.

I arrived in the town just as the afternoon sun was beginning to sink.
It was one of those places that seemed suspended in time, rows of modest
houses, a single diner, and a gas station that looked like it hadn’t
changed since the 1950s. Richard’s house was on the outskirts, a small,
unassuming structure with peeling paint and a front porch that creaked
under my weight as I stepped onto it. I knocked on the door, and a
moment later, it opened to reveal a man in his late fifties, his eyes
wary but curious.

“Richard? I’m Alex Carter,” I said, extending my hand. “Thanks for
agreeing to meet with me.”

He took my hand, giving it a firm shake before gesturing for me to come
inside. “Come on in. It’s not often I get visitors who want to hear
about… well, about that night.”

The living room was small, cluttered with books and old photographs.
Richard motioned for me to sit on a worn armchair while he settled into
the couch across from me. For a moment, we made small talk… about the
town, the weather, the kind of polite conversation that strangers use to
bridge the gap between unfamiliarity and trust. I could see the tension
in his shoulders, the way his fingers tapped lightly against his knee,
as if he were still deciding whether or not to share what he knew.

After a few minutes, I leaned forward, my notebook open on my lap.
“Richard, I know this isn’t easy to talk about. But I’d really like to
hear about what happened that night. Whatever you can remember.”

He sighed, his gaze drifting to the window, where the light was
beginning to fade. “It was about two years ago,” he began, his voice
low. “Middle of summer. I was sitting out on the porch, just enjoying
the quiet. It was late, maybe around midnight. I remember it was one of
those nights where the air felt thick, you know? Like a storm was
coming, but the sky was clear.”

He paused, his eyes narrowing as if he could see the scene playing out
in front of him. “That’s when I saw them. Two kids, standing at the edge
of the yard. A boy and a girl. They couldn’t have been more than ten,
maybe twelve years old. At first, I thought they were lost. They were
just… standing there, not moving, just watching me.”

“What did you do?” I asked, my pen hovering over the page.

“I called out to them,” Richard said. “Asked if they were okay, if they
needed help. They didn’t answer. They just started walking toward me,
real slow. And that’s when I noticed their eyes.” He swallowed, his gaze
dropping to his hands. “They were black. Completely black. No whites, no
color. Just… darkness.”

Richard’s voice wavered as he continued. “I don’t know how to explain
it, but the moment I saw their eyes, I felt this… this dread. Like every
instinct I had was screaming at me to get away. They stopped at the
bottom of the steps, and the boy… he said, ‘Mister, can we come in? We
need to use your phone.’ But there was something wrong with his voice.
It was flat, like he was just repeating words he’d heard somewhere.”

I nodded. “What did you do then?”

“I got up and went inside. Locked the door. I didn’t even say anything
to them, I just knew I had to get away. I watched from the window as
they stood there for a few more minutes, and then… they just turned
around and walked back into the dark. Disappeared like they were never
there.”

Silence hung between us. I could see the fear still etched in Richard’s
face, the way his eyes seemed to search the room as if expecting those
children to appear again. “Have you seen them since?” I asked.

He shook his head. “No. But I still think about them. Every night, I
make sure all the doors are locked, all the windows shut. I don’t know
what they were, but they weren’t just kids. There was something… wrong
about them. Something that shouldn’t exist.”

I let out a slow breath, the weight of his words settling over me. This
was more than just a story… it was a glimpse into something unknown,
something that defied explanation. And as I looked at Richard, I knew
that whatever lay ahead on this journey, it was going to be far darker
and more complex than I had imagined.

I thanked Richard for his time, his story still echoing in my mind as I
made my way back to my car. The sun had fully set by then, the sky a
deep indigo, and a chill had settled over the town. I drove slowly
through the quiet streets, until I reached the small hotel I had booked
for the night. It was a modest place, the kind of roadside inn with
flickering neon signs and an office that smelled of old carpet.

The clerk at the front desk barely looked up as I checked in, handing me
a key attached to a heavy brass fob. My room was on the second floor,
overlooking the empty parking lot. I dropped my bag on the bed and sat
down, the springs creaking beneath me. The weight of the day settled in…
Richard’s fear and the vivid image of those black eyes.

I took a deep breath, trying to shake off the unease that clung to me. I
knew I needed rest, but sleep felt like a distant possibility. Instead,
I pulled out my notebook and began to write, capturing every detail
Richard had shared, every emotion that had flickered across his face.
The words flowed easily, as if by putting them on paper, I could somehow
make sense of the inexplicable.

Eventually, exhaustion began to pull at me, and I set the notebook
aside. I lay back on the bed, staring at the ceiling, the dim glow of
the parking lot light seeping through the curtains. I closed my eyes,
willing myself to sleep, knowing that tomorrow would bring more
questions, more stories, and perhaps, if I was lucky, a glimpse of the
truth hidden in the shadows.

The next day, I stayed in Ohio and went to meet Don, a night-shift
worker in a small-town factory. Don reluctantly agreed to meet me at the
factory where he still worked, a partially abandoned place with only a
handful of workers present during the graveyard shift. The factory
loomed dark and cold as I arrived late at night.

Don was already waiting for me in the break room… a cramped, dimly lit
space with a flickering fluorescent bulb overhead. He looked jittery,
his eyes darting around as if he expected something to emerge from the
darkness at any moment. I introduced myself, and we exchanged a few
awkward pleasantries before I set up my recorder and notebook.

“I appreciate you meeting with me, Don,” I said, trying to put him at
ease. He nodded, his hands fidgeting on the table. The room was filled
with the low hum of machinery from somewhere in the factory, the sound
almost drowned out by the crackling of an old radio in the corner.

“So, you wanted to hear about… what happened back in 2010,” Don began,
his voice barely audible. “I don’t like talking about it, but… I guess
it’s time.”

I nodded, encouraging him to continue. Don took a deep breath, his gaze
shifting to the door as if making sure it was still closed. “It was
late, around two in the morning. I was working the night shift, and I
stepped outside for a smoke. The factory’s a quiet place at night, you
know? Nothing but the sound of the wind. But that night… I saw them. Two
teenagers, standing across the street, just barely visible under a
flickering streetlight.”

He paused, swallowing hard. “They were just standing there, staring at
me. At first, I thought maybe they were lost, or just messing around.
But something about them…” He trailed off, shaking his head. “It didn’t
feel right. They crossed the street, slow, and the closer they got, the
worse I felt. It was like this… dread. I couldn’t move. I couldn’t even
look away.”

My pen hovered over the page as I listened, the tension in the room
palpable. “Did they say anything to you?” I asked.

Don nodded, his eyes wide and unfocused. “Yeah. The boy… he asked me for
a ride. Said they needed to get home. But his voice… it wasn’t right. It
was flat, emotionless. Like he was reading from a script. The girl, she
just stared at me. Her eyes…” He shuddered. “They were black. Completely
black.”

The hairs on my arms stood up, the description all too familiar. “What
did you do?”

“I managed to tell them no. I forced myself to move. I turned around and
went back inside, locked the door behind me. My dog was with me that
night… he always comes with me to work, but even he refused to go
outside. He was whining, barking at the door like he knew something was
wrong.”

Don’s voice cracked, and he rubbed a hand over his face.

“I watched from the window. They just stood there, staring at the door.
They didn’t move, didn’t say anything. They just… stood there. It felt
like hours before they finally turned around and walked away,
disappearing into the night.”

A heavy silence settled between us, the hum of the factory suddenly
seeming much louder. “Has anything strange happened since then?” I
asked.

He nodded slowly. “My dog… he’s never been the same. He’s skittish now,
barks at empty doorways, refuses to go outside at night. And me…” He
looked down at his hands. “I still feel it sometimes. That dread. Like
they’re still out there, waiting.”

I thanked Don for his time, trying to ignore the chill that had settled
over me. As I packed up my gear and left the factory, I couldn’t shake
the feeling of being watched. The parking lot was empty, save for a
single flickering streetlight. Beneath it, I saw a dark figure… too far
away to make out clearly, but unmistakably staring in my direction.

I looked behind me for a second, and as I turned, the figure was gone.

I shook my head, telling myself it was just my imagination, the fatigue
and the power of suggestion playing tricks on me. I climbed into my car,
my hands trembled slightly as I turned the ignition. The engine roared
to life, and I drove away, the factory fading into the distance, but the
unease staying with me.

Fast forward a few days later, there was this interview.

In Vermont, at a remote farmhouse surrounded by dense woods.

The farmhouse belonged to Martha and Greg, an older couple who had lived
in Vermont for most of their lives. They were waiting for me in their
cozy, dimly lit kitchen, the only sound being the crackling of a
wood-burning stove. Martha greeted me warmly, though there was a tension
beneath her smile, an anxiousness that seemed to linger in the air.

Greg sat quietly at the table, a mug of tea in his hands. His eyes were
distant, haunted, as if he was replaying something over and over in his
mind. Martha took a seat beside him, her hands folded on the table as I
set up my recorder and notebook.

“Thank you both for speaking with me,” I began, offering them a
reassuring smile.

Martha nodded, her gaze dropping to the table. “It was the winter of
2016,” she started, her voice shaky. “There was a blizzard that night,
one of the worst I can remember. Greg and I were here, in the kitchen,
just trying to keep warm. It was late, when we heard a knock at the
door.”

She paused, her eyes flicking to Greg, who remained silent. “It was
strange… Even before I opened the door, I felt this… fear. It didn’t
make sense, but it was like I knew something was wrong.”

Greg nodded slowly, his expression grim. “She hesitated. I remember
telling her it was probably someone needing help. I mean, in a storm
like that…”

Martha swallowed, her hands trembling slightly. “I opened the door, and
there they were. Two children, a boy and a girl. They couldn’t have been
more than eight or nine, just standing there in the snow. They weren’t
shivering or anything. They asked if they could come in, said they
needed to use the phone. But their voices…” She shook her head, her eyes
closing briefly. “They were wrong, emotionless.”

I leaned in, my pen scratching across the page. “Did you let them in?”

Martha nodded, her eyes welling with tears. “I did. I don’t know why, I
didn’t want to, but it was like I couldn’t say no. They came in and sat
right there on the couch.” She pointed to the worn piece of furniture in
the corner of the kitchen. “That’s when I saw their eyes. They were…
black. Completely black.”

Greg spoke up then, his voice rough. “The power went out almost
immediately. The whole house went dark except for the fire. I remember
feeling so tired, like I could barely keep my eyes open. My nose started
bleeding, and I just… I couldn’t move.”

Martha wiped at her eyes, her voice breaking. “They didn’t say anything
else. They just sat there, staring at us. After what felt like hours,
they stood up. The boy said, ‘Our ride is here.’ They walked out the
door, and I watched them get into a black car that was waiting at the
edge of the woods. I couldn’t see the driver… just the car’s headlights
disappearing into the snow.”

The room fell silent, the only sound the crackle of the fire. I could
feel the fear that still lingered between them, the way it had seeped
into the walls of their home. “Has anything happened since then?” I
asked.

Greg lifted his sleeve, revealing a series of dark bruises along his
arm. “These started showing up not long after. And our health… it’s been
getting worse. We’re always tired, always feeling like something’s
wrong.”

Martha’s voice dropped to a whisper. “They weren’t children. They were
something else. And I think… they left something behind.”

I thanked them for their time, my own unease growing with every word
they spoke. As I packed up my gear and prepared to leave, I heard it… a
faint sound from outside, like children laughing, carried on the wind. I
tried to dismiss it, to tell myself it was just my imagination, but the
chill settled deep in my bones.

Before I stepped out the door, Martha touched my arm, her eyes serious.
“Once you know about them, they know about you. Be careful, Alex.”

The wind howled as I made my way to the car, the sense of being watched
prickling at the back of my neck. I started the engine, and drove away,
the farmhouse fading into the distance.

A few more days later, there was the last interview.

In a quiet nursing home from Virginia, where I met Harold, a frail man
in his eighties, one of the earliest known witnesses of the black-eyed
children. I found him in a small, sunlit room, the soft hum of the
oxygen machine beside his bed a constant reminder of his age and
frailty. His eyes, though clouded with age, held a sharpness that hinted
at the memories he carried.

“Mr. Harold, thank you for agreeing to meet with me,” I said, settling
into the chair beside his bed. He nodded slowly, his gaze distant, as if
he were looking at something only he could see.

“I’ve heard you had an encounter… a long time ago,” I prompted, my
recorder already running. Harold’s lips parted, his voice barely more
than a whisper as he began.

“It was the summer of 1950,” he said. “I was just sixteen. I remember it
like it was yesterday, though. We were living on the farm back then, and
it was a quiet evening, just after sunset. I was out by the fence,
getting ready to head back in for supper, when I saw him… a boy, leaning
against the fencepost.”

Harold paused, his hand trembling slightly as he adjusted the tube of
his oxygen. “He looked… different. His clothes were old-fashioned, even
for back then. Too neat, too clean. And his eyes…” He trailed off, his
breath catching for a moment. “They were black. Empty. Like looking into
a well at midnight.”

A shiver crawled up my spine. “Did he say anything to you?” I asked.

Harold nodded, his eyes widening slightly. “He asked me to walk him
home. Said he was lost. But there was something… something wrong about
him. His voice, the way he spoke. It didn’t feel right. I felt… like I
couldn’t think straight, like my own thoughts were slipping away.”

He swallowed hard, his frail hands clutching the blanket. “I ran. I
don’t know where I found the strength, but I turned and ran back to the
house. And as I did, I heard him. He called my name.” Harold’s eyes
locked onto mine, and I could see the fear still there, even after all
these years. “But I never told him my name.”

“Did you ever see him again?” I asked.

Harold’s gaze drifted to the window, his expression hollow. “Not in
person. But he came to me in my dreams. For years, I would wake up in
the middle of the night, and he’d be there, standing next to my bed.
Just staring at me” He shuddered, his frail frame trembling. “I don’t
think he ever really left.”

Silence hung between us, the hum of the oxygen machine the only sound in
the room. I could feel the tension, the fear that had haunted Harold for
decades, still lingering like a shadow that refused to fade.

I thanked Harold for sharing his story, the weight of his words pressing
down on me as I left his room. That night, I checked into a nearby
motel. I sat at the small desk, reviewing my notes, the soft glow of the
lamp the only light in the room. The stories were beginning to overlap…
different people, different places, but the same dark eyes, the same
inexplicable fear.

The clock read 1:00 AM when I heard a soft knock on the door. My heart
skipped a beat, the sound cutting through the silence of the room. I
approached cautiously, my pulse pounding in my ears. Peering through the
peephole, I saw nothing but an empty corridor.

A chill ran down my spine as I heard my name being called from behind
the door. Harold’s words echoing in my mind: “He called my name, but I
never told him what it was.” I stood there for a moment longer, before
slowly stepping back from the door.

I returned to the bed, but sleep didn’t come easily. The sense of being
watched lingered long into the night.

The next morning, I received a new lead.

I received an anonymous tip about a ghost town in the Midwest, a place
that had reportedly been visited frequently by the black-eyed children
before it was abandoned. The town was now deserted, an eerie, forgotten
place, swallowed by silence and decay. I knew I had to go there. Maybe
this would be where I could finally piece everything together. Maybe
here, I would find the answers I so desperately sought, or at least some
semblance of closure.

Fast forward a few more days.

The journey was long and lonely, the landscape gradually shifting from
flat fields to a barren expanse, dotted with the skeletal remains of
once-bustling buildings. By the time I arrived, the sun was already low
on the horizon, casting an orange glow over the dilapidated structures
that lined the empty streets.

The town was deathly quiet, the kind of silence that seemed unnatural,
even oppressive. The wind howled through broken windows, sending shivers
down my spine. There were signs of a hasty departure, doors left ajar,
belongings scattered across floors, like everyone had simply up and left
without a moment’s notice. My footsteps echoed as I walked, the crunch
of gravel and broken glass the only sound.

I made my way down the main street, past houses with boarded-up windows
and peeling paint, until I reached a modest house that seemed relatively
intact compared to the others. The door creaked as I pushed it open,
revealing a living room frozen in time, old furniture covered in dust,
family photographs hanging crooked on the walls, and a layer of grime
coating everything.

I searched the house, room by room, looking for any clues, anything that
might explain what had happened here. It was in one of the upstairs
bedrooms that I found a small journal, half-buried under a pile of old
clothes. I picked it up, brushing away the dust, and flipped it open.
The pages were filled with neat, careful handwriting, and I sat down on
the edge of the bed, the journal heavy in my hands as I began to read.

The entries started innocently enough, descriptions of daily life,
mentions of family dinners, mundane worries. But as I read on, the tone
began to change. The entries became more erratic, the handwriting
shakier. The writer spoke of children appearing at night, knocking on
doors, asking to be let inside. Each time they appeared, the sense of
dread grew stronger, the fear almost palpable in the words scrawled
across the pages.

The final entry stopped me cold. The writing was hurried, the letters
slanting wildly across the page: “They’re here again. We made a mistake
letting them in. We can’t leave. They won’t let us.”

My fingers trembling as I traced the words. Then, I heard a sound.

Knocking, echoing through the empty house.

My heart pounded in my chest, the journal slipping from my hands and
landing on the floor. I froze, straining to listen. I turned my head
slowly toward the window, and what I saw made my breath catch in my
throat.

Children. Standing in the street, their faces turned up toward the
window. Their eyes, black, depthless voids, stared directly at me,
unblinking. I felt an icy wave of fear wash over me, my limbs going
numb. I had to move. I had to get out.

Panic surged through me, and I grabbed the journal, stumbling to my
feet. The knocking followed me as I ran… down the hallway, down the
stairs, through the door. It echoed in my ears, relentless and
demanding. I burst out onto the street, my eyes darting around, but the
children were gone. The street was empty, the wind howling through the
abandoned town.

I hurried to my car, my hands shaking as I fumbled with the keys,
finally managing to unlock the door. I climbed in, slamming the door
shut, my heart still pounding as I started the engine. The headlights
cut through the darkness, illuminating the empty road ahead, but I
couldn’t shake the feeling that I wasn’t alone.

As I drove away, the ghost town disappearing in my rearview mirror, the
journal sat on the passenger seat beside me, its pages filled with the
desperate words of someone who had seen what I was only beginning to
understand. There were no easy answers, no clear explanations, only
fear, only the feeling that whatever I was chasing was now chasing me,
too.

The decision to return home came almost involuntarily, a feeling deep
within me that I couldn’t shake. The weight of everything… the
interviews, the faces of the witnesses, the black voids of those
children’s eyes… it was becoming too much to bear. I needed to be
somewhere familiar, somewhere that might offer even a hint of safety. So
I drove, through the night, the road leading me back to the place I
called home.

Eventually, the fatigue became too much. I spotted a rest area, just a
small clearing with a couple of picnic tables, a restroom, and a handful
of parking spaces. I pulled in, the tires crunching over gravel, and
parked beneath a flickering light. The clock on the dashboard read 2:47
AM. I leaned back, closing my eyes for just a moment, hoping to clear my
mind, to shake off the oppressive fear that had wrapped itself around me
since I left that ghost town.

The car’s interior grew colder, the chill seeping in through the
windows, and an unsettling sense of dread filled the space. Slowly, I
turned to look out the passenger-side window, and my heart stopped.

Two children stood there, their faces inches from the glass. They looked
no older than twelve, and their eyes, completely black voids, seemed to
swallow the light. My body frozen in place, unable to move, unable to
look away. One of them, a boy, opened his mouth, forming silent words. I
couldn’t hear them, but I knew what they were saying.

“Let us in.”

My mind flashed back to a story I had read years ago, a Texas journalist
named Brian Bethel describing a similar encounter in 1996. He had been
approached by two black-eyed children in the Texas Hill Country, their
cold, flat voices asking for a ride. He had felt the same overwhelming
dread, the same instinct to refuse them. It was one of the earliest
documented cases, an encounter that had haunted him ever since.

The fear gripped me, my hands trembling as they rested uselessly on my
lap. The boy raised a hand and knocked on the window.

“What do you want?” I managed to croak out.

The girl, her face expressionless, leaned closer to the glass. Her eyes,
black as coal, seemed to bore into me. “We just need a ride,” she said,
her voice flat and cold. “Please, mister. Let us in.”

I shook my head, my breath catching in my throat. “No… I can’t…” I
muttered, my words stumbling over each other. The dread was
overwhelming, a primal fear that screamed at me to stay away from them.

The boy’s knocking grew louder.

Then something warm touched my upper lip.

I wiped at it without thinking and looked down at my fingers.

Blood.

My nose was bleeding.

It poured over my mouth and chin in thick drops, splattering onto my
shirt. At the same time, pressure built behind my eyes until it felt
like someone was pushing their thumbs into my skull from the inside.

The boy stopped knocking.

He looked at the blood on my hand.

And smiled.

That scared me more than anything else he’d done.

I squeezed my eyes shut and kept whispering, “No. No. No.”

By the time I opened them again, the children were walking away, their
small figures retreating toward the edge of the rest area.

At the edge of the darkness, I saw a black car, barely visible, with no
lights on. The children walked straight to it, and the back door opened.
They climbed in, and the car rolled away, swallowed by the night.

The fear remained. I could still feel the weight of those eyes on me,
the void that seemed to pierce right through my soul.

I fumbled with the keys, my hands shaking as I turned the ignition. The
engine roared to life, and I pressed down on the accelerator, the tires
skidding slightly on the gravel before finding traction. I drove away
from the rest area.

I didn’t stop. I couldn’t. I drove through the darkness, the road
stretching endlessly ahead, and I never looked back.

Some time later, back home, I tried to write the story, but the words
wouldn’t come easily. The interviews, the strange events, the black-eyed
children… it all felt like a haze, a nightmare that I couldn’t shake. I
sat at my desk, the cursor blinking on the blank screen, and the weight
of everything pressed down on me. I felt different now, like a part of
me had been left behind in that abandoned town or had been taken by the
child at the rest stop. There was a distance between me and the world, a
fog that clouded my thoughts, making it difficult to focus.

I had always prided myself on my ability to put fear into words, to take
the inexplicable and make it real for others. But now, the fear was too
close, too personal. Every time I tried to write, I saw those eyes
staring back at me, black, unblinking, void of any humanity. I could
hear the knocking, feel the cold creeping into the room, and the words

The clock on the wall ticked steadily, the sound echoing through the
stillness of my apartment. I glanced at it… it was almost 1:00 AM.

A soft knock at the door startled me. It was faint, barely audible, but
it was there. My eyes fixed on the door.

Another knock. I swallowed, fear prickling at the edges of my vision. I
stood slowly, the chair scraping against the floor as I pushed it back.
I took a step forward, then another, my eyes never leaving the door.

The knocking came again, steady, unyielding. My hand trembled as I
reached out, my fingers hovering over the doorknob.

I could feel pressure building behind my eyes, just like I had at the
rest stop.

My hand closed around the doorknob, the metal cold beneath my palm. I
took a deep breath, and then I turned the knob. The door creaked as it
began to open, the darkness beyond seeming to spill into the room, and I
felt the chill wrap around me, pulling me in.

And then… “Mister, can we come in?”
