Part 1: Intentional Isolation

I had actually meant to record my time here earlier, but I’ve always
been a bit of a procrastinator with my side projects. Part of my journey
was to fly somewhere far from home and give myself zero distractions
while I worked on various illustration projects. The hope was to learn
to STOP procrastinating. My active project was a children’s book, but
it’s not been going well. So far, all I have to show for it is 2-dozen
starting outlines and rough character sketches. I can never figure out
where to begin when starting a story. Eventually I will think ahead to
the parts I want to draw or write and burn-myself out with the reality
of how much time it would take me to get there. As a result, I went out
for walks pretty often. Here in Adara county in Ireland, you are pretty
far from anything even closely related to cities. The people tend to be
older and ever working.

With tiny populations, folks remember your face a bit more easily, and a
head nod and wave is common, dare I say, expected. To the credit of the
Irish though, I had not gotten any suspicious looks and people tend to
be more curious than cautious. In retrospect, they probably assume I’m
from Spain or one of the other EU countries, given my darker skin. 

People are chummy too, some might think overly so. In the United States,
the dense over-population tends to make people distant. There’s too many
people to get to know everyone, and everyone has places to get to. This
is especially true if you are from a major metropolitan city like Los
Angeles, Seattle, or Manhattan. Maybe I’m being too nice, but I never
thought of Americans as rude. In an odd way, they just respect your time
enough to not bother asking you about it. Here in Ireland, people seem
genuinely curious about what you’ve been up-to. Men in work-clothes stop
what they are doing to catch up with someone they likely knew their
whole lives. Small talk helps the day move on and folks like to see
regulars. This suited me, as so much of my professional career was
talking, project planning, and convincing people to work with me. I
don’t consider myself outgoing, but conversation always came easy to me.
You’d be surprised how much mileage you can get by just asking
questions.

“What’s been your recent obsession? Oh, I don’t think I am familiar with
that, tell me more about it? Is that anything like this other similar
thing I know about?” Like that.

Ireland feels so small sometimes, and I hope my forgetfulness to wave at
familiar faces or chat down every familiar local the way everyone else
does, wasn’t seen as rude during my time there. 

Up until recently, I have been telling curious strangers and shop owners
that I’m trying to find out if the quiet life was for me, because I’m
older now, this is a decent enough lie. The truth was, I wanted to come
here and quietly work on art. The big 36th birthday was coming, and
having spent my whole life working to support my creative hobbies, I
decided to quit, spend some time to find myself, and make a children’s
book.

I was not this honest with the locals though. The reason I don’t tell
people the truth is a bit complicated, but I will try to explain… Have
you ever told a friend’s friend that you are an artist? If so, do you
remember the face they made? For me, the usual response was, “that’s
cool…” maybe you even show them some art on your phone and they become a
bit more genuine. But for me, the layer under that polite smile is
filled-in with projected thoughts of, “ah-ok, probably a dead-ender, a
clerk somewhere probably.” No one has ever said that to me. I have
always been quick to put that bit of self-trivia behind whatever
tech-management job I was in at the time. I’m self conscious, that’s all
there is to it, and all there is to say about it.

Now, it’s time to talk about an odd event that took place during my time
in Adara county. 50 days ago, when I arrived at the Inn after nearly 14
hours of travel (flights, buses and short walk included) the manager of
the place, Ben, offered to give me a small tour while a younger employee
took my bags upstairs to my room. I was too polite to say no to the
tour, and as a result I don’t remember much of it. What’s was worth
remembering about Ireland, is that it’s VERY old. Things have been here
for a very long time, and no doubt Ben was showing me ancient pictures
and paintings of this Inn. I made an attempt to feign interest, but I
found things that genuinely impressed me. The floor seams were hand
etched to look like a rough wall of cobbles at your feet. In one room,
there was a second floor you couldn’t get to.

I spent the next month in this tiny town. I’m tempted to fill the rest
of this writing time talking about Ireland. I could tell you about how
far the green fields could roll. I can tell you about the huge pockets
of trees where locals say the fairies live. I can tell you about the pub
food and the locals who treat a pint after work like a ritual. I can
tell you about how exposed to the weather the country is and just how
gusty and wet a day can get. I’d only ever scratch the surface. For now,
I will skip ahead to when things become curious. 

Part 2: a different kind of dark.

When I wasn’t secluding myself in my room desperately trying to draw, I
spent time walking the river path by the town. I had learned about it
from a local at an artist meeting I found a flyer for. Part of my
self-help journey was also to limit my phone dependency. So, I didn’t
have an E-sim. If I needed to make calls, I used the hotel wi-fi, but
when I was out in the town, I was refreshingly out of service. I say
this to explain just how lost you can get in small towns without a maps
app. When was the last time you drove somewhere unfamiliar without your
GPS?… The things we’ve grown dependent on.

Normally this wasn’t a problem. Planning was a big part of my working
life, so I can make a solid itinerary when I need to, but when you make
a wrong turn or you step out of a place through another exit, not having
an infinite resource can add extra time you didn’t expect to small
things. 

Thankfully, I had only gotten turned-around twice while on this walking
this path before. For my fellow Americans who might not know. Certain
parts of the world have their sunsets insanely early during some parts
of the year. Imagine everything being dark when you leave home at 7am,
and the sun has set nearly at 5:30pm, just as you are leaving work. It
was nearly 5:20pm when I decided to turn around and head back to the
hotel. It would be 20 minutes of walking. I don’t dislike the dark, but
in my experience, there’s better things to do than walk aimlessly around
towns at night. I don’t drink, so an evening pint wasn’t on my agenda
either. It’s better to be indoors when it’s dark.

In some parts of Ireland, there’s towns that see little to no traffic,
some don’t have street lights either. So when it’s overcast and
moonless, as nights in Ireland tend to be, it can feel oppressively
dark. I clung all the more carefully to my landmarks. 

“Okay, there’s that house with the LEGO flowers in the window seal,
good.”

“Turn the corner and here’s the cross the stone bridge. The sound of the
water is nice.”

“There’s no sound yet, but the town center is just up ahead, yes,
there’s the Moynihan’s Pub. I reached around the corner and grabbed the
window seal with my right hand, as if slinging myself around the bend,
an old childhood habit. The warm light and noise from the pub felt
comforting. Note to self, get their Fish and Chips again, or maybe their
pub house burger.”

“Walk across the minor round-a-about and go left. Turn at the charity
shop. Almost there.”

I know these sound like mad rambles, but it helps me to work through my
steps like a checklist. I did come to a stop though when I heard a
bottle break. In most of the towns, buildings are aligned. On the same
street, you will pass businesses and homes alike. But there’s always
small nooks and alleys. I had guessed that, during the seasons where it
poured more than it drizzled, these were paths that served not only
shortcuts, but passages for water to get to drains and eventually
rivers. The US cities have alleys too, but those usually have some light
going through them from the nearby street lights. Most of the town I was
in didn’t have street lights, so the alley where I heard the noise was
pitch black. I continued. I didn’t hear any moan of distress, or scream,
so whatever was over there didn’t concern me. 

“Was I going the right way?” I still had a reflex to reach for my phone,
but remembered it wouldn’t help me. I kept walking and retraced my
landmarks. Eventually I turned the final corner and found the Inn.

From my second floor view, I could get a look at another street that led
to the town center. I spent some time in the safety of my room,
occasionally turning off the lights and seeing if, after enough staring
outside, I can see better. My eyes weren’t up to the training though. I
could make out a white door on a grey house, but not much else. So much
for trying to see in the dark.

As a form of self-improvement. I tried to spend more time walking in the
dark. I have never been a night owl. From drunk swings from strangers,
to upsetting run-ins with police, there are hundreds of reasons to leave
the night outside. When I leave a cinema and it’s dark out, I get a bit
shaky, and I want that feeling of fear to go away. So, I mapped out
directions that would begin at town square, and loop around the town. 

When I say “town square”, you may imagine a series of parking spaces,
plazas and stores. Think smaller. Here, the town square was occupied by
what was likely the only 6-7 businesses in town. Some stayed open later
in the day, and I made a point of occasionally buying things or getting
dinner to get on greeting terms with the staff. 

Moynihan’s had a young barman named Tom. After learning I’m American,
he’d greet me with a “howdy,” and I’d give him a “Howdy” back. 

In the Centra (something like a 7/11) was an older woman named Margret.
She had a very auntie look about her and was friendly. I also got the
slight suspicion that she may have found me attractive in that “Friend’s
mom looking at you” sorta way.

Gem’s Old Bakery has some of the best baked goods I’ve ever had, and I
made a point to drop in before closing time near daily, just to get an
Almond raspberry cake slice, or a Cream Donut. A women around my age
handled the closing up time, and while we didn’t talk enough to exchange
too much, I knew her name was Ann, and she was nice enough to give me a
free cookie sometimes. 

If these names sound normal to you, it’s because most people have normal
names. That isn’t to say I haven’t met a Cillian, or a Shayla, but some
names like “Ben” and “Tom,” tend to come from other parts of the world. 

As I got used to small talk, I started to feel a bit easier about the
night life of the town. A day or two later, I began taking my long night
walk path around the town.

Part 3: Another kind of dark

The first 3 nights I spent keeping close to the town center, giving me
an excuse to eat at the restaurants nightly. I might have gained weight
because of it. I certainly haven’t been working out the way I did back
home, and the food was lovely and well priced. I knew I’d come to hate
my first visit back to the gym. 

I don’t want to give the impression that I’m any sort of athletic. I’m
of medium build, and a bit on the short side. I tried-out for boxing
once in high school, was hit in the face, and decided sports wasn’t for
me. I called that quitter talk, and tried out for football where, during
the try-out’s blocking drills, I was laid out flat. I don’t even
remember getting hit, I just remember meeting the other kid’s eyes
through our visors, him running at me, then just being on the ground.
All I remember is feeling like the sun felt brighter, and an ache in my
shoulders and neck. This is not a story of some roof climbing,
lock-picking, clever handed, secret martial arts practiced guy. There is
not a segment here where I will reveal some odd talent and leave it
roguishly unexplained with a, “don’t ask.” Oh sure, I imagined being all
those things, but I knew the reality of who I was. 

Still, some part of me wanted to believe that, in a pinch, that
courageous heroic vigor would come when I called for it, and I could
take on 5 men with quick wits and quicker fists. Then I hoped a little
harder, as my path was blocked with a few men, they looked roughly my
age, a bit younger probably. I ran through my thoughts one at a time.

“Don’t cross the street, if they are a few drinks in, they may think
about calling it out.”

“Don’t make assumptions, maybe they’re sober. You have time, look at
their mannerisms, what are they doing?” 

I saw a man in a dark green hoody shove another person, it was turning
into a bit of a play-fight. A couple more shoves happened before fists
balled and the two men shuffled their feet a bit imitating boxing
motions they’ve likely seen on TV. Someone else in a black jacket made a
comment I couldn’t make out and they both dropped their hands to their
side and grinned at him. 

My thoughts came together.

“Get close enough, then walk around them on the street. If any of them
make eye contact, give that polite nod that makes the police immediately
(and hopefully) forget you exist.”

I had a face for just this kind of moment. They were too focused on each
other to notice, but I lowered my eyes, narrowed my eyebrows, and held a
face that I believe said, “I’m older, sad, and just thinking my far away
thoughts.” The benefit of this face was that, if I was doing it as well
as I had hoped, I would just look like another nobody with problems in a
world full of nobodies with problems. 

I made it to their circle of friends, I counted 4. I began walking
around them, one bumped rather roughly into me. Not intentionally, more
like he was pushed and I happened to be behind him mid-stumble. This
scared me, but polite professionalism took over. 

“Oh, sorry, are you okay?” I moved to the twenty-something with the
sweater as if to help steady him.

He then shouted something at his friend with a smile. My grasp of the
accent still wasn’t great, especially this far from the tourism areas.
But I replayed it in my head.

“Look what ye’ve gone and done.”

The man with the black jacket gave me a cool nod, which I took as an
apology and I nodded back. I muttered another, “sorry”, and turned to
walk. Thankfully some gestures were universal. 

I know it may seem silly, an American not understanding English, but
accents are tricky. They can surprise you. An older lady who worked at
Gem’s Bakery was Spanish, and I cannot explain how wild it is to hear
fluent Spanish, spoken with a hint of an Irish accent. It was so far
detached from my Californian Spanish, we found it funny to compare words
and phrases.

“Aye!” 

I snapped back to reality and my heart sank. I turned slowly to see the
dark green hoody man walking towards me, grinning. I made a slight
adjustment to my face, making myself look older and fragile. I was
wearing a leather jacket that I’m sure any number of tough guys would
have loved. But I only hoped that my sunken eyes looked even more
innocent in contrast. A costume and mask of someone who was not worth
fighting because you knew you’d beat them. Someone who couldn’t be
trouble to anyone.

“Beg’yer pardon,” I said, turning with a thin tired smile. I drew out
the words, so I was as clear as possible. It’s useful.

He rattled something out fast, but I caught the key words.

“You’re the American?” 

I smiled politely, “yes, please forgive me for it.” It may have been
near black outside, but I can see the white smiles of the group. The
green hoodie man in-front of me leaned his ear closer. 

“What?” 

I could smell the booze in his breath, but he seemed like he genuinely
didn’t here me. He had been drinking, but I don’t think he was drunk.
Thankfully I was saved from responding when his friend in the black
jacket chimed in.

I heard the bones of, “Kenny, get over here,” and something else, but it
was quick. I got ahead of things. 

“Yes, American, sorry about that. If I sound a bit odd, it’s no doubt
the Southern California accent.” 

His eyes went wide and a grin went from ear to ear, his body language
saying, “oooh one of them fancy Hollywood boys.” He was making wide
turns as if showing me off to an invisible audience. 

I didn’t like how this was going. The man in the red who bumped me
earlier came into my vision more clearly, and he patted Kenny on the
back. Kenny, actually shot him a fed-up look. Two of the three men met
his eyes pleadingly, one looked a silent type of angry. The others gave
me an apologetic nod.

I knew this type of relationship. “He’s my friend, yes he drinks and
gets into trouble, but he’s good underneath it, I swear it officer, no
trouble here. I’ll look after him, I promise.”

Kenny lifted his beer to his mouth, it was the color of his pants, and I
was surprised I hadn’t seen it sooner. It made me nervous. A different
thought pushed to the front, Is it that dark out here tonight, I can
barely see the guys behind him?

He said something quick, and I couldn’t hone-in on the key words.
Something~ “gravel Pit?” The Wu-Tang Clan song? Did they get around in
Ireland? 

“I’m sorry, one more time?” I leaned-in a bit, despite my crawling skin
telling me not too.

In the tone you’d use to speak to a drooling kid, he repeated slowly.
“Did you fuck, Brad Pit or something?” 

Vague insults. I was quick on these though. “I’m actually more of a
DiCaprio guy, but Patterson is climbing my list pretty quickly if he
keeps up the stellar acting.” I said it with a casual smile and I had a
feeling I was going to be okay, I could see the smiles from his friends.
The smiles that said, “come-on, he’s harmless, leave him alone.” Kenny
even gave a half grin himself. His friends moved in, I felt a bit safer.

This time, Kenny was slower when he spoke.

“So what’er doin’ere, avoidin yer ess?”

“yer ess? my ass?” No, I must have misheard. I saw looks of,
you’ve-gone-too-far, from his friends and they moved to get a hold of
him. It was only then did I realize what he actually said. “What are you
doing here, are you avoiding ICE?” 

My heart fell into my stomach, but not because of what he said. Kenny
turned and shoved one of the other three guys with actual spite. It
wasn’t the playful push you give a friend, it had weight and pent up
anger behind it. I’ve seen it before, in the furious faces of 4th
graders who had nothing but pride and fists to shut other kids up if
they talked bad about their family. Just like them, Kenny would have
shoved the world if he could, but settled for whoever was nearby. He got
loud and his words were quick. I tried to catch the key words, but his
friends started speaking up and it became impossible for me to tell who
was talking. They were all nose to nose, but two people were
particularly in each other’s faces, the other two frantically trying to
pull them apart. 

It’s odd what thoughts creep into your head during a tense moment. All I
could notice was how dark it was. If the row of houses next to us
weren’t a faint and worn white, I’d barely have seen them.

As the argument broke out, it felt like the 4 of them moved into the
darkness. I got the shape of things though, and I especially saw the
finger jabbing at someone’s chest, then a punch was thrown. I heard a
sound that sounded like something striking meat with a rubber mallet.
Punches never sound like they do in movies, it made my stomach turn a
bit.

My mind rushed. If I ran, I’d look like the wuss that didn’t help break
up a fight started by people trying to help me. If I got involved, I
might get a similar slug to the face, and I’d have to pray that the
bruise was gone before going through TSA on the way home. I don’t
remember where that line of thought ended, but I remember suddenly being
between the one I knew as Kenny, and one other person. I hardly remember
what I even said, but it was something about me being a silly American.
I must have been trying to find some self-loathing joke to help
de-escalate things. Again, my thoughts began to wonder, where was the
Garda? Was this town so small it didn’t warrant policing? That aside,
what about everyone in these houses, didn’t they hear the commotion?

A scattering of moments went by. My hands were up and between the men in
a calming motion, there were middle fingers thrusted past me and swears
thrown along with spit. Then, something happened. It was too dark to see
who, but two people backed up into the dark, cursing loudly. I guessed
it was Kenny and a friend trying to calm him down. Whomever the third
person was took off in the other direction. Leaving me with someone on
the ground who was clearly knocked out cold.
