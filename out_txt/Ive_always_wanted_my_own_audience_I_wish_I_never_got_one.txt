I did the sides they’d emailed me the night before, a monologue from a
script I never saw the rest of. 

“You got a package from Barcelona,” I rehearsed. “First class.” 

Nobody read the other part. 

I performed to four hundred empty velvet seats and one old man with a
square face sitting off-center, eleven rows back, hands folded in his
lap.

He didn’t direct me or really do anything the whole time. When I
finished, he said “thank you,” and lifted his hand to wave me offstage. 

That was the entire audition.

I was in tears by the time I hit the parking structure. My body decided
to do it for me. Five years of saving. Notice given at a job I’d held
since I was in my twenties. I had told everyone.

A woman in a lanyard found me. 

“Are you Adam? Don’t leave,” she was already walking. “Mr. Vynstein was
interested.”

His office was on the fourth floor of a building whose lobby directory
listed three floors.

Vynstein was the old man from the auditorium. Up close he was about
seventy, a thick-bridged nose, well-shaved. His hair looked powdered. 

He asked me about my books. My freakin’ books. It was the first time
anyone had asked about them since I got them published six years ago. Of
course, I gushed about the whole damn trilogy. 

He listened with his entire face. I would have done anything for someone
to listen to me like that again. 

Then he said, “There’s a part of this that isn’t in the paperwork.”

And he loosened his tie.

I did the math in about a second and a half. My whole life, I’ve thrown
to the gutter for this chance. I decided yes, yes to whatever it was,
before I had any idea what I was agreeing to.

He unbuttoned his shirt. There was a seam on his stomach. Something
stuck from his navel.

Pale grey, slimy, twenty inches long by the time it finished coming out.
It bobbed up and down, a finger testing the bathwater. It had no eyes,
no face at all. Its front end was rather a ring of working muscle, and
inside the ring were teeth in rows going backward, textured like the
teeth of a comb. 

Vynstein gave me a polite look, gesturing with his eyes.

I extended my arm. It didn’t hurt, weirdly enough. It found purchase on
my wrist and set, a pressure like a thumb in a bruise. It pulled, not on
my skin, but somewhere…out of me. A direction I cannot put my finger
on. 

When it detached there was no wound and no blood. Didn’t even smell like
anything.

Vynstein buttoned up, poured two fingers of Black Label, and handed me
one. He said, flat as a board:

“You’ll get points on the back end.”

I came back the following Thursday. And the Thursday after that.

The part was in a movie you probably haven’t seen: Starry Knights. It’s
an indie about a team of four alien mercenaries who have to save the
galaxy from an evil robot. I played the role of a hound-faced detective
on Planet Lax. 

Six hours of prosthetics. Three scenes and a “with” credit. It came out
fourteen months after we wrapped and it was moderately successful.
Strangers started buying my books, and my publisher went back to print. 

Exactly what I’ve always wanted, since I was a kid. It was a lot less
exhilarating than I hoped. 

At the premiere, I was told by one director that I had a good career
ahead of me. I stood there and waited to feel the excitement. It never
arrived. I assumed I was overstimulated, and I checked my watch on the
way to the car.

Fifty-eight beats per minute.

The dinner was six weeks later. I got lost twice on the way to the
house.

There was a long table, but no food. All the guests, north of sixty,
were wearing paper bibs, tucked into their collars over open shirts.

Disappointing, but not totally unexpected. I just hoped someone would
buy me a burger later.

I took my top off, lay down on the table and let all nine of them feed
on me at once while I zoned out into the light fixture.

A little under two hours. Some of them made a sound while they did it.
Snnnouurrlgh.

When it was over I sat up and I felt fine. There were no marks anywhere
on me. Not a drop of blood.

So it isn’t blood. And before you say anything, it ain’t my soul either.
I’ve had two years to sit with this. “Soul” is a word people use when
they stop thinking critically. I’m still me. I still like the same
songs. I still cry when I watch The Lion King.

What I can’t do anymore is be reached by anybody saying anything good
about me.

That feeling went so quietly I mistook it for humility. Every time
nothing landed I assumed the dose was too small and went looking for a
bigger one, which, I understand far too late, is exactly what you would
want out of a crop.

Anything cruel still came through at full volume, surround sound,
forever.

I found that out three weeks later when the book got review bombed.

The biggest critique had to be the prose. I was only twenty-five when I
wrote the first one, and I didn’t get an editor through my publisher, so
most of the kinks were punched out by my own hand. 

Somebody made a thread pulling out the sincerest paragraph I’d ever
written with a sobbing emoji edited over it. 

Another one that got to me was that Wanderer Above the Sea of Fog
painting edited to be the man standing in front of the text. 

There’s no crime online worse than not being in on the joke. Worse when
it’s about yourself. I’d written over a thousand pages without one
punchline, and now I was the laughingstock of the internet. Any
positivity I had accumulated beforehand was now written off as botted. 

My mom called six times that week. I watched all six of them ring the
whole way through.

I told myself it was shame. But that wasn’t it.

She would have told me she was proud of me, and I already knew with
total certainty that it would arrive as nothing. My own mother’s voice
would come through as an errand, as a notification. 

Vynstein called that Friday. He said he had an audience prepared for me.

I told him I didn’t want an audience anymore.

“This isn’t one you’ll want to turn away.”

Stage 12 was pitch dark and warm, and it smelled like wet socks. 

I heard winches before I saw anything. Chains paying out slowly from
somewhere up above. Something coming down out of the dark in stages, the
way they lower a chandelier to change the bulbs.

Bigger than a car. Bigger than a bus. It hung there, no head, nothing
built for anything. It was an accumulation. An abomination. Every inch
of its underside was those wormy things, thousands of them. They swayed
in a nonexistent breeze. 

Ten eyes opened on it, at ten different heights.

All ten went to me.

Its voice was inside my head, like a memory. 

It thanked me for saving it up.

The lampreys came down and fed on me for a long time, and it is the only
occasion in my life on which I have felt important.

Pretty sure I’ve been blacklisted from anything real since the books
happened. I do commercial work now. Two crypto ads and a regional
furniture spot. I’m reading for a third crypto thing in a few weeks.
Honestly fine. 

They still feed on me from time to time. Less than they used to. I’m
told there’s less to take.

My bloodwork has never once come back abnormal.

You’ll say I should have stopped chasing this years ago, and that
anybody with a working sense of proportion could have sat down inside a
life like mine and been content.

You’re right. I know you’re right. I’m not arguing.

Vynstein’s office called about a reservation tomorrow. 

I’m going in.
