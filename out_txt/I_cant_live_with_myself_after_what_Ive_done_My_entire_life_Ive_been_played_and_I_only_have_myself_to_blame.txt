I’m not the kind of person who would ever do this knowingly, but it’s
done. There’s nothing left to do now, so I’m writing this text down so
that maybe someone else can blame me for this one day.

It all started when I was eight. My grandfather – I can’t remember much
of him, but he was always tinkering with artefacts of some sort. My
mother died during childbirth, and my father worked double shifts, so I
spent a lot of time with my mother’s father, as my father’s family lived
in another nation. My father never actually knew my mother very well and
had never met her father until after my birth. Despite spending so much
time at my grandfather’s house, I rarely saw him. He was a tall, thin
man with dark skin that looked like it was a light breeze away from
falling off his face.

One day, he stood up from his desk and handed me what looked like a soda
can made out of some sort of rock. It was heavy and had some variety of
abstract symbols carved into every part of it. He told me that as long
as I followed its instructions, everything would be okay. My father came
to pick me up later in the evening to take me home. On the drive home, I
heard the can whisper to me. It told me to fasten my seatbelt. I had
never bothered with it for as long as I could remember. Sitting in the
back of my dad’s car, whom I barely talked to, sort of disillusioned me
with his authority and made me pretty happy to break whatever little
rules I could. It was my way of feeling like I was in control. Well,
that was the day I kissed that feeling goodbye. I fastened my seatbelt,
and not 20 seconds later we crashed. I remember being coated in my
father’s blood as he was reduced to paste by the corner of a building.
First responders praised me for fastening my seatbelt. They told me it
was the only reason I was still alive. I knew the true reason then and
there, and I would never forget it.

When my grandfather never showed up at the hospital, I told the hospital
his name, and they found his number and address via his records. The
line was dead. His house was empty. There was zero trace of him
anywhere. I never saw my grandfather again. I didn’t spend much time at
the orphanage. I listened to the can; it told me what to do. What to
say. Who to say it to. Everything worked out perfectly for me. I was
housed after a month; my new family was kind and rich, and I had
siblings. We were all different colours, but we got along just fine. I
continued listening to the can. I did well in school, I made good
friends with intelligent people, and I invested in the right stocks. By
the time I was 25, I was worth 8 billion dollars. With a B. Nobody even
knew. I never paid tax. I worked part time at a café and lived with my
parents. I never spent a dime of the stock money. Nobody knew I had it.
I couldn’t tell anyone. I wasn’t allowed. I just had to keep increasing
it, which I did.

My 29th birthday. The can tells me something. Something different than
usual. Not stocks or what to wear or where to go or who to talk to. What
to say or what to eat or whether to drink coffee or tea. None of the
usual stuff. It tells me to buy a company. Not stocks in it, the whole
thing. Not just any company, though. One that ranks in the top 20 most
powerful entities in the world. I was loaded, but not that loaded.
According to the can, I had more than enough money so long as I said
what it told me to say. Well, it was right. By this point I was so numb
to the can’s suggestions I stopped even questioning them. Never
questioned it telling me to buy a bunch of properties in Ghana, nor when
it told me to fill them with mercenaries with weapons. It wasn’t until
it gave me incredibly specific instructions on how to decorate a room
that I began to question what exactly was going on.

I got all the things it specified: desks, drapes, and candles. I put it
all together. It was my grandfather’s office. I sat at his desk and
looked at myself in the small vanity mirror. I had his eyes. I never
knew my mother, but I think I heard her voice in that moment telling me
to stop. I wish I had listened.

Before long, I was tinkering with artifacts I wasn’t even sure I had
actually bought. I was the spitting image of my grandfather. The can
told me to do more and more insane things. I hired more and more
mercenaries; I had my own private military. I mobilised them, moving to
village after village, town after town, city and city, setting up
perimeters. One of the artifacts I had put together was a tape dispenser
made of the same weird rock. I never even found out where it came from.
The can just told me who to contact to get it. We’d draw lines with the
tape around all the cities and towns; it took about two years. Nothing
could have prepared me for what it was all leading to.

By this point I was 33. I’m still 33 as I write this. It was a
Wednesday. I was sitting at my desk, as the can had told me to. I was
staring into the mirror. That much was my own choice, at least. It
wasn’t me looking back, but I was used to that. I wonder how old my
grandfather really was, because I already looked older by then. The
clock struck 9, and the ground shook. Blinding lights blared through the
windows. None of this surprised me; I knew this was going to happen. I
pressed the button in my hand, as I was instructed, and the roof of the
office opened up. Thousands of beams of light pour into the can. I
didn’t know what they were. I never asked. The process took about 20
minutes to subside. If it weren’t for my stone glasses, I would have
been completely blind. When I took it off, I slowly put together what
had just occurred. There was blood. everywhere. I hadn’t seen this much
blood since I was eight. Not since the crash. But there was more. I was
shin deep in it. I called out for my men. No response. I waded over to
the other end of the room and checked outside. I saw their gear on the
ground where they were stationed. Blood flooded out into the hall and
pushed it all away. Before I could even process what I had just done,
the can spoke. I didn’t know how to say no to it anymore. I did as it
said. I had to find someone. I don’t know why it had to be him. This
celebrity, we’ll call him Dave. I always hated him growing up for his
contribution to the illegal animal trade when he was younger. I still
never would have wanted to do this to him, though. Regardless, I obeyed
the can.

I tracked him down. He was inside a recording studio, recording a
voiceover for whatever documentary he was working on at the time. It
mattered not. I waited outside, can in hand. As the 100-year-old man
walked out the door, I ran up to get his attention. He seemed happy to
talk to me, but I didn’t come to talk. I pointed the can at him and
pulled the tab.

I didn’t expect anything. Not that I expected nothing, I literally had
no expectations anymore. I forgot how to expect. I always just knew
because of the can.

Not this time.

Thirty-four million souls, crushed into a space the size of a fist, did
not merely escape. A word does not exist in my language to describe what
happened to them.

Dave had each one of his atoms shredded into subatomic pieces. A beam of
plasma pierced everything in front of me. The atmosphere around me was
incinerated. I don’t even know how I survived. Something close to a
billion kilograms of rogue quarks blasted a good portion of the planet
out of our solar system at relativistic speeds. When it was done, all I
saw before me was a crater that stretched beyond the horizon, the
exposed mantle of the earth’s blinding heat, and a hole in the sky where
I could see the cold blackness of space. It was then that for the first
time in 25 years, I felt alone in my head. The can was silent. It would
no longer tell me what to do. It had accomplished its goal. I sit here
not 2 hours after that. After I write this, I’m probably going to take
my own life. Life on Earth is probably limited to 3 or 4 more
generations now. Every man, woman and child that was in Ghana on that
day is now a tiny fragment of a wave of radiation spreading throughout
our galaxy, along with Dave and his crew and anyone else that was
standing between me and 80km in front of me 3 hours ago.

If anyone ever reads this, please hate me. There’s nobody left here to
hate me. In life, I was seen as some sort of self-made humble
billionaire. I can’t die knowing that that’s how the last people that
remember me will remember me. I can’t bring myself to make them hate me,
though. I’m too weak. So I turn to you. Hate me. Blame me for this.
Please. I’m sorry. Don’t forgive me. Goodbye.
