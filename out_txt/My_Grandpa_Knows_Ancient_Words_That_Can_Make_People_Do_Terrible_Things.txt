What were your first words?

Your parents probably told you it was some variation of mama, or dada.
I’m sure they waited with bated breath after months of baby babble to
experience that moment. A right of passage into our wordy world.

Funny how we put so much stock into the sounds we make. There’s a
reverence to them. Names. Our first words. Our last.

I love you, Sarah, for all eternity.

Those were the final words of James Polk, 11th president of the United
States.

Sweet words. Personal. Paints a picture.

I think my grandma’s last words were: “Ow. Dammit, it hurts Becky.” 

We can’t all be profound.

Then there are bad words. The kind of words that make people wince and
get children in trouble. These words have a sort of social and cultural
power.

I have a vivid memory of my brother saying “shit” for the first time at
five years old. Our mother appeared around the corner of our room and
swiftly administered the age old tradition of soaping his mouth. A
bitter and bubbly ritual. A cleansing of sorts.

There are other words, older words, not many people know about. Ancient
syllables my grandfather taught me about one chilly Christmas Day. I was
seated by his wheelchair while my brother and cousins were scattered
about, high off presents and holiday cheer. Grandpa paid them no mind.
He was busy nursing a beer in one hand and a cigarette in the other,
despite my mother’s half-hearted attempts to make him smoke outside. 

I sat criss-cross, doughy eyes peering up at the 80 year old veteran.
The unsightly green and red tree lights made him look like a gargoyle
perched on his mobile throne, stone-faced and still, only moving to sip
or smoke every few minutes. While my cousins ran by with Legos and
action figures in hand, I sat by my grandfather’s side. The alcohol
would rouse him soon. An offering necessary to muster the enthusiasm to
spin me a new tale. A few sips later, the man spoke.

“Shameful. The things you kids get into.” he said in his hoarse, Boston
accent. “Your games, and cartoons. I didn’t go to war just so you could
fall in love with the enemy’s toys. What’s that oriental shit you’re
watching Kid?”

My cousin, playing a fresh cartridge of the latest Pokémon game, glanced
at my grandfather with wide, terrified eyes. I chuckled. My mother
peered around the corner, brows raised in warning. Watch the language,
her expression said silently. He grumbled an apology and waved her off. 

“Yeah, yeah. Language, language. Goddam words is all they is.” He
muttered under his breath. He sat silent for a few moments. “Say, Tim, I
ever tell you about the old words?” He said before taking a taking a sip
of his Coors.

I shook my head.

“Huh.” He grumbled, twirling the smoke in the air with his cigarette.
“Coulda sworn I did last Christmas.”

He cleared his throat and adjusted himself in his chair, the same way he
always did before telling a story. The gargoyle was no more. The
storyteller was free from the stone.

“When I worked at the agency—this was many years after the war—I worked
on a program. A secret program. We were interested in these
sounds—‘Programming linguistics’ the coats called it. Ancient words.
Army paid a lot of money to dig em up.”

He peered down and noticed my eyes glossing over at the specifics.

“Tim. When I say Apple what comes to mind?” He said plainly.

“What do you mean?”

“What do you see in your head when you hear that word?” He held his arms
out in anticipation, an attempt to draw the words out of me.

“I see a red apple. I guess.” I said quietly.

“There ya go. Programming. It’s like a virus, Tim. You can’t not picture
the apple. When I say the word and you hear it, your brain shows you the
apple whether you want to or not. It’s inevitable.” He peeked over for a
second to see if mom was in earshot. He craned his head closer, his
voice much lower now.

“There’s special words Tim, words that are embedded so deep in our
brains—our biology—that hearing them can make crazy shit happen. It’s
reverse programming. This language came long before we had the tongues
to speak it.” 

I stared blankly and scratched my head.

“What words Grandpa? What can they do?” 

He smiled, satisfied at my hungry eyes.

“Can’t say kid. I say them right here, right now, and this house will go
up in flames by the end of the day.” He shot a look over at my cousin
tapping away at Pokémon. “Actually doesn’t sound so bad.” He grumbled
under his breath.

He proceeded to tell me stories about the words. They were discovered
inscribed into various artifacts around the world. Most people who tried
to comprehend the words by reading or hearing them suffered strange
deaths: spontaneous combustion, brain hemorrhages, and suffocation, to
name a few. All dead without rhyme or reason.

Except for grandpa.

Sometime in the 90s, he and others were given a choice to participate in
the program. They had no idea what they were signing up for, of course.
All they had to do was read some words on a sheet of paper. Out of a
hundred agents, only three survived. My grandpa being one of them. 

“What did you do Grandpa? Did the words give you powers?” I asked.

“No, not powers. It’s more like a curse. Dark magic. A dangerous weapon
for reckless warriors fighting a fool’s war. I spoke words that made my
enemies explode. Some turned inside out. Others, well…” My grandpa waved
the thought away. “Mostly bad men. Mostly…” His voice trailed off.

My mind raced with thoughts. So many questions bubbled in my premature
mind. 

“Tell me a word grandpa! Just one? Please.”

I was so hypnotized by the story that I didn’t notice my mother perched
behind grandpa, arms crossed. My expression must’ve gave it away because
grandpa settled back into his chair and cleared his throat
uncomfortably.

“Hmm sure thing kid—please, and thank you! Most magic words of all.
Oh—and I love my mother. No truer words than that.” 

Grandpa turned to my mom and chuckled as she rolled her eyes and wheeled
him off. “Alright, time for grandpa to finish his smoke outside.” She
announced.

The man’s words were heavy in my mind for the rest of the holiday. I’ll
admit I didn’t really give them the weight they deserved. My grandfather
was a storyteller, after all. He loved to spin tales of his times in the
army and working as a federal agent. They were often adorned with
harmless details: robots, villains, and mysterious artifacts. Two truths
and a lie. A common anecdote of children whose parents and grandparents
weathered times of war. 

It was many years before I realized how much truth was buried in those
words.

The next decade wasn’t kind to grandpa. Alzheimer’s began to eat at him
slowly. A man filled with stories and history was being reduced. The
words spilling out and petrifying in his mind, plaque gumming the rivers
of thought that once defined him. The storyteller was fading away. Only
the gargoyle remained.

I visited when I could. My failed attempts to draw out the grandpa I
knew were mostly met with a dull stare or grumble. Moments of lucidity
made scarce by a hungry disease. The last time I saw him with any light
in his eyes was just before he died.

It was just a casual visit. We were outside. Other residents of the home
were buzzing about with nurses and family. It smelled like spring and a
wash of color beamed from the facility’s garden. A mess of red, yellow
and violet flowers were a stark contrast to the disgruntled residents,
all wearing grey and white. A uniform of sorts.

I wheeled grandpa to a bench and sat beside him. The gargoyle remained
still. Glossy eyes, wide and unblinking, watching everything and nothing
all at once.

Still, I spoke to him. Caught him up on my life. I spoke about my
studies. My friends. The words evoked some movement. A nod here. A
grumble there. Cracks in the stone. My words weren’t magic but they
still managed to give some life to him.

I was in the middle of spilling some girl drama when I noticed grandpa
looking at me, eyes bright with life. Did he recognize me? How long was
he doing that for?

“Grandpa? Do you know who I am?” I asked hesitantly.

The man locked eyes with me. I could see some semblance of familiarity.
Just a shimmer.

“It’s Tim, your grandson. Do you know where you—“

Grandpa started speaking—well, mumbling. A strange, guttural sound, low
and deep. A cold chill slithered up my spine as I watched his mouth form
a string of quiet, ominous syllables.

“Grandpa? What is it?” I turned my ear to him and leaned in.

That’s when I heard it. A word. It was a dialect I’ve never heard in my
life. The sound rattled my brain, like a jolt of lightning. It rippled
through my nervous system, setting fire to every nerve in my body. The
sensation sent me tumbling off the bench, wincing and groaning in pain.

When the pain settled, I sat up on the sidewalk. Grandpa was silent now.
All stone again. Except for his face. A strange expression settled on
him. A look of surprise—of wonder. It was as if whatever the sound was
supposed to do didn’t work, and that surprised him. A pleasant surprise.
The look of a child that screamed “shit” and just realized he has gotten
away with it.

What just happened?

I hurried him back to his room and got the hell out of there.

I tried my best to forget that day. To forget the word. The pain. The
way it ripped through my body. 

It was so alien. The word wasn’t Latin, or Greek, or Hebrew—it was
older. It was truth. The word had no meaning. It was meaning. The sound
was merely the shape of energy that had the power to create, destroy and
everything in-between.

Is this what magic is? 

Is this what curses are made of?

It took many months but eventually I put it out of my mind. I chalked it
up to stress. To anxiety. A hallucination brought on by the pressures of
college life. How silly I was to believe the word of a demented old vet
could harm me.

Sticks and stones.

Still, I couldn’t bring myself to see him after last time. After all, he
was gone, wasn’t he? What’s left of his mind isn’t the man I grew up
with. The man I knew, the veteran—the storyteller—only existed in my
memories.

Now, all we could do was wait. With his mind gone, it was only a matter
of time before the body gave away. I expected a peaceful passing. No
profound last words. There were none left to speak anyway.

Or so I thought.

What happened next was the last thing anyone could have expected.

I answered the phone and the nurse gave me the rundown. Apparently mom
couldn’t be reached and grandpa was exhibiting some “behaviors.”
Peaceful moments. Lucid eyes. I didn’t need her to spell it out for me.
It’s not uncommon for Alzheimer’s patients to show signs of progress
shortly before kicking the bucket.

I headed over to keep grandpa company until mom finished her shift. The
drive was anything but peaceful. Memories of my grandpa came and went
like the passing cars. I kept replaying the stories: colorful words
conveying battles, mischief, and triumph. Those were good times.

Eventually less pleasant events crept into my mind. The ancient words I
heard about all those Christmas’s ago. The time my dying grandpa uttered
something evil and sent me spiraling onto the garden floor. 

Despite my best efforts I couldn’t knock that memory. 

When I arrived, something wasn’t right. The checkin desk was unattended.
You could hear the faint echo of footsteps and hushed voices eclipsed by
doors slamming shut. I jumped in surprise as a group of staff and
residents hurried past me, tearing out the front door. The sound of
yelling and clatter erupted from the pastel hallway to my left.

What is going on?

I approached, the sounds drowning out my hesitant footsteps as I drew
near. My grandpa’s growl became clearer and clearer as the dining hall
came into view. My mind went straight to the worst. I picked up the
pace, worried he was the victim of some demented fit by another
resident.

Oh how wrong I was.

There he was, standing and yelling. His limbs flailing violently in all
directions. The man was no longer wheelchair bound. He stood tall,
covered in blood that wasn’t his.

I tried my best to take it all in, to make sense of the horror.

The broken body of a caretaker lay twitching in the corner, his insides
painting the room and its inhabitants a dark shade of red. His name was
Terry. A nurse, Maria, stood frozen in a state of shock. She held a
sedative in her shaky hand, staring at her fallen coworker. Another
member of staff—John I think is his name—stood braced, chair in hand,
wooden legs facing the assailant—my grandpa.

He continued flailing manically, a loose blade of plastic in hand. The
remnant of a shattered lunch tray that lay in pieces at his feet. He was
much thinner than the last time I saw him. His baggy shirt and sweats
hung heavily on his skeletal frame, Terry’s blood giving them a wet
shine.

I stood frozen, unable to follow the series of events that led up to
this.

John demanded—no—begged my grandpa to drop the weapon. His voice was
shrill and cracked with terror.

What was he so afraid of? My grandpa couldn’t possibly have done this.
How did this happen?

My grandpa was speaking now.

He stopped his aimless swinging and bellowed, face turned up to the sky
in a war cry. The words tore through the room and hit me square in the
chest. I felt that familiar electricity pass through my body, sending me
tumbling.

I watched in horror as the words intended outcome take hold of John. 

It started with a scream. John let out a long, guttural yell that ended
in a pinched whine as his body became undone. An array of horizontal
slices erupted across his body, red lines spaced every few inches,
reducing him to even sized disks of flesh. The slices plopped to the
floor like a pile of demented pancakes and settled with a wet bubbly
sound. 

The nurse had already started running to the exit, her scream drowning
out the curse that minced John.

But she wasn’t quick enough, or loud enough this time.

Grandpa directed his fury in her direction and let out a louder set of
syllables that filled the dining hall. The sound was unshakable,
drowning out her screams, piercing her barrier of sound.

Unlike John, she didn’t scream or shout. She didn’t unspool and plop on
the bloody laminate. No—

She transformed.

A quiet hiss escaped her just before her body was reduced to mist. Her
form erupted into a thick red fog that tore through the air, sending a
gust of hot red towards me. My eyes stung as the fog swept over me,
bloody particles piercing me faster than I had time to blink. 

Everything was blurry now. My grandpa was a dark silhouette against the
bloodied air. As I took a few groggy steps forward, the red mist
thickened above, forming clouds that clung to the ceiling. 

I take a few more shaky steps and it starts to rain. The condensation
formed by the nurse’s remains are pouring down. I hold my hands out,
beckoning towards grandpa, struggling to keep my balance as I navigate
through the cursed flesh littering my path.

He’s still now, no longer flailing with rage. The strain in my eyes and
blood fog wrap him in a black silhouette. He’s less of a man and more of
a statue. A stoic figure in the storm. A gargoyle in the rain. 

Finally, my hands are on his shoulders. I yell over the sound of the
storm, but I can hardly hear myself. Words drowned out by falling
carnage. 

His eyes break away from the clouds above and settle on my face. Bright,
manic marbles staring right through me. 

I try to shake him. To wake him up from his rage. Deep down I know it’s
futile. His mind is mud. This moment is simply a shimmer of gold in the
muck. Soon, it will be swallowed back up.

As the rain begins to clear, grandpa’s knees buckle and shake under his
weight. The adrenaline passes, and the mania passes with it. His heart
gives out before his legs, and grandpa’s stiff body falls to the ground
with the rest of the tattered flesh.

The room is silent.

No more words.

No more screams.

Only the periodic drip of red hitting the wet floor. 

I drop to my knees and weep.

It’s been a lifetime since that day. The day grandpa went crazy. The day
the news reports as an “unfortunate and mysterious accident.” The day
three innocent people lost their lives and left no remains to be buried
by their loved ones.

The memory of that day has found its way into my dreams. Nightmares
filled with booming voices and people reduced to red mist. I’m not sure
what to make of it. Society doesn’t know what to make of it.

Government agencies try their best to. They come and go, rapping on my
door with a series of strange questions stamped into documents labeled
“confidential” or “top secret.” More dangerous words. Ominous labels.

It’s only a matter of time before the visits turn into warrants turn
into arrests.

Best not to think about it.

No, today I have enough to think about. An envelope has been sitting on
my desk for a few days now. My grandfather’s estate settled what little
he had and distributed a series of letters to our family. I nearly
dropped mine when it was first handed to me.

What secrets lie within? I imagine my grandpa nearing the edge of his
sanity, scribbling a series of ancient words. A demented prophet forging
divine weapons and passing it down to his grandson on a napkin. He knows
we are the same. The words have no power over us, so they make us
powerful. 

Even if I read the words, I wouldn’t dare speak them. Ever. But can I
really promise that? Who’s to say I won’t get Alzheimer’s too. Will the
words sear into my brain and rattle off when I’m at the end of my life?
Will I kill three people in a fit of mania? You never know what awaits
you at the edge of oblivion. 

After wrestling with these thoughts for too many sleepless nights,
curiosity overcomes me. It’s time to read the letter. Call it whatever
you want: stress, anxiety, or morbid curiosity. The words that lie in
that crumpled envelope whisper to me. There’s an urgency to it. Can they
really be that bad?

What’s the saying again? Sticks and stones may break my bones but words—

Nope. Words can hurt me. Try telling that to crowds driven to violence
by dictators. Tell that to victims of genocide whose killers spew
hatred. Tell that to the poor caretakers reduced to mist and sliced into
flesh frisbees.

The envelope tears open with little effort. My heart beats loud in my
head as I fold open the old paper with shaky hands.

The paper is dated almost a decade ago. 

“Tim,

Well, it’s Christmas. Couldn’t make it down to see you and your mom this
year. Gettin old is a bitch.

Not sure why I’m writing this. I’ve never been great with words. Maybe
it’s the Jack Daniel’s talking or maybe I’m finally getting sentimental.
Fat chance of that. I guess what I really wanted to say is, I miss ya. I
know I act like a hard ass but telling you stories is what I look
forward to every Christmas. You make an old vet feel like a war hero.
That’s the best gift a stubborn old man could ask for. 

I was gonna mail this to you after the holidays but I think I may hold
onto it. Don’t want you to think your grandpa’s getting soft on ya.
Maybe I’ll save it for when I’m dead so you can have one last thing from
me to open on Christmas.

-Grandpa”

That was it. No curses or ramblings of a broken man. Just a letter from
a grandfather to his grandson.

Despite the horror of his last day on earth, the old man managed to top
it off with some heart. I really thought I’d heard the last from him.
Apparently he had some words squirreled away for a rainy day. An
artifact inscribed with a message from a man that still had his marbles
and loved his family.

Words, man. Some are more dangerous than others. Some create. Some
destroy. Some leave a grown man weeping and missing his grandpa.

Not all words are ancient and cursed, but everyone can say powerful
things. We all have magic syllables that can connect us, change us, and
make us better.

I won’t take my words for granted. I’m going to write more, pieces like
this one. I want to talk about things that matter. Stories that make a
difference. I think grandpa would’ve liked that.

To all you reading this, I hope you do the same. Be wise with your
words. Call out injustice. Tell your mom you love her. 

You never know what words will be your last, but the best words to come
out of your mouth could be the next thing you say.
