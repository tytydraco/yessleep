Part Two

Friday morning came with a sky the color of bruised slate. The drought
was so thick you could smell the parched earth through the window
screens dry, sour, and suffocating.

My dad spent the whole morning transforming our living room into a
staging ground. He wasn’t acting like a father handing his son over to a
nightmare; he was acting like a man preparing for a siege.
He laid out three hunting rifles, two shotguns, and every box of
ammunition we owned on the kitchen table. Next to the firearms were
gallon jugs filled with gas and kerosene, rigged with cloth wicks.

“They aren’t ghosts, and they aren’t demons,” my dad said, racking a
round into his 12 gauge. “They’re earth and rot. When Mikey was taken in
’82, my father made me watch from the edge of the cornfield. I saw them
touch him, Jackson. They don’t bleed, but they dry up. And anything that
dry burns.”

“Mayor Vance said they hold people,” I reminded him, my voice cracking.
“He said Mikey is still out there. Nic too.”

My dad stopped, his hands trembling over a box of 12 gauge shells. “If
my brother is still alive in those woods after forty years… he isn’t
human anymore. And if we go out there tonight just to trade places, none
of us are coming back.”

At 10:00 PM, the wind died down completely. The woods behind our house
fell dead silent no crickets, no rustling leaves, nothing.

Then came the smell. It rolled off the treeline like a fog: the stench
of stagnant river silt and damp, decay blackened corn husks.

My dad threw on his heavy canvas coat, grabbed a rifle and a jug of gas,
and handed me the shotgun.
“Time to pay the debt,” he muttered.

We walked out to the edge of the property where the scrap yard meets the
timber woods. The full moon was high, casting an eerie, pale glow over
the cracked, dry soil.

Standing ten feet inside the shadow of the trees was Mayor Vance and
three other town council members. They were holding lanterns, their
faces pale and drawn in the yellow light.

“You brought guns, John?” Vance called out, his voice thin against the
silence. “You think lead stops a hundred years of obligation?”
“I brought what I brought, Arthur,” my dad replied, stepping up to the
boundary line. “Where’s the boy? Where’s Nic?”

Vance gestured into the dark behind him.
Two tall, gaunt figures stepped out from between the oak trees. They
didn’t walk so much as glide, their movements jerky and unnatural.

In the moonlight, I could see what they were made of tightly woven river
mud, dried husks, and thick, fibrous roots that twitched like exposed
nerves.

Between them, dragging his feet in the dirt, was Nic.
His eyes were wide with terror, but he couldn’t speak.

His skin looked pale, coated in a fine layer of dry topsoil, and thick
twine was stitched tightly through his lips.

Behind Nic, another figure emerged from the deep shadow. It was smaller
than the Hollow Men, hunched, clad in shredded, decayed children’s
clothes. My dad gasped, dropping his rifle half an inch. “Mikey…”

The small figure looked up. Its face was caked in dried clay, its hair
long and matted with pine needles. It stared right at my dad with
hollow, milky white eyes.

It didn’t recognize him. It didn’t recognize anything.

“The trade is simple, John,” Mayor Vance said, holding out his hand for
the brass bells. “You and Jackson step across the tree line.

Nic steps out. The debt for the 1982 deficit and the 2026 drought is
cleared. The soil stays rich for another twenty years.”

I looked at Nic, whose tears were cutting clean tracks through the dirt
on his cheeks. I looked at the twisted shell of my uncle.

Then I looked down at my backpack. I unzipped it, pulled out the 1982
ledger, and raised a jug of kerosene in my other hand.

“Wait!” Vance screamed, taking a frantic step forward. “No! Rule one!
You burn that book and the contract is broken they’ll take the whole
town!”

“Then the town can share the debt,” I said.

I doused the leather bound book in kerosene, struck my lighter, and
dropped the flame onto the oilskin cover.

The ledger burst into a violent, blueish green fire.

A sound erupted from the woods not a scream, but a massive, deafening
roar like a tornado tearing through a dry cornfield.

The Hollow Men shrieked, their wooden limbs thrashing as the air around
us turned ice cold.

My dad didn’t hesitate. He raised his shotgun and fired straight over
Mayor Vance’s shoulder into the darkness.

“Run!” my dad roared, grabbing Nic by his jacket collar and yanking him
across the boundary line. “Run back to the house!”

The ledger is ashes, the woods are on fire, and something massive is
tearing its way out of the earth beneath Blackwood Creek did we just
save ourselves, or did we just end the world as we know it?
