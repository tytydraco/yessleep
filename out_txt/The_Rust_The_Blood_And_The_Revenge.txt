The air inside the old Apex Steel plant smelled like frozen iron, wet
dust, and the sour tang of stagnant oil. It was a dense, heavy kind of
cold that didn’t just sit on your skin—it settled in your joints, making
every movement feel stiff and deliberate.

“Watch the cable, Leo,” Sam said. His voice was barely a whisper, flat
and tight with the kind of tension he’d been holding since we cut
through the chain-link fence three hours ago. He was hunkered down by
the portable audio kit, adjusting the gain on a long shotgun mic tipped
with a grey foam wind-muff. “If you step on the XLR feed again, the
jack’s going to snap. I don’t have a spare.”

“I see it,” I murmured, eye pressed to the rubber cup of the viewfinder.

Through the crisp black-and-white digital display of my Sony FX3, the
world looked colder than it actually was. High above us, fifty feet up
into the ribbed darkness of the main gantry room, rusted steel trusses
crisscrossed like the ribs of a long-dead whale. Sunlight filtered down
through broken corrugated skylights, throwing sharp, diagonal bars of
grey light through the haze.

“Alright, Marcus,” I said, swinging the rig slowly on the fluid head.
“Stand right where the concrete drops into the pit. Take two steps back
toward the shadow, but keep your chest facing camera right. Give me that
look like you just heard something drop in the furnace room.”

Marcus stepped into frame. He was wearing his brother’s oversized dark
green parka, his face pale under the layer of fake grease paint Sam had
smeared across his jawline. He grinned, white teeth flashing through the
dark dirt.

“Like this, director?” Marcus lowered his pitch, trying to sound like a
grizzled survivor instead of a sixteen-year-old junior with a C-minus in
chemistry. “Or should I look more like I’m about to mess my pants?”

“Just look quiet,” Sam snapped, not looking up from his level meters.
“You’re booming your voice too much. The echo in here is insane.
Everything bounces off the corrugated iron twice before it hits the
diaphragm. Talk lower.”

“Relax, Sammy,” Marcus sneered softly, though he kept his volume down.
He took his mark, shifting his weight. His boots crunched on a layer of
shattered safety glass and dried bird droppings. “It’s an indie film.
The grain adds atmosphere. Right, Leo?”

“The audio needs to be clean, Marcus,” I said, adjusting the manual
focus ring. The barrel clicked softly under my fingers. The lens caught
the edge of Marcus’s profile, sharp against the cavernous background of
rusted machinery, dormant conveyor belts, and massive iron hoppers that
stretched into the blackness at the far end of the floor. “If the sound
is trash, nobody cares how good the image looks. Sam’s right. Keep it
low.”

We had been planning this shoot for three months. Apex Steel had shut
down in the late nineties, left to rot on the marshy spit of land three
miles out from the highway. Everyone in town knew about it; everyone had
heard the stories about kids breaking in, or squatters setting fires in
the winter, or the rumor that a maintenance guy had fallen into an
active slag hopper back in ’84 and they never found enough of him to
bury.

But to us, it was a goldmine—a six-hundred-thousand-square-foot
production set that didn’t cost a dime, complete with real rust,
authentic decay, and a scale we could never replicate in my parents’
basement.

“Ready sound?” I asked.

Sam lifted two fingers, his black headphones pressed tight against his
ears. His eyes were fixed on the green and yellow LED bars bouncing on
the screen of his field recorder.

“Speeding.”

“Rolling camera,” I said, thumbing the red button on the side of the
grip. A tiny red tally light flickered on below the cold shoe.

“Scene fourteen, Take One. Marker.”

Marcus clapped his hands together once—our improvised slate—and turned
his head toward the deep aisle between the abandoned stamping presses.

For ten seconds, nobody breathed.

The only sound was the distant, hollow hum of wind shearing against the
loose metal sheeting on the roof, and the high-pitched ping-ping-ping of
thermal expansion in the cooling iron girders.

Then Sam raised his left hand, his eyebrows pulled together into a deep
knot.

“Cut,” he said.

I pulled my eye away from the EVF.

“What is it? That was looking great, Sam.”

“There’s a hum,” Sam said. He pulled off one ear-cup, his thin face
pale. “A ground loop or something. High frequency. No, wait—it’s not a
ground loop. It’s… weird.”

“It’s an abandoned factory, man,” Marcus said, dropping his posture and
leaning his hands on his knees. “The whole place is a hum.”

“No,” Sam insisted, his voice rising slightly. He held out the boom
pole, angling the shotgun mic toward the dark corridor behind the
stamping presses—a gap between two massive, three-story-high hydraulic
housings that led into the old boiler house.

“It’s not wind. It’s too regular. Listen.”

He flipped the toggle on his monitor amp and held out his spare
headphones to me. I stepped away from the tripod, took the padded band,
and pressed the cup to my ear.

At first, I heard only static—the clean, white baseline noise of
high-gain preamps.

Then, underneath it, buried beneath the room tone, there was a sound
like heavy wet paper being torn very slowly.

Trrk.

A pause. Three seconds.

Trrk.

Then a wet, sliding drag, like a heavy bag of wet soil being pulled
across smooth concrete.

“It’s just water dripping,” Marcus said, walking over and snatching the
headphones from my hand before I could stop him.

He listened for a second, then threw his head back and laughed.

“It’s a leaky pipe, Sam! Jesus, you’re jumping at your own shadow. It’s
twenty feet away.”

“Pipes don’t pull themselves,” Sam murmured, taking the headphones back.

He looked past Marcus, staring straight into the black mouth of the
boiler room aisle.

“The pitch changed when you walked over there. It got higher.”

“It’s an echo,” I said, trying to keep my voice grounded, though a cold
needle of unease prickled the back of my neck.

The air coming out of that side corridor felt five degrees colder than
the main hall, carrying a heavy, greasy stench that smelled like old
lard and scorched copper.

“Look, we’ve got about two hours of daylight left before we lose the sky
through the skylights. Let’s get the master shot, then we’ll go check
out the boiler room if you want to set up ambient tracks.”

Sam didn’t answer right away.

He was still staring at the audio meter. The LED bars weren’t peaking,
but they were fluttering in an odd, rhythmic pattern—three short green
bars, a pause, then a single red flick at the top end of the scale.

“Fine,” Sam said quietly. “One more take. Then I want to check the cable
runs.”

We finished the master shot twenty minutes later, but the mood had
shifted.

The light was changing fast, turning from bright grey to a bruised,
violet-tinted dusk. The shadows between the heavy machinery were
lengthening, stretching across the concrete floor like long, black
fingers.

“I need to grab the secondary mic setup,” Sam said as Marcus and I were
packing down the C-stand.

“I left the boundary mic on the platform near the boiler room door when
we were doing the walk-through earlier.”

“I’ll go with you,” I offered, reaching for my shoulder rig.

“No, stay here and keep an eye on the camera glass,” Sam said, waving a
hand dismissively. He pulled a yellow heavy-duty flashlight from his
coat pocket.

“It’s thirty yards away. Just behind the secondary transformer box. I’ll
be right back.”

“Don’t get eaten by the pipe-monster,” Marcus called out, sitting on an
overturned plastic crate and flipping through his script pages by the
light of his phone screen.

Sam didn’t reply.

His flash beam cut a sharp, dusty cone through the darkness as he
rounded the corner of the hydraulic press and disappeared into the
aisle.

I focused on wiping down the front element of my 35mm prime lens with a
microfiber cloth.

The silence of the factory returned, heavier now that the wind outside
had died down.

The only sound was the rhythmic tap-tap-tap of Marcus’s thumb against
the back of his plastic phone case.

“He’s total nervous energy today,” Marcus said without looking up.

“I told him we shouldn’t have come out here if he was gonna be on edge
the whole time. Ruins the vibe.”

“He’s just cautious,” I said, screwing the lens back onto the camera
body.

“If we get caught by the cops or security, he’s the one whose parents
are gonna lose their minds. Yours don’t even know you’re in the county.”

Marcus smirked, but didn’t answer.

Five minutes passed.

The light in the gantry hall dipped another notch.

The high corrugated walls seemed to press inward, the rusty girders
disappearing into total pitch-black above us.

“Sam!” I called out.

My voice bounced:

Sam… Sam… sam…

No response.

“Sammy, come on!” Marcus shouted, leaning back on his crate.

“We’re losing the light, man! Bring the gear!”

Still nothing.

I set the camera rig carefully down on its padded case, picked up my LED
panel light, and switched it to maximum output.

A flood of harsh, 5600K daylight cut through the gloom, illuminating the
thick dust motes floating in the air.

“Marcus, come on,” I said, waving him up. “Let’s go get him.”

Marcus groaned, dropping his script into his bag, but he got up and
followed me.

We walked past the giant steel housings of the presses.

The floor here was wet with a mixture of rainwater and dark, viscous
grease that didn’t smell like machine oil anymore—it smelled sweet,
sickeningly sweet, like overripe peaches left in a sealed plastic bag.

“Sam?” I said, holding the light high.

We reached the entrance to the boiler house.

It was a lower-ceilinged room, crowded with massive, cylindrical iron
tanks wrapped in peeling grey asbestos insulation.

The beam of my light hit the concrete floor in front of the door.

Sam’s flashlight was lying there.

It was still turned on, its beam pointed uselessly against the base of a
rust-eaten control panel.

Next to it lay his shotgun mic, its carbon-fiber boom pole snapped clean
in half—not splintered, but crushed flat, as if a heavy hydraulic weight
had come down across the middle of it.

“What the hell?” Marcus muttered, his casual tone disappearing
instantly.

He stepped forward, reaching down toward the broken pole.

“Sam? Is this a joke?”

“Marcus, don’t touch it,” I said, my voice shaking.

I swept the LED panel across the floor.

Where the mic cable ran into the darkness between two boiler tanks,
there was a thick, glistening trail on the concrete.

It looked like grey grease, but as the bright light hit it, the
substance seemed to quiver—a thick, gelatinous slick three feet wide,
trailing off down the narrow steel steps that led into the sub-basement
drainage corridors.

And sitting right in the middle of that slick was Sam’s left headphone
cup.

It had been torn from the headband.

The black leatherette pad was soaked through with a clear, yellowish
fluid that was smoking very faintly in the cold air.

“Sam!” I screamed, the full force of panic hitting my chest like a
physical blow.

“Sam!”

From deep down in the sub-basement—far below the floorboards, through
the rusted drainage grates—a sound came back.

It wasn’t a voice.

It was an audio signal.

It was Sam’s voice, but it was clipped, distorted, and playing back at
slightly the wrong speed, like an old cassette tape with a dying
battery:

“…watch the cable, Leo… watch the cable, Leo… if you step on the XLR
feed again…”

The sound didn’t come from a person’s mouth.

It came from the air itself, vibrating through the iron pipes
surrounding us, the frequency shifting up and down in a bizarre,
mechanical warble.

Marcus backed up so fast he slammed his shoulder into the corner of the
boiler tank.

“What is that? What the fuck is that?!”

“We’re leaving,” I said.

I grabbed Marcus by the collar of his parka and hauled him backward
toward the main hall.

“We’re getting out right now. We’re going to the car and calling the
police.”

“He’s down there!” Marcus yelled, his eyes wide, his face completely
drained of color.

“Leo, he’s down in the pit!”

“Look at the cable, Marcus! Look at the floor!” I yelled back, my light
shaking violently in my grip.

“Whatever is down there crushed his boom pole like a soda can! We can’t
help him without light and help! Move!”

We ran back to the center of the gantry hall.

The factory was almost entirely dark now.

The skylights above were deep blue-black, showing the first faint
glimmers of evening stars.

The massive space that had felt cool and historic an hour ago now felt
like an open trap.

I lunged for my camera bag, throwing the extra lenses into the
compartments and zipping it up with trembling hands.

Marcus stood by the tripod, hyperventilating, his eyes darting
frantically from one dark corner of the hall to the other.

“The main door,” Marcus whispered, pointing toward the heavy double
steel doors at the far end of the plant—the ones we had come through
after clipping the chain on the exterior gates.

“Let’s go. Right now.”

We sprinted down the central aisle, our boots thundering against the
concrete floor.

The sound echoed off the high walls, multiplying until it sounded like
an army was running alongside us in the darkness.

We reached the double doors.

Marcus grabbed the heavy iron latch handle and yanked up.

It didn’t budge.

“It’s jammed!” he screamed, slamming his hip against the steel plate.

“It’s jammed, Leo!”

“Move!” I stepped up and threw my light on the latch.

The heavy sliding bar wasn’t rusted shut.

It had been sealed.

A thick, web-like mass of dark, fibrous material—looking like a cross
between scorched steel wool and dried tendon—had been pulled across the
seam between the two doors.

It had seeped into the keyhole, wrapped around the iron latch, and
hardened into something that looked as thick and solid as forged iron.

“No, no, no,” I muttered, grabbing the latch and pulling with all my
weight.

It felt like trying to lift a solid block of granite.

The material didn’t give.

It was warm to the touch, and when my bare fingers brushed it, a faint,
high-frequency vibration rattled through the bones of my wrist.

“The side door!” Marcus gasped, grabbing my arm.

“The loading dock by the rail spur!”

“Okay! Okay, run!”

We turned and ran along the perimeter wall, keeping close to the massive
brick pillars that supported the gantry rails overhead.

As we ran, I could hear it.

It wasn’t behind us on the floor.

It was above us.

A heavy, wet, rhythmic thunk-drag… thunk-drag… was moving along the
overhead crane tracks forty feet up in the rafters.

It moved with horrifying speed, shifting between the iron girders
without a sound except for the metallic ring of its weight flexing the
steel.

“Don’t look up!” I shouted, holding the LED light ahead of us.

“Just keep running!”

We reached the loading dock bay.

The large overhead roll-up door was shut, but next to it was a standard
metal pedestrian exit door with a crash bar.

Marcus reached it first.

He threw his weight against the crash bar.

The bar went down, but the door stayed shut.

“It’s on this one too!” Marcus yelled, kicking the bottom of the metal
door.

“It’s on all of them!”

“Look out!” I screamed.

Something came down from the dark upper rafters.

It didn’t drop like a stone; it descended like a heavy spider lowering
itself on an invisible strand of thick grease.

It hit the concrete three feet behind Marcus with a dull, wet thud that
vibrated through the soles of my shoes.

The light from my LED panel hit it directly, and for two horrifying
seconds, my mind refused to process what I was looking at.

It wasn’t an animal.

It wasn’t human.

It was a mass of segmented, wet grey tissue—roughly the size of a small
car—interwoven with twisted lengths of dark wire, shredded rubber
conveyer belts, and sharp splinters of rusted iron that seemed to have
been pulled directly into its flesh.

Dozens of long, thin, jointed limbs—made of a strange fusion of
calcified bone and bent rebar—extended from its central core, planting
themselves into the concrete with sickening precision.

Where its head should have been, there was only a wide, wet concavity
lined with fibrous, vibrating filaments—and jammed deep into the middle
of that wet mass, half-crushed and glistening with bile, was Sam’s blue
audio recording box.

The speaker on the box flickered.

“…take two… marker…” Sam’s voice burst out of the creature’s central
mass, sharp and crackling with static.

“…look more like I’m about to… mess my pants…”

Marcus let out a high, ragged shriek.

He didn’t run away—his terror locked his joints.

He stood frozen against the sealed exit door, his arms pinned to his
sides.

“Marcus! Run!” I screamed, dropping the camera bag and lunging forward
to grab him.

A long, thin segment—ending in a triple-pronged tip made of exposed
copper strand and hardened cartilage—whip-cracked through the air.

It hit Marcus squarely in the chest.

There was a wet crunch.

Marcus didn’t even have time to scream again.

The impact lifted him two feet off the floor and slammed him back
against the steel door.

Before he could fall, three more of the jointed steel-and-flesh tendrils
shot out, wrapping around his throat, his torso, and his legs.

They didn’t just pull him—they absorbed his movement, pinning him
against the creature’s central mass with a sickening, hydraulic
pressure.

“Leo!” Marcus’s voice was a choked, gurgling gasp.

His eyes bulged in the harsh beam of my light.

“LEO!”

I didn’t think.

I couldn’t process it.

Survival instinct took over, cold and absolute.

I turned and fled back into the interior of the plant.

Behind me, I heard the sound of metal tearing, the wet, heavy slide of
something massive dragging across concrete, and then Marcus’s
voice—loud, clear, and perfectly pitched—echoing from the gantry ceiling
high above me:

“…it’s a leaky pipe, Sam… it’s just a leaky pipe…”

I ran until my lungs burned with cold dust and my knees gave out.

I collapsed behind a row of heavy, rusted electrical junction boxes in
the deep sub-basement of the stamping room.

I killed the LED panel light, throwing myself into total, absolute
darkness.

I sat there in the pitch-black, my back pressed against the freezing
steel casing of a transformer, holding both hands over my mouth to
smother the sound of my ragged breathing.

The factory was alive with sound now.

Above me, on the main floor, the creature was moving.

It wasn’t hunting by sight—it didn’t have eyes.

It was responding to vibration and sound.

Every time a loose piece of sheet metal rattled in the wind outside, I
could hear the heavy thunk-drag shift toward that side of the building.

And from its central mass, it kept playing back the tape.

It played Marcus’s laugh.

It played the sound of my boots crunching on the safety glass.

It played the three-second burst of static from Sam’s microphone setup.

It was stitching them together, looping them, building a terrifying,
chaotic soundscape that bounced off the steel walls until I couldn’t
tell if the monster was fifty feet away or right on the other side of my
junction box.

“…watch the cable, Leo…” Marcus’s voice said from the darkness thirty
yards to my left.

“…relax, Sammy…” Sam’s voice answered from the rafters above my head.

I squeezed my eyes shut, tears hot against my dirty cheeks.

My chest hitched, but I kept my hands clamped over my lips.

I looked down.

In the darkness, the tiny LCD screen of my Sony FX3 was still glowing
faintly.

I had forgotten I was holding the rig; I had carried it by the top
handle the entire time I ran.

The tally light was off, but the screen was still powered.

The camera had been running in standby mode.

I reached down with trembling fingers, turned the brightness on the LCD
down to its absolute minimum, and looked at the display.

Through the infrared night-vision mode on the camera sensor—a feature I
had turned on for shot setups in the dark corners—the blackness of the
sub-basement resolved into shades of luminous green and grey.

I raised the lens slowly over the lip of the electrical box.

The basement was a forest of thick support pillars and heavy iron
drainage pipes.

Forty feet ahead of me, near the central sump pit, the creature was
coiled.

It was larger now.

It had incorporated Marcus’s green parka into its side, the fabric torn
and matted with that grey, fibrous resin.

His cell phone—still lit up with his script PDF—was partially embedded
in one of its jointed calcified limbs, the screen flickering erratically
as the creature’s internal electrical charges pulsed through it.

It was crouched over the edge of the sump pit, its thin, rebar-like
tendrils probing deep down into the black water below.

I watched through the viewfinder, terrified to move a muscle.

The creature lowered its central mass toward the pit.

It let out a long, low vibration—a sound so deep it made the metal
casing behind my back hum like a tuning fork.

Then, it pulled back.

Pinned beneath one of the heavy steel grates at the edge of the sump
pit—twenty feet below the main floor level—was a figure.

It was Sam.

My heart nearly stopped.

Sam was alive.

He was trapped in the narrow drainage trench beneath the iron grate
where he had fallen when the floor gave way under him in the boiler
room.

His clothes were soaked and torn, his face smeared with black grease and
blood, but he was breathing.

He was wedged into a space too narrow for the creature’s main limbs to
reach him, holding his breath, his eyes staring in absolute horror up
through the bars of the grate at the monster above him.

The creature probed the grate again with a copper-tipped tendril, but
the thick cast-iron bars held.

The monster let out a sharp, clicking screech—a sound made of three
different voices overlaid on top of each other—and began to scrape its
heavy front limbs against the concrete floor, searching for a way to
widen the gap.

It was going to break through the floorboards above him in less than a
minute.

I looked at the camera in my hands.

I looked at the dark exit passage twenty feet to my right—a
low-ceilinged concrete tunnel that led toward the old drainage outfall
pipe on the marsh side of the plant.

A tunnel small enough for a person to crawl through, but far too small
for the creature to follow.

I could make it to the tunnel.

I could crawl out.

If I made a sound, it would come for me instead of Sam.

If I stayed quiet, it would finish tearing up the grate, reach down into
the pit, and—

I stood up.

I didn’t think about it.

I didn’t plan it.

I just reached out, grabbed the heavy metal battery case off the top of
the electrical box, and hurled it with all my strength toward the far
corner of the basement floor.

CLANG.

The heavy metal casing hit an iron hopper fifty feet away, echoing
through the sub-basement like a gunshot.

The creature spun around instantly.

Its central mass swelled, the fibrous filaments vibrating with a
deafening, high-pitched whistle.

“LEO!” it screamed—using Marcus’s voice at maximum volume, distorted and
screaming through the audio box speaker.

“HEY!” I yelled, stepping out into the open space and raising the
camera.

The LED panel flickered to life in my hand, washing the monster in
blinding white light.

“HEY! OVER HERE!”

The creature let out a sound like a tearing metal sheet and lunged.

It moved with unbelievable speed, its jointed rebar legs slamming into
the concrete, sending sparks flying through the air.

I didn’t turn to run toward the tunnel.

I knew I wouldn’t make it.

Instead, I raised the camera to my eye, locked my finger on the red
button, and held my ground as the wall of rust, bone, and grey flesh
tore through the darkness toward me.

The lens caught everything: the wide, wet mouth of filaments; the
shredded green fabric of Marcus’s jacket; the glint of Sam’s broken
headphones; the terrible, impossible geometry of its jointed limbs
reaching out to close around the frame.

The last thing I saw through the tiny glass viewfinder was a flash of
exposed copper strand hitting the front element of the lens.

Then, the monitor went to noise.
