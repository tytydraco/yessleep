UPDATE: I left things out of the first post. I’m at the House right now
and I have the pages.

Okay. I’m typing this on my phone, at the desk, my old desk, with my
thumbs, so if it reads like a ransom note that’s why. I’m going to tell
you what I left out first, in order, because I need it in order. Then
I’m going to tell you where I am right now, which is here.

I left things out on purpose. I wanted to see if anybody who’s worked a
House would read it and say the word before I had to. A couple of you
came close. Somebody commented we have a binder too and deleted it
inside of ten minutes, and if that was you, I’m not mad, I get it, I’d
have deleted it too.

Here’s the rest.

The headcounts stopped matching in October.

Evening log: 31. Morning log: 32. I did what the card said. I wrote 31
and I did not recount, I did not, I want that on the record. What I did
instead was walk up to the second floor and count doors, because the
card said don’t recount and the card didn’t say one word about doors.

Fourteen doors. Same as September. Rooms 1 through 6 and then 8 through
15 and I had never once noticed the skip, which is the thing about a
gap, you don’t see a gap, you see whatever’s filling it. What was
filling it was a stretch of beige wall with a framed crayon drawing on
it, donated by some former resident, of a smiling clown holding hands
with a little girl in a hospital gown.

The clown in the drawing had six fingers on each hand.

Kids draw hands wrong. Kids draw everything wrong, that’s half of what’s
great about kids. I told myself that all the way down the stairs and
then I went and stood in front of the statue anyway and counted. Five.
And five. Fine. That is fine. See, it works.

The next week a boy came down.

2:40 in the morning. Maybe seven. Hospital bracelet, dinosaur pajamas,
the kind with the feet. He didn’t look at me. He walked straight across
the lobby like he had an appointment and sat down on the far end of the
bench from the clown and started swinging his legs, and I wrote 2:40 —
bench in the binder and I shut my mouth, because the card said so and
because, if I’m honest, I was afraid of what would happen if I opened
it.

He watched the front doors. I watched him on the monitor. And the arm,
the arm that had been stretched along the backrest beside him, was
behind him.

I did not see it move. I need you to understand I’m not telling you it
moved. I’m telling you at 2:40 it was beside him and at 3:15 it was
behind him, around him, the big white glove resting easy on his far
shoulder, and both of those things are true and there is nothing in
between them.

At 3:15 he got up. Walked to my desk. Put both hands on the edge and
looked up at me with the calmest face I have ever seen on a kid and
said, “Where’s my mom?”

So I walked him to Room 7.

Down the hall, past 6. I stopped at the crayon drawing because that’s
where the gap was and I did not know what I was supposed to do next,
stand there like a dope I guess, and then I realized I wasn’t looking at
a drawing. I was looking at a door. White, same as the others. Brass 7.
It had always been there, in exactly the way the gap had always been
there. He let go of my hand. He went in. I did not look inside. I don’t
know what I would’ve seen and I’ve decided I don’t want to.

I went back downstairs. I wrote walked him — came back alone — that is
fine. Then I sat with my palms flat on the desk and watched the clown’s
shoes and waited for the sun to come up and it took a very long time.

His mother came down at 7 for coffee. She was crying, and I got ready
for it, I had my whole face ready, and then she said the oncologist had
called at six. Scans were clean. Clean. They were going home. She hugged
me so hard my back popped and she said into my shoulder, this place is a
miracle, you people are a miracle.

I looked at the statue over her shoulder while she hugged me. I counted.

Five. And six.

I quit two weeks later. But I’d already found the thing I actually need
to tell you about, because the card has a source. Dolores keeps the real
handbook, not the fire-exit one, in a locked gray cabinet in the
manager’s office, and in the back of it there’s an Appendix F, and
Appendix F is forty pages, and the night before I quit I got through two
of them before her Buick pulled into the lot.

It’s blacked out. Most of it. Not with marker, printed that way, black
bars like something a senator waves around on C-SPAN, and the header’s
in a typeface that is not the Foundation’s typeface, and the words I
could make out were instances and site liaison and recovery and one
whole sentence that I have not been able to get out of my head since:

The organization’s charitable function is genuine and must be preserved;
it is the most effective containment measure yet devised.

That was November. I sat on it all winter because I’m a coward. Then I
posted the first half of this and sat on the second half for another
week because I’m a coward, and then Tuesday I heard Dolores was going to
a conference in Hartford, and I know where she keeps the cabinet key
(coffee can, second shelf, behind the Folgers), and here we are.

Got in at 11:20. Still had my fob. Nobody asked for it back when I quit,
nobody asked for anything back, not the fob or the supply closet key or
the card, and I didn’t think about that at the time and I am thinking
about it now.

Lobby lights on. Monitor on. Nobody at the desk, which, okay, when I
quit Dolores said she’d cover overnights herself till she found
somebody, and that was four months ago and I guess she never found
anybody, and Dolores is in Hartford, so who’s covering. Nobody.
Twenty-four families asleep upstairs (evening log says 24, her
handwriting, 10:04 PM) and nobody at the desk and the front door locked
and him on his bench with his arm along the back and his shoes pointed
at the door, and I stood there in my coat and counted before I did one
other thing.

Five. And six. Eleven. Okay. Okay.

The coffee can was where I said. The key was behind the Folgers. The
cabinet opened like it had been waiting to.

Appendix F is forty pages. I photographed thirty-eight before my hands
got too shaky to hold the phone still, and I’ll get the other two, I
will, but first I need to tell you about the uploading because this is
the part I need somebody smarter than me to explain. I have the photos.
They are on my phone. I’m looking at them right now in my camera roll,
forty little gray thumbnails. And every time I attach one to this post
the bar fills to 100 and then the picture’s just gone. Not an error.
Gone. Like the site politely declined. I tried imgur, I tried texting
them to my sister, I tried emailing them to myself and they go out and
they do not arrive, and I have been standing in a children’s charity at
one in the morning watching my own phone lie to me.

So I’m going to type what I can read. Most of it’s black bars, I told
you that. Here’s what isn’t.

The header on every page is a symbol. A circle with three arrows
pointing in at the middle. Not out. In. Like something closing. Under it
is a number and it’s the same number on every page, 9311, and about four
pages in I worked out that 9311 isn’t the House. It’s him. The House is
9311-14. There’s a table on page six, forty-some rows, one per House,
city blacked out but the state left in, and a column of numbers with no
heading on it. 10. 12. 31. 19. Fourteenth row, New York. (Yeah. I know
what I said. It stopped mattering about an hour ago.) Fourteenth row
says 11.

Eleven.

I counted eleven when I walked in. They know. They know what he’s got on
his hands at every House in the country and they put it in a table.

Page nine is the card. My card, all six rules, except in here it has a
title, Document 9311-A, and a line under it I had to read four times.
Developed empirically, 1996–2004. Empirically. You know what that word
means. It means they figured out the rules by watching what happened to
the people who didn’t follow them.

Page eleven I can only get pieces of. Room 7 is not to be entered by
personnel. Residents who enter Room 7 are to be logged and released
without interview. Then a long black bar and four words sticking out the
end of it like fingers: stable at one (1) per.

One what. Per what.

Page nineteen is a list. Names and dates, one column. Overnight
coordinators, 1996 to now, and I know that’s what they are because I
know the names, I know them from the red binder, the purple gel pen was
a girl named Kayleigh in 2011. Every name has a date next to it and the
word RESOLVED. Kayleigh, RESOLVED. The 2004 guy who wrote that is fine,
RESOLVED. Nineteen names.

Twentieth name is mine. The box next to it is empty.

Page twenty-two says, in its own box, bold, not one black bar on it,
like they wanted whoever opened this to be able to read it in the dark:
UNDER NO CIRCUMSTANCES RECOUNT. UNDER NO CIRCUMSTANCES COMPARE.

Compare what. Compare what to what.

And Dolores is in here. Page thirty. Site liaison, House 14, since 1996.
She told me nineteen years. I did the math on my fingers like a kid, I
did it three times, and it’s twenty-three, and I don’t know why a woman
would shave four years off a job at a children’s charity and I dont
think I want to.

1:41 AM. Lights just went out. All of them, lobby, hall, the monitor. I
did not go to the breaker. I sat in the dark in the office doorway with
my phone light off because I did not want to be the only lit thing in
that building, and I counted to sixty twice, and they came back.

His shoes are pointed at the office.

1:52. There’s a girl on the bench.

I didn’t see her come down. One second the monitor shows an empty lobby
and the next she’s there, far end from him, little, braids, hospital
bracelet, a stuffed something in her lap. It’s 1:52. The card says after
2:00. The card doesn’t say one word about 1:52, and I’m sitting here
realizing the card never once said what the rules were for, it only ever
said what to do, and I did it for eleven months like a good dog.

She’s looking at the front doors.

His arm is behind her now. I didn’t see it move. I’m not going to say
that part again, you know the drill.

2:01. She got up. Came to the desk. Put both hands on the edge. Said
where’s my mom.

I couldn’t.

I’m sorry. I’m sorry to her and I’m sorry to whoever has to deal with
whatever I just did, but I could not walk a little girl up those stairs
to a door that isn’t there with page nineteen lying open on the desk
between us. I said honey your mom’s upstairs, go on up to your room, and
she looked at me the way you look at somebody who’s got the words to a
song wrong, and then she went. By herself. Up the stairs. I watched her
on the monitor till she turned the corner past Room 6.

Then I went and looked at him. I had to.

Five. And seven.

Seven. It was six when I walked in. She went up alone and now it’s seven
and I don’t know what I did, I don’t know if I saved her or if I just
made it cost more, and the pages won’t tell me because the part that
would tell me is a black bar.

2:07. Car in the lot. Not Dolores. Gray sedan, lights off, no hurry. Two
people getting out. Nobody’s in a hurry at two in the morning unless
they already know how this goes.

I’ve got thirty-eight pages on a phone that won’t let me send them and a
laminated card in my coat pocket and I’m going out the kitchen door. If
this posts, it posts. If it doesn’t, if you’re reading this somewhere it
isn’t supposed to be, then it got out anyway and I need you to do the
one thing I asked.

Go look at his hands.

Count.

2:09.
