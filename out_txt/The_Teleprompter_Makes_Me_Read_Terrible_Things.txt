I’m a news anchor at a mid-sized local station. 47 years old. Gray
starting at the temples. The kind of face people trust enough to leave
the TV on while they make dinner. I’ve been doing the evening broadcast
for eleven years. Same desk, same lights, same routine. Smile for the
open, steady voice for the hard stories, warm close at the end.

The teleprompter has always been my safety net. Words scroll up in clean
white letters and I read them. Simple. Only it stopped being simple
three months ago.

The first time it happened I almost didn’t notice. We were coming back
from commercial. Weather was next, then sports, then a soft feature
about a local bakery. The prompter showed the usual copy. I started
reading. Then the words changed in the middle of my sentence. Not a
glitch. Not a jump. The text simply became something else while I was
looking at it. “Breaking news out of the industrial district. At
approximately 9:40pm this evening a chemical storage tank at Darrow
Chemical Supply ruptured without warning. Initial reports indicate at
least fourteen workers were inside the facility when the cloud of toxic
vapor was released. Emergency services are responding but the area has
been declared a hot zone. Residents within a two mile radius are advised
to shelter in place and seal all windows. We will bring you more as this
develops.”

I read every word. My voice stayed level. Training takes over even when
your brain is screaming. When I finished, the prompter returned to the
weather script as if nothing had happened. I glanced at the floor
director. She gave me the all clear signal. No one in the control room
said a word. The cameras kept rolling. We just went to the weather. I
walked off set after the broadcast and found the producer.

“What the hell was that about the chemical plant?”

He looked at me like I had asked about the weather in a different city.
“What chemical plant?”

I told him exactly what I had read. He pulled up the rundown. Nothing.
The teleprompter operator swore the script had never changed. The
writer, a young guy named Marcus who had been with us for two years,
insisted he had only loaded the approved copy.

I made them play back the recording. There it was. My voice, clear as
day, reading the chemical plant story that no one else remembered
putting on the prompter.

The next morning the plant exploded. Not exactly the way I had described
it. The tank didn’t rupture at 9:40 the previous night. It went up at
6:15 a.m. during the shift change. Seventeen dead. Dozens more injured.
The cloud drifted farther than anyone expected. The official report said
a faulty valve and a series of ignored maintenance warnings. No one
mentioned a news anchor reading the story the night before. I told
myself it was a coincidence. A sick, specific coincidence. I went to
work the next day and tried to forget it. Four days later it happened
again…

We were doing a live remote from a charity fundraiser when the prompter
cut away from the soft questions I was supposed to ask the organizer.
New text scrolled up.“Authorities have confirmed the identity of the man
found dead in his apartment on Eastbrook Avenue early this morning.
Thirty-four-year-old Daniel R. Romero was discovered by neighbors after
reports of a strong odor. The victim appears to have been bound and
subjected to prolonged trauma over a period of several hours.
Investigators believe the attack began sometime after midnight. No
suspects are in custody. Anyone with information is asked to contact the
tip line.”

I read it. Every clinical, horrible word.

When I finished, the prompter flipped back to the charity questions. The
organizer was still smiling, waiting for me to continue. No one on the
crew reacted. After the segment I cornered the field producer. She had
no idea what I was talking about. The recording showed me saying it. The
script files did not.

Two days later the body was found. The details matched what I had read
for the most part, just like the chemical plant explosion there were
minor differences. The police report listed the time of death as
sometime after midnight the night but onthe same I had read the story on
air.

I had Marcus fired that afternoon. I had to assume he was the one
changing the words. The small differences in the stories had at the time
given me just enough doubt to mark them in my mind as coincidences. And
I had convinced myself Marcus was sabotaging me. Marcus if you are
reading this, I’m sorry. I was wrong.

I told the news director the teleprompter operator had to go too. We
brought in a new writer and a different operator. Fresh people. Clean
scripts. I checked every line myself before we went live. It didn’t
matter.

The third incident came during a quiet Tuesday broadcast. School board
meeting coverage. Routine. Boring. Then the words changed.

“A multi vehicle collision on the interstate just south of the Newton
River bridge has left at least six people dead and several more
critically injured. Witnesses report a commercial truck crossed the
median without apparent cause and struck oncoming traffic at high speed.
The truck’s driver is among the deceased. Traffic is backed up for miles
in both directions. Emergency crews are still extricating victims from
the wreckage.”

I read it because the alternative felt worse. When I tried to stop, when
I felt the words coming and clamped my mouth shut something happened in
the studio that I still can’t explain, without sounding insane.

Everyone froze.

Not the polite stillness of people waiting for a cue. Complete
stillness. The camera operators. The floor director. The audio tech. The
producer in the control room whose face I could see through the glass.
Every single one of them stopped moving. Their eyes opened wide and
stayed that way. Tears began to run down their cheeks in continuous
streams. They did not blink. They did not breathe in any way I could
see. The only sound was the soft hum of the equipment and the faint wet
noise of tears hitting the floor. I stood there for maybe ten seconds.
It felt longer. The prompter waited. The red light on Camera 1 stayed
on. The frozen faces watched me with those wide, wet, unblinking eyes. I
started reading again. As soon as the first word left my mouth the tears
stopped. The people in the studio blinked once, almost in unison, and
went back to work as if nothing had happened. No one mentioned the
pause. No one wiped their faces. The floor director simply gave me the
signal to continue when I finished the disaster copy.

After the show I asked three different people if they had noticed
anything strange. Each one looked at me with mild concern and asked if I
was feeling all right. The playback showed the gap. Twelve seconds of me
staring at the camera in silence while the studio remained perfectly
still.

Then my voice resumed with the accident report that did not exist in any
file.

The crash happened the following afternoon. Seven dead by the final
count. The truck driver had suffered a massive stroke at the wheel,
according to the medical examiner. No one could explain why the truck
had accelerated instead of slowing.I stopped sleeping properly after
that.The pattern continued. Every few days the prompter would insert a
new horror. A house fire that would kill a family of five. A workplace
shooting that left nine dead. A bridge’s partial collapse during morning
rush hour. A child abduction that ended in the worst way possible three
days later. Each time the details were precise enough that when the real
events arrived they felt like confirmations rather than coincidences
now. Each time the written scripts and the digital files remained clean.
Each time no one in the studio remembered the copy I had read.

And every time I tried to refuse, the same thing happened. The entire
studio went still.

Tears.

Wide open eyes that did not blink.

Absolute silence except for the soft sound of crying without sobs. The
moment I resumed reading, everything returned to normal. No one ever
acknowledged the interruption. Not once. I tried everything. I started
reading from printed scripts held just out of camera view. The prompter
would still change, and the pressure in the room would build until the
freezing started. I tried ad-libbing around the bad copy. Same result. I
tried walking off the set mid-broadcast. The freezing began before I
reached the door. The tears started. The eyes stayed open. I came back
and read. I went to the station manager. I played the recordings. I
showed the timestamps of the real disasters. He listened with the
careful expression of a man who is already calculating how to handle a
liability. He suggested I take some time off. He suggested I talk to
someone. He did not suggest that the teleprompter was haunted or cursed
or possessed by something that wanted the news of coming atrocities
spoken aloud before they arrived.

I took three days. Each day I had a screaming pain in the base of my
skull. It felt like something was clawing on my brain stem. The pain
became so overwhelming, I had to go back to work. The teleprompter
beckoned me.

When I came back, it was waiting for me. Like an impatient puppy waiting
for its owner.

The disasters grew larger. A factory explosion in the next state. A
train derailment that dumped chemicals into a river. A small plane that
went down in a residential neighborhood. Each one I announced. Each one
came true within days. The body count climbed. My face on the recordings
looked the same as always. Professional, concerned, steady. Inside I was
coming apart. I started checking the prompter hardware myself. New
machine. New software. Air gapped from the network when I demanded it.
It still changed. I watched the screen with my own eyes while someone
else loaded a clean script. The words rewrote themselves in front of me,
letter by letter, as if an invisible hand were typing. No one else ever
saw it happen in real time. Only on the recordings after the fact, and
only the audio of my voice. The visual of the prompter itself never
showed the altered text in any playback we could isolate. It was as if
the machine only lied to me, and only long enough for me to speak the
lie into the world.

I had become some kind of unwilling tragedy merchant, a prophet of doom.

Last week it escalated again. The prompter began inserting personal
details. Names of victims I would later see in the papers. Exact
addresses. Times of death down to the minute. I read them because the
alternative was the frozen studio and those terrible open eyes. After
one particularly bad night, a home invasion that left four people dead
in a quiet suburb.I locked myself in the bathroom and dry heaved until
there was nothing left.

I considered quitting.

Walking away from the job, the city, the entire industry. Then I
imagined the next anchor sitting in my chair. Would the prompter simply
move on to them? Or would the freezing start in the studio the moment I
was gone, and stay that way until someone read the words? I didn’t know.
I still don’t.

Yesterday was the worst yet. We were in the final segment of the evening
broadcast. Soft close. Human interest. The prompter had been clean all
night. I almost let myself believe it was over. Then the text shifted
one last time.The words that scrolled up were longer than usual. Denser.
They did not describe a single accident or a single murder. They
described something far more bizarre.

“In the coming days the oceans will begin to change. Deep water sensors
will register movements on a scale never before recorded. Coastal
populations will report unusual tides and the appearance of vast shapes
beneath the surface. These reports will be dismissed. They should not
be. What rises will not be a whale or a previously unknown species of
any catalogued kind. It will be a great winged and tentacled beast,
larger than any structure built by human hands. It will rise from the
trench and it will not stop. Cities along every coast will fall first.
The sky will darken with its wings. The land will break under its
weight. There will be no evacuation sufficient to outrun it. There will
be no weapon equal to it. The earth as we have known it will end under
the shadow of the thing that has waited in the deep. This is not
metaphor. This is the final story. He is the Dark Priest and he will
bring this galaxy once more beneath his sway.”

I read every word. My voice did not shake until the last sentence. When
I finished, the prompter went dark for a full second before returning to
the closing script. The studio remained normal. No freezing. No tears.
Just the ordinary quiet of a broadcast ending. I do not know if that
means the last message is different, or if the thing that controls the
prompter simply no longer needs to force the issue. I am writing this
now because I do not know what else to do. The final message is still
sitting in my head the way the earlier ones did. The chemical plant. The
bound man in the apartment. The truck on the interstate. The house fire.
The train. All of them came true after I spoke them.

I do not know how much time we have. Days, the prompter said. That is
all.

If you are reading this, understand that I am not asking you to believe
in ghosts or curses or haunted machines. I am telling you that for three
months my teleprompter has made me announce catastrophes before they
occurred, and that every one of them arrived on schedule. I am telling
you that when I refused, the people around me stopped being people until
I complied. I am telling you that the scripts never contained the words
I spoke, and that no one who was in the room with me will admit anything
happened. And I am telling you the last thing it made me say.

I do not know if speaking them made it more real or if it was always
coming and the prompter only wanted a witness.

I only know that every previous message was accurate, and that this one
felt heavier than the rest.

Watch the coasts. Watch the deep-water readings if you can find them.
Watch the sky.

I have to go back to the station tomorrow. The prompter will be waiting.
I do not know what it will show me next, or if there will be a next. I
only know that I will read whatever appears, because the alternative is
those eyes and those tears and the silence that is somehow worse than
any disaster I have ever announced. If this is the last thing I am able
to post, remember what the machine made me say. He is coming.
