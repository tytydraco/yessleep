The first dent looked as though somebody had pushed a football into the
hedge and left it there.

I noticed it on Wednesday while bringing the bins in. The privet runs
across the front of my garden, between the house and the pavement. It’s
taller than me, thick enough that you can’t normally see through it. The
dent was on the garden side, about five feet up.

I thought somebody had lost a ball in there. I poked around with a
bamboo cane, found nothing, and forgot about it.

On Friday, there were shoulders.

That sounds ridiculous written down. It looked ridiculous in person,
too. An oval depression with a shallow groove extending out from either
side. Like someone had been standing with their back pressed against the
hedge for so long that it had grown around them.

Nothing had been cut. I checked the ends of the branches. No broken
wood, no clipped leaves. They curved away from the middle of the
depression, and the pale new shoots followed the curve.

I took a photo and sent it to Sam.

He’s a friend who actually likes gardening. When we first met, I asked
him whether the weeds between my paving slabs counted as a lawn. He
still brings that up.

He replied, “Someone’s making you a topiary bloke.”

He thought there would be a wire frame buried in the hedge. You can
train plants into shapes, apparently. He said he’d come over on Sunday
and have a look.

Saturday morning, an arm had appeared.

It came out from the right shoulder and followed the face of the hedge
at waist height. At the end were five narrow channels, each bordered by
little white-green shoots.

I stood at the window with my coffee going cold.

There was no trunk or branch running down the middle of that arm. The
shoots came from either side and curled around the empty space. A couple
of them extended beyond the surface of the hedge, turning back on
themselves in little hooks.

I went upstairs and looked out of the bedroom window. From above, the
shape was almost impossible to make out. Just a slightly uneven hedge.

Downstairs, it looked like someone offering me their hand.

I sent Sam another photo. This time he rang me.

“Have you found the frame?”

“No.”

“Don’t pull anything out before I get there. Some of those branches
could be under tension.”

That was sensible advice. I didn’t go near it again that day.

On Sunday, Sam arrived around eleven. He parked by the back gate and
came through the house carrying a pair of secateurs. We stood on the
front path, looking at the hedge.

He didn’t make another joke.

The fingers were much clearer now. Tiny paired leaves had opened along
their edges. Inside the depression where the head should have been, a
short twig curved around a smaller empty oval.

“An ear,” I said.

Sam leaned closer.

“Yeah.”

He took a photo from the side. Then he crouched to look underneath the
arm.

“There ought to be wire here.”

I had thick gardening gloves on. While he was looking underneath, I
reached between two of the curled shoots, thinking I could feel for
whatever they’d grown around.

My fingertips touched something smooth.

I moved them a little farther along. There was a ridge, then a hollow,
then another ridge.

Knuckles.

I could see my glove. I could see the leaves behind it. I could see Sam
crouching on the other side of the arm.

I couldn’t see what I was touching.

“Sam.”

Something closed around my hand.

It happened gently at first. The pressure of someone returning a
handshake. I remember that part very clearly because for a second I
actually relaxed. Some stupid bit of my brain was relieved to have found
a person.

Then it squeezed.

The shoots around the five channels peeled away, one after another. My
glove rose out of the hedge, pulled by something neither of us could
see.

Sam stood up.

“What are you doing?”

“Get it off.”

“What?”

“Get it off my hand.”

The depression at head height deepened on one side. The little curve
around the ear flattened out. New leaves bent backwards along a
different line.

It was turning its head.

I could hear breathing. Close, slow breathing through a nose. There was
nothing in front of my face except a few privet leaves trembling in
otherwise still air.

My hand was being crushed. I tried pulling away and was dragged a step
forward instead. My shoulder hit the hedge.

I started saying, “That’s my hand.” Over and over. I don’t know why. Sam
told me afterwards that he thought I’d caught it in some kind of trap.

He grabbed my wrist and pulled.

The thing holding me pulled back.

For a few seconds, Sam and I were both leaning away from the hedge,
heels scraping the path. The oval around the head tilted towards us. I
heard a breath drawn in sharply, right beside my ear.

Then my glove came off.

I fell backwards into Sam. We both went down hard.

The glove stayed in the air.

Its cuff hung open. The fingers were folded into the palm, clenched so
tightly that the leather creaked.

Sam scrambled up first and hauled me towards the house.

I looked back once.

The hedge was straightening. Branches sprang forward into the empty
outline, filling in the head and shoulders. The glove hung a little
farther out from it now.

Below it, two patches of grass folded flat.

One lifted again. Another patch flattened nearer the path.

We got inside. Sam shut the front door and turned both locks.

My hand hurt too much to close properly. There were red marks across the
back of it and a deep indentation at the base of my thumb. He got a bag
of frozen peas from the kitchen and wrapped it in a tea towel.

For a while we just stood in the hall.

Sam kept looking at the locks. I kept looking through the little pane of
glass beside the door. The hedge looked ordinary again. There was
nothing on the front path.

My glove was gone.

“We need to ring someone,” he said.

I asked him what we were going to say.

He got his phone out anyway. Before he could dial, he stopped looking at
the screen.

The door to the front room was open. From where we stood, we could see
the basil plant on the windowsill.

I’d bought it from the supermarket on Tuesday. It was still in its
plastic pot, standing in a cereal bowl because I hadn’t found a saucer.

The leaves on the room side were bending down.

Slowly. Separately.

Five little grooves formed across the top of the plant.

Then they shifted sideways.

The stems sprang upright behind them. Another cluster of leaves folded
over.

Sam took my good arm.

We went out through the kitchen, across the back garden, and into his
car. He didn’t stop to shut the gate.

I’m at his house now. My hand is bruised, but I can move all my fingers.
He has the photographs on his phone. I’ve looked at them so many times
that I can see the outline when I close my eyes.

I used to think the strangest part was how quickly the hedge had grown.

Now I keep thinking about the basil. How little of whatever touched it
we could see. How it moved across the leaves and then continued beyond
them.

There was nothing there to show us where it went next.

Sam has a big fern in his kitchen. We’ve put it on the table between us.
Nothing has bent or flattened so far.

When he sat down to read this, I asked him to move his chair closer to
the plant.

He started to laugh, then moved it.

I’m watching the leaves behind his head. He’s watching the ones behind
mine.
