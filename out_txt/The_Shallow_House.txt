It was go time.

We started in my neighborhood and branched out from there. House by
house, street by street, passing blacked-out houses without the barest
attempt to throw up a decoration or two, houses whose denizens had long
since given up the hopes of being visited, and the occasional Halloween
party - lights and music booming away into the night, oblivious to the
outside world. Despite his initial lack of enthusiasm, Marc wasn’t wrong
about the candy. Of the few houses that kept their lights on at this
hour, most were just eager to get rid of the stuff. They cared… not at
all about our age or how we carried ourselves, most people were just
happy to see anyone out at all these days - or they were desperate to
unload the remnants of their bowls into our pillowcases.

That was the thing. It was quiet. Empty. I remembered when I was a kid -
like, actually a kid. I remembered streets crowded with kids up until
practically midnight, regardless of whether or not it was a school
night. Nearly every house decked out - or close to, anyway - with morbid
decorations. Every now again, we passed a small group of older kids, or
a family of night owls pounding the pavement, but… it was almost sad, in
a way.

It was the first realization I’d had that night. It wasn’t something
that I’d ever noticed before, but now that it was almost over for us, I
thought about how there’d be one less group of kids out there knocking
on doors, yelling “Trick or treat!” at adults who probably just wanted
to go to bed and being rewarded for their efforts.

The second thing was, Marc was right about something else, even if he
probably… didn’t intend to be. We didn’t have to pretend to be twelve.
That feeling just came naturally with the season. We might not’ve gotten
the best haul, and it might not’ve been the busiest or most enthusiastic
Halloween of our lives, but over the course of that night, things
softened. In spite of the darkened streets, everything seemed a little
brighter. Any and all conflict over the costume situation died as we
started to reminisce,- and Marc stopped gazing longingly at the roaring
parties we passed by when we brought up the year my mom made me dress up
like a pumpkin.

I was eleven. I wanted to be, ironically enough, Ghostface.

That made him laugh, and from there, everything just fell into place.
Clicked like it should. We joked about the few kids we saw in
embarrassing costumes, we pointed out cool houses and tried the doors,
even if the lights were long extinguished. Lori’s eyeliner got smudged,
and it looked like someone’d went and decked her in the face. We laughed
about that too, and slowly, over time, our pillowcases only grew in
size. It was getting late now, late enough that most of the straggler
houses had put out their lights. It was bittersweet, the whole thing. I
was glad we did this, glad we had one last hurrah, but I knew now that
we’d probably never get another shot. This, as far as I was concerned,
was the end of Hallowe-

“Hey,” Marc startled me out of my thoughts, “What about that one?” With

Despite the lack of porch light, there was… something else I noticed

“Jesus, what is wrong with you guys? It’s a bowl of candy, who cares?”

towards us. Grinning, he reached inside and stuffed the last two

care about any of this Halloween crap. It’s for kids. I just thought

“Because I thought we’d have fun. I didn’t think I’d get lectured like
you’re my mom. First it was the costume, now this shit. Go figure - I

“Yeah… fun.” Lori said quietly, her eyes cast downwards towards the
sidewalk. “I guess I was wrong too.”

The walk home was awkward, to say the least. None of us talked. We
mostly kept our heads low, stared at the ground. Our bags were full,
sure, swaying and heavy - but there was dirty candy in there. Didn’t

bit, but I could tell Lori was pissed. Would barely look at him.

The original plan was that we’d have a sleepover. Make some pizza, hell
maybe we’d watch Scream - but any notion of a fun hangout was dashed.
Lori just grabbed her stuff and walked home, but Marc didn’t have that
luxury. His parents were long asleep, which meant we were stuck. There
was no pizza, there was no movie, just silence - save for the occasional
nod, grunt, or other noncommital form of acknowledgment. I wasn’t that
mad at him, and I doubted he was that mad at me, but I could tell he
probably thought he made an ass out of himself.

He was right, but it was fine. I didn’t care. I gave him space anyway.
We set up on the couches, and that was that. We went to sleep.

Fuck. Alright, that was it. I didn’t know what the fuck he was even
talking about. Was he sleepwalking? Just crashing out? Whatever. I
headed upstairs, got my shoes on, threw on my coat, and hit the pavement
on my bike. Fifteen minutes - that’s not far. I doubted he was running,
and even on the best of days, he wasn’t exactly an Olympic-level
sprinter. It had gotten colder since earlier in the night, the wind
biting at my skin as I pedaled.

I knew the direction he’d have to go to get home, at least, so I
followed the trail I knew, through the various neighborhoods. Left, and
right and… there was no sign of him. Not that I could see.Did he manage
to reach the ravine already? That would’ve been impossible, even on a
bike I’d have to ride for half an hour at least. Where the hell did he
go?

I got my answer when I passed the road that ended with the Shallow
House.

I squeezed my brakes, the sudden stop almost throwing me over the
handlebars. “Marc!” I yelled, “What are you doing?” He was standing on
the patio, right about where the candy bowl was. Standing there, his
back to me, doing… nothing. “Fuck,” I whispered under my breath, leaning
my bike against the nearest streetlight and scrambling towards the
house. “Hey! Dude! C’mon! Like I said, I’m not mad at you! Just come
back, maybe I can get my mom to take you home, I know it’s late but…”

I trailed off as I walked closer, as I noticed something. The door was
open. I’d never seen that door open, I’d never seen anyone come or go,
I’ve never seen anyone live there.

“Hey… Dude, just… come on.” My breath was ragged and my nerves were
firing on all cylinders. “Get away from there, man.” I reached out a
hand, and froze as he turned to look back at me. His eyes weren’t right.
His pupils were huge, like after you go to the eye doctor - and his
expression just… wasn’t. It wasn’t a neutral expression, like a blank
face. It looked like the kind of face you make when you’re in a staring
contest and trying not to laugh - but he didn’t look like he was going
to laugh. He looked like he wanted to cry, like his eyes were really
hurting.

“Home,” he said in a raspy, breathless voice - before he walked straight
in.

“Marc!” I called out, catapulting over myself as I ran up the walkway.
My body, my brain, and whatever constitutes a human soul were all
screaming at me to back off, that this was the Shallow House, that this
place was bad news. That something was very, very wrong. The stairs
creaked beneath my feet. One step, then another. I was standing on the
patio then - and I could see inside. It was dusty, unmaintained with
rotting hardwood and. No one had lived here, not for years at least. It
was just an empty, abandoned house - but the dread I felt cut through my
nerve endings like a knife.

I saw Marc there, his hand on a doorknob in the hallway only a few feet
from the door. “Marc!” I called again from the patio. “Okay, yeah you
got me. You’re right I’m a pussy, you freaked me out. Now please just,
get the fuck out of the-“

I didn’t have time to finish. With one more of those strange,
imperceptible looks backwards, he turned the doorknob and opened the
door. Red light spilled out from the frame, a sickly, pulsating color
that lit up his face in such a horrible way. It was no color I’d seen
before. No color I’d seen since. Seconds inched by like eons, and he
spoke - the last word I’d ever hear him say.

“One.”

He took a step, and descended the stairs.

My own terror be damned, the adrenaline finally did its work. I bursted
through the Shallow House’s doorframe like a heat-seeking missle,
hurtling towards that hallway door - but my feet met only concrete
beyond its frame. There was no light, there were no stairs. There was
just me, a shitty old house, and an inaccessible basement, filled up to
the floorboards above with cement.

I turned around, and I went home. What else was there to do?

No one ever believed me about Marc. About the Shallow House - except for
Lori, but even then I could tell that as the years went by, she started
to believe the story that everyone else did. Marc left in the middle of
the night to walk home after an argument, and he was never seen again.
Maybe he got abducted, maybe he fell down the ravine and the animals got
him. Maybe he just ran away.

Sure enough, we never did Halloween again, at least not
trick-or-treating. I’d stay home and hand out candy, maybe put on a
horror movie in the background - but no matter what, I’d make sure to go
out for a walk past that house every year. Just to see, to check if the
bowl’s there. It always is, and sometimes I see kids with their parents
walk up to that bowl and take a single piece - just like you’re supposed
to. Sometimes I see older kids mostly, grabbing handfuls at a time.
Sometimes I wonder about them, about what happens to them later.

I think, if I’m ever a parent, I’ll raise my kids like the former group.
I’ll teach my kids the rules of trick-or-treating, rules so old and
central to the world that even the Shallow House itself seemed to
respect that age old mantra displayed on every sign in every bowl left
standing on a patio. I don’t know what’s in that house, in that
basement. I don’t know what puts the bowl out - and not for lack of
trying to catch a glimpse each October.

Whatever it was though, it knew the rules, and it followed them- just
like you should. See, Lori, Marc and I… No matter how we obtained it,
willingly or not, we all walked home with the candy from that bowl.

But the Shallow House? Well… it only took one. Thank god for that.
