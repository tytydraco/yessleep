His front porch had this weird burnt plastic smell. I remember thinking
that during our initial conversation as I pick up his package. Mr. Gondo
was a nice man. He was old and kind of burly, like a Pixar dad that
Facebook moms would make idols of. He had never fit into that weird
neighbor stereotype, and I had known him for about 3 years now since I
moved into the house across the street. So when he asked me to watch his
packages for a week or two, I didn’t think anything of it. I had moved
into the decent, one-story, brick house across the street three years
ago to focus on my music. I was pretty good, not Coachella-bound or
anything yet, but I had a good following and I had basically headlined a
few popular festivals in recent years. It’s essentially been a studio
for me, since my move. Any room that couldn’t fit a bed in it was a
routing space for decks, amps, speakers, compressors, etc. The “home
office” had been completely gutted and turned into my main recording
area. I kept a bedroom and the kitchen the same, and took mighty fine
care of whatever section of the living room didn’t have a guitar or a
notebook lying around in it at the time. Mr. Gondo had seen into the
place before. I never let him inside given the loose cables scattered
across the floor that made every room and hallway a death trap.

“You’re very sweet, Mr. Gondo, but I really don’t need payment.”

My smile is so sweet I can almost taste it in my mouth. I remember the
way he smiled and thanked me for the declined payment, like I was doing
the old man a favor by not taking his money. It was during that
conversation that I remember getting the burnt plastic smell from his
wooden porch.

“So, you take the box and just slide it through that there garage
window.”

He pointed to the perfectly removed glass on the rightmost window of his
garage door.

“The Amazon-ers never listen to the directions on the app!”

‘Amazon-ers’, is what he called Amazon delivery drivers. Why in the
world he couldn’t leave these on his porch, or even have me take them to
my place for the week was truly beyond me. We lived in a rich, white
neighborhood where the most severe offenses were HOA violations. But
nonetheless, I agreed to slide his packages into the garage while he was
cross-country at a granddaughter’s wedding.

The first day came, and I was on his front porch by 5pm, about 2 hours
after his package had been delivered. It was a small box. One of the
more square ones. It was damp and cold to the touch, and it immediately
(rather forcefully) explained that porch smell to me in excruciating
detail.

“Ugh… The hell are you ordering, Gondo…?” I muttered to myself, forcing
a gag back as I carried the box to the garage door.

It hit the cold stone ground quietly, or at least the box was so wet
that it cushioned its own fall. I stood there for a second, facing the
tan metal door. When the realization hit me that I was done, I turned
and went back to my house. I stepped over the wires to get to my
kitchen, where I immediately scrubbed the wet feeling off my hands. I
made a displeased sound once more as I rethought my agreement to do
this. Even if this were an unsafe neighborhood, I’m sure any package
thief would also have had second thoughts about snatching some wet box.
The worst crime I had ever heard of here were these campfire stories
about a little girl who was abducted while playing outside of the house
I live in now. She was a sweet little blonde girl with red polka-dot
bows in her hair. It almost killed the poor people here when she got
kidnapped. That was almost 5 years ago now, and as far as I could tell,
it was the most excitement any of my prune juice-guzzling neighbors had
in a long time.

The next few days are pretty rinse and repeat. I grab the package,
disregarding the smell and feel of it, and drop it through the window.
On Wednesday, the rinse and repeat came to a full stop. I grabbed the
package as usual, carrying it over to the window. I drop it through the
missing glass and hear it hit the ground. But that’s what stopped me.
The ground. I had dropped these packages in the exact same spot every
night for 3 nights. So why the hell would the boxes not be landing on
each other?

I try to beat the curiosity. I look behind me at my warm house and try
to force myself to walk across the street to it.

But I can’t.

Before the job, Mr. Gondo had very explicitly told me not to look
through the window I dropped the boxes into. At the time, I assumed it
was some embarrassing old person stuff. Rifled through porn mags lying
around, old photos of an ex-wife, boxed wine. Mr. Gondo quite liked me,
and seemed to care what I thought of him. So it made sense he wouldn’t
have wanted me to see something like that.

I tried my best to respect his wishes. I really tried. But by the time I
had lifted myself up to the window to look in, the only thing I was
trying not to do was throw up. The sickness that hit my stomach was
heavy. Like, physically heavy. I fell backwards onto the ground of the
driveway, almost immediately vomiting on the white stone.

“What the fuck was that?”

The question was tearing my mind and body apart as I tried to swallow
down the next round of bile climbing my stomach.

Inside the garage weren’t porn mags or boxed wine. Instead, a lanky,
pale figure with thin strands of blonde hair crawled around on the cold
ground. There was a collection of discarded wet cardboard behind it,
almost three days’ worth. I watched it snatch the box I had just placed
and immediately start ripping into it with its huge mouth. Its maw went
from ear to ear. Jagged, discolored teeth filled it in rows. Some of
them looked like they had come from other animals. Its arms and legs had
scars at the knees and elbows that signified dismemberment. Connected to
these scarred parts were long bony limbs, maybe human, maybe horse;
neither made the predicament any less disturbing. Its eyes had been
stitched shut, and little black pupils were carelessly scattered around
the rest of the face. The way it tore at the box told me that the
wetness was from some sort of food.

I practically crawled my way back to my place. I tripped over wires that
I swore I had memorized as I stumbled my way to my room. I covered my
mouth, the sick feeling still ravaging me. I fumbled around my bedsheets
and found my phone, calling the police with shaky hands. I could barely
make my emergency out to them, but they must have understood me because
they arrived in minutes.

I didn’t sleep that night. I sat in bed, sick, seeing such a disgusting
creature writhing on the garage floor of a man I had trusted.

Mr. Gondo never got back from his trip. My best guess is that he fled
the country, hoping whatever he had created in that garage would’ve
killed me after I found it.
