It started because I was bored. That is the whole reason. I want that on
the record before anything else,

because everybody who reads this is going to want a better reason than
that, and there isn’t one.

I was in my room at twenty to midnight with the ani song called from<
Macross F>, a Japanese animation of the early 20th century

had stopped reading. I had nothing due until Thursday. I was scrolling
the way you scroll when you are

not looking for anything, and the title went past and I went back up to
it.

SHADOWMAN. All capitals. Underneath, in the first line, “please read the
steps twice before you do

anything.” Which is exactly what somebody writes when they want you to
read the steps.

I read it the way you read a menu in a restaurant you are not going to
eat at.

The post said he was a man once. It said he was studying an old book of
black magic, and the book shut

him inside a mirror, and that is what he is now — something that only
exists in reflections and cannot

leave the glass. It said he wears a black raincoat and a white mask, and
that under the raincoat his skin is

wound around with yellow and black hazard tape, and that every strip of
it is a summoner he took.

Somebody had clearly enjoyed writing that. I remember thinking: whoever
this is, they have a better eye

than most of the people on this board.

Then the steps. Four of them, and I can still do them from memory, which
I hate.

An empty toilet with a mirror. Nobody else in the building.

A candle, lit in front of the mirror. And a small cup of tomato juice,
because it is his favourite drink. That

was the line that made me laugh, and the reason I remembered the whole
thing. It is such a stupid

detail. It is also, if you think about it for two seconds, not stupid at
all.

Then: face the mirror and say it, slowly.

SHADOWMOON, SHADOWMOON, WHERE ARE YOU?

SHADOWMAN, SHADOWMAN, GLAD TO SEE YOU.

SHADOWMAN, SHADOWMAN, WHERE ARE YOU?

SHADOW MAN, SHADOWMAN, COME FOR YOU.

And then the fourth step. Pick up the candle and run, because he hunts
you until sunrise, and if the

candle goes out while you are running, he comes over you, and the only
thing you can do is pray.

The park near my flat closes at ten. The main gate is chained and the
little side gate has never once

latched properly, which is the kind of thing you know if you have walked
home through there at night.

There is a toilet block at the end of the path with one tube light over
the door that has been buzzing

since before I moved in.

I put on the jade pendant my grandmother gave me when I turned eighteen.
I am not going to pretend

that means anything. I have never worn it. I put it on because I was
going to do something stupid and it

felt like the only thing I could bring.

Inside it smelled like wet cement. Four sinks, four mirrors, one tube
stuttering every few seconds. The

water in the basins had not drained. I put the candle on the ledge
between two basins and lit it, and

then I poured the tomato juice into a paper cup I had brought from home,
and I stood there feeling like

an idiot with a paper cup.

The first time through the rhyme I got the first word wrong. I said
“shadowm” and stopped, because I

had read it as shadowman and it is not shadowman, it is shadowmoon,
twice, before it ever gets to his

name. You try saying a made-up word in an empty tiled room and see how
far you get without feeling

like you are about to be laughed at by a building.

I got through it. Then I waited.

Nothing. The tube buzzed. Somewhere a tap was dripping into a basin, and
the sound took a second to

come back off the walls. And I laughed at myself — one short laugh, out
loud, the way you laugh at your

own joke in an empty room — and it bounced off the tiles about four
times before it died.

That is when I looked at the mirror instead of at the cup.

The third mirror had fogged at the corner, the way they do, and the fog
was moving. Not drifting.

Moving, like something on the other side had just breathed on it. And
then there was a shape inside the

fog, and the shape had a white face, and the face was looking over my
shoulder at the back of my head.

I did not turn around. I want to be honest about that part. Everything
in me said that if I turned around I

would be looking at the exact place where he was not.

I picked up the candle and I walked out of that room, and then I ran.

The path is about two hundred metres to the side gate, with trees on
both sides. I got about forty

metres before I heard a footstep behind me that stopped when I stopped.
I have never understood why

that is the thing that gets you. Not the fog. The interval.

I called June. June is the person you call at three in the morning
because she picks up, and she does not

ask what is wrong, she tells you what to do. I said I had done something
stupid from a forum and

someone was behind me, and she said: keep the candle up. Do not put it
down. Get inside something

and wait for the light.

She did not ask me if I was joking. That is the moment I understood how
I sounded.

Then the footsteps stopped being behind me, because they were not behind
me any more.

I turned round. He was between the trees, dead centre of the path, maybe
ten metres, not moving at

all. The candle lit him from below, which is the worst way to light
anything. The tape at his wrists caught

it — yellow and black, and clean, and wound tight, like he had rewound
it recently.

I ran, and he came after me, and he was not fast, he was just always
there.

He did not swing at me. I want to be clear about that too, because it is
the only reason I am writing this.

He was going round a tree to cut me off and he put the knife into the
trunk to swing himself past it

faster, and it went in and it stayed in, and he had to stop and use both
hands to get it out.

That is how I got to the toilet block.

I went into the last stall and put my back against the door and sat down
with the candle between my

feet. He started at the far end. Bang. Bang. Not fast. He had all night
and I had until about six.

I could see the gap under the door — two fingers wide, I measured it
later with my own hand — and his

shadow going across it.

Then June called, and her ringtone is that same song, because I set it
years ago when it still made people

laugh on the bus, and I never changed it back.

The screen lit up my whole stall, and the ringtone went out into the
tiles and came back twice as big. I

got my thumb on it and pressed decline, twice, hard. Too late. Half a
second of that ringtone had already

gone out of my stall and down the room.

The feet stopped. Turned. Came back along the row, and stopped on the
other side of my door, and the

gap went dark.

I put my hand over my mouth. I would like to say I prayed. What I
actually did was count.

He kicked the door. I do not remember deciding to do this, but I pulled
the door open at the same

moment, and his boot went through the space where the door had been, and
his whole weight went

with it. I went past him along the sinks and out of the building.

I got as far as the low fence at the edge of the park and my ankle went,
and that was that.

I sat against the fence with the candle in both hands, and the phone was
in the puddle beside me, and

then it said my name.

It said it was over. It said he was gone. It said it four times, in
June’s voice, from the phone on the

ground, and I was so relieved that my whole body went cold and then warm
and I laughed and I said

thank you, thank you, and I let my breath all the way out.

The candle went out on that breath. My own. Not the wind, not the rain.
Mine.

I opened my eyes, and he was standing over me, close enough that I could
see the seam where the mask

meets the hood.

That is when I finally understood the one thing I already knew better
than I know my own name. I hung

up on June twenty minutes ago. Nobody knows where I am. Nobody was ever
going to tell me anything.

The laughing started coming out of him, in her voice, and then it kept
going, and by the end of it the

voice was strange.

I am going to press post. If it goes up, it went up before he did.
