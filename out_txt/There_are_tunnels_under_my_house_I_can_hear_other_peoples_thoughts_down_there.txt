We had just come home when our cat-sitter Sofia met us at the door, her
lips parted, ready to speak.

“Welcome back! Hey, I wanted to mention something. I heard some strange
noises while you were gone.”

“What kind of noises?” I asked, setting my keys on the coffee table.

“Like something shuffling around. It sounded like it was coming from
underneath the house. I actually went looking for the basement, but I
couldn’t find a way down.”

We didn’t have a basement.

“Was it loud?”

“Not really. I only heard it once or twice at night when things were
quiet. It was probably just some animal.”

She rubbed at her temple, then glanced toward the hallway.

“Oh, and I’ve been getting these random headaches. I’m thinking maybe
I’m allergic to cats. I’ve never really spent this much time around
one.”

Jacob stepped in behind me and set his suitcase down.

“Something underneath the house?” he asked.

“Yeah, but it’s probably nothing,” Sofia said.

He nodded, then made little “psst psst” sounds and looked underneath the
couch and around the corners.

“Thanks for holding down the fort,” he said before disappearing down the
hall to search for Ruffle.

I figured the sound had carried in from somewhere outside.

***

“Mariana, wake up.” It was Jacob. He shook me gently but persistently.
“Don’t you hear that?”

It was dark when I opened my eyes. Jacob wasn’t beside me. An outline
bled from the darkness, an elongated shape on the floor like spilled
laundry. The shape didn’t move. Neither did I.

I clicked on the bedside lamp. Jacob lay stomach-flat on the bedroom
floor, his ear pressed to the ground.

I cleared my throat to speak, but Jacob shushed me.

I slid out of bed as quietly as I could and assumed the same position as
him. I heard a noise like something patting across stone. Thud, thud.
Pause. Thud, thud. The sound grew fainter, as though whatever made it
were moving away beneath us, until it disappeared entirely.

Then two more thuds came from somewhere close again. I glanced at Jacob.
He was still listening, his ear pressed flat to the floor.

We fell asleep on the floor, still waiting for the noise to resume.

***

We woke up on the bedroom floor the next morning, stiff and exhausted,
having managed only a couple of hours of sleep. Jacob remained there for
some time, his ear pressed to the floor, listening for the sound to
return.

“It must be some animal, right?” I said. “A groundhog?”

Jacob bared his teeth in a pained smile. “It sounded kind of big. More
like someone running than an animal scratching around. But maybe.”

“Some weird ventilation thing? We’re catching sounds from outside and
hearing them through some… vent.” I knew it wasn’t very convincing.

“I don’t know of any vents beneath the bedroom. There shouldn’t be
anything under us except the foundation.”

We went outside and checked along the house, looking for holes or signs
of animal damage. We examined the foundation, the places where pipes
entered the house, and the narrow strips of dirt along the walls.
Nothing appeared disturbed. We even looked for vents or a crawl space we
might have overlooked.

The garage was the last place we checked.

Our garage is small, crowded with clutter inherited through time. I
passed a mountain of bags, an old soggy box melting into the ground, and
moved three bikes out of the way.

Near the back wall, I noticed a slight difference in the coloring of the
floor. It continued beneath an old dresser pushed against the corner.

I called Jacob over, and together we slid the dresser away from the
wall.

The floor beneath it was coated in dust. I knelt and wiped some away,
revealing the edge of a wooden panel flush with the concrete. There was
no handle, only a narrow seam, like a zipper on flesh.

I tried slipping my fingers beneath the edge, but there was nowhere to
grip. I found a screwdriver and worked its point into the small opening.
The wood resisted at first. I pressed harder, felt something give, and
pulled upward.

The floor split open, leaving a dark wound at my feet.

***

“Do we call someone?” I said.

“Who would we call?”

“I don’t know. Someone who deals with foundations?”

Jacob crouched beside the opening. “Let’s at least see what’s down there
first.”

I nodded. “Maybe I should wait up here. In case I need to call for help
or anything.”

He chuckled a bit, then grabbed a flashlight and pointed it into the
opening. Little wooden steps peered from the dark. Jacob pressed his
foot to a step, testing its integrity. It squeaked but held his weight
as he started down.

I watched as the illumination from his flashlight disappeared beneath
the floor of the garage. His footsteps grew quieter. He was swallowed by
the square of black ink at my feet. I held my breath, listening for him
to reach the bottom, for the scrape of his boots.

“What do you see?” I shouted down into the hatch. His footfalls had
stopped. He had either reached the bottom or decided he couldn’t go
further. When he didn’t respond, I shouted once more. “Hey! What’s down
there?” I waited. “Is it a basement or what?”

His voice came slowly. “Like a… big hallway,” he said. I could hear he
was already heading back up. Reaching the top of the hatch, he placed
his hands on the garage floor and pulled himself out. He sat staring
into the opening for a moment.

“A hallway? Like a tunnel?” I asked. “Maybe it’s an old root cellar that
they built over.”

“I don’t know. It went further than my flashlight could even reach. It
looked like it was carved out carefully. Support beams and all that.”

“I want to see.” I held out my hand for the flashlight.

Jacob hesitated. “It doesn’t seem like a good idea to go further in
there. We don’t know how stable any of it is.”

I snagged the flashlight from him and crawled down into the hatch.
Jacob’s voice became quieter above me as the outside world shrunk to a
pinhole. I could only hear my breath playing back to me as if on a
recording, an unfamiliar breath over the phone. I wondered whose it was.

Under my feet was dusty ground, unfinished floor. Above me, wooden
support beams and cut stone. I touched the wood expecting rot to give
way under my touch. The tunnel smelled of sawdust, of fresh wood. It
felt new and maintained as though someone often came through to check
that everything was safe. This was my first impression. Safe.

Jacob kept shouting for me to come back up. I ignored him, wanting to
continue into the dark and see what was ahead, but he wouldn’t stop. I
heard him, then, thudding down the stairs.

I continued down the tunnel, listening to his footsteps behind me. The
thought of him catching up irritated me. He would turn me around, take
me back to the surface before I’d seen anything. I walked faster. His
footsteps grew closer. I couldn’t let him stop me. Not yet. I began to
sprint down the tunnel.

I didn’t make it far before I realized how insane I was, running off
into unknown tunnels. I stopped and waited for him to catch up. The
urgency left me almost immediately. Being there still felt terribly
important, but I couldn’t have said what I’d been running toward.

“What are you doing? This whole place could just collapse on you,” he
said. His voice was a whisper. Who did he think would hear us? “Why did
you run?”

“I just wanted to see what was down here. I’m going further in. Come
with me or go back… or just… wait there. Whatever.”

He sighed and continued behind me as we walked slowly down the hall.

The hall was generous when it needed to be. The walls had periods of
compression, where our shoulders touched against the sides just enough
to make your breath stall, just enough for a birth canal, for a rat
through a pipe, before they would dilate into a small antechamber. We
stepped into the open space. I illuminated the corners and noticed four
recesses cut into the walls, their wooden frames reminiscent of
confessional booths. I pointed them out to Jacob.

I couldn’t see his expression well, but he shook his head. “No. I don’t
like it.”

He remained near the entrance as I stepped forward. The space was quiet.
I could hear Jacob swallow and moisten his lips.

None of the confessional booths had doors, just open archways. Creeping
ahead, I shone my light into one. The flashlight caught on something
reflective. A metal bench. Moving closer, I saw that the recess was
otherwise empty.

“Just a place to sit,” I said. I walked inside and noticed something
strange. A long cylindrical protuberance jutted from a nearby wall like
a lost root or buried cable.

I set my flashlight down so it shone towards the ceiling, then sat on
the bench. Darkness encased me as Jacob called to me from behind a wall
of water. I touched the strange hanging object. I could feel, at its
end, a small speculum like an otoscope the doctor would insert into your
ear. The thing wavered when I touched it, swaying gently but always
resting back in the same position it started. I leaned close to try and
look inside, but the opening was too small, and it was far too dark. I
couldn’t see anything.

My ear began to itch as I moved closer. I heard a light noise. Wind, or
static, or far-off voices, whispers outside the door, or someone
whispering near your window when it is late and you are about to fall
asleep. I leaned closer to hear the noise. Jacob was saying something,
but I couldn’t make out the words. I leaned closer still, until he broke
my reverie.

“Hey, I think it’s time we go back.” He looked back and forth. “I don’t
think we should be here.”

I stood up, disoriented. I took a moment to gather myself, to see again
where I was, to nod and say, “Yeah, let’s go back.”

***

“It’s now or never,” I said as Jacob woke. “You’re back to work
tomorrow, so we need to get down there again. I can’t stop thinking
about it.”

Jacob brushed me away and tried to pull the sheets over himself, but
with enough persistence, I got him out of bed with the promise of ham
and eggs for breakfast.

“We’ll need some more stationary lights. There are some old lanterns in
the garage we could bring, and even some of those stick-on wall lights,”
Jacob said as he cut into a piece of ham.

I jabbed at the air with my fork. “I’ll bring a knife in case we
encounter… whatever made those noises.”

We both stayed quiet for a bit at that.

“It could just be an animal that got trapped down there,” I continued.
“Probably got in from a different opening.”

Jacob nodded and leaned back in his chair. He rubbed at his beard.

“I’ve been thinking about that hallway,” he said. “The one that
continued past those benches. I wonder how far it goes.”

“I thought you didn’t want to go back.”

“I didn’t. I still don’t know if it’s safe.” He paused. “But someone
built all that. I’d like to know where it leads.”

I pushed my plate away. “Then let’s go.”

“Not without some preparation. We should bring a spool of string,
something to follow back if we get turned around. And we stay together
this time.”

“Fine.”

Jacob got up and disappeared down the hall.

“Yeah, I’ll just clean up then,” I called after him.

He came back with a large legal pad, a clipboard, and markers.

“For drawing the map,” he said, setting them on the table. He opened the
pad and began sketching what we had already seen.

***

Lanterns, stick-on lights, snacks, water, the knife, Jacob’s notepad,
and a spool of string filled a hiking bag.

We made our way to the hatch. It felt much easier the second time since
we had some idea of what to expect. Jacob went down first. The wooden
steps were too narrow to manage with the bag, so I stood above and
waited for him to reach the bottom.

While I stood waiting, I felt Ruffle weaving around me, brushing against
my legs. I bent to pet him, then my stomach dropped. I scooped him up
and hurried him back inside. It hadn’t occurred to me he might go down
there.

When I returned, Jacob was calling up from below. I dropped the supply
bag down to him and followed.

The place already felt more like a basement than some mysterious
chamber. I imagined showing a friend the hatch, enjoying her disbelief.
She would be too scared to go down, complaining that her head hurt, that
it itched behind her eyes, that nausea overtook her in the garage.

I remembered the anticipation of our first descent, when I didn’t yet
know what was waiting beneath our house. I wanted to feel that again.

Jacob took to one of the benches and pulled out the legal pad. He drew a
small dot for where we started, traced two parallel lines for the
corridor, then a square opening from those lines where he wrote ‘Chamber
1.’ Inside the square, he marked four spots with rectangles to symbolize
the benches.

Before we left, he stopped beside one of the benches and examined the
metal fixture where the cylindrical thing emerged from the wall. He ran
his thumb across a small engraving.

“Alethophone,” he read aloud.

“What?”

“That’s what it says here.” He shone his flashlight across the
lettering. “Alethophone.”

I looked at the word, then at the thing hanging beside it. Jacob copied
it onto his legal pad.

“Getting familiar already,” he said as he capped the marker. “Well,” he
gestured further down the hallway. “Shall we?”

The next stretch of hallway looked similar but, after a few steps, it
split three ways. Jacob put an arm across my chest, stopping me in
place.

“This seems a bit bigger than I imagined.”

He looked from one passage to another, shining the flashlight into each.
I could see little beyond where the light reached.

I glanced at the different paths. I felt hesitant but excited, spurred
on. I closed my eyes for a second, tried to let some form of intuition
take me the right way. My head felt hot for a moment, and I heard
something like a whistle down the rightmost path.

“Did you hear that?”

Jacob was drawing the branching paths on his legal pad.

“Can you knock it off and pay attention?” I reached out and lowered the
map. He looked at me, confused.

I pulled the spool of string from the bag and secured one end around a
wooden support near the junction.

“This way.”

We took the rightmost passage, and I paid out the string as we walked.

We walked a distance, a bit more than I thought was reasonable. The
spool wasn’t depleted, but we’d used more than I anticipated. The tunnel
opened into another chamber with three recessed booths. At a glance,
they seemed no different from the ones we’d already found. Each
contained a metal bench, with another of those strange cylindrical
things hanging nearby.

“Is it just benches?” I asked.

Jacob paused his drawing.

“Maybe this is like some old catacombs, or a monastery, and these are
prayer benches. I don’t know what those things are for, though.” He
gestured to the other end of the chamber, where another passage
continued.

“It can’t go further,” I said. I let out a short laugh. “I mean, our
house is like—” I waved my hand back the way we’d come. “Way over there.
Right?”

Jacob turned and looked behind us, as though trying to imagine the house
somewhere above.

I moved close to him and grabbed his hand. “This is really something.”

His hand was trembling. I couldn’t tell until I held it.

He pulled away after a moment and jotted something on his pad.

More recesses and side passages appeared as we walked. Jacob marked the
unexplored branches with notches and began numbering the benches, adding
an A for the rightmost route we’d chosen. In a larger chamber containing
eight benches, he stopped to count them and labeled it Chamber 4.

By then, we’d passed beyond the garage and probably beneath somebody
else’s property. I pictured sidewalks and driveways overhead, people
going about their mornings without knowing what lay beneath their feet.

In one of the smaller chambers, Jacob stopped before a bench that had no
Alethophone. There was nothing hanging from the wall beside it, only the
metal seat and its ornate latticework. He searched around it with the
flashlight.

“That’s strange,” he said.

“What’s strange?”

“There isn’t one of those things here.”

He ran his hand over the wall where the apparatus should have been.
There was no indication that anything had been removed. He examined the
wall a little longer before returning to his pad. I watched him write
‘18A’ beside the bench, followed by ‘NULL.’

We walked until I held the last bit of string between two fingers.

I stopped. Jacob looked back at me, then at the empty spool. We secured
the end around another support and stood there for a moment. Neither of
us said much.

The tunnels were much vaster than we thought. Jacob’s map stretched
multiple pages, with branching passages disappearing off their edges.
Even then, we’d followed only one route.

Without speaking to each other, we started walking back along the
string, toward the exit.

Along the way, I stopped at a bench—Bench 16A, according to Jacob’s map.
Something about it caught my eye. The metalwork was different from the
others. I moved closer and examined the latticework on the bench. Each
curling length of metal had impressed in it a sense organ: an eye, a
nose, an open mouth. I touched each impression before looking ahead into
the darkness.

I leaned my head to the side and heard a faint whispering.

I turned and noticed the protruding device hanging near my ear. I moved
in close, allowing the speculum to settle into my ear, like a Forficula
searching for warmth.

…isn’t rational isn’t rational that’s how he says it. Isn’t rational. I
hate how he says that. Jesus Christ, don’t look at me. Damned. If I do
or don’t and I don’t want to go with him today out to the lake house.
It’s so nice…

I pulled away.

Was it a recording? I looked at the thing hanging from the wall, turning
it slightly between my fingers. There was no visible opening except the
one at its tip.

I leaned back in to listen again.

…you piece of shit. I’m being so cruel. What you think determines how
you feel about others. I feel poorly but our house is nice…

I heard Jacob say something behind me. I wanted to answer him, but the
voice continued, and I was having trouble holding on to any one thought
long enough to understand it. I leaned closer.

It’s hard to say how long I listened, but I was eventually ripped away
by the grumble of my stomach and my dry mouth. I detached from the
speculum. My eyes were watering, and I wasn’t sure why. I grabbed a
water bottle from the bag and took a drink.

Jacob wasn’t around—I hated him for it.

I looked toward the passage, expecting him to come around the corner. I
remembered hearing his voice behind me, but not what he’d said or how
long ago. I couldn’t tell how long I’d been sitting there.

My flashlight caught the string on the ground. I followed it until I
heard the crunching of footsteps somewhere ahead.

I stopped. The footsteps continued.

I held my tongue, afraid to ask who it was.

A light appeared around the corner.

It was Jacob. He had the legal pad tucked beneath one arm.

“Where’d you go?” I asked.

“I told you I was checking that other opening. I called you twice.”

“I didn’t hear you.”

He looked at me for a moment. “What were you doing back there?”

“Listening to one of those things. There was a woman talking.”

“Talking?”

“I think so. It sounded like someone thinking out loud.”

He looked back toward the recess I’d come from.

“What was she saying?”

I tried to remember. Something about a house, an argument, someone being
unreasonable. I couldn’t arrange it into anything that made sense.

“I don’t know,” I said. “It was hard to follow.”

Jacob glanced down at his map and made another note.

“We should get back,” he said.

I followed him along the string. My ear was still itching.

***

Exhausted, lying on the couch, we turned on the TV.

An actor on the screen mouthed words. I turned the volume up, then
again, unable to make out what he was saying. I shifted on the couch and
became aware of the pressure of my fingertip inside my ear. I hadn’t
realized I’d been holding it there. When I pulled it away, the
television was much too loud.

Looking at Jacob, I saw he had at some point retrieved the legal pad. He
flipped through the large yellow sheets, each page like a circuit
board—lines, dots, letters, and numbers all meshing into something
significant.

I looked at Jacob, absorbed in his map. I felt cut off. Not ignored, and
not even due to Jacob’s actions. Just a deep disconnection. The
impossibility of truly feeling how it is to be someone else, to know
someone else. There’s a powerful lonesome in being a singular person.

I laid my head back and closed my eyes, tried to play back what I’d
heard. I could remember bits of it. Something about a lake house, an
argument, someone being unreasonable. But whenever I tried to arrange
the words into something meaningful, I was met instead with a sensation.
A feeling of elevation, a specific euphoria, the same rush you might get
from shoplifting. Sneaking into an occupied house at night to watch
somebody sleep doesn’t get you their thoughts.

Jacob hadn’t listened to any of the Alethophone. He was content to
wander, to catalogue, label.

***

Jacob kept notes separately from his maps. I’ve included some of them
here. He noticed things I didn’t.

***

Jacob returned to work, I think. His usual work things were gone that
morning. I had a listless feeling about me, so I made a smoothie and sat
on the couch in silence. My ear itched.

The legal pad rested on the coffee table in front of me, the annotations
even more extensive than the previous night. I picked it up, flipped the
pages, traced my finger along the paths, off the map, into the air, and
around the house until I was outside.

The garage door sat a few inches above the concrete. A crack of dark
peeked out, conspicuous against the dewy sun. I lifted the door and made
my way to the corner. I realized I still had the map in hand and decided
to take it with me as I descended into the hatch.

I used the light from my phone to guide me as I clicked the stick-on
lights along the walls. We’d brought them down during our last visit,
and I remembered putting up a few near the entrance. There were more
further along than I remembered. They cast dim auras that provided more
psychological relief than light.

I wanted to test the other Alethophone, so starting in order made the
most sense to me. I decided I would go until the current map ended.
Studying it, I could see that there were not always four benches to a
chamber. The numbers varied, with at least one catalogued bench having
no Alethophone at all: Bench 18A, marked NULL in Jacob’s handwriting.
There were so many unfinished branches that I was left a speck at the
feet of God.

I settled with an Alethophone in Chamber 1. I sat on the bench and
fingered the markings in its metalwork. An eye, a half-formed mouth,
curls of metal that folded into one another. Carefully, I moved my head
to the side, tilted it up, then fit the speculum into my ear.

I waited for the whispering to begin.

Instead, something terrible came over me. My heart raced. My mouth went
dry and my tongue stuck to my teeth as I blinked rapidly. I couldn’t
make out a voice, couldn’t even say that I’d heard anything, but there
was an unbearable feeling that something was wrong. Something dreadful
had happened, or was about to happen, and there was nothing I could do
about it.

The conviction was so complete that I searched my memory for whatever
had happened. There was nothing to find.

I dug my fingernails into my thighs. I wanted desperately to stand and
run for the exit, or put my forehead to a cement wall and scrape it like
a paintbrush until the fire in my head stopped.

I pulled away.

My hands trembled and my head pounded as my body worked to undo the
adrenaline. I sat there for some time, trying to account for the fear.
There had been nothing to frighten me. No words, no images I could
remember. Just the feeling, arriving fully formed, without a thought to
attach it to.

I did not return to that Alethophone.

I walked across the chamber to another bench. Its metalwork formed a
pattern of overlapping curves, each returning to itself in smaller and
smaller repetitions. I leaned in and fitted the piece into my ear.

I’m not ready for this, but who is? It’s what everyone does. My neck
feels tight. You carry your tension in your neck in your neck you carry
your tension in your neck. Well, do something about it.

I listened a while longer. The voice circled back to the same words, and
I pulled away.

Another bench, farther down the passage.

A beach, a sunset, a blanket.

I couldn’t say whether I’d heard those words or remembered the things
themselves. Then they were gone.

I pulled away and moved on to the next.

The smell of hand soap came to me, then something musty, an unfamiliar
smell like bodies in a tight space, like compression and sweat as a
stranger rubs against you on a subway car.

The smell of the tunnel had disappeared. I could still feel the bench
beneath me, but it seemed far away, as though the pressure of the metal
against my legs belonged to someone else.

There was a hand around mine. The pressure of fingers against my skin,
someone holding on too tightly. I tried to open my fingers. I could feel
them moving against the bench beneath me, but the grip remained.

I wanted to pull away. I felt someone moving closer, though I could not
have said where they were or what they looked like. I searched for help,
for someone to help me. Of course, no one was there.

Something about a face, worn smooth—pocked stone. The dim glint of a
knife. I didn’t see these things exactly, but they were there, and I
knew there was nowhere to run.

I pulled away.

The chamber returned to me. My hand was resting against the bench,
empty. I spread my fingers, then closed them. I looked around,
illuminated the corners with my flashlight. Nothing had changed. The
unfamiliar smell was already fading.

The pressure around my fingers remained a moment longer.

I continued along the passage, checking Jacob’s map more often than I
needed to, until I reached Chamber 5A.

I sat at Bench 15A. This Alethophone rested quite low to the bench, so I
went supine and gently moved my head to the side, fitting myself into
the speculum. I could rest my head against the metal, close my eyes, and
listen intently.

…arty no one even talked to me. What am I supposed to say? If someone
asked me the right questions, then they’d like me. ‘It’s your
responsibility to ask questions not—’ asshole.

A floral blue dress. Concrete pavers.

And everyone fucking laughs. I suck. ‘You just suck sometimes.’ I can’t
always be fun. I really liked her dress. She wouldn’t talk to me.

I continued listening. There were pauses, changes in subject, small
worries that led into other worries. Nothing particularly remarkable
about them. I found myself comfortable there, lying on the bench,
listening to someone struggle to remember what she’d said at a party.

Eventually, I pulled the speculum from my ear. My head was filled with
pleasant static.

I lay there for a moment, holding the Alethophone in my hand, anxious
that I might miss something revealing if I stopped listening. I
considered putting it back into my ear.

Then I thought of where I was, alone in these tunnels. I released the
Alethophone and made my sluggish way back to the house.

***

There were other benches I visited, ones I haven’t described here. Not
every Alethophone could be approached in the same way. While the
speculum was mostly the same, some were positioned high enough that I
had to stand, others required kneeling on the bench, and, at times,
lying down with a slightly raised head. There was a feeling to each, an
invasion of my body by a tiny parasite that entered through my ear,
skittered across my tongue, and escaped through my mouth in a sigh.

It’s hard to piece together who owns each voice. In memory they bleed
together. When I write them down, when I try to recall them, I can’t
always say whether the words coming from my hand are memories or
thoughts, or if there is a way to tell the difference. There is a
feeling to memory, to analysis, to imagination. Even hearing is a kind
of recollection. In the time it takes to process a sound, a voice,
words—they change so much.

Jacob tries to change me.
