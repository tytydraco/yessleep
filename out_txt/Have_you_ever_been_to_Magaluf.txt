I’ve never really been a party person. The truth is, I’ve always
struggled with the social game. Sure, house parties with my closest
friends have worked out sometimes even been genuinely fun but I’ve
always needed that safe core to keep from falling apart. People say you
don’t need alcohol to have fun, but for me, it was the opposite; without
it, I was constantly on edge, like a string ready to snap. It’s ironic,
then, that I ended up in the ultimate party capital of the world. On an
island in Spain lies a city custom-made for fresh graduates: Magaluf.
You reading this have probably heard the horror stories. About the
robberies on the beach, the drugs in the drinks, and the tragic balcony
falls. They’re true, all of them. But what I’m about to tell you… I can
promise you’ve never heard before. Few can tell it, and those who can,
don’t want to.

We checked into the hotel on a Monday. Me and my three closest friends:
Jack, Charlie, and Magnus. It was our graduation trip, the week that was
supposed to be a memory for a lifetime. It was, but for all the wrong
reasons. The week started deceptively calm, no one wanted to burn out on
the first night. It was on the third day that the unease began to seep
in. I was standing on the balcony when I saw them for the first time:
two men in impeccable black suits moving through the sea of people. Odd,
I thought. Who wears a full suit in forty-degree heat? I dismissed them
as bouncers on a break, but the image of them lingered. Later that same
evening, I saw them again. Now they were walking with two young girls.
It almost looked as if they were helping them, escorting them home, but
something about their movement was too mechanical, too synchronized.
When I asked Jack if he thought they looked sketchy too, he just gave me
a shifty glance. “You’re overthinking as usual, Leo,” he said and downed
his beer. I wanted to believe him. I needed to believe him.

Day four changed everything. We were sitting by the pool after
breakfast, drowsy from the heat. I was scrolling aimlessly on my phone
when a Spanish news broadcast popped up in my feed. A woman was speaking
urgently, and in the background, pictures of two girls were shown. With
my limited school Spanish, I managed to decipher fragments: “young
girls… last seen yesterday… missing.” A cold chill ran down my spine.
The faces on the screen were the ones I’d seen with the men in suits.
Panic began to grow, but before I could formulate a thought, Magnus and
Charlie dragged us off to a new club, a dark basement venue we’d never
visited. Inside the dim, thumping chaos, it happened. I reached for a
drink when I felt a sharp prick in my upper arm. A needle. I turned
around, but all I saw was a blurry wall of people. I tried to call out
to Jack, but my voice wouldn’t carry. The world began to spin, darkness
closed in around the edges of my vision, and just before I hit the
floor, I felt a pair of strong arms catch me.

I woke up to the cold against my cheek. My hands were tied behind my
back, and my body felt like lead. When I managed to focus, I was met
with a sight straight out of a fever dream. We were in a large hall. My
friends were lying next to me, just as dazed as I was. In front of us
stood men and women in a perfect semicircle, all wearing those
characteristic black suits. Standing in front of each person in black
was a young person in a blindingly white robe. They didn’t look like
prisoners; they were eerily calm, almost blissful, with small, stiff
smiles on their lips. Four of the people in black stood empty-handed.
Then it clicked. At the very edge stood the girls from the news in their
new, white dresses. They weren’t missing anymore. They were found.

Before I could scream, we were pulled up and dragged into four separate
rooms. My room was small, sterile, and contained only a chair and a TV.
A man tied me down, leaned over, and placed a heavy hand on my shoulder.
“Everything will be fine, Leo,” he whispered. “You are my apprentice
now.” He walked out and locked the door. The TV screen flickered to
life. It was the news again, the same woman as before, but now it was
our faces filling the screen. We were the new headlines. Then the image
cut out and was replaced by a soft, pulsating sound. A voice began to
chant, over and over: “Everything will be fine. We will take care of
you. You are safe now.”

When I woke up next, the fear was gone or rather, it had been
suffocated. I knew intellectually that I was kidnapped, that I should be
screaming, that the panic should be tearing me apart. I forced myself to
focus on the fear, on the horror of what had happened to my friends. But
every time I tried to grab hold of those feelings, they slipped away,
like sand through my fingers. It was as if my brain had been cleaned,
emptied of everything that was “Leo.”

Instead, the void was filled with a strange, almost numbing warmth. It
was a gratitude that wasn’t my own, but which felt more real than
anything I had ever experienced. I fought back, but the resistance felt
pointless, like trying to hold back a tidal wave with only my hands. My
mentor came in, carrying a white suit that seemed to glow in the sterile
light. “You are safe here,” he said. “And one day, you will wear my
black suit.”

Now I understand. There is nothing more important than the black suit. I
feel enlightened, liberated from my old, nervous self. The only thing I
want is to help you see what I see. To make you feel this boundless
happiness. So if you ever visit Magaluf and see us, the men and women in
black suits don’t run. Don’t be afraid. We only want what’s best for
you. We just want you to come home.
