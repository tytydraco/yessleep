Growing up in that isolated valley meant learning to live with a very
specific kind of silence. The town was small, tucked away between steep
forested hills that blocked out the horizon, and the people who lived
there liked the isolation. They kept their lawns manicured, their houses
painted, and of course their secrets buried.

Everyone knew the law. It was not written in any civic charter, but it
was taught to every child the moment their bodies began to change.

The transition into adulthood brought the man.

On the day of my thirteenth birthday, my mother died. The sheriff told
us she had slipped in the steep ravine behind our property, but the
details were kept vague, and the casket was kept closed during the
service. In the middle of that devastating fog, while the smell of
funeral flowers still clung to the living room, my father sat me down at
the kitchen table. He looked older than his forty years, his hands
shaking as he pushed a glass of water toward me.

“There is something you are going to see now,”

he said. His voice was cracked, his eyes refusing to meet mine.

“He will stand in the background of your life. He might be at the edge
of the woods when you walk home from school, or stand near the mailbox
while you do the dishes. But you must never look him in the eye, and
never speak to his face. To acknowledge him is to break the order of
this valley.”

“Who is he?”

I asked, my voice barely a whisper.

“He is the one who follows,”

my father replied.

“You are the only one who will see your specific follower. Everyone has
one. We do not talk about them, or even look at them. That is the only
way we stay safe.”

The reality of his words hit me three days later. Walking home from the
bus stop, the gravel crunching under my sneakers, a sudden sensation of
cold air brushed against my neck. Turning my head slightly, a tall
figure in a dark, outdated coat was standing near the old oak tree at
the corner of our road. His face was somewhat obscured by the shadow of
a wide-brimmed hat, but his posture was rigid, his attention locked
entirely on me.

My heart tried to escape my chest, but my father’s warning echoed in my
mind. I forced my gaze back to the road and kept walking.

Living with him became a slow, grinding torment. He was always there.
When I did my homework, he stood in the twilight of the back yard. When
I went to the grocery store, he sat on the bench outside the entrance.
Only I could see him. People would walk right through the space he
occupied, their bodies passing through his dark form like mist,
completely oblivious to his presence.

My father did not handle the grief of my mother’s death well. As the
years passed, he fell deep into drinking, spending his nights locked in
the basement or passed out on the living room sofa. The smell of cheap
whiskey became a permanent fixture of our home. He stopped checking on
me, stopped asking about school, and only spoke to warn me whenever he
noticed my eyes lingering too long on the dark corners of the yard.

By the time I reached my senior year of high school, the tension in the
town had reached a boiling point.

One of my classmates, a quiet boy who sat in the back of my English
class, vanished without a trace over a weekend. His locker was left full
of books, his bicycle still chained to the school fence. The rumors
spread through the hallways like wildfire. The elders of the town held
an urgent assembly in the gymnasium, their faces grim under the bright
gym lights.

The principal stood at the podium, his voice stern.

“We must remind you all of the rules of our community. The boy who went
missing forgot his place. He broke the silence. Do not make his mistake.
Keep your head down and focus on your studies.”

The warning did not satisfy me. The vanished boy had been a friend of my
closest classmate, a girl who lived down the road from me. After school,
we sat on the hood of my car, watching the sun dip behind the steep
hills.

“His parents are completely destroyed,”

my friend said, her fingers twisting the strap of her backpack.

“They won’t even open the front door. The elders told everyone to stay
away from their house.”

“We should go see them,”

I said.

“Just to offer some comfort. It isn’t right to leave them isolated like
this.”

My friend hesitated, looking toward the dark woods bordering the school.

“If the town council sees us there, we will be in trouble.”

“We will go after dark,”

I insisted.

“Through the back trail.”

We made our way to the vanished boy’s house later that evening,
navigating the overgrown path behind the old orchard. The house was
dark, the only light coming from a single lamp in the living room. When
we knocked, his mother opened the door only a crack, her face hollow and
pale.

“We just wanted to say we are sorry about him,”

my friend said quietly.

The mother looked at us for a long time, her eyes darting to the shadows
behind us. Eventually, she stepped aside and let us into the dusty
hallway.

“He stopped sleeping,”

the mother whispered, leading us into the living room. “He kept staring
at the corner of his bedroom. We told him to look away, but he said the
man was crying. He said the man wanted to tell him something.”

“Did he leave any note?”

I asked, keeping my voice low.

The mother shook her head.

“The council came and cleared his room. They burned his journals in the
yard. But they missed his old biology textbook. It was still in his
closet.”

While my friend talked to the mother, I quietly slipped down the hallway
to the boy’s bedroom. The door was unlocked. The room had been stripped
bare, leaving only an empty mattress and a pile of discarded school
papers. In the back of the closet, half-hidden under a loose floorboard,
I found the biology textbook.

Tucked inside the back cover was a single, partially burnt page from a
notebook. The handwriting was frantic, the ink smudged.

“He wanted me to follow,”

the note read.

“He did not look angry. He nodded to me. I think he wants to show me how
to end this madness. If I do not come back, know that I went to find the
source. I am going to look him in the eye.”

My hands shook as I folded the paper and slid it into my pocket. I went
back to the living room, grabbed my friend, and we left the house
through the back door.

As we walked through the dark orchard, I pulled the paper out and showed
it to her under the light of my phone.

“I am going to do it,”

I said.

My friend stopped walking, her face turning pale in the blue screen
light.

“Are you out of your mind? You saw what happened to him. He is gone.”

“He went to find a way to stop this,”

I argued.

“We spend every single second of our lives in fear, pretending we don’t
see them. My father is drinking himself to death because of this town.
My mother is dead because of this town. I want to know the truth.”

“If you look at him, he may kill you,”

she said, her voice rising in panic.

“And of course the council won’t just let people break the law. If you
do this, I cannot help you. I fear that I am not going to watch you
die.”

“Ok ok, I won’t do it”

I lied to her; she took a deep breath and said

“Promise me”

That’s when I couldn’t just do it, I stayed silent for a long time, just
looking through her eyes, then I saw a small tear on her face. Then
suddenly, She turned and ran back toward her house, leaving me alone
under the dark branches of the apple trees.

I walked back to my empty yard. My follower was waiting for me. He stood
near the rusted garden gate, his tall, dark silhouette perfectly still
against the gray mist of the evening.

My father was passed out on the kitchen floor inside, an empty bottle of
bourbon resting near his hand. Nobody was going to save me. Nobody was
going to save this town.

I took a deep breath, walked down the wooden steps of the porch, and
marched directly toward the gate. I stopped three feet away from the
tall figure.

For the first time in my life, I raised my chin and looked directly into
his face.

Under the shadow of his hat, his skin was incredibly pale, almost
translucent. His eyes were dark, sunken, and filled with a profound
sadness.

“I see you,”

I said clearly.

At first, nothing happened. Then, the man’s rigid posture snapped.

A loud, wet cracking sound echoed through the yard, like dry branches
breaking under a boot. The lunged. His body elongated, twisting over the
rusted iron of the gate with impossible speed. His hands, pale and gray,
clamped around my throat before I could even flinch.

The touch was a burning freeze that immediately locked my lungs. My
knees buckled, but he held me upright, his grip lifting me slightly off
the ground.

His hat fell backward into the dirt.

The face beneath was a nightmare. His mouth split open, unhinging like a
snake’s jaw, revealing a dark throat coated in wet, black soil. A
rattling, choked wheeze erupted from his chest, spraying a dust onto my
cheeks. His eyes, previously hidden, widened into deep, empty sockets
that seemed to pull the very light out of the yard.

My vision began to darken at the edges. My lungs screamed for air, but
every time I tried to inhale, the air just refused to come.

Yet, I did not close my eyes. I refused to look away. I stared directly
into the void of his face, holding his freezing, dead gaze with a
desperate, furious resolve.

The creature froze. The snapping joints went silent. Slowly, the dark
void in his eyes receded, replaced by the pale, sunken eyes of a human
being. His unhinged jaw clicked back into place. His grip on my throat
softened, his fingers gently sliding down to my collarbone, then
releasing me entirely.

I collapsed to my knees in the dirt, gasping for air, grasping for air.

Looking up, the terrifying specter was gone. The man stood there again,
his hat back on his head, looking down at me. Slowly, a gentle, tragic
smile appeared on his thin lips, and a single tear rolled down his pale
cheek. He nodded once, a slow, deliberate movement, and turned toward
the dark path leading into the forest behind my house.

He took a few steps, then paused, looking back over his shoulder. It was
clear to me somehow that he wanted me to follow.

I followed him deep into the woods, past the boundary lines of our
property, into an area of the forest where the trees grew so close
together the starlight could not reach the ground. We walked for nearly
an hour in silence, the only sound being the rustle of my own footsteps.

Eventually, we reached a hidden clearing.

In the center of the clearing were two simple, unmarked graves. They
were just mounds of soft dirt, covered in wild ivy and damp moss.

The man stopped at the edge of the mounds. He pointed his long, pale
finger down at the earth, then looked at me, nodding again. He wanted me
to touch them.

I knelt in the wet grass, my heart pounding, and pressed my palms into
the damp soil of the graves.

A violent shockwave of heat rushed up my arms. The forest vanished, and
my mind was pulled into a torrent of vivid, overwhelming visions.

I saw the same valley, but it was the previous century. The houses were
different, built of rough-cut timber and stone. The streets were filled
with thick mud. People were dying in the dirt, clutching their chests
and gasping desperately for air. A terrible plague was sweeping through
the settlement, turning the water sour and the air toxic.

Then, I saw a young woman and her husband. They were both doctors,
working tirelessly in a small wooden clinic. They boiled herbs, washed
the sores of the sick, and tried to ease the immense pain of the dying
town. They did not ask for money; they only wanted to save the people.

But the fear of the plague turned the townspeople into monsters.

I saw an angry mob gathering in the muddy square. A local leader, his
face twisted with hatred, pointed his finger toward the wooden clinic.

“They brought the sickness!”

he roared.

“They are practicing dark medicine! They are witches, and they are what
caused this plague!”

The mob stormed the clinic. They dragged the young doctor woman out into
the mud, binding her wrists with thick rope. Her husband managed to
escape through a back window, fleeing into the dark forest.

I watched through his eyes from his hiding place behind the trees. He
cried silently, biting his own hand to stifle his screams, as the mob
tied his wife to a heavy wooden stake in the center of the square. They
piled dry brush around her feet and lit the fire. Her screams echoed
through the valley as the flames rose, her eyes locked on the dark woods
where she knew her husband was hiding.

The vision shifted.

The husband was inside a cold, dark cavern deep beneath the mountain. He
was entirely consumed by grief and madness. He used a sharp knife to
wound his own hand, letting the blood drip onto the stone floor to draw
a large circle. He held a strange, leather-bound book in his other hand,
reading aloud from the yellowed pages.

“If they will punish us for something we did not do,”

he whispered, his voice trembling with a mixture of sorrow and rage,

“then I will at least die doing it to curse them. Every soul in this
valley will bear our shadow.”

But as he finished the final words of the ritual, the sound of shouting
echoed from the cave entrance. The mob had tracked him. They burst into
the cavern with pitchforks and spades, driving a sharp iron tool
directly through his chest before he could close the circle.

He died with the curse unfinished, binding his spirit to the physical
plane of the valley. He became the following man, a permanent, haunting
shadow forced to follow every descendant of the mob, keeping the memory
of their crime alive through a cycle of silent torment.

I snapped out of the vision with a violent gasp, my hands slipping from
the wet dirt of the graves.

I was lying on my back in the grass. The sky was still dark, and my
father was kneeling beside me, holding my shoulders. His face was
incredibly pale, his clothes smelling of alcohol and earth.

“Get up,”

he whispered, his voice frantic as he pulled me to my feet.

“We have to go. Now.”

“What are you doing here?”

I asked, my head spinning from the force of the vision. “How did you
find me?”

“Your friend,”

my father said, dragging me through the trees toward his old station
wagon parked on a dirt logging trail.

“She got terrified, and told her parents what you were planning to do,
and they gathered the town council. They are coming to deal with you.”

“I don’t understand,”

I said, stumbling over a root.

“Deal with me? Why?”

“They killed your mother for the same thing,”

my father said, his grip tightening on my arm.

“She looked at her follower. She wanted to help him. The council found
out, dragged her into the woods, and buried her alive to ‘cleanse’ the
curse from the town. I was a coward then. I did not stop them, then
drank to drown the guilt, but I am not letting them take you.”

We reached the car, and he shoved me into the passenger seat, slamming
the door shut. He climbed into the driver’s seat, started the engine,
and threw the car into gear just as a dozen bright flashlight beams
pierced through the trees behind us.

“They deserve to die,”

my father said, his knuckles turning white as he gripped the steering
wheel, driving the car recklessly down the narrow, bumpy mountain road.

“This entire valley is built on a murder. The people here have spent a
century killing their own children just to keep a secret they don’t even
understand.”

“What are we going to do?”

I asked, tears of panic finally starting to spill over my cheeks.

“I made a deal with my follower years ago,”

my father said, keeping his eyes locked on the dark road. “I figured out
how to finish the doctor’s ritual. The curse was meant to release the
actual plague they spent a century fearing, but it was left incomplete
when he died. I spent years researching it in the basement, but I just
couldn’t do it. I always thought that maybe you could live, maybe no one
has to die, but now, now I have to do it to save you”

He slammed on the brakes at a dark junction near the state highway
boundary. He reached across the console and pushed my passenger door
open.

“Get out and run to the highway,”

he commanded.

“Call my phone. Keep the line open. Run as fast as you can.”

“No!”

I screamed, grabbing his arm.

“You have to come with me! We can escape together!”

“They will track this car,”

my father said, his voice softer now. He reached out and gently touched
my face.

“I have to go back to the house. The circle is already prepared in the
basement. I have to finish the ritual to end this town once and for all.
If I do not do it, they will hunt you to the ends of the earth. Run.”

He pushed me out of the car. I hit the gravel shoulder, scraping my
knees, as he slammed the door shut and spun the tires, driving the
station wagon back up the mountain toward our house.

I scrambled to my feet, clutching my phone in my hand, and began
sprinting down the dark highway under the looming pine trees.

After some time of running, I just stopped, I couldn’t run more without
him, the phone in my hand crackled to life. I dialed the number of my
father phone, then pressed the speaker button, holding the screen close
to my chest as I ran.

“I am in the basement,”

my father’s voice came through the static, sounding calm and clear over
the speaker.

“The circle is active.”

Over the phone, the sound of our front door splintering open echoed
loudly. Shouting voices filled the background.

“He is in the basement!”

a voice roared through the phone.

My father began to chant. It was the same low, guttural language I had
heard the doctor speaking in my vision.

“Stop him!”

another voice screamed.

A loud gunshot shattered the connection, followed by my father releasing
a sharp gasp of pain. But he did not stop. He kept chanting, his voice
growing weaker but more determined with every syllable.

“Finish it,”

I sobbed into the phone, running as fast as my legs could carry me
toward the distant lights of a highway truck stop.

“Please, finish it.”

Over the line, the shouting of the townspeople suddenly changed. The
anger in their voices turned to confusion, then to terror. I heard them
coughing. It was a wet, suffocating sound, exactly like the people in my
vision. They began to gasp for air, their lungs filling with the ancient
plague that had been locked away for over a century.

One by one, the choking sounds faded into silence.

The final sound I heard before the line went completely dead was my
father taking one last, peaceful breath.

That was five years ago.

I never went back to that valley. The state police found the entire town
dead of a sudden, highly concentrated outbreak of a rare pulmonary
infection, and the area was permanently quarantined. They never found
me, and I made sure of that.

I live in a large city now, hundreds of miles away. I have a decent
office job, a quiet apartment on the third floor, and I am in a
relationship with a kind, supportive man who knows absolutely nothing
about my past. I have spent every single day trying to forget the smell
of the pine trees and the shadow of the following man.

But three days ago, the cold air returned.

I was sitting in my living room, watching my boyfriend cook dinner in
the kitchen. The smell of garlic and olive oil filled the warm
apartment, and everything felt completely safe.

I looked toward the dark hallway leading to our bedroom.

Standing near the coat rack, perfectly still in the shadows, was a new
follower.

He is pale, with a dark, wet stain spreading across the chest of his
faded flannel shirt. His bloodshot eyes are locked entirely on me,
filled with a deep, silent sorrow.

It is my father, and I do not know what to do.
