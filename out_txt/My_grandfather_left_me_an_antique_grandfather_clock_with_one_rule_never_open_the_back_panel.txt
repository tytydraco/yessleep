I inherited my grandfather’s house in upstate New York last autumn. It
is a beautiful, isolated property surrounded by dense pine forests.
Along with the house came all of his old furniture, including a massive,
ancient grandfather clock that stood in the corner of the living room.
It was made of dark oak, and the pendulum swung with a deep, echoing
tick that could be heard throughout the entire house.

My grandfather was a quiet, meticulous man, but he was incredibly
superstitious about this clock. In his will, he left me the house under
one strict condition: “Keep the clock wound, and never, under any
circumstances, open the back panel.”

For the first few weeks, I respected his wishes. The ticking was
soothing, a rhythmic reminder of his presence. But as the winter nights
grew longer and colder, the house felt increasingly silent, save for
that clock. One evening, around 2 AM, the clock abruptly stopped
ticking. The silence in the room was sudden and heavy, almost
suffocating.

I grabbed the winding key, opened the glass door, and wound the
mechanism. Nothing happened. The pendulum remained still. I gave it a
gentle push, but it swung a few times and stopped again. Frustrated, I
remembered the back panel. I knew his rule, but I figured it was just an
old mechanic’s quirk. I shined my phone flashlight behind the clock.

The back panel was secured by a small brass latch, covered in dust. I
hesitated, my grandfather’s warning echoing in my mind. But curiosity
won. I flicked the latch and pulled the wooden panel open.

Inside, there were no gears. No brass wheels, no springs, no clockwork
mechanisms.

There was only a hollow space, lined with rotting black velvet. Resting
inside the hollow was a human skull, wrapped in silver wire, with two
polished pieces of obsidian placed in the eye sockets. Written in dried,
dark ink along the jawbone were symbols I couldn’t comprehend.

I stumbled back, dropping my phone. As the light hit the floor, the
clock suddenly shivered. The pendulum began to swing on its own. *Tick.
Tock. Tick. Tock.*

But the sound was different now. It wasn’t the metallic click of oak and
brass. It sounded like a wet, heavy heartbeat.

Terrified, I slammed the back panel shut, locked the latch, and pushed
the clock back against the wall. I barely slept that night. Every tick
felt like it was echoing inside my chest, matching the rhythm of my own
heart.

The next morning, I decided I was going to get rid of the clock. I
called a local antique dealer, but when he arrived and inspected it, he
looked confused. “Son,” he said, “this clock doesn’t have a back panel.
It’s solid oak. Look.”

He shined his light behind it. The back of the clock was a single,
seamless sheet of wood. There was no latch, no seam, no panel.

Since that day, the clock has not stopped. But the heartbeat is getting
louder. And last night, when the clock struck midnight, I didn’t hear
the usual chime. I heard a raspy, gasping breath come from inside the
wood, whispering my name. I am sitting in my living room right now,
staring at the solid wood back of the clock. I can hear the heartbeat.
And I think whatever was locked inside that panel is clawing its way out
from the inside.
