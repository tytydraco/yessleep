I thought it would be dark.

I mean, in case something really weird and scary ever happened to me, I
was expecting it would be around midnight, on a lonely country road or
in the deepest of forests, maybe with some rain and wind, and at least a
half-Marathon from civilization.

I was wrong. And then some.

But before I begin, some background is required. I am a single father of
a wonderful 2½ -year old son (when they are that young, we parents tend
to use fractions) named Sergey, a healthy, active, curious and
altogether irresistibly lovely boy who can also bite like a vampire,
kick like a mule, and scream like Black Canary when he’s throwing a
tantrum, although, thankfully, this doesn’t happen very often. His
energy is boundless, so I often take him to playgrounds and parks even
after he’s supposed to have expended it during his day-care hours.

Unfortunately, winter in my town tends to be quite cold and snowy, so I
was very happy when I discovered that my favorite home deco store –
going by the charming name Imenno to, chto ya khochu!, but everyone
simply calls it Imenno – had provided a solution to the problem what to
do with a powerhouse like Sergey at times when you can’t take him
outside. Immeno is quite large, on three floors, and part of the top
floor is taken up by a kafe-konditerskaya, comparable to a tea room,
where they have excellent cake and even the coffee is decent.

Now the owners are well aware of the fact that probably eighty percent
of their customers are female and many of them mothers with small
children, so they have added a large playroom called Detskaya Gavan
Veselya, a name that does not exactly roll of the tongue, so it’s Gavan
to everyone. It’s really a lovely place. There’s lots of space for the
kids to run or crawl around; the floor is solid but relatively soft and
not slippery at all, and there are several softer mats lying on it, so
even if a child slips and falls, it’s highly unlikely they will hurt
themselves. There are also some games, a very nice slide from which the
sweeties can stumble head over heels into a ball pit – and if they have
emptied it again by throwing most of the balls around, which they do
with an alarming frequency, the landing will still be soft – and tables
and chairs for their parents so they can enjoy their Medovik or Napoleon
with their Jardin, some milk, lots of sugar.

And then there is the Lego area. If you’re not a parent, you may not be
aware of the fact that Lego produces soft, rubbery bricks the size of …
well, bricks, actually. Gavan has hundreds of them, and the children
love building walls, towers, houses, staircases, and even castles that
sometimes reach up more than two meters (with parents’ helping hands,
naturally). The kids probably love this area even more than the other
parts.

Of course, parents are supposed to watch over their offspring, and they
do, but since the place is so safe and easily overlooked (with the
exception of the Lego area, which is a bit like a separate room, only
there is no door, just a 40-cm rubber wall most kids can easily climb
over), it is actually not difficult to keep your eyes on several of them
at the same time, and many parents have developed a quid pro
quo-culture, meaning they leave and you take care of their young ones
and then you leave and they take over, so it sometimes happens that only
one or two adults watch over a dozen children. Don’t get me wrong: at
most times – especially during what we call the chas pik, or rush hour –
the room is full of grown-ups chatting with other grown-ups, sometimes
even chatting up other grown-ups; I won’t lie pretending that was not at
least part of my motivation to visit Gavan, even on days when the
weather was fine so Sergej could in theory romp outside in the sunshine.
Since the day my wife left me and returned to their home country, I’ve
been hoping to meet my personal Natalia Vodianova there, preferably with
no more than one child (OK, two, it is Natalia). But, quite often, the
adult-child ratio is a steep 1:6 or even 1:8, and sometimes, you are
alone with a bunch of them.

As I said, not a problem – normally, they don’t pay a lot of attention
to you, and some of us have created a WhatsApp group, so in a very rare
case of emergency, the parents can quickly come back. It was, therefore,
not unusual for me to be left alone with six children, all of them
apples of their parents’ eyes, and one apple was mine. As they seemed to
be happy playing along with and next to each other, I allowed myself to
drift away a little and check my BK social media account. After making
sure I was in the loop as to what Lera was up to with that German fellow
and getting green with envy scrolling through Dima and Sasha’s Thailand
holiday photos, I suddenly heard something I had never before heard in
this place.

Nothing.

Well, that’s not quite true, but I abruptly realized that the drop in
the noise level was striking, to say the least. You can imagine the
ruckus half a dozen toddlers and small children normally produce;
compared to that, the faint sounds I was still able to hear were as
close to silence as any noise can get. For a second, I thought Oh no,
they’ve opened the door to the tea room and they’ve all run away, and
the parents will skin me alive – then I heard them.

Now I wish I hadn’t.

I wasn’t able to see the children from where I was sitting, but I could
hear their voices … if you want to call it that; at the time, I thought
someone had left open a window and a flock of birds had invaded the
room, but then I remembered it was early December, and the windows
couldn’t be opened without a key, anyway. No, that was something else. I
got up and looked around, but even after checking some of the popular
places, I found no one.

“Sergey? Seryozha? Come to Papa! Where are you?”

No answer. The twittering appeared to come from around the corner, the
Lego area, so I went there and –

Wow. Look at that. They can’t do that on their own … can they?

I still couldn’t see any of the kids, but that wasn’t because they
weren’t there – they most certainly were. It was because someone had
built a Lego brick wall closing off the whole room from left to right,
from top to bottom; it must have been at least three meters high and as
solidly constructed as you can expect from a material like this. From
behind, the strange noises grew louder, and I could tell there were
several different ‘voices’, but, all in all, it sounded like a
parliament of oversized birds, with barn owls, green woodpeckers, and
some others with particularly unpleasant shrieks all present.

How would a bunch of two-to-five year olds build something like that?
Even I wouldn’t have been able to reach up that high, and I’m almost two
meters. Somebody must have –

Then, all of a sudden, the noise stopped. I hesitated for a moment and
called out to my son again. I was answered by the sounds of children
walking behind the wall and a scraping noise I was only able to identify
when I saw the wall come crashing down on me. I jumped backwards and
avoided most of it, and the part that struck me did no harm whatsoever,
just confused me – these bricks weigh only between 200 and 400 grams, so
they can’t do a lot of damage. Still, I felt disorientated and landed on
my backside, and when I had gotten a grip on myself again, I saw the
children standing there, looking at me with eyes that did not
communicate anything: no anger, no curiosity, no joy, nothing – only
complete neutrality. None of them were smiling, but neither did they
look annoyed; I had the impression they were simply assessing or
evaluating me.

“Seryozha! There you are!”, I exclaimed, getting up again and beckoning
my son to come to me, but his expression was identical to the other
children’s, and he did not move at all. A four-year-old girl, the
tallest among them, suddenly opened her mouth and emitted one of these
strange twittering shrieks I had heard before; the other children
reacted by shrieking to each other in turn, giving the impression that
they were discussing or debating something. I glanced around; there were
no adults in the room and, what was worrying me more, there appeared to
be no one sitting in the tea room and no staff dealing with customers or
just cleaning tables or something.

A feeling of panic began to assault me, but then the rational part of my
mind kicked in, and I thought: Bozhe moy, there is something terribly
wrong with them. They must be sick or crazy. I need to call 103 at once.

Cell phone. I had left it in my jacket, about ten meters away. I was
about to start in that direction when I saw that one of the children, a
three-year-old blonde boy whose name I remembered as Maksim, was
standing next to the chair where I had put my stuff, and the object he
was holding in his hand was unmistakably my phone. He emitted a shriek
and began to run away with his booty, and amid a cacophony of shrieks
from his playmates I made two careful steps in his direction; I was
half-surrounded by little children, and I didn’t want to knock one of
them down or hurt them in some other way.

Unfortunately, they didn’t have the same compunctions. When I wanted to
turn left after my first two steps, the second tallest of them, an
utterly adorable little whirlwind called Artyom, ran into me at full
speed. Just a preschooler, you say? Think again. I looked it up later: a
four-year-old usually weighs between 16 and 18 kg and can run at up to
15 to 17 kph, meaning the force he generates is more than strong enough
to make an adult stumble and fall, especially when said adult is caught
unawares.

My first fall, after the wall had come down, had been more or less
controlled; this one wasn’t. I came down hard, hit my head on the floor
(as I said – relatively soft), and ended up prone on my back, with a
sharp pain shooting through my head and almost all parts of my body. I
must have been stunned for a few seconds, because when I had pulled
myself together, I was surrounded by them, and they were closing in.

This is madness. They’re just a bunch of kids gone insane. I need to
call out for someone.

Then, all of a sudden, I felt the touch of several small hands on my
face, above all my eyes and my mouth, and my scream for help was nipped
in the bud – what came out was more like ‘grrgggl’, which would have
been woefully ineffective even if there had been any adults in the next
room. I wanted to flail around, but more hands were exerting pressure on
my arms, and although I’m certainly stronger than your average toddler,
berut chislom – strength in numbers, aided by a natural tendency to
avoid hurting children, and here I was, being overwhelmed by half a
dozen of them shrieking excitedly to each other – whether out of joy or
in triumph, I have no idea. No clear thoughts. No controlled movements.
Pandemonium.

There was only one thing I could get a mental grip on, and I let it out
between fingers, palms, and wrists making speech difficult – but not
impossible.

“Sergej! Seryozha! Solnyshko moyo!”

I suppose what really came out sounded less clear, but it had an
immediate effect: suddenly, the number of hands on my body went down,
and two children actually stumbled and fell; the rest moved away from me
and kept their distance. Only one child was still making any noise:
Sergey. With a stance that would have made a Roman emperor proud, he
gave off a staccato-like sequence of loud shrieks in all directions,
earning him silent stares from everyone, but nobody moved or
communicated anything. Then Sergey looked at me, placed his hand on my
forehead and said in a soft voice:

“Moy papochka!”

And the whole room became a merry-go-round without the merry part, and I
needed to close my eyes.

“Lyosha! Lyosha! Aleksey! Are you alright?”

I opened them again. I was surrounded by the parents of the children
who’d ganged up on me and a member of staff, and all looked concerned.

“You’ve taken quite a fall, starina.”, Andrey said and helped me up. He
was one of the few fathers who regularly took their kids to Gavan, but
his taste ran more to Anna Semenovich, with no more success than I had
enjoyed so far. “Knocked you out for good. Are you fully there?”

I nodded and took several deep breaths. My head hurt, but otherwise, I
seemed to be OK.

“How many fingers?” He raised only one, and you can imagine which one; I
answered “Four if you happen to work at a sawmill.”, and he chuckled and
declared me fit for parental duty. Sergey was standing together with the
other kids; some of them were crying, others were hugging their parents’
legs, and he began running towards me to jump into my arms. I squeezed
him so hard I thought he might start to cry, but I’ve mentioned he’s
made of sterner stuff than most, haven’t I?

“Sergey was very sweet. He was standing next to you and petting your
forehead, I suppose to wake you up.” That was Olga, the mother of the
tall four-year-old girl whose name I still can’t remember. I looked at
the other parents and at their children and back at the parents and then
at my son, but I couldn’t bring myself to smile at him.

Of course I haven’t told them or anyone about what happened, and if you
think of putting on your deerstalker and trying to find me, trust me,
you won’t. I still go to Gavan with Sergey, but I make sure it’s chas
pik, so I always have company. But I’ve become sullen and silent when
I’m there, and even if Natalia’s twin sister should one day be sitting
next to me and trying to hit on me, I wouldn’t respond.

I simply don’t want to be alone with them anymore.

And Sergey … you know, sometimes I watch him and wonder. He doesn’t
appear to be different from the Sergey I knew before that day, and yet …
Toddlers sometimes shout, cry, and even shriek, but when he does it, for
a moment I feel as if someone had parked a T-90M in my stomach. Toddlers
also sometimes kick, slap, or bite you, but when he does it, for a
moment I feel as if there was something beneath the usual contest of
wills between parents and offspring … something sinister, from beyond,
cold and unfeeling. And when he says “Papochka!”, I still get a jolt.

None of these feelings have diminished over time, and when I look at
him, I sometimes have strange thoughts. Three times a year, he spends a
few weeks abroad with his mother, and I used to hate taking him there: a
long drive, bad roads, often lousy weather … but I have come to enjoy
the outbound journey and dread the return trip.

And there are two things I haven’t mentioned yet.

The first is my crying out Sergey’s name when the children were
attacking me; it wasn’t out of affection, it was out of fear: a call for
help to my 2½-year old son.

And the second is the way he said “Moy papochka!” I desperately want to
believe he had come back from whatever madness had befallen the children
and returned to normal, but what if the possessive article was literally
about … possession?

Sometimes, when a group of parents stand together and their children are
playing in the distance, one parent might ask another: “The dark-haired
boy with the red sweater, is he yours?” Then the other parent will nod
or shake their head: yes, he’s mine, no, he’s not mine.

I used to think Sergey was mine.

But maybe I am his.
