My name is Cairo. By the time you read this, I’ll probably already be
gone. I’m using a phone I took off a guard who wasn’t paying attention,
and I don’t know how long I have before someone notices, so I’m going to
write this fast and I’m going to write it honest.

I grew up in a house that looked normal from the outside and was never
once normal on the inside. My mother and father, and my grandmother, who
lived with us my whole life. I don’t remember a version of my childhood
that didn’t involve someone in that house making me feel small.

My grandmother was the worst of it, for the longest stretch of my life.
When I turned fifteen, something in her seemed to decide I was suddenly
a problem that needed constant correcting. She screamed at me for the
clothes I wore, even in my own room. She screamed at me for how I wore
my hair. She screamed at me once for laughing too loud at something on
television, like joy itself was something she needed to ration out of
me. I remember sitting in my room once, just thinking about a boy from
school, just smiling to myself about nothing at all, and she walked in,
saw my face, and spent the rest of that day telling me I was disgusting,
that I needed to stay away from boys, that I was already turning into
something shameful just by smiling in private.

I stopped smiling in my room after that. I stopped smiling in a lot of
places.

My father was worse in a different way. Quiet, mostly, until he wasn’t.
He had this way of watching me that made my skin crawl even when he
wasn’t saying anything at all, like he was keeping some kind of tally
behind his eyes. when I was 8 years i saw my parents completely naked in
the living room having makeout in front of there daughter not even
realising i was watching them even after that they didn’t even apologise
to me for . When I turned nineteen, I finally did something for myself
for once — went out to a night swimming party with my friends, no
permission asked, because some small stubborn part of me had decided I
deserved four hours outside those walls.

I came home late. He was sitting on the sofa in the dark, waiting,
completely still, and asked me where I’d been in a voice so flat and
cold it didn’t sound like him at all. I told him I’d been enjoying my
life outside those four walls for once. I walked to my room, opened the
door, and found Ketty, my cat, dead on my bed.

Ketty was the only thing in that entire house that had ever made me feel
safe. She used to sleep curled against my side every single night, the
one warm, uncomplicated thing in a house full of people who made me feel
like a burden just for existing. Finding her there broke something in me
that I don’t think ever went back together right.

My father came up behind me while I was still staring at her and said,
in that same flat voice, that I wasn’t allowed to leave that house
again. He said I was his property.

I want to be honest about something. For years, I believed all of it was
somehow my fault. That’s what they trained into me, slowly, patiently,
over almost two decades — that my feelings were the problem, that my
reactions were the problem, that if I could just be smaller, quieter,
more agreeable, none of it would happen anymore. I spent so long
swallowing every single thing they did to me and calling it my own fault
instead.

After Ketty, something in me finally stopped believing that.

Over the next two years, I found spaces online where people talked about
things my family would have called evil. Group chats, forums, quiet
corners of the internet where people traded knowledge nobody else wanted
to admit to knowing. A boy I talked to on Reddit walked me through
something specific, patient about it in a way nobody in my own house had
ever been patient with me. I don’t know if he understood what he was
really teaching me, or if he even cared.

I performed it alone, in my room, one night, aimed at every person in
that house who had ever made me feel like I deserved what they gave me.

Nothing happened for five days. I remember almost laughing at myself,
telling myself I’d wasted two years of research and hope on something
that was never real to begin with.

On the sixth day, my mother’s nose started bleeding at breakfast and
wouldn’t stop.

By that evening, it wasn’t just her nose. Blood came from her eyes, thin
lines of it tracking down her face while she screamed for someone to
help her. My father collapsed in the hallway not long after, blood
coming from his mouth in a way that didn’t look like anything a human
body should produce. My grandmother started vomiting blood in the
bathroom, over and over, until there was more of it on the tile than
there was of her left standing.

I stood in the hallway and watched all three of them and I felt
something I hadn’t felt in that house in years.

I felt like laughing, and for the first time in my life, I let myself.

I took what money I could find in the house that night and I left before
anyone else arrived. I made it as far as Australia before they caught up
with me. I won’t walk through the trial. I’ll just say that I told the
truth, every bit of it, and none of it mattered to a courtroom the way
it mattered to me.

I’m not writing this to ask anyone to forgive me. I don’t think I need
forgiveness the way people expect me to. I’m writing this because for
eleven years, three people made me feel like the smallest, most shameful
thing in every room I ever stood in, and for one single night, for the
first time in my whole life, I made them feel small too.

I don’t know what happens after they take this phone away from me. I
don’t know what happens after tonight at all.

I just know that whatever it is, it can’t possibly be smaller than the
life I already lived.
