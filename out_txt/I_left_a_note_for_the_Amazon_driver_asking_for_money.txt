The wooden coins are pushing through my chest wall now. I can see the
outline of them under the skin like a line of buttons along my sternum,
and when I breathe, they shift against my heart with a grinding sound
like wood scraping bone. My checking account tripled overnight after I
left that note on my door asking the Amazon driver for money to stop the
foreclosure, and instead of currency or pity, he dropped a cardboard box
at my feet containing twelve wooden discs. The delivery receipt sits on
my nightstand with the return window clearly printed: fourteen days. It
has been thirteen.

I know what a cardiac event feels like. My father had one when I was
sixteen. This is not a cardiac event. The ER doctor felt the coins
through my skin two days ago when he was listening to my heart. His hand
went very still. He didn’t run any tests after that, just told me to
follow up with my primary care physician and avoid strenuous activity.
He knew what they were. I could see it in how he wouldn’t look at me.

My roommate made her wish three days before I made mine. She’d been
living here for eight months and paying rent late, talking about her job
in that hollow way people do when they’re about to quit. One afternoon,
an Amazon truck rolled up without any order placed. A different driver
this time, but same uniform, same metallic gray uniform that seems to
catch light wrong. She got a box of these wooden coins too. Nine of them
for her.

She started pulling at her forearm last night. I found her in the
hallway with a strip of birch bark peeling away from her skin, exposing
raw muscle underneath in a line perfectly straight as if something
surgical had separated skin from flesh. She was whispering when I found
her, something about the diesel engine idling at the curb. She doesn’t
sleep anymore. I hear her pacing at three in the morning, listening for
that sound. The coins she wished for are still inside her. She says she
can feel them moving up her arms now, branching like roots.

By day seven, I noticed the coins didn’t stay where they entered. They
moved. I felt one slide across my ribs, a sharp point trailing under my
skin like a needle through fabric. The ER visit happened on day nine
when I couldn’t hide how shallow my breathing had become. The coins had
redistributed. Some were lodged against my lungs. The doctor’s hand
confirmed it, and I watched his face make the calculation: fourteen-day
return window. Policy is policy, even for things like this.

The infection isn’t the right word. The coins aren’t infecting me.
They’re settling. The box said twelve, and I can account for ten that I
can feel moving freely in my abdomen and chest cavity now. Two more are
stationary, and I know why. One is lodged in my left lung. I cough and
taste copper. The other has wedged between the base of my ribs on my
right side, and when I lie down, I feel it pressing upward with a slow,
deliberate pressure that makes my breath catch. It’s not trying to harm
me. It’s just making room for what’s coming.

The roommate is in her room now. She hasn’t come out since last night. I
can hear something wet moving under her skin, a sound like a muffled
heartbeat that’s not hers. The coins are taking up space. The human body
is mostly empty space that’s supposed to be filled with specific things:
blood, organs, breath. When you introduce solid objects into that space,
the body compresses around them. I can feel my organs shifting. My
stomach has moved up. I have pain in places I don’t have names for.

It is 2:47 AM on day thirteen. I’m sitting on the bathroom tiles with a
paring knife because I thought I could dig them out, excavate them like
archaeological objects. I made two cuts before I realized the edges of
the wound weren’t closing. The skin is peeling back on its own, very
slowly, revealing the coins underneath. They’re not embedded. They’re
integrated. There’s tissue bridging across them now, white connective
fiber that looks almost deliberate. Like my body is building something
around them. Stabilizing them.

My heart rate is erratic. Sometimes it skips for what feels like hours,
then thunders back. The coins are shifting again, making room for what I
think might be more coins, though the original box held twelve and I can
only account for ten and I don’t understand where the logic breaks. The
roommate screamed at 1:30 AM. Just one scream, and then nothing. I
haven’t knocked on her door. I think I already know what I’d find. I
think we’re both past the point of individual harm. I think we’re
becoming something with multiple centers.

The knife is still in my hand. My fingers have gone numb from the wrist
down. The return window closes in fourteen hours. I can feel them
working deeper now. The grinding has stopped. There’s only pressure and
the sensation of making room. I don’t think they’re coins anymore. I
think they became coins so we would accept the delivery, and now they’re
becoming something else.

The cursor is getting harder to see and my right hand isn’t responding
like it should. Whatever this is, it’s using my body to write itself
into the real world, word by word, and I can’t stop it and I’m not sure
I want to.

It’s 2:51 AM. I need to stop typing now. The thing inside me is leaning
on my heart and it finally learned how to push back.
