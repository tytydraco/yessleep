My brother Daniel was seven when he drowned.

I was seventeen.

I think it’s important that you know that because everybody always says
I was just a kid too, like thats gonna make a difference.

It doesn’t

I was his big sister.

I was supposed to be watching him.

Instead I was on my phone arguing with my boyfriend over something I
can’t even remember anymore.

I remember everything else about that day.

I remember Daniel was wearing his stupid dinosaur swim trunks. The green
ones with the orange raptors .

I remember he asked me if fish got thirsty.

he asked if american fish could understand cuban fish.

I remember telling him to stop asking dumb questions.

And I remember the last thing he said to me.

“Maddie, Can you look at me for a second?”

I didn’t.

I told him to wait.

When I finally looked up, there were only ripples in the water.

five years.

I’ve had five fucking years to think about that.

five years to wonder if he was scared. If he called for me. If he
thought I was coming.

Sometimes I imagine him standing on the bottom of the lake, looking up
through the water, waiting for me to put my phone down.

I know that’s not how drowning works.

But my brain won’t let me picture anything else.

My mother never really forgave me.

She never said it outright, but she didn’t need to.

She stopped buying my favorite cereal.

She took down all the family pictures that had me in them.

On Daniel’s eighth birthday, she made a cake.

Chocolate with vanilla frosting.

His favorite.

I came downstairs and found her sitting at the kitchen table with eight
candles burning.

There were two plates.

One in front of her.

One in front of an empty chair.

I sat down.

She looked at me for a long time.

Then she got up, took the second plate, and put it back in the cabinet.

I moved out three months later.

We still talked sometimes.

Birthdays. Christmas. The occasional phone call about the weather.

She died last October.

Her brakes gave out and she skidded off a bridge.

The hospital called me at 3:34 in the morning and told me they were
sorry.

I remember thinking how cruel it was that I could lose my entire family
twice.

I inherited the house.

It’s a small place in northern Michigan, about twenty minutes from the
lake where Daniel drowned.

I didn’t want it.

Honestly, I would’ve sold the thing without ever stepping foot inside if
the lawyer hadn’t told me there were some documents I needed to collect.

So I drove up in November.

The house smelled  the same.

Musty old carpet. Cinnamon candles. That weird oil smell from the
kitchen sink.

My mother had kept Daniel’s bedroom exactly as it was.

His firework sheets were still on the bed.

His little blue slippers were underneath it.

His backpack  on the floor of his closet .

I stood in the doorway for what felt like hours.

Then I noticed the phone.

It was sitting on his pillow.

A little red plastic toy phone.

One of those cheap ones that plays  Ai, ai, ai, I’m your little
butterfly you press the buttons.

Daniel loved that thing. 

I hated it.

The batteries had corroded years ago.

But when I saw it all I could picture was him chasing me around the
house playing that stupid song over and over and over again, I hoped to
hear it one last time ,I held it to my ear, I heard something.

Instead of a warped version of ai, ai, ai I froze all I Heard was 

Water.

A faint bubbling sound.

I pulled it away.

Nothing.

Put it back.

Bubbling.

Then a little boy’s voice said:

“Hello?”

I dropped it.

It hit the floor and the plastic cracked.

I stood there staring at it.

Then I heard him again.

“Maddie?”

My knees gave out.

Because nobody had called me Maddie since Daniel died.

My name is Madison.

Everyone calls me Madison.

Daniel couldn’t pronounce it when he was little, and somehow Maddie had
stuck.

I crawled toward the phone.

“Daniel?”

A pause.

Then, very quietly:

“Are you mad at me?”

I started sobbing.

I couldn’t even speak.

He sounded exactly like he had at seven. That little lisp he had before
his front teeth came in. The way he dragged out his vowels.

“I’m sorry I went too far,” he said.

I pressed the phone against my ear so hard it hurt.

“Monkey, no. No, I’m sorry.”

He was quiet for a moment.

Then:

“Can you come get me?”

I know what you’re thinking.

I know how insane this sounds.

And if you had told me this story a month ago I would’ve thought you
were either lying or having some kind of psychotic episode.

But I heard him.

I heard my baby brother.

He told me things nobody else could have known.

He told me about the time we buried a dead bird in the backyard and made
a little cross out of popsicle sticks.

He told me about the tooth he lost at school and hid under my pillow
because he wanted the tooth fairy to give me money instead.

He told me he remembered the song I used to sing when he couldn’t sleep.

I sang the first line.

He sang the second.

And I swear to God, for the first time in five years, I felt something
other than guilt.

I felt happy.

He asked me if I could bring him home.

I told him I would.

“Promise?”

“Promise.”

Then he said something that made my blood go cold.

“Mommy promised too.”

I searched the house that night.

My mother’s bedroom was almost empty. She had apparently gotten rid of
most of her clothes before she died.

But beneath her bed was a cardboard box.

Inside were dozens of notebooks.

Every page was filled with dates.

Times.

Measurements.

And the same sentence written over and over.

HE IS GETTING CLOSER.

The first notebook was dated three months after Daniel’s death.

October 18, 2019.

He called again tonight. He was crying. I could hear water behind him.
He says it’s cold.

October 26.

I brought his blanket to the lake. The water took it. He slept better
after.

November 4.

He says there are other children. They dont have their own voices yet.
He says they wanna borrow his sometimes.

November 19.

Something touched my ankle while I was standing on the dock. It felt
like a hand but there were too many fingers.

December 2.

He asked me to bring him something warm. I brought the electric blanket.
He said that wasn’t what he meant.

The entries got stranger.

My mother began writing about dreams.

In one, she was walking through a house made of soft, pink walls.

In another, she saw Daniel sitting at a kitchen table, eating cereal
with both hands.

She wrote that his mouth kept opening wider because he was trying to
smile.

One entry was underlined so hard the pen had torn the paper.

He asked me why I stopped loving Madison.

I stared at that sentence for a long time.

Beneath it, my mother had written:

I told him I never stopped.

He said he knew.

He said that’s why it hurt so much when I made her leave.

The last notebook contained only four pages.

The final entry was dated three days before her death.

I think I understand now.

It doesn’t know what a child is.

It knows what a mother needs.

I didn’t sleep.

At 5:30 AM, the toy phone rang.

Not a little electronic jingle.

An actual telephone ringing.

I ran upstairs.

Daniel was crying.

“Maddie, I don’t like it here.”

I sat on the floor with my back against his bed.

“Where are you?”

He sniffled.

“Under.”

“Under what?”

“The people.”

I didn’t understand.

He kept crying.

Then he whispered:

“They get bigger when nobody remembers them.”

I asked him what that meant.

There was a noise on the other end.

A deep, wet clicking.

Like somebody tapping two stones together underwater.

Daniel went silent.

“Daniel?”

Nothing.

“Monkey?”

Then he whispered so quietly I almost missed it:

“Don’t tell it that you love me.”

The phone went dead.

Im going insane
