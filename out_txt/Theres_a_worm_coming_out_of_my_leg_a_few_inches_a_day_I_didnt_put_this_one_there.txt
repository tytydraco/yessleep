The first one was an accident.

Costa Rica, two summers ago, a cheap trip with a guy from work. I came
home with what I thought was a bite on my shoulder that wouldn’t heal.
It had a little hole in the middle that wept clear, and some nights it
moved. I’d be lying there and feel something shift under the skin, a
knuckle rolling over, and then it would stop.

The clinic nurse looked at it for about two seconds. She said botfly,
the way you’d say hangnail. She cut a slab of fat off the pork in her
lunch, taped it over the hole, and told me to sit in the hallway for the
afternoon. I sat. After three hours something started pushing up against
the fat from underneath, and she came out and peeled the tape back and
put a thumb on either side of the hole and pressed.

It came up through the hole backwards, tail first, and every ring of
spines on it caught on the way out, and the place it had been closed up
behind it, and it was the best thing I have ever felt with my body.
Better than sex. Better than the first drink of the day. A cramp letting
go that I’d had my whole life without knowing it was there. She dropped
it on a paper towel and showed me, a fat white grub with two black
hooks, and I said thank you, and I meant it more than I have meant
anything.

The hole closed in a week. I did not think about anything else.

The videos were first. There are millions of us, it turns out. People
sit at their kitchen tables and film themselves squeezing things out of
themselves and out of each other, and the comments underneath are all
the same word, and I understood every one of them now. I watched for a
month. Then I read the comments more carefully, and under the comments
there are forums, and on the forums there is a man in Belize who will
put eggs in a sealed tube for cash and a mailing address.

You learn the timing like wine. Botfly is five to eight weeks in the
skin depending on where you put it and how warm you keep it. You tape
the eggs down on a patch of skin and the larvae hatch off your body heat
and go in on their own, and you don’t feel it. I put the first one in my
forearm so I could watch. Long sleeves at work. The guy from work asked
once what happened to my arm. I said a trip. He stopped sitting with me
at lunch. I slept with the arm on top of the covers. At six weeks it
started pushing against the skin at night, and on a Saturday I taped the
fat over it and sat on the kitchen floor with the timer going on my
phone.

It was good. It was not as good. I knew what that meant. Anybody who has
ever had a second drink knows what that meant.

So I did what you do. Two at once. Then four. The forearm was too easy
to watch, and the watching was taking something off the top, so I went
to places I couldn’t see. A man in Lagos sells cloth. Overnight it, he
says, or don’t bother. You damp it and leave it against your skin and
what’s on the cloth goes in, and in nine days you have a row of them,
and they come out faster than botfly, with a squeeze, no fat, no
waiting. I did a row of six across my lower back and took them out with
two mirrors and an arm bent the wrong way, and it took an hour, and when
the last one came I had to lie down on the bathroom floor until I could
stand.

Then I went deeper. Thigh. Calf. The ones in the calf take longer, and
the skin there is thick, and the drag against it when they come is
nothing like the arm. The feeling scales with the place. I learned that
the way you learn anything, by paying for it.

The man in Belize had something else. He wouldn’t say what. He said it
wasn’t for first-timers and it wasn’t for anywhere you needed, and it
needed a cut to start from. I gave it one. I put it in my left calf.

It did not sit in a pocket. The others sit in a pocket and breathe
through the hole and wait. This one ate. By the fourth day the calf was
hot to the touch, and by the sixth there was a smell, and when I finally
cut the tape off and looked there was no hole, there was a cavity, and
it was moving in there, a lot of it, and the edges were not skin
anymore. I drove myself to the ER with the seat all the way back and my
left leg out straight.

They flushed it. I watched. I was not supposed to watch. A nurse held my
shoulder and said look at me, look at me, and I did not look at her,
because what was coming out of my leg into the basin was the most I have
ever felt in my life, and it did not stop for eleven minutes, and when
it stopped I was crying and she thought it was the pain.

I told them it was a trip. Central America, a river, I didn’t know. They
believed me because why wouldn’t they. They packed it and sent me home
with two weeks of antibiotics and a referral I didn’t use. There is a
hole in my calf now I can set my thumb in to the first joint. It will
never fill. I walk with a hitch. I don’t think about it.

I thought about the other thing, though. It was bigger because it took
more. The feeling scales with what it costs. I tested that, because that
is what I am now, a man who tests that.

The webbing between my right thumb and first finger. One egg from the
tube, taped. You cannot not watch that one. Eight weeks. When it came
out it brought something with it, and it was bigger than the calf, and
the finger doesn’t close all the way anymore. I am typing this with it.
The space bar is the hard one.

I didn’t understand the rest of it until this spring.

The holes heal fast. They always healed fast, and I took that as luck,
or being young, or whatever you take it as. But they heal too fast now,
and too clean. Four days and the skin is tight and smooth and a little
pink and a little warm, a bed somebody has made and turned the corner
down on. Ready.

And I have not ordered anything since December. The man in Belize
stopped answering in October. Lagos stopped in December. I kept meaning
to find somebody else. I didn’t need to. In February I found one in my
thigh, in a site I had used before and never put anything back in,
pushing against the skin at night the way they do. In March, two in the
back row. By May I had stopped checking the sites I knew and started
checking the ones I didn’t, and they were there too.

Every one I ever pulled out and dropped in the sink had already left the
next one behind, and not always where I’d been. I never saw it happen. I
don’t know how it happens. I know that fruit is sweet so an animal will
carry the seed. I know the best feeling of my life is the feeling of one
of them leaving. I know I have carried the seed for two years, gladly,
and told myself I was the one running the farm.

I thought the wanting was my problem. The wanting is the part they made.

Which brings me to the one in my leg.

It started nine days ago as a blister on my right shin, on a patch of
skin that had gone tight and pink the week before for no reason. Then
the blister opened, and there was a white thread in it, and I knew what
it was before I let myself know, because I have read everything there is
to read, and I have never been anywhere it lives. It is not a larva. It
is long. It comes out a few inches a day, as far as you wind it and not
a hair more, and the only way to take it is the way people have taken it
for three thousand years, wound around a stick, once a day, and if you
pull, it breaks, and the rest goes back in, and you start over with a
worse leg.

There is a pencil on the nightstand with three days of it wound on.

It is the biggest feeling yet. It is not close. And I get one go at it a
day. That is what it is charging me.

That is what I keep landing on. The others I could take when they were
ready, when I decided. This one has a schedule. I wind the pencil, a few
slow turns with my bad hand, and it is the best thing in the world for
about ten seconds, and then it stops, and the next one is twenty-four
hours off, and nothing I do makes it sooner. I tried. On the second day
I kept going. It held. I could feel it hold, all the way up inside the
leg, a rope with something on the other end that knows exactly how much
I weigh.

So I wait. I have the pencil and the leg up on a pillow with a towel
under it and the phone, and this is what my hands are doing instead of
the pencil.

The next one is at six. I set an alarm, which is funny, because I have
not needed an alarm in nine days.

After that I’m going to shower and go in to work, first time in two
weeks. I’ll post an update when it’s out. I’ll be fine. The sites are
all healed over. I’ve been checking them, and they’re ready, and so am
I.

It’s 5:40. It’s not ready yet.
