I’m locked inside an empty steel shipping container in Aisle 42. My
phone’s at 34%. The only place it picks up the warehouse Wi-Fi is
against a little square vent halfway up the wall, so I’m typing this
sitting on my jacket and I’ll have to stand up to send it. I’m using
made-up names for the people I worked with. Somebody welded a bolt to
the inside of the door, and Jen’s got her fingers through the handle
because she doesn’t trust it to stay put. If we don’t make it out of
here, I want somebody to know what happened to the night crew,
especially the guy we sent out on the truck.

At eleven last night I was standing in front of the time clock trying to
take a picture of my punch. I’d lost six hours off my last check loading
furniture, and the guy at the agency said a picture of the screen would
have settled it. The damn thing only showed my name for about a second.
A woman behind me said, “Give me your phone and do it again,” and that’s
how I met Jen. She took about thirty pictures in a burst, including one
real good one of the inside of my nose.

I’d worked freight docks for years, and this was the best hourly rate
I’d been offered. It paid a few bucks more than my last job, with a full
eight guaranteed even if they ran out of freight. My rent was three days
late and the starter in my truck had gotten to where I parked on a hill
whenever I could. I’d parked facing downhill that night, in the first
row by the employee entrance. I remember thinking it was nice of a place
that size to leave the good spots for the people who showed up first.

They had coffee going in a glass pot with a white line cooked onto it
about where the last cup had sat all day. While I tried to get the first
swallow down, Jen ate half a ham sandwich, then wrapped up the other
half and put it in the fridge with her name on it. We had both come
through the same agency, and we had both been told to bring a heavy coat
by a woman who’d done the entire call with her turn signal clicking.
“Did she hit you with ‘opportunity for advancement’?” Jen asked. When I
said yeah, she did the turn signal sound with her tongue so exactly I
got coffee up my nose.

The other new hire was up on a chair fixing the break room clock. He
said he couldn’t stand to spend eight hours looking up to see how much
time he had left and finding out it was still ten after six. He swapped
its battery with one from the TV remote, set it off his phone, and left
a piece of masking tape on the remote that said YOU OWE THE CLOCK. His
name was Ben. He’d worked two holiday seasons for the agency and talked
about that like he had tenure.

Ray came in carrying three orange vests and found Ben still up on the
chair. He made him get down, checked all four legs himself, then got up
there to straighten the clock. “That would’ve bothered me all night,” he
said. When he passed me my vest I noticed he was missing two fingers on
one hand. He’d sewn the empty fingers of his work glove flat against the
palm with little black stitches, the kind my dad used to put in a split
truck seat.

There was supposed to be a night lead to give us orientation. Nobody had
signed out the night radio, so Ray called somebody on his own, listened
for a while and told us we were stuck with him. He usually worked three
to eleven and he had a change of socks in his locker, which seemed to be
the deciding factor in who got to stay. Jen asked if they’d at least pay
him double. “Honey, they took the forks out of the vending machine
because people were taking two,” he said, and she went back for the good
plastic fork she’d left with her sandwich.

He walked us down the strip of green paint that kept people on foot
clear of the lifts. We got hard hats, radios and orange foam plugs out
of wall dispensers along the way. Jen went back to the first aid box for
the skinny purple plugs because one of her ear canals was narrow and the
orange ones worked their way out when she chewed. She shook two spare
pairs into my hand. “They’ll tell you one size fits everybody till you
ask the guy who orders them to stick one up his dick,” she said, in the
same ordinary voice she’d used to tell me where the bathroom was.

Our end of the dock had two truck doors side by side. Nine was for
receiving, Ten was for whatever Nine brought in, and the space in
between had a yellow square on the floor big enough to turn a lift in.
The rest of the building did regular warehouse work. Through a wire
partition I could see cases of canned peaches, a couch standing on end,
a toilet with the tank banded to the bowl. I spent a fair amount of that
first hour listening to Ben put together a perfectly livable apartment
out of shit he could see through the fence.

The forklift we got had a grease-stained laminated card zip-tied to the
cage above the seat. Ray wiped it on his sleeve and held it still so we
could read the heading, OSHA & Operations Addendum. Under Pallet
Integrity Standard 12-B, every crate off Bay 9 got three full layers of
opaque black stretch-film before it went out on Ten. The label went on
the outside of the third layer, and if the wrapping it came in had gaps,
you kept your face away from them and wrapped over them. Somebody had
drawn a little row of three boxes in grease pencil on a piece of tape
below the steering wheel, and you could see where somebody kept rubbing
the check marks out with a thumb.

Ben wanted to know who’d be counting if the stuff was black. Ray pointed
to the rows of softwood uprights they used for training, built up on a
pallet about the size of a washing machine. He put a flashlight inside,
gave us the roller and told us to show him three layers. By our count we
went around that thing five times, with me doing the top and Ben
stooping for the bottom, and felt pretty good about it until Jen killed
the work light. Right at the height where we’d traded off there was a
thin stripe we could read each other’s name tags by.

“That’s where you’re both too goddamn considerate to get in the other
guy’s way,” Ray said. He showed us the job again, slow enough for us to
see where the film narrowed when we pulled it tight over a corner. Then
he made me walk around in the dark and look at what I’d done from the
other side. My lower back was already starting up, and I guess I made
some noise standing up because he’d put an upside-down milk crate by my
coat the next time I went to get a drink. He never mentioned it and he
moved it along with us twice.

The lower half of the card was Acoustic PPE Policy 4.2. Foam plugs had
to stay seated any time the overhead conveyor ran above its normal
pitch, even if somebody called to you from an unlit aisle. That last
part struck me as awfully specific. Ray had me try hearing a radio
through the plugs and showed us the difference between his hand up flat
for stop and a fist for hold the load where it was. “You don’t pull a
plug to ask me what I want,” he said. “If my hands aren’t clear you just
stand there and let me look like an asshole until they are.”

A little after eleven thirty a run of rollers started up behind the
wall. I felt each set kick on through the soles of my boots, one after
the next down the length of the dock, and a puff of cold air came out
under the green door we’d used to get in. A yellow light next to it
changed from YARD to TRANSFER. Ray moved my milk crate away from a seam
in the floor before a steel guide came up through the slot and would’ve
tipped me off onto my ass. None of the three of us had noticed the
matching wear marks up both walls.

While we waited for Nine, we sorted returns by hand and learned a little
about each other against our will. My boot had a split where the leather
met the cap, and every trip past the dock drain worked another teaspoon
of water into my sock. Ben traded sides with me because his boots were
new and he wanted to see if the waterproofing was any good before he
lost the receipt. His left one leaked worse than mine. We did the last
half pallet with our outside feet up on a dry board like two big
flamingos, and Jen took a picture that I’d give quite a bit of money to
have on my phone right now.

Around one Jen asked if I’d hold her phone by the vending machine, the
one place she could get the hospital billing site to load. She could pay
forty bucks on Friday, and the little box kept putting the other
seventy-two back in for her as soon as she hit submit. She asked if I
knew what it cost to get something taken out when the hospital took your
insurance but the guy who put you to sleep didn’t. I said I wouldn’t
even try to guess. She said, “I asked for the damn thing back at that
price,” and Ben laughed with his mouth full and had to go pick noodles
out of his vest.

Around quarter to two Ray’s radio finally spit out a time and a load
count. He called back to ask who was coming to relieve him and got the
time and load count all over again. He set me to spotting for him and
put Jen on the paperwork because she was the only one with enough sense
to ask where the freight actually went. The blue copy stayed at the dock
and the yellow copy traveled with the load, in a clear pouch by the
trailer door. There wasn’t a street address on either one, just a
transfer number and a blank for exceptions. Ray said if we saw a
problem, write it on both, because the people reading the blue one and
the people reading the yellow one would each assume it was the other
guy’s job.

At two on the dot the rubber pads outside Nine squealed against the back
of a trailer. I felt the bump through the rail I was leaning on, and
underneath it came a second bump, a lot softer, like a heavy bag getting
dropped on wet cement. The refrigeration units kicked high when Ray
opened the dock door. They were mounted on the wall over our heads and
the bolts had worked loose on one of the covers, so every few seconds
you got a buzz in your molars along with the regular drone. I could feel
my damp sock go cold between my toes.

We were looking down a refrigerated trailer with two rows of black
squares all the way to the nose. Some had little beards of ice along the
bottom where the pallet runners held them off the aluminum floor. The
air smelled of sour grease and wet cardboard, although all the boxes we
could see had wooden feet. There was a narrow run of yellowish water
making its way along one of the grooves in the floor, and when the lift
rolled over the dock plate the water drew back along the groove a good
six inches. I figured the trailer had rocked under the weight.

Ray would drive in, pick a box and set it in the yellow square,
alongside a battery-powered walkie jack we kept handy in case a load
needed turning. The truck at Ten had already backed in for the transfer.
We got each box covered, Jen stuck a numbered label on the finished
wrap, then Ray loaded it straight across. There were twenty-six on the
sheet and enough room in the trailer for exactly twenty-six, two
abreast. Ben did the math on how fast we had to move to get ten minutes
sitting down at the end and revised his estimate every time we ran into
something he hadn’t included in the math.

The first four were heavy for the size. You could tell a lot about a
load from the way it settled, and those settled twice, once when the
forks went down and again when I was most of the way around with the
roll. On the fifth one my knee was against the side when it happened.
Something slid down the other side of the wood and came to rest against
me, and I felt a single little bump move across my kneecap from left to
right. I stepped back hard enough to put my heel over Ben’s toes.

He took it pretty well, considering the boots, and squatted to see if
the pallet had dropped a nail. I told him what I’d felt. He put his
knuckles near the wrapping and I caught his sleeve, which got a laugh
out of both of us because I’d done it exactly the way Ray did. On his
way back up he said it was probably bags of liquid. I’ve handled plenty
of those big plastic bladders of cooking oil and fruit concentrate, and
for a little while that worked for me.

About halfway down the load the yellow water had got far enough to drip
off the dock plate. Jen slid a white spill pad under it and got a broad
brown stripe down the middle of the pad, with tiny off-white chips
caught in the weave. Ray folded it dirty side in without touching any of
the chips. He radioed to ask for someone from receiving to come down to
Nine, and in the quiet between calls we heard a small animal cough in
the trailer. Anybody who has ever held a sick cat against their shirt
would have called it that.

It came from the front right corner, behind the freight still on board.
Ben walked up the dock plate and called “pss pss pss” a couple of times
before Ray caught up and walked him back by the loop on his vest. “We
got something riding loose up there,” Ben said. Ray asked if he’d seen
it loose, and Ben admitted he hadn’t. “Then tell me the part you know
and quit there,” Ray said, and I thought that was a pretty shitty way to
talk to a guy who’d spent the last two hours down on his knees doing the
work nobody wanted.

We took our ten with the door open. Jen put the exception down on both
copies, liquid coming off the last pallet and possible live animal in
trailer, and left a space for the crate number. Ben looked at the line
over her shoulder and thanked her, and she said if a possum came out he
was the one who could pick it up because she had met a possum and they
could go to hell. He said you used a broom and a garbage can, you didn’t
pick it up, what was the matter with her. Ten minutes later she was
still smiling a little, fitting labels in the gun.

I went to Ray about it myself once the others started up again. He had a
crescent of black grease under his jaw where he’d wiped it with the back
of his bad hand. “Say it’s something alive in a box,” I said. “Who comes
and gets it?” He tapped the talk button, made me listen to receiving
ring all the way through again, and said, “You get me somebody who’ll
come down here and open one on purpose, I’ll shake his hand.”

I’d have liked to hear the rest of that and I should’ve stood there
until he told me. Instead I went back and told Ben that if there really
was an animal on the trailer we’d see it when we got the last box off.
That satisfied him enough to go on working. Somewhere around five my
right ear started to hurt from the plugs and my undershirt had got cold
enough to stick to my back every time I bent over. I wanted to be home
eating eggs over the sink, and I was willing to believe about anything
that got me there.

When we got to the last two crates the sound quit. The left one came off
clean, and the right one sat there by itself with a loose tail of film
stuck to the trailer wall. It had evidently been rubbing a rivet the
whole trip. When Ray got the forks under the pallet and backed up, the
film stayed on the rivet, peeling a long strip off the front right
corner the way the skin comes off a blister. A little hinged board
showed through, part of one of those access flaps they put on reusable
crates, and the cloudy liquid ran right off its bottom edge.

I had to guide Ray out backward, so I was level with that corner most of
the way. Something inside shifted its weight and made the wet, heavy
slap we’d heard when the truck bumped the dock. Then I heard a scuffling
that went from near my waist all the way down to my ankle in three
little bursts, and a single impatient scratch at the wood. There was a
gap the width of a quarter between the boards of the flap. I made myself
look at the rear wheel of the forklift until Ray got it to the square
and set it down.

Our last roll of black film had about four turns left on it, nowhere
near enough for one full layer. The supply cage was just across the dock
but you needed Ray’s key, and the new rolls came two to a long carton
somebody had put on the shoulder-high shelf. He put Ben behind the green
line and made him stay there long enough to look Ray in the eye. “I got
this close to one of those gaps once,” Ray said, holding up his bad
hand, “so give it some room till we get back.” He sent Jen for the
trailer number off the wall at Nine while I helped him wrestle a carton
down. We’d just got the carton down when Ben said, “Hey, I see its
little foot.”

I looked back over my shoulder. He had his box cutter in one hand and
two fingers of the other hooked under the strip that had peeled loose.
“Leave it a minute, man,” I said, and he said the plastic was tight
around its leg and he just wanted to take the pressure off. From where I
stood I could see the wood moving in and out a little against the strip.
I had time to drop my end of the carton and get back there, and I stood
there holding the stupid thing and waiting for him to do what I’d said.

He ran the blade up six inches, caught the film in his fist and pulled.
The flap fell open toward him and the work light went straight in. I saw
a patch of bare skin about the color and thickness of the inside of my
cheek, and under that, packed flat in a couple inches of space, a row of
white joints all doubled back on each other. One straightened out and
the next one straightened with it. Ben still had his hand up from
peeling the wrap when the hooks went into the soft part of his forearm.

He walked backward three good steps and brought the whole box off center
on the pallet. I remember being so glad he could move it. I dumped the
rolls and caught him around the waist, and Jen got both hands through
the back of his belt, and for one pull we got enough of that pale arm
out to see there were hooks all the way along both edges, brown on the
tips like a dog’s nails. Then the joints folded back together. I felt
Ben’s wrist hit his own upper arm and heard the bones go separately, two
hard ticks I could feel through his ribs.

“Get my hand out,” he said against my ear, real clearly, like he thought
I was mixed up about which end of him needed help. His boot heels were
slipping on the runoff, so I dropped down to give him my thigh to push
off. Another arm came out under his armpit and picked him up far enough
for both heels to clear the floor. The edge of the flap caught him just
below the ribs, and all his weight came down across that board with my
arms still around him. He screamed with his mouth about an inch from
mine and I got chicken broth on my face. I felt a couple of ribs give
under my arm and his scream cut down to what you’d hear from a guy
trying to talk through a bad coughing fit.

I lost him when the third arm came out. It went around the back of one
knee, and the knee and the boot both went in past his ear, toe first,
through an opening I couldn’t have got a spare tire through. His other
leg kicked me in the chin and I went down looking at the little grease
fittings underneath the walkie jack. For what felt like a long time
those were all I could see, each with its little red cap so nobody would
paint it shut. I could hear Jen saying my name like she was trying to
wake me for school.

By the time I got my head up his belt had caught across the flap and
pulled his jeans low on his hips. Jen still had hold of the belt with
one hand, and the buckle pulled clean through the leather as I watched.
I saw his face down next to the sole of his boot, cheek mashed into the
tread, mouth opening in the little triangle between the heel and the
wood. There was blood in there but his eyes followed Jen’s hand.
Something pulled his hips around with a quick little jerk and that space
closed up and took his face with it.

Ray kicked the flap shut. The board bulged back out hard enough to shove
his boot off and he put it right back, yelling at us to get the black
film. “Get three layers on here and get it on that truck,” he said, and
when I started toward him empty-handed he screamed it again. “Look what
it’s doing to the wood. That son of a bitch gets loose on the floor,
none of us sees seven.”

The near side had split between two screws. Through it I could see the
gray cloth of Ben’s shirt rising against a white length of bare flesh,
then dropping away, and there was so little room in there I couldn’t
tell which one of them was breathing. Jen got the roll started on the
back corner and passed it to me. My hands were wet inside my gloves and
when I turned my wrist the whole glove turned and the roll hit the
floor, spinning off a six-foot length of black that stuck to itself. I
kept pinching at it and getting nothing between my fingers.

Jen tore my gloves off, put the cardboard tube over my bare hand like a
bracelet and held the loose end against the box with her hip. “Walk it
around me,” she said. “Start at my ass and work up.” It was such an
ordinary thing to hear from her that my feet started doing it, and when
I came around a second time she’d moved over just far enough for me to
pull the wrap tight across the flap where her hip had been. Ray kept his
boot there till the last second and the three of us got a bottom and a
top course on without having to shine a light into that box again.

Something hit the wrap directly over the split and raised a bump you
could have set a coffee cup on. Jen pushed the end of a second roll into
my shoulder and went around low as I went around high, overlapping us a
good eight inches across the middle like Ray had taught us. On our third
layer the bump started going down. I could hear a faint dry scratching
inside under the drone from the coolers, and when I came around to take
the end from Jen I saw her lower her mouth close to the box and tell Ben
she was there. I wish I’d thought to say something to him too.

Ray ran for the lift. I bent over behind a pallet of empty trays and
threw up so hard my wet boot came off the floor. When I straightened up
he had the load six inches up on the forks and was watching my hands for
the all clear. It was close to a minute before I gave it to him, with me
standing there thinking whether I could let him put a man on a truck
like that and him sitting up there in the grease with his foot on the
brake waiting for me to decide. Then the top board made a cracking sound
you could hear the length of the dock and I sent him in.

Jen was at the pay phone on the column by Ten. It had an emergency
button where the coin slot used to be, and a picture of a fire engine,
an ambulance and a cop car all coming cheerfully toward you. I could
hear the night radio ringing in the break room every time she pushed the
button. My cell said emergency calls only, then spent nearly a minute
trying before the call failed. Ray put the crate down as close to the
trailer doors as he could get it and backed the forks out with long
strings of bloody plastic stuck to the tips.

We had a pretty bad fight over closing those doors. Ray wanted them shut
immediately and Jen wanted the driver to get back there and see what he
was taking, and I kept trying to get them both to be quiet so I could
hear if Ben was making a sound. Jen slapped the dock call button until
we heard air brakes release on the other side of the wall. It was the
first time Ray had done anything that night without making sure we were
clear. He caught Jen by the back of her vest and hauled her off the
plate as the trailer started to move. The lettering around the button
she’d pushed was so worn all you could read was DRIVER, but as soon as
that trailer moved, a greasy little bulb marked RELEASED lit up
underneath it.

I got about a second’s look through the gap as it pulled away. Two steel
arms on rollers caught the open trailer doors, swung them in against the
frame and drove the locking rods home with a pair of little air pistons.
Where there should’ve been gravel under the back wheels there was an
oily channel set into concrete, with a chain pulling the axle along. The
light only reached as far as the rear tires. Jen was still holding the
yellow copy in the hand she’d used to hit the button, and the last thing
I saw before the dock door came down was the empty clear pouch on the
trailer.

She made me write my name next to hers on the exception line. She put
down what happened from the moment Ben cut the film until the trailer
left, and she made Ray spell his last name for her even though it was
right there on his vest. I finally understood what she was doing when
she wrote that we’d loaded him alive. My first impulse was to tell her
she didn’t know that for sure, and then I remembered just how long I’d
stood there waiting to hear him. I signed the goddamn thing and folded
the blue copy in behind my driver’s license. I’ve got it out in front of
me now and my name is in the wrong box, down where the receiver’s
supposed to sign.

The way up front was through the green door and along the fence past
returns. We got as far as returns and found a stretch of steel rollers
running through the gap where the green paint used to carry on across
the floor. There were old black marks on the posts at either side of the
gap, about as high as my chest, and a folded yellow crossover bridge
standing on end against one of them. Ray put his good hand on the button
marked LOWER and held it till the knuckle went white. The bridge stayed
up. Somewhere all the way down the line another set of rollers started
up.

We spent most of the next hour following that fence, looking for a gate
wide enough to get a man through. Half the building was on the other
side moving along at a walking pace, couches and cases of canned goods
going past headed the same way as Ben’s truck. There were places we
could have climbed if we’d wanted to drop twelve feet onto moving
freight. I asked Ray when they’d installed all this and he gave me an
honest answer that scared me worse than if he’d known. “I clock out at
eleven,” he said. “That boy in the box is the first one I ever had to
put on a truck.”

Jen found us a way across, a pedestrian underpass with an arrow painted
so low on the curb we missed it coming the other way. We crawled under
the rollers on our hands and knees through about ten yards of hydraulic
grease and oily water. Ray stopped halfway and I had to go back for him
because he couldn’t take his weight on his bad hand anymore. I pulled
him through the rest of the way by his belt, and when we got out I sat
with him on the floor till he got his breath back. He told me he’d let a
hospital put down his two fingers as a saw accident because he couldn’t
afford to lose the job, and now he had no idea what he was going to tell
Ben’s folks. He asked if I’d seen enough of Ben at the end to know
whether he was still awake, and I had to tell him yes.

We came up by the break room fridge. Jen took her half sandwich out,
looked at it like she couldn’t remember why she’d gone in there, and put
it in her vest. The clock Ben had fixed said six fifty-five. We were
covered in dried blood and hydraulic grease, all three of us still
wearing our hard hats and those fucking earplugs, and I was thinking how
glad I was that I’d parked close. I had a picture in my head of the
three of us sitting across the bench seat of my truck calling the state
police with the heater going full blast on our socks.

We walked to the front employee exit together. Jen got her phone out
ready to make the call the second she had bars and Ray asked me if I
could give him a ride too, like there was any chance I’d leave him
standing on the curb. The red metal crash bar had a crescent chipped out
of the paint where my belt buckle had hit it when I’d gone back out to
my truck for my coat before I punched in. I put both hands on that same
bar and pushed, and the door scraped over a floor on the other side that
was a good inch higher than the sidewalk had been.

A bolt sticking out of a rack upright stopped the door about three
quarters of the way open. Cold sprinkler water blew in across my boots.
I was looking down a long straight aisle of steel shelves with ladders
bolted up the ends, ten feet between levels and ten levels that I could
count before the tops disappeared into fog. Across the aisle a shipping
container sat on the floor with its door ajar and a white 42 painted on
its side. Beyond it were more aisles and more shelves with little red
clearance lights at their tops, and breaks in the fog where you could
see the lights keep going for miles.

There was a board on our side of the first upright with a small
rectangle marked EMPLOYEE SERVICES at the edge of a floor plan. It took
me a second to find the little red dot for where we stood, right against
the edge of that rectangle, halfway down a sheet of ninety numbered
aisles labeled RACKING CANOPY. Jen had one foot out the door with her
phone up when the overhead conveyor started to scream. Both her hands
went to her ears, and her phone fell faceup on the wet concrete between
us and the open container. I turned to go back inside and saw where the
belt went through a slot above the door, all the way back over our break
room. There were gray feet hooked over the frame on either side of it,
and one pair at a time, coming toward us down the line, they were
letting go.
