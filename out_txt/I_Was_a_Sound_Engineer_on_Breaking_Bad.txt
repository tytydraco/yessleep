I was a sound engineer on Breaking Bad. I’ve told this story very few
times.

What matters is the night before we filmed the opening scene of
Ozymandias.

That scene was supposed to be shot in the morning, with the low desert
sunlight, and To’hajiilee is far away from everything.

To avoid wasting time setting up the equipment, they sent a small crew
to spend the night there beforehand. Five people and me.

We had to set up the work tents, test the generators, run cables, and
charge batteries. I also wanted to record nighttime and dawn ambience so
I’d have some clean audio to work with.

We’d filmed at the reservation a few days earlier, so the place held no
surprises for us. We knew every rock.

Someone had to stay awake and keep an eye on the equipment. If it rained
unexpectedly, some incredibly expensive gear could get ruined. And at
night, coyotes sometimes wandered around, occasionally something bigger.

I volunteered. I was going to be awake recording anyway.

The others crawled into their tents at around three in the morning, and
within ten minutes, I couldn’t hear anyone.

It was cold. That dry kind of cold that settles in suddenly after the
sun goes down.

I had a thick jacket, a thermos of coffee, and a work lamp hanging from
the pole of the large tent. It was the only source of light for miles.

I pointed my shotgun microphone toward the open desert, put on my
headphones, and started recording.

The desert at night is louder than most people think.

Crickets, the occasional distant coyote, a highway God knows how many
miles away, the wind dragging sand against the microphone’s foam
windscreen.

And even when there’s nothing else, the recording equipment itself has a
noise floor, a faint hiss that you stop noticing after five minutes.

I know that hiss better than the sound of my own breathing.

Sometime after four in the morning, the sounds began to disappear.

Not all at once.

First, the crickets stopped chirping, one by one. I figured it was
normal, that the temperature was dropping.

Then the wind stopped.

Then I realized the hiss was gone too.

I looked at the recorder’s meters.

They were completely flat.

I thought something had come unplugged. I took off my headphones to
check, and that’s when I realized the problem wasn’t the equipment.

There was nothing outside either.

The tent canvas had been flapping all night, but now it hung completely
still.

I snapped my fingers.

The snap sounded dry and short, without that tiny bit of surrounding air
that every sound normally has.

The hair on the back of my neck stood up.

It made no sense.

And yet, the only thing I could bring myself to do was stay perfectly
still, like when you’re a kid and hear a strange noise somewhere in the
house.

That’s when I saw her.

Very far away, directly in line with the light from my lamp, something
was moving across the open desert.

A dot.

At first, I thought it was an animal. Then I thought it might be someone
from the crew who had woken up.

But everyone was inside their tents.

The dot kept getting bigger, and once it started taking shape, I
realized it was a person.

A woman walking toward me.

She walked in a straight line, without a flashlight, without stumbling.

The ground out there is completely uneven. Bushes, rocks, holes.

Anyone walking through that terrain at night would keep their head down,
watching where they stepped.

But she kept her head up, looking straight toward the campsite, walking
with the calm confidence of someone returning home.

She walked as if the entire reservation belonged to her.

It took her about twenty minutes to reach me.

I thought about waking someone up, but somehow I felt that would be a
bad idea.

I couldn’t explain why.

So I stayed.

When she was about a hundred meters away, I realized I was gripping my
thermos so tightly that my hand hurt.

I put it on the ground, stood up, and walked toward her.

I think I did it because the alternative was sitting there waiting for
her, and somehow that seemed worse.

I wanted to speak to her the way you’d speak to any ordinary person,
just to make everything feel normal.

When she got close enough, I could see her clearly beneath the light.

She looked to be in her late thirties or maybe forty. I couldn’t really
tell.

Jeans and a short-sleeved T-shirt.

In that cold.

No jacket, no bag, nothing in her hands.

I said, “Good evening.”

She said, “Good evening,” and smiled.

There was nothing obviously wrong with her.

And that’s the part I have the hardest time explaining.

She moved normally. She spoke normally.

But her face was too symmetrical, one side almost perfectly identical to
the other.

And her eyes barely moved.

When people talk, they glance to the side, at their hands, at the
ground.

She looked directly at me the entire time, blinking only as much as
necessary.

After a while, I stopped looking at her face and focused on her
shoulder, right at the edge of the light.

And I could still tell when she was staring at me, even without looking
at her.

You can feel it on your skin, like when someone is staring at the back
of your neck.

Her voice was normal, slightly deep, but something was missing.

It took me a while to realize what it was, which is strange because I’m
a sound engineer. Sometimes the most obvious things escape me.

Her voice had no echo.

When I spoke, my voice bounced faintly off the truck and the tents
before returning to me.

Hers simply came out and died right there.

As if she were speaking inside a padded room.

And her breath didn’t fog in the cold either.

Every time I spoke, little white clouds escaped my mouth.

With her, even when I looked against the light, there was barely a trace
of mist, and it disappeared almost immediately.

We talked about meaningless things.

I told her what I was doing there, that we were filming a television
series, that I was responsible for the sound.

She nodded as though she already knew.

I asked what she was doing walking around at that hour.

She said she lived over there.

She gestured with her chin toward the open desert, in the direction
she’d come from.

I looked.

There was nothing.

Miles and miles of nothing, with the dark outline of the mesas in the
distance.

She said she liked going for walks at night.

I pointed out that she wasn’t even wearing a jacket in that cold.

She laughed.

One second too late.

Like laughter arriving after the joke had already ended.

In Breaking Bad, the Salamanca twins barely speak, and watching them
teaches you that with certain people, the safest thing to do is say very
little.

I remembered that that night with a strange sense of irony, because with
her, it was the exact opposite.

Every time I stopped talking, even for a few seconds, something in the
air began to feel wrong.

A pressure.

A sound that didn’t exist, but somehow kept growing louder.

So I talked about anything I could think of.

I talked a lot.

I don’t know how much time passed.

Forty minutes, maybe more.

At some point, I heard myself speaking and suddenly realized something.

I had told her my full name.

The one printed on my driver’s license.

I had told her I lived in Albuquerque, exactly which part of the city,
and that I lived alone.

I had told her my birthday.

The day, the month, and the year.

She had been asking me these things gradually, with small, natural
questions.

And I had answered every single one without hesitation.

Meanwhile, she hadn’t told me anything about herself.

Nothing beyond “I live over there.”

I didn’t say anything.

I didn’t even make a gesture.

But she knew.

Or maybe the air knew.

Something switched off.

That sense of a person standing in front of me, that subtle feeling that
someone was there, suddenly came undone, like a knot being untied.

No monster appeared.

She still had the same face.

The same clothes.

Except now, there was nobody behind them.

The closest comparison I can make is when your dog or cat stares at you,
and you know there’s something thinking behind those eyes, but you have
absolutely no idea what it is.

Except I’ve never given my cat my date of birth.

She stopped smiling.

She didn’t become serious or angry.

Her expression simply turned neutral.

Empty.

A face with no instructions.

And that frightened me more than if she’d bared her teeth.

Because at least with an angry face, you know what to expect.

I started talking again, as best I could.

About the show.

About the cold.

About how terrible the coffee at craft services was.

Nonsense.

She didn’t answer.

She didn’t even nod.

She just stared at me.

And I could hear my own voice growing thinner and thinner, without an
echo, alone in the desert.

Like I was talking inside a room without walls.

Then, in the middle of a word, she interrupted me.

“You should check on them.”

She said it in her usual voice, without changing her tone.

She didn’t say who she meant.

She didn’t have to.

I glanced toward the tents without meaning to.

When I turned back, she was still standing in exactly the same spot,
staring at me.

It wasn’t a threat.

It didn’t even sound like one.

But I received it as an order.

And my body obeyed before I could make any decision.

Thank God it did.

Because it gave me a way out.

I walked toward the tents.

I didn’t run.

Something told me that running would make things worse.

I could feel every step in my back.

I didn’t turn around until I reached the first tent.

When I finally looked back, the desert was empty.

Nobody was walking away.

And there was nowhere to hide out there.

You can see all the way to the horizon.

At that exact moment, the crickets started chirping again.

All at once.

Deafeningly loud.

Like someone had switched on a machine.

I went from tent to tent, opening each one.

Nobody was missing.

Nobody appeared to be hurt.

But every time I said someone’s name or touched their shoulder, their
eyes opened immediately, as though they had already been on the verge of
waking up.

Two of them, Kyle and Dana, sat upright suddenly, breathing heavily.

Their shirts were soaked with sweat despite the cold.

Kyle grabbed my wrist.

Dana looked at me.

Then toward the entrance of the tent.

The six of us sat beneath the work lamp, wrapped in blankets.

Kyle and Dana described their dreams separately, without discussing them
beforehand.

And their dreams were almost identical.

Both had dreamed they woke up inside their tents, unable to move.

Someone was sitting beside them.

A woman.

Her hands resting on her knees.

Completely still.

Watching them.

She wasn’t doing anything.

They both used those exact words.

“She wasn’t doing anything.”

And that was the worst part.

Because it seemed like she was waiting for them to run out of strength
trying to stay awake.

Dana said that, in the dream, she somehow knew that if she truly fell
asleep, that thing would take something from her.

She couldn’t explain what.

Kyle described almost the exact same feeling, with only a few small
differences.

They had been sleeping in separate tents.

Neither had told the other anything.

I decided not to tell them about the woman.

We were already shaken enough, and the sun still hadn’t risen.

It wasn’t the time to make any decisions.

We stayed there for another hour and a half, until the sky finally began
to brighten.

The rest of the crew arrived at six in the morning, bringing coffee and
the cheerful energy of people who had slept well.

None of us six said a word.

Kyle and Dana exchanged one glance.

That was all.

We filmed the scene that morning.

Everything went perfectly.

If you watch the episode, you can hear the ambient sound I recorded that
morning.

Nothing from the night before.

That night, I had accidentally left the recorder running.

There’s an audio file more than three hours long.

I’ve never opened it.

It’s stored on a hard drive inside a drawer.

I haven’t deleted it either.

Since that night, I don’t give my date of birth to anyone who doesn’t
absolutely need it.

I know that sounds excessive.

But I think about how easily she got that information out of me.

She hadn’t even needed to insist.

And sometimes, when a silence lasts just a little longer than it should,
I feel an overwhelming need to start talking about anything at all.

Anything to fill the empty space.

I still don’t understand why she told me to check on the tents.

I prefer to think it was a warning.

I’m not really sure why.

As for the others, I think they all quit over the following weeks.

Last night, I connected the hard drive without giving it much thought.

What pushed me to do it was an idea that somehow got into my head and
refused to leave.

I needed to listen to it.

I needed to convince myself that what I’d experienced was some kind of
strange hallucination.

Just an exhausted man, alone in the desert in the middle of the night,
talking to himself.

Then I could call it exhaustion.

A waking dream.

Whatever it was.

And finally stop thinking about it.

i pressed play.

“Good evening.”

“Good evening.”
