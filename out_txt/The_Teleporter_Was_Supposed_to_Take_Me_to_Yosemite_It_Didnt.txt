I’ve been waiting almost ten years to tell this story.

Until a few weeks ago, I legally couldn’t.

I’m not going to tell you exactly where I live. I’m also not giving my
last name, the names of anyone else involved, or anything that could
identify the town. Those are among the restrictions that are still in
place. Andrea is enough.

Almost ten years ago, the small town where I live was selected for a
transportation pilot program run by a company called TeleQwest. Small is
probably an understatement. We’re isolated enough that getting anywhere
useful takes planning. Shopping, appointments, entertainment, most of it
requires a drive. So when TeleQwest approached the town with what they
called “instantaneous point-to-point transportation,” people listened.

They held a town meeting and packed nearly everyone into one room. Their
representatives explained that the technology itself was not
experimental. According to them, the teleporters had already undergone
years of testing and had successfully transported people thousands of
times.

They weren’t testing whether they worked.

They were testing whether people liked using them.

They described it as the equivalent of test-driving a car that had
already passed every safety inspection. They wanted to know how ordinary
people would incorporate teleportation into everyday life. Where would
we go? How often would we use it? Was the booking system convenient?
Were the booths easy to operate? What would we change?

Our town was small enough that they could install the necessary
equipment throughout the community and study an entire population
instead of recruiting scattered individuals. There was one catch. The
project only went forward if the town agreed to participate, and
everyone who enrolled had to sign an enormous confidentiality agreement.
We couldn’t discuss the technology, the equipment, problems with the
system, or anything that happened during the trial with anyone outside
the program.

At the time, that didn’t bother me. Why would it? They told us the
teleporters were safe.

And for quite a while, they were.

The trial eventually ended. The teleporters never became the
revolutionary new transportation system we had been told they would
become, and for years I assumed my NDA would follow me to the grave.
Then, a few weeks ago, I received a letter from TeleQwest informing me
that portions of the confidentiality agreement had been terminated. I
had a lawyer read it before I said a word to anyone.

There are still things I cannot disclose. I can’t identify the town or
the other participants. I can’t share documents, technical information,
or certain details about the program. But according to my lawyer, I can
finally talk about something that happened to me personally.

So I’m going to.

This happened on what should have been an ordinary trip to Yosemite
National Park.

I roll out of bed and stretch toward the ceiling. My orange cat drops
from his perch and winds around my ankles, purring hard enough to
vibrate against my shins.

“All right, all right, Paprika,” I say, bending to run my hand down his
back.

He answers with a sharp little meow and follows me from the bedroom into
the living room. I grab the bag of cat food from the coffee table on my
way to the kitchen, fill his bowl, then crouch to give him a few more
strokes while he eats as though I have starved him for a week.

“What should we do today, Pappy?”

He pauses and looks up at me with the solemn concentration of someone
considering an important proposition. Then he lowers his head and
continues chewing.

“Guess I’ll go for a hike in Yosemite National Park. Maybe take lunch
with me.”

I return to my room, pull on a pink tank top, black yoga pants, and
tennis shoes, then collect my earbuds from the computer desk. In the
kitchen, I make a sandwich, wrap it, and tuck it into my small backpack.
I put on my Yankees cap and head outside.

The teleporter booth is two blocks away. Mrs. Huddleston is inside when
I arrive, dragging a grocery wagon through the opening as slowly as
possible. I glance down at my wristband impatiently.

ANDREA 4590 glows in blue letters.

I’m not late yet.

But then the band turns orange. I begin tapping my foot.

“Mrs. Huddleston, can I help you? My wristband has turned orange, and I
really don’t want to rebook the trip on the app.”

“No, no, no. I can do it myself. Young people these days have no
patience. I’ll be out of your way in a jiffy.”

She hauls the wagon clear of the booth. I try to squeeze past her, but
she lifts one hand.

“Wait there, missy. I left my milk.”

She waddles back inside, retrieves a gallon jug, and finally steps
aside.

“It’s all yours,” she says curtly. Then, not quite quietly enough, she
adds, “Pushy little thing, aren’t we?”

I ignore her and enter the booth. The door slides shut behind me with
its familiar pneumatic sigh. I put in my earbuds. A woman’s voice speaks
from above.

“Destination: Yosemite National Park. Please step through the…”

I press play before she finishes. A Queen song begins playing as the
portal opens, its surface shimmering like heat over pavement. I step
through. The chorus blares in my ears as the ground vanishes beneath me.
For one impossible second, I am suspended in darkness.

Then I fall.

The air tears a scream from my throat. I glimpse a black plain rushing
upward and have only enough time to throw out my hands before I slam
into the earth. The impact drives the breath from my body. I lie twisted
on my side, unable to inhale, while the music continues brightly in my
ears. My chest convulses. Nothing happens. Panic blooms with cold
precision.

Then air finally claws its way back into me. I roll onto my back and
suck in a ragged breath. There is almost no light.

Far above me hangs a violet sphere the size of a moon in the abyss. No
stars puncture the darkness. The orb seems less like something in the
sky than an opening cut into it, a single purple wound suspended over a
landscape I cannot see. For a moment, I have the nauseating impression
that it is not shining down on me.

It is looking at me.

I shake the thought.

“Oh, my God.”

The air is wet enough to drink. Every breath enters warm and heavy,
coating my throat. Sweat gathers almost immediately beneath my tank top.
I sit up too quickly. The darkness tilts.

Where the hell am I?

Something blue moves in the distance. I grab my phone and switch on the
flashlight. The beam reveals pale sand, stones with glassy black seams,
and shallow grooves that curve across the ground before disappearing
beyond the light’s reach.

No trees. No trail. No granite cliffs.

No Yosemite.

“They sent me to the wrong place.”

I frown down at my wristband. The letters are gone. The entire face of
the band glows red.

What the hell?

My phone shows no service and fifty-five percent battery. I should not
have watched that video in bed this morning. It ate through my power.
The blue light is coming closer. It looks like a smiley face floating by
itself through the dark.

I stop the music. The silence that replaces it is so complete that I can
hear the blood moving in my ears. There is no wind blowing, no crickets
chirping, and no owls hooting in this place. Only a low mechanical hum
drawing nearer.

“Hello?” I call out. My voice sounds small and wrong in the open dark.
“Is someone there?”

The blue face approaches. I aim the flashlight at it and reveal a
metallic orb hovering several feet above the ground. A simple digital
smile is displayed across one side. It stops just outside the brightest
part of my beam.

Then it speaks.

The language is not merely unfamiliar. The sounds overlap in ways that
do not seem possible for a single speaker. Wet clicks pass beneath a low
electronic drone. Several sounds rise together, separate, then collapse
into one another. The hair lifts along my arms.

“I can’t understand you. Can you speak English?”

The Orb remains motionless. Its smile flickers. Blue becomes green.

“You are a Class Five human,” it says in a flat voice. “I am not
programmed to interact with your species. I possess limited language
data for your species. Do best I will. Proper chain has been reported.
Follow, please, for safety.”

“Proper chain?”

The Orb turns and begins gliding away. Green light spills from its rear
surface and stains the sand behind it. I stay where I am. The purple
sphere hangs above me. The darkness beyond my flashlight feels immense,
but not empty. I cannot explain the certainty that something occupies
it, perhaps many things, all of them remaining just beyond the beam.

The Orb continues moving. Standing alone suddenly seems worse than
following it. I force myself to my feet and go after it.

“Where am I?” I ask.

The Orb gives no sign that it heard me. The ground slopes almost
imperceptibly downward. My flashlight reveals only pale sand and
scattered black stones, all of it disappearing into the darkness beyond
the beam.

After several minutes, a wall emerges from the dark. At least, I think
it is a wall. Its surface rises beyond the beam of my flashlight and
stretches in both directions farther than I can see. It has no windows
anywhere along it. The material looks black and perfectly smooth,
swallowing most of my flashlight beam.

By the time we reach it, my clothes are soaked from the humidity and my
own sweat. A circular opening contracts into existence. The Orb
disappears into the hole. A set of doors slides apart below it. The
opening is wide. The Orb waits within, its green face smiling.

I turn and look back. The desert has vanished behind me. My flashlight
reveals only blackness beyond a few feet of sand, as though the world
ends at the edge of the beam.

So I step inside.

The doors close immediately. The chamber is small and windowless. Violet
light leaks through dozens of tiny apertures in the walls. The holes are
arranged without a pattern I can understand, yet when I move, the
brightest ones always seem to remain opposite me. I tell myself they are
ventilation openings.

The Orb disappears through another circular passage. A second set of
doors opens.

Hot, wet air strikes me like a hand across the face. I wipe the sweat
from my eyes and stare. The chamber beyond is a forest, but the word
feels inadequate. It is a vast interior wilderness whose ceiling
disappears into violet haze. Lavender light falls through distant
openings above.

Plants crowd every surface. Veins of pink and blue pulse beneath
translucent leaves. Green markings open and close along black trunks.
Fine filaments drift through the air like spider silk, glowing in the
violet beams. It reminds me of a barn party I attended when I was
seventeen, where black lights made clothing and body paint shine vibrant
in the dark and smoky space.

The memory gives me anxiety.

Am I drugged?

The Orb hovers ahead on a narrow path. I realize my phone is hanging at
my side, illuminating only my shoes. I raise it.

The flashlight cuts through the purple haze. Trees tower toward the
unseen ceiling. They resemble pines only in the broadest sense. Instead
of needles, their branches carry clusters of lavender appendages like
sea-urchin spines made from soft flesh.

When the beam touches them, every appendage withdraws at once. Away from
the light and into the wood. The branches close over them without seams.
I sweep the beam across low growth. Leaves fold inward. A vine slips
into a crack. Several pale flowers turn their openings toward the
ground.

The entire forest recoils from my light in a widening circle.

What in the world is going on?

The doors slam shut behind me. Startled, I spin around and stare at the
closed door. Then I try jamming my fingers into the seam. Nothing.

I pound the surface with the side of my fist.

“Open!”

The wall gives no response.

Guess I’m stuck here.

“Follow,” the Orb chimes.

I turn toward it. The path winds between roots broad enough to be walls.
Every shadow thrown by my phone resembles something crouched with its
limbs pulled close. When I move, those shapes seem to lag a fraction of
a second behind the plants that cast them.

I check my phone. Forty-three percent. My stomach tightens.

“That can’t be right.”

It was only fifty-five percent minutes ago.

I switch off the flashlight and pocket the phone. The ambient glow is
barely enough to follow the Orb, but my eyes begin adjusting. I take off
my cap and fan myself. The moving air is no cooler. A pressure starts
behind my eyes. I rub my temples but get no relief.

Ahead, blue light glimmers through the trees. The path opens beside a
pool fed by a narrow fountain pouring from the wall. The liquid is too
luminous to be water. Pale pink bodies resembling jellyfish drift
beneath the surface, opening and closing in slow unison. Two stone
benches face the pool.

Something lies beside one of them.

I stop.

The outline is humanoid. For several seconds, I convince myself it is a
fallen branch tangled in gray moss. It does not move. I turn the
flashlight back on. White and purple spots burst across my vision. When
they clear, the form sharpens.

It is built somewhat like a person, but the proportions are subtly
wrong. The arms are too long. The chest is narrow and divided by ridges
that do not correspond to human ribs. Its hands have four fingers, each
jointed once more than my own. Its skin is gray and shines like oil on
water. A clear secretion coats its naked body. It resembles a human
crossed with a salamander, but there is no tail and no visible nostrils.

“Wha… what is it?”

The words crawl out of my mouth.

I should back away. Instead, I move closer. Its wide mouth hangs open.
There are no teeth, only a dark inner membrane that flexes faintly with
each shallow breath.

So it’s alive. Should that make me feel better?

It does not.

I crouch and reach toward the nearest leg.

Don’t touch it.

My fingers make contact. The skin is cold, soft, and slick enough that
my fingertips slide before I can pull away. A translucent strand
stretches from its leg to my hand. It narrows, glistening in the
flashlight beam, but refuses to break. Something is secreting from the
monster. It remains still.

“Oh, fuck. What am I doing? Touching the fucking thing.”

I wipe my hand on my yoga pants. The slime spreads into a thin film and
clings between my fingers. If you have ever held a snail or slug, you
know the kind of residue I mean. Except this has a faint metallic smell,
and when the violet light touches it, tiny colors ripple through it.

I scrub harder. Some comes off. Not all.

“Yuck.”

“Follow,” the Orb demands behind me.

I jump so violently that my heel strikes the edge of the pool.

“I will,” I snap. “What else am I going to do?”

We continue. The path narrows between bushes. I shine the flashlight
ahead, wondering how many more bodies lie outside the beam.

Thirty-five percent.

The battery is falling too quickly.

I turn it off again and wait for my eyes to adjust. After a few moments,
I continue down the path. A vine curls around my ankle. I jerk free. The
nearby bushes tremble in a coordinated shiver. Their leaves close over
one another, then slowly reopen after I move away.

I reflexively reach for my phone again but think better of it. Ahead,
another body lies across the path. This one is facedown, one arm trapped
beneath it and the other extended toward the wall. In the dimness, it
looks as though it collapsed while trying to crawl away.

I don’t turn on the flashlight. I don’t want to see its face. I step
over it quickly. For several paces, I feel certain that its long fingers
are going to close around my ankle. They do not.

The Orb leads me into a narrow corridor. One side is rough black stone.
The other is thick glass. Beyond the glass is a chamber filled with
floating oval cylinders.

They resemble Japanese lanterns from a distance. Each contains a
luminous core that moves independently of its shell. Pastel colors drift
through them in slow pulses. Whenever two pass near one another, their
light briefly synchronizes.

Bodies lie scattered beneath them. One is curled on its side as though
asleep. Another is slumped against a wall. A third hangs half across a
low platform, its fingers almost touching the floor. The beauty of the
lanterns makes the stillness worse.

The Orb continues down the corridor.

Glass doors open into a broad chamber of giant toadstools. Light
radiates beneath their gills and through large spots on their caps.
Stone benches stand among them. One especially tall mushroom supports a
gray figure draped across its cap. Its arms hang on one side. Its feet
hang on the other.

It looks less like someone who went to sleep than someone whose body
stopped receiving instructions.

Are they all sleeping? Is there a sun here? Will it rise and wake them?

These thoughts just add to my uneasiness.

The Orb directs me toward a bench.

“Sit. Stay.”

“I’m not a dog.”

I sit anyway. The Orb hovers nearby. A row of doors lines the far wall,
each with a symbol above it. Most of the symbols glow red. One is green.

After several minutes, I stand. The Orb does nothing. I approach the
green door. Another motionless figure lies beside it. This one wears a
wristband similar to mine, glowing red. Symbols burn across its surface.

I look at the markings above the doors. Three doors away, one matches
the band on the sleeping creature.

“Is that your room?” I ask the unconscious being. “Is that your name
above the door?”

The question sounds absurd as soon as I say it. I walk the row anyway.
None of the symbols become letters. Yet if I stare too long, some seem
close to things I recognize: a profile of a face, a hand with too many
fingers, a being inside a circle. The impressions vanish whenever I
blink.

None say my name.

“That is probably a good thing.”

The Orb watches without reprimanding me.

I decide to test its limits. I enter the lantern room. The Orb follows.
The chamber is brighter than the others, but the light is gentle. It
does not stab at my eyes. The floating cylinders drift overhead without
sound.

I approach one. The closer I get, the calmer I feel. My heartbeat slows.
The pressure behind my eyes retreats. Before I understand what I am
doing, I place my palms on either side of the oval shell.

The room disappears.

A small childlike being runs through a forest.

The forest.

Wet heat fills my lungs. I feel four toes press into living soil. I hear
layers of sound above and below the range of human hearing. Somewhere
inside the child’s narrow chest, three quick pulses beat out of rhythm
with one another.

The child is laughing. It reaches into a bush and pulls free a
plum-shaped fruit covered in moving hairs. It bites down.

For one impossible instant, I taste color.

Lavender is sweet. Green is sharp. A thin silver sensation passes
through me that is neither flavor nor sound, but the certainty that I am
loved.

The child laughs again. The joy is so complete that it hurts.

Then the lantern releases me. I stumble backward, crying without knowing
why. The room returns around me. The floating light drifts exactly where
it was before.

“That was a child.”

I approach another lantern, deep blue, and touch it. A teleporter opens
inside my mind. An older being waits beside it, its body bent and
wrinkled, its skin lighter than the others. The same child runs forward
and embraces the elder.

The contact floods through me.

Relief. Recognition. A sense of belonging so vast that the human word
love feels too small.

The memory vanishes. I release the lantern, then seize it again. The
same scene returns.

“It’s like home videos,” I whisper.

But it is not like a video. It is memory stripped of distance. Memory
that does not care whose mind receives it.

I look at the bodies scattered beneath the lights. I had been thinking
of them as monsters because I did not understand their bodies. But the
lanterns leave no room for that thought. These beings remember their
childhoods. They wait for family members to return. They preserve
moments of love and relief.

And every one of them has fallen unconscious where they stood.

The realization is more frightening than believing they were beasts.

Something has silenced an entire community. And the machine guiding me
behaves as though this is routine.

I sit on a bench again and fan myself with my cap. Sweat runs down my
neck. My headache has become a steady pulse once again. If something did
this to everyone here, sitting still may be the worst thing I can do.

I force myself upright. A second set of doors opens when I approach.

The next chamber is nearly dark. I turn on my flashlight with my eyes
closed, then open them gradually. The room is about fifteen feet square,
with riveted metal walls and six enormous portholes. Between two of
them, a ladder climbs upward until it disappears into darkness.

I shine the beam along the rungs. The light reaches no end. The ladder
continues beyond sight.

I lower the flashlight. Through the nearest porthole is a submerged
forest. Long stems drift in black water. Broad leaves pulse with faint
violet light. Pale root systems hang downward from nothing I can see.
The plants recoil from my flashlight.

I stare through the glass. Sweat runs into my right eye. I wipe it. When
my vision clears, a face is pressed against the other side of the
porthole.

I scream and stumble backward.

The alien’s eyes are open. Its pupils are huge, black, and without
reflection. For one awful second, I know it is looking at me. Then the
body rotates in the water. Its arms drift loose.

It is unconscious.

At least, I hope it is.

Thin plants curl around one leg and draw it slowly into the foliage.
Their tendrils pass over the body with the delicacy of fingers searching
a sleeping face. I shine the light deeper. Another body floats between
the plants. Behind it is another. And farther back, beyond the reliable
reach of the beam, pale shapes hang at different depths throughout the
water.

The flashlight catches one final face. Its eyes are closed. Its mouth
opens. A stream of silver bubbles rises, but instead of floating upward,
they drift sideways into the dark.

I step away from the glass. The ladder waits behind me. I could climb
it. The thought feels inserted rather than chosen.

I grip the wall.

“What am I thinking? I can barely stand.”

My phone vibrates. Twenty percent.

“Ahh, shit.”

I shut off the flashlight. Darkness closes around me. For several
seconds, the face beyond the glass remains visible in the afterimage
burned into my sight.

Then it opens its eyes.

I cannot tell whether that happens in the room or only inside my head. I
back out and return to the mushroom chamber.

I sit on the stone bench and try to remember what the Orb said outside.
Was someone coming for me? Is that what it said, or did it say it had
alerted the authorities? My head hurts too badly to sort the words out.
The humidity is making it difficult to breathe. Making it difficult to
think.

The Orb hovers ten feet away.

“Is someone coming to rescue me?”

“Yes. Extract they will. Stay for safety. Protocol.”

A thunderous boom erupts somewhere beyond the forest chamber. The entire
structure shudders. Dust drifts from the mushroom caps. Their glowing
spots blink out one by one, then return.

“What was that?”

The Orb ignores me.

I stand and walk toward the corridor. Another impact echoes through the
building. The walls tremble as I step through the glass doors into the
corridor. The doors shut behind me. I continue toward the forest.

Halfway there, I hear the glass doors open behind me. The Orb follows.

“Follow,” it commands.

“You’re following me,” I respond.

The sound comes again. Maybe the rescuers found me. Maybe they are
trying to get in.

When I step back into the forest, I stop for a moment and let my eyes
adjust to the violet gloom. It isn’t nearly as dark as it seemed before.
I can make out the path now, the trunks of the trees, even the tangled
shapes of branches farther ahead.

Good. At least I don’t need the flashlight.

I leave my phone in my pocket and keep moving. I follow the path to the
blue pool. By the time I reach the benches, nausea rolls through me. I
sit and try to breathe.

Another violent quake nearly throws me to the ground. This time the boom
seems to come from behind the fountain. I force myself up and step over
the first alien I found. From this side, a green light shines beneath
its chest. The glow comes from a wristband on the arm pinned underneath
its body.

Green.

Mine is red.

I continue along the path. Behind the fountain, a cave tunnel opens
through the wall. Purple apertures glow along its sides. Clusters of
glowing fungus attached to the narrow walls cast a cold blue light.

“Follow!” the Orb demands as it pursues me.

I ignore it.

The tunnel bends. At the end is a cavern lit by mushrooms. A green
teleporter shimmers against the far wall. My heart jumps.

“Oh, thank God.”

A symbol flashes above it. I do not recognize the shape, but the portal
is unmistakable. It moves with the same liquid shimmer as the one I
entered that morning.

I hurry forward and walk into it. My body strikes a hard surface. A
sharp electrical vibration passes through my skin and throws me
backward.

“What the hell?”

The portal continues to ripple. I press one palm against it. The surface
hums, almost responsive, but it does not open around me. I look down at
my red wristband.

“Come on. Please.”

The Orb speaks rapidly in its own language. It looks from my band to the
green symbol above the portal.

“I don’t understand you.”

Its face flickers.

“Follow.”

The portal is active. It simply will not take me home. I stare at it a
moment longer, then turn away.

Another impact explodes from somewhere beyond it. The floor bucks. I am
thrown onto my hands and knees. Whatever is causing the quakes is
somewhere beyond that portal.

I get up and follow the Orb back into the tunnel.

Voices reach me before I enter the forest chamber. I freeze. The main
doors are open.

I duck behind broad glowing leaves and move through the undergrowth
until I can see the entrance. Four humanoid figures stand in the smaller
chamber beyond. They are unnaturally tall and thin, with long necks
rising from narrow shoulders. Their pale skin is rough and patchy,
marked by dark scars and deep pitting.

Their faces are subtly asymmetrical. One eye sits lower than the other.
Their mouths are tight seams above almost absent chins. Light armor is
strapped close to their bodies. Belts, pouches, and unfamiliar devices
cover their chests and hips. Each carries a short, bulky launcher.

They are armed. Their weapons are already raised. Every part of me says
to remain hidden.

One speaks into a device near its mouth. The language is sharp and
layered, similar to the Orb’s but harsher. All four step into the
forest.

The doors close.

Then open again.

A fifth figure enters. This one appears female. She is the same species,
tall and spare, with a long neck and narrow, uneven features. Ridges
cross her pale skin. Her mouth is almost lipless. She wears less armor
and carries no visible launcher. Her movements are smoother than the
others, nearly graceful.

One of the armed beings speaks to her. She steps forward.

“Andrea,” she calls. “Please come out if you are in here. We just want
to help you.”

Her voice is sweet. Melodious. Completely human. Her mouth barely moves.
The words do not seem to come from her throat. They emerge from
somewhere beneath her armor, perfect and breathless.

A chill crawls down my back.

That voice does not belong with that face.

“Your teleporter merely malfunctioned,” she continues. “We are here to
take you home.”

The statement is too polished. Too complete.

The Orb rises directly above the plants concealing me. Its face flashes
green. It calls out in the other language. All four armed aliens turn at
once. Their goggles lock onto my hiding place. They raise their
launchers.

The Orb has shown them exactly where I am.

I crawl backward. Leaves brush my face. Vines slide across my shoulders.

“Andrea,” the female calls in a singsong voice. “Come out, come out,
wherever you are.”

My stomach tightens.

“These plants will eat you alive if you do not come forward. You are not
doing well in this heat, are you? The humidity is far too high for your
human body.”

The voice remains gentle.

“Step out, and we will take you somewhere air-conditioned. You will feel
much better.”

I move farther into the foliage.

“Is delirium beginning to set in?” she asks. “You have been here for
nearly an hour.”

Nearly an hour? It feels as though I have been here for days.

Something curls around my ankle. I look down. A vine has wrapped around
my foot. I try to kick free without making noise. Another lashes around
my other ankle. Both tighten.

The plants yank me backward.

I scream.

Branches rake across my arms and face as I am dragged through the
undergrowth. I grab at stems and leaves, but they break in my hands.
Bright white fluid pours from the wounds. It glows beneath the violet
light.

The smell reaches me.

Rot. Wet soil. Meat left in heat.

My stomach convulses.

Ahead, the base of one of the false pine trees opens into a dark cavity.
The vines extend from inside. The opening resembles a mouth only in the
way a sinkhole resembles a throat. It is not shaped for eating, yet
every part of me understands that this is what it does.

Something wet shifts inside.

I claw at the flagstones. My fingers catch one edge. I grip it with one
hand and fumble for my phone with the other. The vines pull harder. My
shoulders burn.

I yank out the phone and activate the flashlight. The beam strikes the
vines. They recoil. The coils around my ankles loosen. Tendrils twist
away from the light. I aim the beam into the cavity.

The opening contracts.

The forest around me shudders.

Not the tree alone.

The entire forest.

For a brief, impossible moment, every luminous marking in the chamber
closes at once.

“Yes,” I gasp. “Come on.”

The light weakens.

“No.”

For half a second, it brightens again, throwing the vines into sharp
relief. Then the screen turns dark. The vines tighten immediately. They
jerk me backward so hard that my fingers nearly tear loose from the
stone.

“No!”

I hammer the power button.

Nothing.

A pair of armored boots lands on the next flagstone. I look up. One of
the armed aliens stands over me. Up close, he is even taller, all long
limbs and knobby joints. Scarred skin shows between the straps of his
armor. Green light shines from his goggles.

For one terrible moment, he watches. The vines pull harder. My fingers
slip. The alien draws a blade from a sheath at his side. The metal is
black until it moves. Then symbols flare along its edge in colors my
eyes cannot hold.

He swings.

The vines part. Their severed ends writhe across the stones, pouring
white fluid. From inside the tree comes a sound too low to hear and too
deep not to feel.

Relief barely reaches me before a sharp pain strikes my right shoulder.
I gasp and release the stone. My hand finds something protruding from my
skin. I pull it free.

A tiny syringe dart lies in my palm.

“What…?”

My fingers stop obeying me. My hand falls. My face follows.

The woman’s voice reaches me through the dark.

“Oh, good. We got her.”

She says something in the alien language. Then she makes a soft,
crackling sound.

It might be laughter.

Or it might be the first honest sound she has made.

I woke up on a park bench in Yosemite.

For a while, I just sat there, trying to understand how I had gotten
there. My clothes were filthy, my arms were scratched, and there were
red marks around my ankles. My shoulder was still sore from the dart.

Unfortunately, I was also a thousand miles from home. I had no realistic
choice but to use the teleporter one more time. I don’t think I’ve ever
been more afraid to step through anything in my life, but this time it
worked. I came out two blocks from my house, exactly where I was
supposed to.

Afterward, I filed a report with TeleQwest and described what had
happened. They never gave me much of an explanation. There was also a
section asking how satisfied I was with my experience.

I gave them the lowest rating possible and left a single comment:

“I did not enjoy my trip through the teleporter.”

I never used one again.

Not once.
