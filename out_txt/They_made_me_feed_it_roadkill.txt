My name is James Morrow, but to the State of Florida I’m offender
779854. I made bad choices in my life, and those choices put me in an
orange vest on the shoulder of US-301 south of Starke, working off my
court-ordered community service hours. The job was disgusting but not
difficult. We collected the animals.

There were five of us on the crew, six counting Vasquez, the supervisor
who drove the truck and signed our hour sheets. The work was simple. You
walked the shoulder with a grabber pole and a box of contractor bags and
you collected what the road left. Deer, mostly. Possums, armadillos,
every couple of weeks a dog that still had its collar on. Reyes, who had
more hours left than the rest of us put together, taught me to breathe
through my mouth and check for tags first. You get used to the smell in
about three days. That part surprised me. The part I never got used to
came at the end of every shift.

The county incinerator sat a quarter mile off 301 behind a chain-link
fence, a green metal building with a smokestack and a steel chute on the
side. We backed the truck up to it and fed the bags in one at a time. I
must have stood at that chute a hundred times, and there are two things
about that building I noticed my first week and never stopped noticing.
The stack never smoked, no matter how many bags we sent down. And the
wall never got warm. You could lay your palm flat on the metal on a
ninety-degree afternoon and it was cool as a well pipe, and if you kept
it there long enough, you could feel something on the other side,
moving.

Vasquez logged every bag before it went down the chute. Weight,
contents, mile marker where we found it. I took that for normal
government paperwork until I actually watched him do it, because he did
not do it like paperwork. He did it like a man balancing a checkbook
that had scared him once before. The crew had its own weather too. Reyes
was the best of us. He had more hours left than the rest of the crew
combined and he still shared his thermos and covered for me the day my
bag count came up wrong. Hutto was the other direction, a wiry guy out
of Palatka who wanted everybody to know exactly how many hours he had
left, like the number was a door he could already see through.

The chute had a steel lid you lifted with one hand while you swung the
bag in with the other. The lid was heavy and the hinge was stiff, and it
came down behind each bag on its own weight with a clang you could feel
in your back teeth. That was the whole job, most days. Bag, log, lift,
swing. The only part of the routine I ever wondered about was the apron.
Most mornings there would be small things sitting on the concrete
outside the box. Dog collars. Ear tags. Once, three feet of chain, laid
out straight. Whatever was in there did not take everything. It put back
what it did not want. Vasquez would rake the apron, bag whatever he
raked up, and send it back down.

Three weeks in, we found the first one. Southbound shoulder, just past
the Lawtey speed zone. From the truck it looked like a raccoon. From ten
feet it looked like a raccoon and a possum that had been hit in the same
instant. From two feet it was one animal. Not tangled, not crushed
together. One body, one hide, gray fur running into brown with no seam
anywhere, a spine that started as one animal’s and finished as the
other’s, mid-stride, like they had been poured into the same mold while
both of them were still moving. Vasquez did not bag it. He photographed
it from four angles, sent the pictures somewhere from his phone, and
stood looking down the road toward the incinerator for a long time. We
worked until dark that day, and the next day too. He never said why, and
we never asked, and that Friday, reading his log sheet in the cab, was
the first time I ever heard him use the word short.

The real shortfall started with the heat. Late August laid down on that
stretch of 301 like a dog that would not move, and the animals stopped
moving with it. You would think dead animals have nothing to do with
weather. They do. Everything out there does. We walked miles of shoulder
that week and came back with six bags, and I watched Vasquez read his
own log sheet three times like the numbers might improve on the third
pass. Then he took us out again after lunch, which he had never done, to
stretches of county road I did not know we covered.

The second fused one turned up that Thursday. A deer and a dog this
time. One body, one hide, and the dog’s collar still on what was left of
its neck, tags and all. Vasquez did not take pictures of this one. He
read the tags, wrote something in the log, and had us bag it double. It
barely went down the chute. I remember the lid clanging shut behind it
and being glad of the clang.

The same week, the box went quiet. I had a habit by then of putting my
palm on the wall while we unloaded, the way you would check a pulse.
Cool as ever. And nothing on the other side. Nothing moving at all. It
should have been a relief. It was not.

Friday, Vasquez radioed somebody, listened for a while, and told us we
were going back out after dark. We had never run after dark. Nobody
argued. You did not argue with how Vasquez was carrying himself that
week, the same way you would not argue with a man carrying something
heavy down a staircase.

We fed the box the last load close to eleven. Hutto had been counting
all day, out loud, the way he did. Fifty-eight hours left. Then
fifty-six. I was at the truck hauling bags with Reyes, my back to the
chute, when one hung up partway down. You could hear it, a soft catch in
the throat of the thing where there should have been a slide and a
clang. Hutto said a word his mother would not have liked, and lifted the
lid, and leaned in after it. I know that is what he did because it is
what any of us would have done. When I turned around, the lid was
standing open on its own, and a lid that heavy does not stand open. No
clang. No Hutto. The shoulder was empty all the way to the fence line,
and inside the box, the sounds had started again.

We looked for him for an hour. I will not pretend we looked well.
Vasquez ran his flashlight down both ditches and called Hutto’s name up
and down the road, and it took me until the drive home to understand
what had bothered me about that. He never once pointed the light at the
box. He searched everywhere he knew Hutto wasn’t. Saturday morning there
was a form on the clipboard with Hutto’s name on it and one word I had
to read twice. Absconded. Walked off. Ran. Fifty-six hours from the end
of his number, and he ran. That is what the paper said, and paper is
what the state believes.

Four mornings after the form went on the clipboard, I saw the apron from
the truck while we were backing up, and I remember thinking the rejects
looked bigger than usual. Vasquez got out first. He stood over the spot
for a while with the rake in his hand, not raking. Then he told
everybody to stay in the truck, and then he changed his mind and told me
to bring a bag.

Hutto’s boots were standing on the concrete. Side by side, square to the
building, like he had stepped out of them at the front door of a house
he respected. The laces were tucked down inside. I did not understand
that the feet were still in them until I was close. There was no blood
on the concrete. Not a drop. Whatever got done had been done somewhere
else, and what came back had been set down carefully, the way you set
something on a neighbor’s porch when you are returning it.

The routine was the routine. Apron rejects went back down. Vasquez held
the bag open and I put the boots in one at a time, and the weight of
them is a thing my wrists still know, and we fed the bag into the chute,
and the lid clanged, and neither of us said one word the whole time.

That week there were no night runs and no afternoon doubles. The routes
went back to normal. Vasquez’s log sheet stopped scaring him. It lasted
six days. I did the math on the seventh, in the shower, where I could
pretend the water was the reason my face was wet, and the math came out
like this: a man is worth six days. Hutto would have hated that number.
It was smaller than his.

The next shortfall came in October, and by then I knew the signs the way
you know weather. Counts down. Doubles after lunch. Vasquez quiet. The
box quiet, which was worse. When he told us we were running after dark,
I did not argue. I would like to say I spent the drive thinking about
Hutto. The truth is uglier than that. I thought about the schedule. It
had gotten into me by then. I wanted the box fed.

We were on the last bags when the floodlights died. The generator log
says the outage lasted ninety-two seconds. Something took my arm in the
dark. Above the wrist, all the way around, no fingers anywhere in the
grip. Warm. That is the part I have never told anybody, how warm it was,
like asphalt after the sun goes down, like the road holding its heat. It
pulled, and my heels cut two furrows toward the chute, and I did not
scream, and I am not saying that like a brave man. There was no time to
find my voice. Somewhere to my left, Reyes made a sound I am not going
to try to spell.

Then the lights came back. I was standing four feet from where I had
been standing, and my arm was my own again. The chute lid was standing
straight open, the way it had stood open once before. Reyes was gone. A
drag mark ran from his boot prints to the chute, wet, dark grease and
deer hair. On the lip of the chute his vest sat folded in quarters, the
way they hand your things back when you get out of county.

The grease stripe on my arm did not wash off. It wore off, over about a
week, like a tan going. Under it a bruise came up, one clean band, no
finger marks in it anywhere, and when my probation officer asked, I said
a cargo strap caught me, and she wrote that down. Paper is what the
state believes.

They filed Reyes absconded too. The Monday after, Vasquez sat on the
tailgate while we loaded and, without looking at me, said the only thing
he ever said about any of it. You were never in danger, he told me. It
doesn’t take short-timers. Then he signed my hour sheet like we had been
talking about the weather.

I finished my hours the first week of November. The release paperwork
has a box in the bottom corner I was not supposed to notice, marked
RE-REFERRAL, with a Y typed in it. Nobody would tell me what that meant.
I have a guess.

It is Sunday tonight. There is a shovel in my trunk and a box of
contractor bags, the same brand the county buys, and after dark I will
drive 301 south of Starke with my flashers on, running a route nobody
assigned me, picking up what the road leaves. My wrist aches when I get
near the fence. It always does now. I think it knows my weight. I do it
for Reyes, and I do it on Hutto’s math, because I know exactly what
balances the ledger on a slow week, and somewhere in Bradford County
tonight there is a man standing in an intake line, signing his name next
to a number, and he is going to need every bag I can find.
