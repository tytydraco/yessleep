Part I: Mason (The Guide)
The secret to a five-star rating isn’t historical accuracy. It’s
commitment.
The corporate script provided by Haunted Crescent Charters is garbage.
Page after page of dry dates: “Built in 1888,” “A fire claimed seven
lives in 1912.” Boring. Tourists don’t pay forty-five dollars on a
Friday night for a seventh-grade history lecture. They pay for the
illusion of danger. They pay for adrenaline.
That’s why I modified the company van.
It took me three weekends in my garage. I ripped out the middle six rows
of vinyl bench seating and installed six vintage, velvet-lined oak
display caskets bolted directly to the steel floorboards.
Tonight’s group was perfect: four rowdy bachelor-party frat guys, a
middle-aged woman taking notes on an iPad, and a quiet guy in a faded
army jacket sitting near the back. Six total.
“Ladies and gentlemen,” I purred into the retro chrome microphone,
flicking the overhead ambient LEDs to a deep crimson. “Most tours talk
about the 1898 Yellow Fever epidemic. But here at Blackwood Charters, we
believe in sensory immersion.”
I pulled the van over along the pitch-black shoulder of River Road, kill
switch engaged, engine idling low.
“To truly understand the victims buried alive in the mass trenches,” I
announced, turning around with a wide, theatrical grin, “you must occupy
their space. Inside your designated box, you will find noise-cancelling
headphones playing authentic binaural recordings of the 1898 quarantine
wards. Five minutes of absolute darkness. If you tap out early, you
forfeit your commemorative flask.”
The frat guys cheered, already climbing into the velvet interiors. The
iPad lady hesitated, chuckled nervously, and stepped in. The guy in the
army jacket just shrugged and laid back.
One by one, I swung the heavy oak lids shut.
Click. Thud. Click.
The brass hasps engaged. I slid the padlock through each latch, twisting
the dials until the tumblers locked.
Total sensory deprivation. A masterstroke. I sat back down in the
driver’s seat, pulled out my clipboard, and hit the stopwatch on my
phone.
Five minutes on the clock.
Part II: Julian (The Passenger)
The lid closed, and the world vanished.
The padded velvet inside smelled of mothballs, lavender, and damp wood.
The tiny red LED above my nose flickered and died, leaving me in total,
suffocating blackness.
Through the headphones Mason handed out, a low, scratchy audio track
crackled to life. It sounded like an old phonograph playing white
noise—hissing static, distant church bells, and the faint, rhythmic
sound of wooden spades digging into wet earth.
I chuckled to myself. Cheesy, but effective.
Then the audio stopped.
Not a fade-out. A sharp pop, like a wire being severed.
In the sudden silence, the true geometry of the box closed in around me.
My nose was four inches from the polished lid. My elbows brushed the
satin sides.
I reached up and pressed my palms against the wood above me. It didn’t
budge.
“Alright, good gag,” I muttered, knocking my knuckles against the top.
“Hey, Mason! Audio cut out!”
Nothing.
I pressed harder. The wood was solid oak, heavy and cold. I reached to
the sides, feeling for a release latch or emergency handle.
Nothing. Smooth wood and brass upholstery tacks.
That was when the vehicle shifted.
It wasn’t the smooth acceleration of a van pulling back onto the
asphalt. It was a violent, downward tilt, followed by the dull,
sickening scrape of metal against stone.
Through the thin wooden floor of the casket, I didn’t feel tires rolling
over pavement. I felt the slow, steady vibration of something heavy
sliding downward at a steep, forty-five-degree angle.
Then came the sound.
Thud.
Something heavy hit the lid of my casket.
Thud. Shovel. Scratch.
A shower of loose, coarse grit rained down on the exterior wood.
I began to hammer my fists against the lid in earnest. “Mason! Open the
damn box! Mason!”
From the casket to my left, I heard muffled, frantic screaming. The
sound of boots kicking violently against wood.
He didn’t just lock us in.
Part III: Mason (The Guide)
03:14 on the stopwatch.
I adjusted the rearview mirror to admire my handiwork. Six closed
caskets under the crimson glow of the LED strips.
Except… something was wrong with the dashboard.
The digital speedometer was fluctuating erratically: 0 mph… 45 mph… 80
mph… 0 mph.
The headlights outside weren’t illuminating the trees along River Road
anymore. The beam hit a solid, glistening wall of black shale and packed
mud less than three feet from the front bumper.
“What the hell?” I muttered, leaning forward over the steering wheel.
The van wasn’t parked on the shoulder. We were underground.
The tires were sunken eight inches into wet, gray clay that oozed up
around the rims. The windows were entirely caked in black, crawling
earth, blocking out the night sky.
Bang! Bang! Bang!
A frantic hammering erupted from the row of caskets behind me.
“Mason! Let us out! Goddammit, Mason!”
“Hang on!” I yelled, adrenaline spiking hot in my throat. I unbuckled
and scrambled into the back, grabbing the master key from my pocket.
“The parking brake must have slipped! We’re in a ditch, I’m getting you
out!”
I jammed the brass key into the padlock of Casket #1.
The key turned. The padlock popped open.
I grabbed the brass handle and hauled the heavy lid upward.
It swung wide.
The interior was empty.
No passenger. No headphones. Just a mound of damp, black earth filling
the silk cushions, crawling with hundreds of pale, translucent
centipedes.
“What… what did you do?” I whispered, stumbling backward.
Bang! Bang! Bang!
The lid of Casket #6—Julian’s casket—was bowing outward, the wood
groaning under the pressure of someone kicking from inside.
I lunged for his padlock, hands shaking so badly I dropped the key onto
the floorboards.
I dropped to my knees, clawing through the mud on the floor to find it.
Above me, the radio on the dashboard flicked on by itself. The volume
knob slowly rotated clockwise to maximum.
A raspy, theatrical voice—my own voice, recorded on the van’s
promotional tape—boomed through the speakers:

“And for our final stop of the evening… the burial is permanent.”

The driver’s side door clicked shut. The locks engaged.
And from the ceiling vents, black dirt began to pour.
