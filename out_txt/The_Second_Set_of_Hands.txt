My mother in law died of pancreatic cancer six weeks before my due date.
Mark and I had already converted her old bedroom into the nursery. We
thought that would be the end of it. We were wrong.

The first night after the funeral I woke up to the feeling of someone
adjusting the blanket over my stomach. The room was empty. The baby
kicked hard enough to bruise. I told myself it was grief and hormones.

The next night it happened again, only this time the hands stayed. They
pressed gently along the sides of my belly, measuring, counting, the way
a midwife would. When I turned on the light the pressure vanished, but
four perfect fingerprints remained on my skin, slightly cooler than the
rest of me.

Mark said I was imagining things. He said the stress of losing his
mother was making me paranoid. I stopped arguing. I started recording.

On the third night the camera caught nothing visible, but the audio
picked up a soft, familiar voice humming the same off key lullaby Ruth
used to sing when she thought no one was listening. The voice was coming
from inside the room. From the direction of my stomach.

At thirty seven weeks the Braxton Hicks contractions started coming in
waves that felt directed. Each one would begin under my ribs and travel
downward in a deliberate pattern, as if something was practicing how to
push from the inside.

Between contractions I would feel those same cool hands smoothing over
the skin, soothing, encouraging. Once I distinctly felt a second pair of
smaller hands, tiny, incomplete, pressing back from within, answering.

I went to the hospital early. The monitors showed a healthy heartbeat.
One heartbeat. The ultrasound tech kept frowning at the screen and
adjusting the wand. “Everything looks fine,” she said, but she wouldn’t
meet my eyes.

On the printout there was a faint second shape curled behind the first,
almost like a shadow. The tech deleted it before I could ask.

Labor began at 2:14 a.m. on a Thursday. Mark was there. The midwife was
there. And someone else was there too. I could feel her.

Every time a contraction peaked, an extra set of hands would brace
against my lower back from the outside, even though no one was touching
me. The midwife kept asking who kept adjusting the pillows. No one
answered.

When the baby began crowning, I felt a second pressure, smaller, wrong,
trying to follow. The midwife’s eyes went wide. She said, “There’s
something else.” Then she went very quiet.

They delivered my daughter first. She was perfect, pink, screaming. Then
they delivered what came after. It was not a twin. It was a pair of
hands.

Fully formed, adult sized, the exact shape of Ruth’s hands, down to the
arthritis knobs on the knuckles and the thin white scar across the left
palm from a kitchen accident in 1994. They were still warm. They were
still moving.

They flexed once, as if testing the air, then went still. The doctors
called it a medical anomaly. Fetal tissue. Rare but documented. They
took the hands away in a metal tray. I never saw them again.

But that night in the recovery room I woke up to the weight of those
same hands resting on my chest, right above my heart. They were
invisible. They were cold.

And they were counting my heartbeat the way Ruth used to count the
seconds between contractions when she was still alive and certain she
would be the one to deliver this baby.

My daughter is four months old. She does not like to be held by anyone
except me, and even then only if I keep my hands exactly where the
invisible ones prefer.

Sometimes in the middle of the night I feel those second hands slide
under mine, correcting my grip, teaching me the proper way to support a
head that is no longer entirely mine to protect.

Once I tried to pull away. The hands tightened until my wrists bruised
in the perfect shape of Ruth’s fingers. Mark still says it’s stress. He
says the dead do not raise the living.

He has never felt the hands. He has never woken to the soft, off key
humming that only stops when the baby is perfectly still and listening.

Last week the baby reached up during a feeding and wrapped her tiny
fingers around something I could not see, just above my shoulder. She
smiled the exact smile Ruth used to give when a plan had gone exactly as
she intended.

Then she looked at me and, clear as day, said the first word she has
ever spoken. “Grandma.” The hands on my wrists squeezed once, approving,
and did not let go.
