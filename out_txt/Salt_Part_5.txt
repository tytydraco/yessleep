Parts 1, 2, 3, 4

The knocking stopped a little after ten.

I’d been staring at the kitchen microwave clock so long the green
digital numbers were burned into my retinas. Three hours of waiting for
the wood to splinter, and then—nothing.

I didn’t sleep. I sat at the table in the dark until the sky turned
blue. When I finally stood up and unlocked the door, I was tired of
waiting.

The porch was empty.

Except for a neat line of table salt poured across the outside of my
door.

I stepped over it, got in my car, and drove to the county jail, needing
to see Harkness. He was the last person to see Reynolds alive, and the
only person left in this town who hadn’t tried to feed me a lie.

They gave me ten minutes. The public defender’s office moves quickly
when it thinks a reporter might embarrass the sheriff.

Harkness looked small and beaten behind the scratched plexiglass. He’d
shrunk since they locked him up.

“So you’re the reporter,” he said.

“And also your neighbor,” I said. “Or, I guess, I was.”

He let out a dry, hacking cough. “You wrote about me.”

“I wrote what I could prove.”

He leaned in until his forehead nearly touched the glass, leaving a
smudge of breath on the plastic. “That’s it, though, isn’t it? Nobody
can prove a fucking thing. That’s how they built this place. Reynolds
spent twenty-four years running their legal department. She didn’t leave
trails, and she made damn sure the paper vanished.” He glanced at the
deputy by the door, then dropped his voice. “Did you look at the school
registries?”

“Tell me about the kids,” I said.

His eyes narrowed. “They don’t take them out of cruelty. Kids are
malleable. You give a seven-year-old a green uniform, a scholarship, a
nice lady who remembers their birthday—they’ll start to love the thing
that’s carved them out. By the time they’re old enough to notice, they
don’t want to look for very long.”

“Is that what happened to you?”

He looked down at his cuffed wrists and didn’t answer.

“Ask Reynolds,” he said.

“Reynolds is dead, Harkness. They found her car. I saw the stretcher.”

“No,” he said, staring right at me. “She isn’t.”

The deputy tapped his keys against the doorframe. Time was up.

I went straight to the library.

The older woman who usually ran the reference desk wasn’t there. A kid
named Trevor was. He was about nineteen, wearing a faded polo and
staring at his phone, waiting for his shift to end.

“I need the Gazette archives again,” I said. “The microfilm from last
time I was here.”

He tapped his keyboard, squinted at the monitor, and tapped again. “I
don’t see any checkout logs for those.”

“I sat right there in the corner,” I said, pointing toward the dusty
reader. “It was like four hours. The woman with the reading glasses
pulled them for me.”

Trevor looked up, mildly annoyed. “There is no other librarian. It’s
just my dad and me. It’s been that way for years.”

“A short-haired woman,” I said. I felt like I was going crazy. “She
helped me find the ’97 reels.”

“Look, I’m not calling you a liar. But my dad has held the county
contract here since 2009. It’s a two-person gig. He does mornings, I do
afternoons. There isn’t anyone else on the payroll. I do the schedules.”

He turned the monitor and showed me a basic spreadsheet: two names,
alternating back and forth for years with no mention of a third name.

“Have you ever heard of a family named Colby?” I asked. The name had
been stuck in my head since I saw it on a plaque at the community
center.

Trevor blinked. “Yeah. A Colby went to my high school. Played safety on
the JV team.”

“Did he have a sister?”

He rubbed the back of his neck, staring at the desk. “I don’t think so.
Pretty sure he was an only child. Why?”

“Just checking on something,” I said.

I went out to my car and sat there. My hands were shaking against the
steering wheel.

As a reporter, you learn to look for the inconsistencies and the paper
trails. But there was no trail here. That was the fucked up part of it.

Emily called.

“You sound sick,” she said. “Are you eating?”

“I’m fine,” I lied.

She started complaining about her office copier, and I just closed my
eyes and listened. For a few minutes, I got to have a normal sister, not
a list of who might disappear next.

I wanted to ask her. I wanted to ask if we’d always been just the two of
us, or if there had been a third bedroom in our childhood home. Someone
maybe we knew and just disappeared.

On the way home, I drove past the old Laurel place. It was still wrapped
in yellow police tape from whatever the sheriff covered up in February.

A man was standing near the ditch. He was in civilian clothes—just jeans
and a faded flannel jacket. He was holding a ballpoint pen, frantically
writing on his own wrist.

I rolled down the window. “Hey, you okay?”

He looked up, his shoulders dropping.

“Depends,” he said.

“Amelia,” I said. “I live two streets over. I’ve been asking questions
about the murders. You a cop?”

He studied me, deciding whether or not I was acceptable to talk to.
“Bryan Keller,” he said after some time, looking back down at his wrist.
“I used to work for the county. They put me on administrative leave for
stress.”

“Was it stress?”

“No,” he said, walking to the car. “My partner, Ramirez. We rode
together for six years. When I went to his house after the incident, his
wife didn’t know who I was talking about. Our captain told me we hadn’t
run two-man patrols in years. But I still have his locker key in my
pocket.” He showed me his wrist. He’d written RAMIREZ. J. LOCKER 14 in
smudged blue ink. “People are disappearing around here.”

“How much of this do you understand?”

“Hardly any,” he said. “Just that it’s real. And it has something to do
with whatever is under this town.”

I started to tell him about Trevor and the library, but Bryan wasn’t
listening. He was staring past my shoulder, out at the abandoned Harvest
Drive field.

The sun was almost gone. The sky had turned a soft orange.

“Hey,” he said, his voice dropping to a whisper.

“What?”

“Don’t turn your head fast. Just look in my side-view mirror and tell me
if you see that woman.”

I looked, and sixty yards out, at the edge of the tall grass, was a
woman in a dark blazer and high heels.

It looked like Reynolds from the distance. But her head was tilted too
far to one side. Then she dropped out of sight.

I felt my pulse drumming in my ears.

Four seconds later, she rose back out of the grass—thirty yards to the
left, and closer. The dark blazer looked wrong. It had no seams, like it
had been molded over her frame rather than sewn. Nobody could have
crossed that waist-high brush in four seconds without making a sound.

“Bryan,” I whispered.

She dropped again. When she came back up, she was closer still. The
movement was jagged, jumping from spot to spot.

Then, she made a sound.

A shriek or a growl would have made sense, but this didn’t. It was a
baby crying.

It was perfectly human. But it went on too long without a pause for
breath. The noise began to stretch, the pitch sliding down into a long
scream.

Then she dropped into the weeds.

And she ran.

The grass parted as she ran at high speed, ripping through the field at
a pace no animal could match. She reached the tree line, and the weeds
went completely still.

Neither of us spoke.

“Okay,” Bryan said, his voice shaking. “Okay.”

“That clearly wasn’t Reynolds,” I said. I needed to hear the words in
the cold air.

“No,” Bryan said, staring at the woods. “But now we know what they’re
doing to the bodies.”
