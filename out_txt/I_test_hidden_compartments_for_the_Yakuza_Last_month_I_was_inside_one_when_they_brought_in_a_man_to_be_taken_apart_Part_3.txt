Battery’s at 31%. I’ve turned the brightness all the way down and I’m
typing with my right thumb. My left hand is under my thigh, where it’s
been since the last post, and I’ve decided it’s going to stay there.
Moving it means moving my elbow, and my elbow is about a finger’s width
from a layer of Mr. Arai.

A lot of you asked why I don’t just push the panel and get out. I’ll
answer that once, properly, and then I’d like us never to discuss it
again.

The front panel opens from the outside. It always has, because that’s
how a box works. The back wall, the one they slid him in through, has no
handle on my side. Furuya doesn’t put handles on the inside of anything.
Even if he did, I’d have to reach through the arrangement to get to it.

So no.

He talks when I’m not looking at him. I worked that out at about the
hour mark. When I look at his eyes, he waits. When I look at my phone,
he talks. I think he’s being polite. I think he knows what it’s like to
be stared at while you’re working.

Now that I’m close enough to really hear him, his voice comes out wrong.
It doesn’t come from one place. It passes through the layers one after
another, each slice a hair later than the one before, so every word
arrives with a little echo behind it. It sounds like someone repeating
you from the far end of a long hallway.

“How long,” he said, “have you had her?”

I didn’t pretend I didn’t know who he meant. I said eleven years. Then I
felt stupid, two of us in a box making conversation like strangers on a
ferry.

“Eleven.” The echo ran down the hallway. “I had mine for twenty-six.”

Had.

I asked him where she was now.

He didn’t answer for a long time. Then the layer nearest my right cheek
got colder, the way a fridge does when you open it, and I understood I’d
asked a stupid question. She’s in here. She’s in the gaps. That’s what
the cold is. That’s what the slices are hanging on, and why they don’t
touch. Something is standing between every layer of him like a hand
between the pages of a book.

“I went to a clinic in Umeda,” he said. “To have her taken off. A laser.
Four sessions. After the third I stopped sleeping. After the fourth she
came off.” The echo took a long time to finish. “All at once. From the
inside. Very neatly. The way you’d fillet something you wanted to keep
fresh.”

I asked him if it hurt.

“It doesn’t hurt,” he said. “That’s what I want you to understand. It
doesn’t hurt at all. It’s only cold.”

24%.

There are people in the room above me.

I heard the bell again, one note, and then the sliding door, and then
knees on tatami. Several people, kneeling slow and careful, the way you
kneel in a room where you want to be seen to be careful. Somebody poured
something. Nobody talked for a long time.

Then a man’s voice, very close, right over the alcove: utsukushii.

Beautiful.

I’d like to believe he meant the flowers. The seam I spent two hours
looking up through is right under the vase. When I tilt my head I can
see the light through it. A thin gold line. A minute ago something
passed across that line and the light went out and came back, the way it
does when someone leans over a keyhole.

Mr. Sone’s voice answered him, warm and modest, the way you talk about
your own garden. I couldn’t catch all of it. I caught forty-one years,
which I think was about the scroll. And I caught very still, which I
don’t think was.

I think I know what Furuya builds now. I don’t think it’s compartments.
I think it’s display cases.

19%.

Arai asked me if I’d ever looked closely at her nails.

I said of course I had. I’ve had her eleven years. I’ve looked at her in
every hotel bathroom mirror from Macau to Sapporo.

“Closely,” he said.

So I did something I should not have done. I took my right hand off the
phone, very slowly, and pulled up the left side of my shirt, and put the
phone light against my ribs.

I need you to understand that I have looked at this tattoo thousands of
times. The nails are hatching. Fine parallel lines, that old-school
shading the woman in Busan did by hand in a shop under the fish market,
the shop that smelled of ice and squid ink. That’s what I always thought
they were. Shading.

They’re not lines. They’re writing.

Tiny. Smaller than rice. The same three characters over and over,
curling down every nail, hundreds of times, thousands. My name. The one
I was born with. The one on the passport I haven’t used since Macau. She
wrote it into the nails and never told me, or she told me and I didn’t
understand, because I didn’t speak Korean then and I was nineteen and
I’d just paid cash.

The linework on the nails. Lovely. That’s what Sone said. He wasn’t
complimenting her technique. He was reading.

And Arai’s hag, the one standing in the gaps between him, had read it
too. Eight inches away. Through my shirt.

That’s how he knew.

The nails are longer.

I’m not imagining this, and I’m not speaking loosely like I did about
Sone’s. I’ve had eleven years with this tattoo. The lowest nail always
ended just above my hip bone, at a mole I’ve had since I was a kid. It’s
past the mole now. It’s curling round toward my back. While I was
holding the light on it I watched the last character of my name write
itself onto the end of the nail, stroke by stroke, the way frost forms
on a window.

And the end of that nail isn’t flat any more.

It’s lifting. Like the corner of a sticker. A paper-thin edge of ink,
coming up off the skin, the tip pointing at the cold layer beside my
hip.

Arai saw it. His eyes went down to my side and then back up to my face,
and for the first time since they slid him in, the eyes looked afraid.

“Don’t let them see each other,” he said. “They don’t share.”

I put my shirt down. I don’t know if a shirt counts.

Two, Sone said. We simply need to know that the seam will hold with two.
I’d done the maths on two people. One box, measured to me, 41 by 58 by
112, and a man in slices folded in around me. I did the maths and it
didn’t work, and then it worked, and I was proud of it for about a
second.

He wasn’t counting people. He was counting them the way they count.

There’s one in the gaps of Mr. Arai, and there’s one on my ribs, and
Furuya builds the only seam in Kansai with nothing to get through.
That’s the test. Not whether I fit. Whether they can get out.

11%.

It’s started.

I felt it about ten minutes ago, low down on the left, below the last
rib, where the nail turned toward my back. It doesn’t hurt. He was right
about that, and I hate that he was right. It feels like someone drawing
a line on you with an ice cube. Then the line stays cold after the ice
is gone.

I held the phone up and looked. There’s a seam. A hair-thin dark line
through the skin, perfectly straight, a few centimetres long. When I
breathe in it stays shut. When I breathe out it opens, just slightly,
and the phone light goes into it a little way and doesn’t come back out.

No blood. Furuya would be proud. Nothing is touching anything.

I understand now why Sone thanked me for being still in the furnace
room. He wasn’t being polite. He was taking notes. Stillness is what
keeps the layers lined up. Arai’s are hanging perfect in the dark
because he stopped moving years ago. If you hold still they go in clean,
and if you hold still they stay where they’re put, and if you hold still
you can be carried through a house and down a step and nobody has to
clean anything up.

I have spent eleven years and four countries teaching myself to hold
still for a living.

I was never the tester.

7%.

There’s a second line now, parallel to the first, a finger’s width
higher. Then a third. I’m counting the gaps because counting is what I
do. My hag is very tidy. She’s doing the left side first.

Arai has stopped looking at me. His eyes have turned the other way,
toward the back wall, and I think he’s doing it for my sake, the way you
look away from someone crying on a train.

Above me the guests are leaving. Knees coming up off the tatami, one at
a time. The sliding door. Somebody laughed quietly, a pleased little
laugh, the kind you give after a good meal.

Then two taps on the front panel. Right beside my ear.

“Hako-san.” Mr. Sone, very soft. “Is it holding?”

I opened my mouth to tell him the truth, and what came out was “Thank
you.”

Polite. A little tired.

And I heard it arrive a hair after I said it, and then again a hair
after that, smaller and further off, all the way down a long hallway.

4%.

Not much light left now. It doesn’t matter. I can feel where I am
without it. The cold is coming off me in slices.

I want to tell you it’s awful. It isn’t. It’s the stillest I’ve ever
been. Nobody is touching me. Nothing is touching anything.
