All towns have local legends, and mine is no different. In fact, mine
has quite a few of them. Many of them can’t be verified to any credible
degree, and so they pass into the annals of folklore like most stories
of their type do. There is one local legend, though, that I can
verify—because I was unfortunate enough to have lived it.

And that is the legend of the witches’ maize.

There is a generations-old farm in my town that can trace its roots back
to colonial times. It has been jointly run and owned by the same handful
of families for all of those many, many years, but that doesn’t mean
they were the only ones to ever use the space. Legend has it that the
farm’s cornfield used to host yearly witches’ sabbaths on what
eventually became Halloween night. This repeated, ritualistic
desecration wound up cursing not only the land, but also the crop that
grows there. Any corn cultivated in this field is referred to as
witches’ maize, and is said to bestow bad luck onto anybody who eats it.

That farm now hosts a yearly attraction during the Halloween season,
during which time that same field is turned into a corn maze.
Horror-loving locals are drawn to this maze every year in hopes of
experiencing something paranormal, and while a few overly imaginative
individuals like to report strange happenings in the maze, none of them
have actually experienced anything substantial.

Because none of them have ever gone during the witching hour.

The farm typically closes by 9:30, even on Halloween, which means that
nobody ever comes close to entering the maze during the most crucial
time of night: the hour between midnight and 1 a.m., which is when the
witches’ sabbaths were said to have taken place. When we were a good bit
younger, my friend and I came up with the bright idea to sneak onto the
farm a few minutes before midnight on October 31st so that we could
enter the labyrinth of corn and see just what the witches’ maize had in
store for us. In our minds, if we could navigate the entire thing
without encountering anything abnormal, then we could officially put the
legend to rest.

We arrived at the farm deep into the night, well after the attraction
had gone dark, and long after the last trick-or-treaters had shuffled
off to bed. All that stood between us and our trespass was a chest-high
wooden fence that we were easily able to scale. Once we were on the
property, it was only a short walk across the farm to the start of the
corn maze.

“This place is a lot creepier at midnight, huh,” my friend said as we
stood before the looming labyrinth. She held her flashlight up to the
maze’s entrance, illuminating it in a bright circle that only served to
highlight the supreme darkness that lived just beyond the meager halo.
In her other hand she held a lit cigarette, which she brought to her
mouth one more time before tossing it to the dirt in front of her and
stomping it beneath her shoe.

“Yeah,” I agreed, my own flashlight pointed toward my feet. We had both
navigated many variations of this maze all throughout our lives,
including earlier that very Halloween season, but never had we stood
before it during the witching hour. It shouldn’t have felt any different
than entering the maze during the early evening hours, but it did. In
fact, I struggle to put into words just how different it was. It was as
if an invisible haze had spread over the entire farm, originating from
the center of that deep ocean of corn.

We took a few more moments to stare at the labyrinth before stepping
inside.

Our first few minutes spent walking along the dirt path of the maze were
largely uneventful. We were both very obviously trying to hide our fear
and discomfort at being there all by ourselves at such an hour, but
these nerves were to be expected. Even if we didn’t fully believe in the
legend of the witches’ maize, we were still creeped out by the idea of
what we were doing. A little bit of apprehension did nothing to confirm
the authenticity of the legend, and we fully expected to be laughing the
entire thing off during our drive home later that night.

We went on for a little while before reaching a small, circular
clearing, which branched off into three different routes ahead of us. I
frowned at the sight of this juncture, because I didn’t remember it from
our previous visit to the farm just a couple of weeks earlier.

“This is new,” my friend said, mirroring my own thoughts.

“Yeah,” I said. “I guess the owners must have cleared out part of the
maze in order to make room for this. Strange that they’d make the change
midway through the season, though.”

My friend shrugged, silently dismissing the slight oddity.

“So which way do we go?” I asked.

“I guess we can each pick a path and see where it goes,” she said.
“We’ll meet back here in a couple of minutes. If neither of ours turns
out to be the right way, then we’ll just go down the third one.”

I resisted the urge to frown for a second time. “You don’t think we
should go together?”

“That’ll take too long,” she said. “I don’t know about you, but I’m
already getting pretty tired. We should get this over with quickly so
we’re not falling asleep during our drive home.”

“Well, alright then,” I said. “But don’t go on without me if you realize
your path is the right one, okay?”

She nodded her agreement, and we each chose our path. She went right and
I went left, leaving the center for us to explore together should the
need have arisen. Within a few moments, she was out of sight, and I was
completely by myself.

The eeriness of that maze was only amplified by my solidarity. Walking
along that path alone, accompanied solely by the tall, impartial corn
stalks on either side of me, filled me with an intense dread that I
could not shake, and which only grew with each step deeper into the
labyrinth that I took. I was only able to walk on for about a minute
before I came to a dead end and I realized, with no small amount of
relief, that I needed to turn back.

I returned to the circular juncture hoping to find my friend there
waiting for me. She was not. Some of that dread, which had receded a
great deal as I entered familiar territory, suddenly returned to my
stomach, but I told myself that it was unwarranted. She would be back
any moment now, and we would continue on together.

A minute went by. Then another, and another. Then a few more. My friend
did not return. I called her name, but received no response, my words
quickly lost to the chilly autumn wind which somehow managed to
penetrate that thick fortress of corn.

I felt a creeping terror reach for my heart, but I managed to fight it
back using a sudden pang of anger. My friend had clearly continued on
without me. She had evidently gone down the correct path, and had
thought it would be funny to leave me to navigate the maze by myself.
She would be getting an earful from me as soon as I found my way out.

Using that growing anger to bolster my nerves, I followed after her down
the rightward path.

I called her name a few times as I continued through the corn, but I
quickly realized that such an effort would be fruitless. If she had
purposely left me alone, then there was no way she would answer my call.
Instead I chose to save my breath, and focused on keeping my anger
alive. As long as I was mad, I could keep my worsening fear at bay. I
dreaded the moment my terror grew so strong that it overwhelmed me, and
I hoped to be clear of the maze long before that happened.

The sound of rustling in the corn caused me to freeze in my tracks. At
first that strengthening fear began to shoot up into my chest, but I
managed to deflect it with a fresh flare of anger. Clearly my friend had
taken her little prank one step further, and was now stalking me through
the maze. I wouldn’t let her enjoy the satisfaction of seeing me afraid.

“Very funny,” I said, directing my words at the wall of corn in front of
me, ”but I’m on to your game. If you come out now, I promise I’ll
forgive you.”

No response. She obviously intended to keep this charade going.

“Alright,” I said, mustering as much annoyance into my voice as I could.
“Have it your way.”

I approached the wall of corn. My flashlight’s soft halo illuminated the
stalks as I searched for a breach in the barrier. After a few moments I
found my intended route, and I forced my way out of the maze and into
the maize.

The darkness which had seemed so overwhelming while in the labyrinth was
only amplified when I stepped into the thicket of corn. My flashlight
did little to cut through the shadows as I carefully navigated my way
through the dense collection of stalks. As I reached the far side of the
thicket, a new sensation struck my nose which caused me to once again
come to a halt: the unmistakable smell of smoke.

I frowned as I took in this unexpected scent. Had my friend lit another
cigarette? Surely she wouldn’t have been so dumb as to do that in such a
flammable place. But then what could have produced that unmistakable
odor of burning? Driven by a burgeoning sense of curiosity that, along
with my anger, helped me to ignore the voice in the back of my head
telling me to turn around, I continued my way through the corn.

The smell of smoke only grew worse as I continued my trudge through the
maize. Soon I noticed flickering shadows splashing against the stalks
that indicated the presence of fire. I could also hear voices ahead:
quiet at first, but growing from little more than whispers into full-on
cackles with each step that I took. Soon the heat of the unseen fire
struck me in oppressive waves, slicking my brow with sweat. 

I found a gap in the thicket that looked out into a large clearing in
the corn. Lowering the beam of my flashlight to the ground, I stuck my
head through the opening so that I could get a better look. There,
situated in the center of the clearing, was a large, roaring fire. Both
the blaze and the clearing were impossibly massive: there was no way
that either could have existed in this corn maze without my having
noticed them earlier. I thought little of this inconsistency, though,
because it was overshadowed by the other presence in the clearing that
commanded the majority of my attention: the presence of the dancing
women.

There were at least half a dozen of the shadow-drenched figures moving
and undulating in a circle around the mighty flame. I couldn’t make out
any distinct faces in the darkness, but I could tell they were all women
by the sound of their voices, which had grown so loud and wild with
excitement that they almost completely overwhelmed the sound of the
inferno that they were gathered around. The sight of them in the middle
of that clearing was enough to shatter the dying ball of anger within
me, replacing it entirely with the fear that could now spread through me
completely unchecked. I was so frozen with terror that I almost didn’t
even react as the figures began to float in the air.

My flashlight fell from my numb fingers and struck the earth at my feet.
The sound was enough to silence all of the chattering figures, whose
shadowy heads all turned to look in my direction. I don’t remember how
long I stared up at the floating strangers before I turned and ran. I
don’t remember much about what happened after that, to be honest. I
recall the vague sensation of corn stalks smacking against my sweaty
face as the shrill sound of cackling filled my ears, rattling my brain
in my skull, but everything else is largely a blur.

I also don’t remember if I ever made it back into the maze proper before
the corn was replaced by the visions that were suddenly forced into my
mind. I wasn’t in the cornfield anymore; I was on a snow-blanketed farm
in a small village surrounded by a dense forest of white and green. I
watched as weeks of suffering passed before my unblinking eyes; watched
as people perished to the cold and the hunger and the disease that
seemed to be so plentiful in this new, foreign land. I experienced the
collective regret that they all felt as more bodies piled up around
them, along with the desire to return to a home across the ocean that
none of them would ever see again.

I witnessed their fear turn to anger, and their anger turn to blame. I
watched as angry villagers dragged crying, wailing figures from their
homes and strung them up to makeshift gallows, where they would hang
limp and lifeless before their pleas for mercy could even grow cold on
their purple, putrid lips.

These deaths didn’t stop the villagers’ suffering, but having less
mouths to feed allowed them to survive until spring. They were more
prepared for the next winter, as well as for the one after, and the one
after that. Soon those dark, early months became a distant memory to
most of them, eventually to be completely forgotten as the generations
moved on.

But there were those who didn’t forget. There were those who could never
have forgotten the injustice thrust upon their loved ones. They
remembered. They remembered, and they festered.

Soon the village began to flourish. Crops grew in abundance; the
cornfield sprung up so vast and so tall that the villagers would never
need to want for food again. It became a symbol of the village’s
unwavering resilience.

But it also served as a reminder of their sins.

Months turned to years. 

The loved ones of those who had been unjustly killed began to meet in
the cornfield during the harvest season, where they would honor the
fallen with dark rituals and acts of blasphemy that would have seen them
sent to those very same gallows, had their activity been discovered. But
they were never found out, and so their vile acts continued.

I watched as these rituals continued on year after year, only growing
more sinister and more profane with each passing season. I watched as
waves of evil emanated from massive, rolling flames, whose influence
caused the surrounding lengths of corn to sway and even bow in a cruel
mockery of divine reverence. Years turned to decades, and decades to
centuries. I bore witness to every single moment of unquenchable,
unforgivable sin.

And I soon felt an overwhelming desire to participate.

My mind returned to my body as my foot stepped beyond the threshold of
the corn maze. I was so overwhelmed by the sensation of coming back to
myself that I almost toppled to the ground. My head spun for what felt
like several more centuries as I settled back into a reality that no
longer felt like my own.

“There you are,” my friend said. I turned to look at her as she
approached me. It wasn’t until she stood in front of me that I fully
understood where I was. Turning around, I saw the corn maze resting
behind me, its stalks watching me from where they stood silent and
judgmental.

I turned back to look at my friend. I felt a fresh anger bubbling up
within me that dissipated as soon as I saw the genuine concern plastered
across her face.

“Where were you?” she asked.

“What do you mean?” I said. “I was in the maze, looking for you!”

“I went back to where we had separated, but you never showed up,” she
said. “I figured you had gone on without me, but I made my way through
the rest of the maze, and I couldn’t find you anywhere.”

I frowned as I considered her words. After a few moments, I offered her
a weak smile. “Sorry,” I said. “I must have gotten turned around in
there.”

“I guess so,” she said, her arms crossed in front of her chest. “Didn’t
you hear me calling your name?”

I shook my head. Her face told me that she didn’t believe me, but she
ultimately decided to let it go.

“In any event,” she said, “it looks like we both made it through the
maze unscathed. I guess that means we can put the legend of the witches’
maize to rest, huh?”

“Yeah,” I said. “I uh… I guess we can.”

“So does that mean we can get out of here?”

I nodded. “Yeah,” I said again. “Let’s go.”

She returned my nod, and together we made our way back to her car. We
didn’t speak much as we drove back to my apartment, and we only
exchanged the briefest of goodbyes before she left. I know her lack of
energy came from her exhaustion at having been up so late. I allowed her
to think that mine came from the same place.

It’s been years since that night. My friend and I kept in touch for a
little while after that, but life eventually dragged us away from one
another. I moved away from our hometown, and I haven’t been back to that
farm since that night. The memory of what I experienced there lingered
with me for a while, but the longer I spent away, the more it seemed to
fade into an almost dreamlike place in my mind. If I had stayed away
forever, I just might have forgotten it completely.

But now I’m back. I’ve been here for a few days to attend the funeral of
a relative, and I decided to stay through Halloween weekend before
heading home. I haven’t stopped thinking about that corn maze since
getting here. I find myself returning to that night every chance that I
get. My mind goes back to all that I had witnessed, and the desire that
had felt so overpowering to me.

I’d originally planned on avoiding that place at all costs, but the
closer we’ve gotten to Halloween night, the more I’ve felt drawn to that
old field. Halloween is here now, and the witching hour isn’t all that
far away. As I sit here, writing this in the daylight, I feel that I can
still hold back my desire to go there tonight. I don’t know if I’ll be
able to say the same by the time night falls. I also don’t know who or
what I’ll find if I do choose to pay that corn maze a visit during the
witching hour tonight.

But something tells me that their feet won’t be touching the ground.
