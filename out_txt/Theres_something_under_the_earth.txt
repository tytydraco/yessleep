I remember the first earthquake. It was a magnitude 6.3, and it struck
New York City. Earthquakes in that region were rare enough that most
people dismissed it as an unfortunate coincidence. I had little interest
in earthquakes at the time, but it would soon become clear that this was
the most consequential event of my lifetime.

Then came the second earthquake. It narrowly missed New Jersey’s
southern shoreline. The first had killed just over five hundred people
and caused nearly three billion dollars in damage. The second claimed
only a handful of lives and caused roughly two hundred and fifty million
dollars in damage.

Following the second earthquake, the government began investigating the
incidents. At the time, I worked for an unacknowledged Special Access
Program funded through the government’s black budget.

Our primary objective was to investigate non-human intelligence and
activity beyond Earth. In other words, we investigated aliens. In truth,
the job was rather uneventful. Most days, we did very little beyond
reviewing reports of unidentified flying objects, which usually turned
out to be aircraft from other countries or experimental military
projects. Being summoned to Washington, D.C., for a meeting was highly
unusual.

I lived close enough to make the drive. The address led me to an
ordinary-looking building. Once inside, a woman approached me.

“Hello. We’ve been waiting for you. Follow me,” she said, gesturing down
the hallway.

The walls were bare, and the polished marble floors reflected the
overhead lights. As we walked, we passed a long row of tall mahogany
doors. Eventually, she opened one and ushered me inside. About fifteen
of my colleagues were gathered around a large oval table. A tall, older
man in a suit stood at the head.

“Well, I’m sure most of you have seen the news. All these… earthquakes
coincidentally happening near New York City.” He paused, taking a long
drag from his cigar. “At first, we thought a foreign enemy of ours might
be using some kind of seismic weapon. A stupid theory, I know.”

He fell silent for a moment, seemingly considering how to proceed.

“Well, our boys in the Navy and NOAA have been doing some digging around
the ocean floor. We’ve been receiving significant amounts of underwater
seismic activity roughly one hundred miles off the coast of New York,
concentrated around the Hudson Canyon. As they investigated with survey
ships and underwater drones, they discovered something breaking through
the seafloor.”

A tense silence settled over the room.

“Certainly a lot to take in, sir,” John, a coworker and friend of mine,
said. “Do we know what’s down there? Hell, shouldn’t we be more
concerned about tsunamis?”The old man gave him a flat look before
responding.

“As far as we know, there’s no tsunami risk. But, back to the more
important matter, we don’t know what’s under there. And we’d like your
help. Whatever it is, it’s extremely large. Large enough to cause
earthquakes simply by moving around. The visible cracks in the Earth’s
surface are about a mile wide. We’re still measuring their length.”

He looked around the table, waiting for someone to respond.

I glanced at my colleagues. Their faces were filled with dread and
confusion. I’m sure mine was no different. Sunlight filtered through the
blinds, illuminating the dust floating in the air. The man slowly
approached a projector and switched it on.

“Here’s some of the sparse footage we have from the underwater drones,”
he said.

Little could be seen beyond the deep, dark water. Someone stood and
closed the blinds. The room fell into darkness, and the drone’s light
gradually began illuminating the water ahead. A low, meaty hum
penetrated the water, causing the drone to shake.

As the camera bounced, its light finally reached something. Dirt and
sediment began falling away as something slowly emerged from the ocean
floor. The footage was far too grainy and low-resolution to make out its
shape or appearance. As the recording continued, what I can only
describe as a faint black vapor began escaping from the fissure.

The drone shook violently before the footage abruptly went dark. The
sounds of the ocean remained audible for a few seconds before fading
away. The man switched off the projector, and someone opened the blinds.

“That’s the clearest footage we have of this… thing. I’m an admiral in
the Navy, and we were hoping for some clarification on what we’re
looking at,” he said, pacing from one side of the room to the other.

“Maybe it’s a gas deposit?” someone asked. The admiral scoffed, stroking
his mustache.

“We’ve considered that. Our readings don’t indicate a significant gas
reservoir, and the pressure measurements don’t correspond with what we’d
expect from an underground release,” he replied. I decided to speak.

“Well… there’s a possibility it could be a massive biological organism,”
I suggested. The admiral initially seemed interested, but quickly
dismissed the idea.

“Possibly… but that’s not logical or realistic, now is it?” he asked. I
nodded and looked up at the ceiling.

“It might be something non-terrestrial for all we know, Admiral. We need
more information before we can reach a conclusion,” John said. The
admiral nodded and began walking toward the door.

“This footage isn’t going to be released to the public. And if any of
you decide to spill the beans about this, you’ll regret it,” he added
before closing the door behind him.

We were left in silence. The only sound was the soft buzzing of the air
conditioner.

“Well…” someone quietly said, but nobody responded. Everyone seemed
nervous, though I was still hesitant to jump to conclusions.

“I don’t know what it is, but I’m sure if we investigate a little more,
there’ll be a logical explanation,” I said, reassuring myself as much as
the others.

A few people nodded. The rest remained silent. I leaned back in my
chair, trying to gather my thoughts. A woman suddenly entered the room,
startling everyone.

“Hello, everyone. The admiral asked me to give you your orders. Most of
you will simply be going home. However, six of you will be taken to the
site to help us research and determine whether these occurrences are
extraterrestrial. If you don’t want to go, you’re free to leave,” she
said. Most people got up and left. I stayed. There were now five of us.

“Alright, one man short. But we can work with this.”

The room felt more oppressive now that most of the people I knew had
left. The woman, whose tone remained distant and dull, instructed us to
provide our information as she handed out a sheet of paper. I filled in
my name, gender, age, height, and weight.

Afterward, she gave us the coordinates of the site. We were told to be
there by the following morning and that we’d be working for roughly
seven hours.

I slept poorly that night. Even in my sleep, I felt a constant pressure,
an uncomfortable sensation that seemed to itch beneath my skin.

My phone alarm woke me the following morning. When I turned on the
television, a news headline reported another earthquake along the
Virginia coast.

I entered the directions into my phone and began driving. The trip took
about an hour. The roads grew increasingly desolate, and the darkness of
the early morning made the journey feel particularly foreboding.
Eventually, I turned onto a narrow forest road. My headlights were the
only source of illumination, cutting through the darkness and revealing
the path ahead. Near the end of the road, I spotted a flimsy-looking
guard post. I parked beside it.

“Your name?” a muffled voice asked as a man knocked on my window.

“Michael Ro—”

The man interrupted me before I could finish.

“I know who you are,” he said.

He returned to his post and pressed a few buttons. The gate opened, and
I drove forward. Beyond it was a beach and what appeared to be a naval
station. The familiar cars of my coworkers filled the small parking lot.
I got out and entered the building.

“Michael, come on! We’ve been waiting for you. We gotta get on a boat
soon,” John told me, patting me on the back.

“Sorry about that…” I replied.

He led me down the building’s corridors and toward an exit. When he
opened the door, I was greeted by dozens of boats and underwater drones.
The woman from the previous day was speaking with some of my colleagues.

“Jesus… how has nobody seen this?” I asked, looking around the well-lit
area.

“Don’t ask me,” he replied, walking toward the woman.

She guided us onto a rather small, albeit expensive-looking boat. I
followed John aboard and looked around, noticing several unfamiliar
faces.

“Hello, everyone. You all know why we’re here,” the woman began. Shortly
afterward, the admiral appeared.

“You can go, Mary,” he told her.

She left for the cockpit.

“For the next few hours, we’ll be heading to the Hudson Canyon. Once we
get there, we’ll meet with a few people whom you’ll all speak to. I’m
confident that by the time we arrive, they’ll have some new,
higher-quality footage,” he told us.

“There’s been more… activity beneath the surface, so don’t expect calm
waters. Y’all better get comfortable, because it’ll take a bit. I hope
you guys bring some value to the table,” the admiral added before
heading to the cockpit himself.

In the distance, I could see the lights of other boats moving across the
dark ocean. We wouldn’t be making the journey alone.

As time passed, the sky gradually began to illuminate, revealing more
boats scattered around us. The land slowly disappeared behind the
horizon. White lights glowed from the boat, illuminating the underwater
drones mounted along its walls. We were required to wear life jackets,
which would come in handy considering I didn’t know how to swim.

“Anyone else nervous?” John asked, looking out over the ocean.

“I am…” a woman replied. “I don’t think we’ve spoken before,” she added,
looking toward John.

I tried to listen, but the water splashing against my face made it
difficult to concentrate.

“My name’s John. And you are?” he asked.

“My name’s Maria. I—”

Before Maria could finish, someone interrupted.

“Hope you guys don’t mind, but I’d appreciate some silence,” someone
shouted from farther down the boat.

Any further attempts at conversation were quickly discouraged. After
that, nobody spoke much.

Not long before we arrived at the site, I heard the admiral yelling at
someone inside the cockpit.

“What are the seismic readings this time?” he asked, still looking
toward the instruments.

“2.3,” a woman replied.

The admiral shook his head and walked over to us.

“Well, everyone, we’re nearly there. We’re getting some more seismic
activity underwater, but that shouldn’t impact our investigation too
heavily.”

He paused, looking down at the floor.

“Now, when we get there, we’ve brought much better drones to observe
whatever it is. Any questions?” he added.

“Does the public know about this yet?” Maria asked.

The admiral hesitated before answering.

“No, not yet. We’ve blocked off the air and water around this area,” he
replied.

As we got closer, a sense of unease began to settle over me. Not long
afterward, we arrived. There must have been about a dozen naval warships
surrounding us, stretching as far as I could see. The admiral
communicated with someone over the radio, though their voice was too
faint to make out.

I turned my attention toward the ocean and noticed something unusual.
The water was pulsing rhythmically, just slightly. I looked back at
everyone else. Nobody seemed to have noticed. I carelessly assumed it
was simply the wake from one of the surrounding boats.

I turned back toward the admiral. We were alongside one of the warships,
and everyone was climbing up a ladder. I soon followed.

“Alright. There are a few fancy computers up here to properly watch the
drones’ cameras,” he told us as we continued climbing.

I’m afraid of heights, so I tried not to look down. Eventually, we
reached the top, and I stepped aboard the warship.

“Why didn’t we just bring this thing out here to begin with?” John
groaned after pulling himself aboard.

“Less noise and activity. I like it more that way,” the admiral replied
as he walked down the steel stairs toward the CIC.

We all followed. As we made our way down, I heard someone bang their
head against a pipe.

When the admiral pushed open a large metal door, the darkness of the
room briefly overwhelmed my eyes. There was little light beyond the
buttons and screens of the monitors. The room was occupied by numerous
people, many of whom appeared to be high-ranking government officials
and military leaders.

“Alright, let’s get this started. This is the command center, so to
speak. A few of my guys told me they already sent a drone down there,”
the admiral paused.

“Robert! Get the camera feed up on here!” he yelled, pointing toward one
of the monitors.

A few seconds later, the feed appeared on the screen, showing the dark
ocean floor. Everyone fell silent, even John. For the first few minutes,
nothing interesting happened.

“What do the magnetometers read?” the admiral asked his assistant.

“Zero, sir,” the assistant replied.

The drone continued forward. The sound of water rushing along its sides
was the only thing audible, while the dark abyss ahead remained almost
entirely obscured. As it approached the fissure, the subtle shaking
began again. The drone’s light penetrated the darkness, illuminating the
dusty ocean floor.

“What the hell is that?” one of my colleagues asked.

Nobody answered.

As the drone continued forward, its light finally settled on something.
It moved closer, shaking more violently with every passing second. As
the light reached farther, it became clear what we were looking at. The
camera caught the dark vapor escaping from the fissure, which had grown
considerably since the previous footage. The meaty hum persisted, now
with much greater force.

The fissure appeared to have doubled in size, its far end nowhere in
sight. It looked as though a mountain were being erected from the bottom
of the ocean, its dark, scaly surface slowly emerging from the seafloor.
Whatever lay beneath it was rising much more rapidly than before. A few
people gasped.

“Any other footage?” the admiral whispered to one of his assistants.

“There’s footage from a second drone about two miles from that one…” the
assistant replied.

The admiral whispered something to him, and the second drone’s footage
replaced the first on the monitor. Its image was much lower in quality,
and the camera shook considerably more.

“Send the drone into it…” the admiral whispered.

The assistant seemed confused but carried out the command. The footage
showed the drone slowly approaching the black mass before disappearing
into it. Its feed went black.

The first drone’s footage returned to the monitor. The low, meaty hum
intensified, shaking the camera even more. The black mass seemed to
shift, cracking the surrounding seafloor further. The image became
increasingly unstable.

Then the feed went dark.

“Shit,” the admiral said.

He tapped his finger against the metal table as he looked around.

“More drones have been sent, Admiral,” his assistant quickly said.

He nodded.

“Well… do any of you have anything of value to offer?” he asked, looking
around at each of us.

I was lost for words.

“I could’ve sworn I saw tendrils… sir, that damn thing is sentient!” a
coworker yelled, pointing at the black monitor.

“Sentient?” the admiral asked, looking confused.

“A gas reservoir doesn’t react to pain, sir,” Maria said.

The realization seemed to dawn on the admiral that this thing was alive.

“I’ll have to tell the higher-ups about this,” the admiral stated.

I paced around the room, looking for something to occupy my mind.
Despite the fact that I worked in a program dedicated to finding
non-terrestrial intelligence, the thought that I was in the vicinity of
something like this disturbed me.

“How do we know it’s extraterrestrial and not just… an undiscovered
aquatic creature?” I asked.

The admiral turned around to face me.

“Our magnetometers can’t read this thing. Hell, this thing causes
goddamn earthquakes when it moves… I would doubt it’s an earthly being,”
he said, walking directly toward me.

I diverted my gaze to the ground, intimidated.

“Y-you never gave a valid explanation. There’s nothing saying it’s an
alien. Doesn’t that seem a little… outlandish?” I asked.

The admiral turned away again.

“I guess it might be. But if it’s that big… then we outta treat it as
such,” he said, turning to his assistant once more.

My colleagues turned toward me, staring as though I were an idiot.

“You know we’re the ones that believe in aliens, right?” John asked.

I nodded and turned away.

After a few more minutes of procrastinating, the admiral spoke.

“Alright, you’ve all served your purpose. Thank you. The boats are
outside waiting on you,” he said, not even turning to face us.

One by one, we left. As I emerged, the sunlight briefly blinded me.

“Well, the damn boat is late!” John yelled, looking over the railing.

I looked down. The boat that had taken us was nowhere to be seen. I let
out a sigh and turned back toward the ocean. Even now, the water
continued to pulse slightly. Nobody else seemed to notice. After a few
seconds of staring, I heard the boat rapidly approaching.

“Hey, John! Look at the water!” I yelled.

“The water? Alright…” he laughed as he peered over the railing once
again.

His expression shifted from amusement to confusion. He looked back at
me.

“That’s… odd,” he told me.

It bothered him, I could tell. It bothered me as well. I just wanted to
get back to land, so I tried not to think about it. I took a seat and
looked toward the sky. It was filled with clouds, but in the far
distance, I could see dull gray clouds rolling in. My coworkers sat to
my left, quietly laughing and shouting jokes to one another.

Warships stood intimidatingly in the distance, moving ever so steadily
across the water. I could still hear the subtle humming from the drone
footage. Truth be told, I couldn’t tell whether it was my imagination or
not. The water’s rhythmic pulsing and rippling resembled boiling water
in a pot.

To ease my anxiety, I looked back toward the approaching boat. It was
nearly here.

Before I could think, the ship jolted as though it had been struck by a
torpedo. A sudden, violent impact threw me and my colleagues off
balance. Immediately afterward, high-frequency vibrations rattled the
ship. Workers began running toward the doors, their stomping boots
echoing through the air.

“I think it’s a fuckin’ seaquake,” one of the workers said to another as
they ran.

I got back up and looked down at the water. The boat had just arrived.
Through the commotion of workers rushing past, I could hear the admiral
shouting. His voice was filled with anger, each word seemingly louder
than the last.

I walked toward the staircase and headed down. When I pressed my ear
against the door, I could hear his words more clearly.

“What do you mean it’s moving? The fuck do you mean!” he yelled at
someone.

Someone quietly responded.

“No, no, show me the footage!” he shouted.

“Goddamn… that’s the clearest shot we’ve gotten…” he murmured.

I pushed open the door. It creaked slightly, but nobody seemed to
notice. Everyone was huddled around a monitor.

“Jesus…” a worker said, hunched over.

As quietly as I could, I closed the door. One of the workers turned and
briefly locked eyes with me before looking back at the screen.

I pushed past a few people until I could finally see the monitor.
Through the dirty, murky water, the black mass was clearly visible. Its
surface resembled a snake, and it was forcing itself out of the Earth
with more force than I had ever seen.

As it surfaced, it seemed endless, growing larger with every passing
moment. Its tendrils extended from the mass, gripping the surrounding
ground. The black vapor spread through the water, obscuring the camera’s
view. The admiral let out an angry sigh and grabbed his forehead.

Then something snapped open.

It was pink. It might’ve been an eye.

“Shit!” the admiral yelled, jumping backward.

I’m not sure whether it was the admiral’s shout or the eye opening, but
everyone seemed to jump back as well. Through the black vapor, a tendril
pushed toward the drone. It wrapped around the device, obscuring the
camera’s view entirely. A moment of darkness followed before the drone
went offline.

The admiral quickly got up and shoved his way through the small crowd,
pushing the door open. Everyone seemed shocked, meandering around the
claustrophobic room. Someone stomped down the stairs and peered into the
room.

It was John.

“Jesus, Michael, what are you doing down here?” he hurriedly asked,
grabbing my shoulder and leading me out of the room.

The humming in the air pierced my ears, and the ship continued to shake
slightly. The sky was no longer blue, but gray. Storm clouds had
overtaken it, leaving everything beneath them dim and colorless. A light
drizzle began to fall against my face.

“What’s that noise?” I asked, holding my ears.

“Probably just… something wrong with the ship,” John said as a running
ship worker shoved past me.

In the distance, the stationary warships began to sail away.

“It’s our time to leave, Mike,” he said, climbing down the ladder.

The ladder vibrated slightly, but I continued down.

“Jesus, the damn thing is moving!” someone yelled from above.

I finally planted my feet on the smaller boat and looked around at
everyone. They seemed annoyed, but there was an unmistakable sense of
worry among them. The water had begun to rise and fall in violent waves.
I put on my life jacket and sat down.

A new, older man was captaining the boat. He spoke through his small
radio.

“I’m takin’ the… alien guys back to land,” he said as he spun the boat
around.

“We’ve been ordered to leave, so stay at the base,” a woman told him
across the radio.

“Yes, ma’am,” he replied, seeming indifferent.

Everyone sat in silence, occasionally glancing at one another. The
general feeling among our group was that we had found something we were
never meant to find. I know I certainly felt that way. Perhaps that’s
why the journey back felt so hostile.

Eventually, we arrived at the naval outpost.

Everyone seemed on edge. Amid the shouting, it was impossible to tell
who was actually in charge. As we left the docking area, a woman
approached us.

“I’d like you to follow me,” she said, leading everyone into a room.

She shut the door behind us, and the chaotic yelling faded into the
distance. Everyone took a seat.

“What you saw at the ocean will not be spoken of outside this room. It
is, and will be, classified information. We know your names and family.
It would be wise to remain silent,” she said.

“That aside, we thank you for your help with this situation. It is
possible that we may contact you in the future about this… incident,”
she added. We looked at one another, still silent. She guided us out of
the room and into the hall.

“The exit is that way.” She pointed down the crowded hallway. “Again,
thank you all,” she added.

I walked past the people filling the hallway and pushed my way into the
rainy parking lot. Between the trees, I could still see the dark clouds
hanging overhead.

“Well, shit,” John said, patting my back.

“What do you think that thing was?” I asked, pulling my hood up.

“Don’t know… might be the kraken for all we know,” he told me as he got
into his car.

“But we’ll never know, probably,” he added.

He pulled out of the parking lot. Everyone else gradually followed, but
I could tell from their faces that the experience had affected them.
Hell, it had affected me. It still does.

The government hasn’t contacted me about the thing since. I don’t think
they will.

As I sit in my apartment, I find myself thinking about whatever is
beneath the ocean. I remember the footage, the humming, and that awful
sight of something forcing its way through the seafloor. Sometimes I
wonder whether it ever stopped moving.

I know it’s still down there. I know they’ll continue sending things to
investigate it, even if they never tell us what they find.

The earthquakes haven’t stopped, either. Every now and then, another one
strikes somewhere along the East Coast. In the last few weeks, they’ve
become both more frequent and more powerful.

I know it’s coming. I know it’ll be here soon.
