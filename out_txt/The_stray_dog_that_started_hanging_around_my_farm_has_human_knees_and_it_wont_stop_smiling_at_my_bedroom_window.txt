I live about forty minutes south of Sandusky, out in the flat stretch of
Huron County where the cornfields run right up against the thick patches
of oak and hickory timber. Nearest real town with a stoplight is
Norwalk, and even that’s a twenty minute haul down two lane county
roads. It’s quiet out here. Just me, my wife Sarah, our eight year old
boy Wyatt, and about eighty acres of soybeans, sweet corn, and old
pasture.

I’m thirty eight years old, spent my whole life working dirt and fixing
busted tractors, so I like to think I know a thing or two about animals.
Coyotes, raccoons, stray mutts dumped by city folks who got tired of
feeding them I’ve seen them all.

That’s what I figured it was at first. Just another dumped dog.

It started showing up around the edge of the north woodline about two
weeks ago, right around dusk when I was finishing up in the pole barn.
From a hundred yards away, it looked like a massive black German
Shepherd or maybe some kind of wolf hybrid. Broad chest, thick dark
coat, big pointed ears. It just sat there in the tall grass, watching
the house.
I told Wyatt not to go near it. Last thing I needed was my kid getting
bit by some rabid stray.

Of course, eight year old boys don’t listen worth a damn when they think
an animal needs help.

A couple days later, I came back from hauling seed in Willard and caught
Wyatt out by the rusted hog pen behind the barn. He had an old stainless
steel mixing bowl from Sarah’s kitchen, and he was dumping leftover pot
roast and dry kibble right onto the dirt.
“Wyatt, what did I tell you?” I yelled, walking over. “Get back up to
the house. That thing ain’t a pet.”

“He’s too skinny, Dad,” Wyatt said, kicking at the gravel with his
boots. “And he’s nice. He sits when I tell him to. He just looks funny
when he walks.”

“What do you mean, looks funny?”

“Like his back legs are hurting,” Wyatt said. “Like Grandpa when his hip
acts up.”

I figured the poor thing had been hit by a truck out on Route 20 and had
a busted pelvis. I didn’t yell at Wyatt anymore, just told him to wash
his hands and stay on the porch after dark.

The next evening, Sarah mentioned it while we were doing dishes. She was
looking out the kitchen window over the sink, her hands still in the
soapy water.
“Mark,” she said quietly. “That dog is out by the clothesline.”

I walked over and looked out into the yard. The security light on the
barn was buzzing, casting a yellow circle over the grass. Sure enough,
the big black dog was sitting right next to the T post where Sarah hangs
our sheets.

Except the way it was sitting made my stomach drop.
Dogs sit on their haunches. Their back legs fold up tight against their
ribs. This thing was sitting like a man crouching down to tie his boots.
Its back legs were bent at sharp, awkward angles, with kneecaps jutting
forward under the matted black fur. Its front legs were way too long,
hanging down between its knees, and instead of paws resting flat on the
grass, its front feet were curled inward like loose fists.

“Look at its shoulders,” Sarah whispered, gripping the edge of the
counter. “Dogs don’t have shoulders like that, Mark. That looks like a
grown man wearing a coat.”

I grabbed my flashlight and my old 870 from the hall closet. I wasn’t
going to shoot it unless it charged, just figured I’d fire a warning
shot into the dirt and run it off for good.

When I stepped out onto that porch and flicked the heavy Maglite on, the
beam hit it square in the chest.

It didn’t flinch. It didn’t tuck its tail or run for the cornfield.

Slowly, smoothly, it pushed itself up. Not onto four legs. It stood
straight up onto its hind legs, unfolding to a height that had to be
pushing seven feet tall. Its chest was broad and muscular, covered in
coarse black hair, and its long arms hung down past its thighs with
thick, clawed fingers twitching at its sides.
My finger tightened on the shotgun’s trigger, my heart hammering against
my ribs. I shined the light up into its face.

It had the long, heavy muzzle of a timber wolf, ears pinned forward,
eyes glowing a dull, wet amber in the flashlight beam. And then, while I
stood there frozen on my own porch, the corners of its black lips slowly
pulled back.

It didn’t snarl. It didn’t bare its teeth like an angry dog. It
stretched its mouth wide into a flat, tight lipped grin, showing rows of
thick white teeth, holding that expression while its eyes locked dead
onto mine.
I didn’t even shoot. I just backed straight through the screen door,
slammed the deadbolt shut, and yelled for Sarah to get Wyatt into our
bedroom.

We pushed the heavy oak dresser in front of the bedroom door, and I sat
on the edge of the bed with the shotgun across my lap while Sarah held
Wyatt under the covers. Our bedroom is on the second floor, looking out
over the front yard and the gravel driveway.

Around two in the morning, the house went dead quiet. Even the crickets
outside stopped chirping.

Then I heard a soft tink against the glass.

I looked up at the bedroom window.

The roofline of our porch doesn’t reach the second floor. There is
nothing under that window but twelve feet of empty air and siding.

Yet there it was. Its massive, hairy shoulders were blocking out the
moonlight, standing completely upright in the flowerbed below, tall
enough that its head was level with the second story sill. One of its
long, human like hands was pressed flat against the outside of the
glass, leaving greasy smudges on the pane.

And pressed right up against the window, staring straight into our room,
was that same wolf face. Its lips were still peeled back in that wide,
mocking smile, watching me hold my gun, waiting for morning so my boy
would come back outside to feed it.
