I’ve lived in 11 different places over the last three years. Different
cities, different states, and even different countries. I’m a travel
nurse, so frequently packing up and starting over somewhere new is part
of the job.

And yet, every Halloween, wherever I’m at, the same four children come
to my door.

I mean the exact same children. Their costumes change, but their faces,
their heights, and the strange way they stand together never do.

In three years, none of them has aged a day. For kids that young, it’s
impossible to miss.

The first time, it was easy to rationalize. But this quickly became
something much more horrifying, and its reach extends further than
simply a single ominous annual visit.

For the last three Halloweens, a different child has stepped forward at
my doorstep while the others watched. This year, it’ll be the fourth
child’s turn.

Next Halloween is just over a week away. I’m posting this because I need
to know whether anyone else has seen these children, and whether
anything unusual in your life happened after they appeared.

Let me explain.

The first time they visited me was October 31, 2023.

I was working a contract at a hospital in a midsized American city,
renting a duplex near the emergency department.

I’d gotten off a double shift that afternoon. My landlord left a plastic
pumpkin full of candy in the common space between the duplex units, so I
put it beside the door and handed out treats between bites of reheated
takeout.

By 9 pm, the street was quiet. I turned off the porch light, put on a
horror movie, and started eating the leftover candy myself.

I must have fallen asleep on the couch. When the doorbell rang again,
the movie had ended and the stove clock read 11:45 pm.

It was a Tuesday night, and I couldn’t imagine who was still taking
children around so late.

I almost ignored it. In hindsight, maybe I should have. Most likely,
considering how this turned out, it wouldn’t have made a difference. 

At the time, thinking it was just a rogue band of older kids looking for
a final score, I glanced at the plastic pumpkin. I saw a handful of
pieces at the bottom, and decided somebody else might as well eat them.

I opened the door.

Four small silhouettes stood shoulder to shoulder at the edge of the
porch. The streetlamp across the road cast long shadows behind them, but
their faces were completely dark.

“Jesus! You kids scared the hell out of me.”

They didn’t laugh. None of them even flinched.

“Excuse my language, sorry. Little late to be out, isn’t it?”

I looked past them toward the street, expecting to see a parent waiting
on the curb, with none to be found. The houses and sidewalks were dark.

I flipped on the porch light.

I was instantly struck by how young they were, five or six years old at
most. The first wore a white tunic, a gold plastic crown, and had a bow
slung over his shoulder. His face was half-hidden behind the hooked beak
of a plague doctor’s mask. He looked like he was wearing two very
different costumes.

Beside him stood a boy in a red soldier’s uniform with a plastic sword.
The third wore a black scarecrow costume. The fourth had on a pale grey
robe with a hood.

Under the light, they looked less frightening. Somebody’s kids in
homemade costumes, probably enjoying how badly they’d startled me,
though they didn’t show it.

“Well?” I said, lifting the pumpkin. “Aren’t you going to say it?”

“Trick or treat,” said the little king. His voice was calm and clear.
The others stayed silent.

He stepped forward, selected a piece of candy, and put it in his bag. I
waited for his friends to reach into the pumpkin, but they remained
where they were, watching.

“Thank you.” He said flatly.

“Happy Halloween, guys.”

He returned to his place. All four turned at once and walked away.

“Hey, don’t the rest of you want anything?”

They didn’t look back.

I watched them disappear down the street, then shut the door. Strange
kids, I thought. Strange parents, too, letting them wander around alone
at that hour.

By the time December arrived, the encounter had faded into the category
of odd things that happen when you spend enough time living in
unfamiliar places. I had more immediate concerns.

I had accepted an assignment with an international relief organization
responding to a cholera outbreak. They needed nurses with critical care
experience who could leave on short notice. That’s my bread and butter.
I’d worked with the organization before, and I knew what I was signing
up for.

Or thought I did.

Cholera is absolutely treatable. In most cases, the challenge is
replacing fluids and electrolytes before dehydration causes organ
failure. It sounds straightforward when you’re hearing it explained in a
climate-controlled classroom, with clean water and supplies always
within arm’s reach.

The textbook doesn’t convey how quickly and aggressively a person can
empty themselves of water.

By the time I arrived, the hospitals were overflowing. An open-air
soccer stadium had been converted into a treatment center. Patients lay
in rows of cots with openings cut into the mattresses and collection
buckets underneath. There was no practical way to change the sheets fast
enough.

The smell was everywhere: disinfectant, vomit, and human waste, baked
into the heat. It settled in my hair and clothes. Even after a shower, I
could taste it in the back of my throat.

As awful as it is to say, in situations like that, the patients start to
blur together. Distinct memories are few and far between.

One case I’ve tried to forget was of a little girl whose mother kept
rubbing her hands because they were so cold. The child watched me
prepare an IV without making a sound. Her mother asked if she would be
all right. Of course, I told her we were doing everything we could.

Her mother was still holding her hands when I left to attend another
patient. By the time I returned, the cot had been cleared. I never had
the spine to ask where they had taken her.

We worked until our hands cracked from constant washing. Every time a
bed opened up, somebody else was waiting to take it. I learned to sleep
through shouting and to wake when someone called for help.

Then one morning, I woke up with stomach cramps.

I knew exactly what was happening, which made it worse. I could feel my
pulse racing. I was thirsty enough to think about nothing but water, but
I couldn’t drink fast enough to replace what I was losing. Salt and
potassium packets weren’t cutting it. Every time I stood up, my vision
narrowed.

Eventually, I realized I was too weak to manage this myself. I made it
as far as a colleague’s tent, pulled back the flap, and tried to say her
name. She caught me by the arm as my knees gave out.

The next thing I remember is waking with an IV in each arm.

My kidneys had started failing. For two days I drifted in and out of
consciousness while other nurses monitored my electrolyte levels. I was
fortunate. I had triaged my own symptoms as much as I could, and was
surrounded by colleagues who were all too familiar with how to treat
cholera.

One afternoon, I woke to find a little boy standing just outside my
tent. He wore a pristine, unstained white T-shirt and shorts, and
couldn’t have been more than six.

He was looking directly at me.

Unlike the other children I’d seen at the treatment center, he wasn’t
crying or clinging to an adult. He stood perfectly still, watching me as
staff and volunteers hurried past.

One of the nurses walked straight toward him. I thought she was going to
run into him, but at the last moment, she stepped aside without looking
up.

Something about him seemed familiar.

I tried to sit up for a better look, but the movement made me dizzy. By
the time I settled back against the pillow, he’d disappeared.

Several of our patients died during the days I was unconscious. So did
the nurse who’d caught me when I collapsed. I found out about her when I
was finally well enough to sit up and ask where everyone was.

I spent a month recovering and went home. Certain smells still take me
straight back to that stadium.

Halloween 2024 found me on the opposite side of the US from where I’d
spent the previous, working another domestic contract. I was renting a
little one-bedroom house in a quiet neighborhood. After the outbreak, a
gig dealing with routine seasonal flu felt almost luxurious.

I had the evening off. I picked up a bag of candy on my way home, made
myself dinner, and settled in with a movie. By ten, even the teenaged
trick-or-treaters had gone home. I switched off the porch light and went
to bed.

At 11:47 p.m., the doorbell rang.

I remember the time because I checked my phone before getting up.

Through the narrow window beside the front door, I saw four small
figures. They stood shoulder to shoulder, perfectly still.

I recognized the arrangement before I recognized anything else.

That was ridiculous, of course. I’d moved across the country. And four
children standing together on Halloween wasn’t unusual.

Still, I turned on the porch light before opening the door.

The first child wore a white robe, a laurel wreath  trimmed in gold, and
a toy bow and arrow. Hooked around his ears and tucked beneath his chin
was a disposable surgical mask.

I knew that face.

It belonged to the boy I’d seen standing outside my treatment tent
during the cholera outbreak. I’d been so sick at the time that I’d
convinced myself any familiarity was a trick of a dying mind.

But here he was, months later, back in the United States, thousands of
miles from that treatment tent, looking exactly the same.

Beside him stood a boy in red plastic armor and a knight’s cape, with a
foam sword at his hip. The third child wore torn black clothing and
zombie makeup instead of a scarecrow costume. At the end stood a little
undertaker in an oversized ash-gray suit, a top hat, and white gloves.
His powdered face was almost colorless.

I kept looking from one to another, comparing them with the four
children I remembered. Their costumes were somewhat different, but their
heights, their positions, and the way they lined up were unnervingly
familiar. 

A year had passed. Surely I was mistaken. None of them seemed remotely
surprised to see me.

That was what frightened me most.

“Do I know you kids?”

Silence.

“I saw you all last year, didn’t I?” I asked. 

Nothing.

I looked to the first boy, the one I remembered from the tent.

“You…I’ve seen you recently, I’m almost sure of it.” 

The little king watched me without blinking.

I looked toward the sidewalk, hoping to spot an adult whom I could
interrogate. The street was empty.

“Where are your parents?!” The question came out angrier than I
intended.

The boy dressed as a red knight, second from the left, stepped forward.

“Trick or treat.”

I looked at the little king beside him.

“Not you this time?”

He didn’t respond.

I picked up the candy bowl from the entryway table and held it out.

The knight chose one piece. “Thank you.”

He stepped back into line. All four turned together.

“Wait. How did you know where I live?”

They kept walking.

I followed them as far as the porch steps, then stopped. I watched them
pass beneath the trees and disappear.

I locked the door and searched online for the costumes. Nothing I found
explained the similarities, and it didn’t help that the costumes were
just different enough between both years to make any connection tenuous
at best.

Eventually, I convinced myself the resemblance was a coincidence. I’d
been critically ill when I’d seen the boy outside my tent, and a year
had passed since the first Halloween. I had plenty of reasons to
distrust my memory.

The alternative was believing four children had followed me across the
country, and that one of them had somehow appeared - just a few months
ago - at a treatment center in a developing country suffering through a
serious epidemic. Absurd, when I put it to words.

I shut my laptop and massaged my temples. The children hadn’t threatened
me. They’d taken one piece of candy and left.

I went back to bed.

In the spring, I accepted another international assignment.

A hospital near an active conflict zone needed trauma nurses. I’d
followed the war on the news and knew that civilians were being hurt
every day. I also knew I had skills the hospital desperately needed.
When I asked about security, the organization assured me the facility
was far enough from the fighting to be considered “relatively stable.”

I should have asked what they meant by relatively.

At first, the hospital was functioning. We had intermittent electricity
and shortages of medication, but the staff were capable and the wards
weren’t full.

Most of our patients were civilians. One farmer had lost three fingers
while clearing unexploded munitions from his land. A teenager came in
with shrapnel wounds across his abdomen, and an elderly woman had burns
down her back after an explosion set her kitchen on fire.

In emergency medicine, you learn to concentrate on what can be fixed.
Airway. Bleeding. Circulation. If you stop to imagine what somebody was
doing an hour before they arrived on your table, you won’t be able to do
your job.

Over the following weeks, the fighting moved closer. At first, the
explosions sounded like distant doors slamming. Then they rattled our
windows. Within weeks, the hallways were crowded with stretchers and
patients sat on the floor holding bloody towels against their wounds.

Eventually, the hospital administrators started discussing evacuation. A
nearby clinic had been destroyed. Roads that had been considered safe
were no longer passable. We were told to keep our identification and
emergency bags ready.

Three days later, just after two in the morning, I was helping another
nurse change a dressing when I noticed a little boy standing in the
doorway.

He wore a shirt so thoroughly soaked in blood that I couldn’t tell what
color it had been originally.

“Are you hurt?” I asked.

He didn’t answer.

I set down the gauze and stepped toward him. He raised his head to meet
my gaze.

I recognized his face.

It was the little boy in the red knight costume who’d come to my door
the previous Halloween. He’d been a red soldier before that.

As I opened my mouth to speak, the first explosion hit.

The floor jumped. Dust sifted down from the ceiling.

Then came a second blast, closer.

Somebody shouted for everyone to get away from the windows.

I turned toward the hallway as the third explosion struck.

I don’t remember the sound of that one. I remember a flash, an enormous
pressure against my chest, and the sensation of being thrown backward.

When I opened my eyes, I was lying beneath part of the collapsed
ceiling. My ears rang so loudly I couldn’t hear myself breathe. The
emergency lights were out. A flashlight flickered somewhere beyond the
doorway.

I tried to sit up.

Pain shot through my left thigh. A jagged piece of metal was embedded in
it, and blood was soaking through my scrubs. I knew better than to pull
it out. I pressed my hands around the wound and tried to judge how much
blood I was losing.

Then I saw someone struggling beneath the rubble.

It was the nurse I’d been working with when the explosions started. We’d
worked almost every shift together since I’d arrived. A section of wall
had fallen across her chest. She was trying to push it off.

I dragged myself toward her. She shouted something, but the ringing in
my ears swallowed the words. I tried to lift the concrete. It wouldn’t
move.

Blood ran from her mouth. I wiped it away with my sleeve, and more
bubbled up when she tried to breathe.

I knew what that meant.

I kept telling her help was coming. I told her to stay awake. I promised
we’d get her out.

At some point, she stopped trying to breathe.

I kept talking long after she stopped responding. Eventually, I gave up
trying to move the wall fragment and just lay beside her with one hand
pressed against the wound in my leg. I stayed there until rescuers found
us.

Outside, the hospital was barely recognizable. Smoke billowed from the
upper floors. Ambulances and private cars were tossed haphazardly
through the street like a child’s toys. People carried patients away
from the main entrance on makeshift stretchers using doors torn from
their hinges.

I remember looking back and seeing the windows of the ward where we’d
kept patients whose lungs couldn’t draw air without machines.

The electricity was gone, and the entrance to that part of the building
had collapsed. I knew what that meant for the people still inside.

I was evacuated to another facility, where surgeons removed the metal
from my thigh. I’d suffered a concussion, a soft-tissue injury, and
damage to my hearing. The shrapnel had missed the major blood vessels. I
was lucky again.

To this day, when a car backfires, I still duck. I have recurring ENT
appointments because the ringing in my ears hasn’t gone away. Sometimes
I wake up convinced I can feel that nurse’s blood on my sleeve.

I also kept thinking about the little boy in the doorway.

The nurse who’d been beside me was dead, and I never found anyone else
who remembered seeing him. In fairness, it was impossible to narrow down
“a child whose shirt was drenched with blood” that day.

I tried to even convince myself I’d confused him with one of the wounded
children we’d treated. The concussion certainly hadn’t helped my memory.

Still, I could picture his face more clearly than almost anything else
from that night.

Before I could face another assignment, I started seeing a therapist. My
agency was willing to give me time to recover, and I needed it. After
several sessions, my therapist suggested a vacation somewhere warm and
quiet.

I booked a trip to the Philippines.

I wanted to spend a few weeks doing absolutely nothing useful for
anybody.

The bungalow I rented was on a relatively isolated stretch of beach. It
looked better in the listing photos than in real life, but it had a bed,
running water, and a porch overlooking the ocean. The nearest town was
several miles away. Most of the neighboring rentals were empty.

For the first time in months, I could sleep without expecting an
explosion.

I swam, read books, and drank cocktails made from liquor I bought on my
weekly trips into town. My phone stayed on silent. I stopped checking
the news. I barely knew what day it was.

Which is why I didn’t realize it was Halloween.

That evening, I was sitting on the porch listening to the surf when I
heard a child singing.

At first, I thought the voice was an illusion of the wind. The melody
was slow and repetitive, almost like a hymn.

Then I saw four small figures standing near the waterline.

They were standing shoulder to shoulder.

I recognized them immediately.

I stood up so quickly I knocked over my chair.

“No,” I said aloud. “No, no, no.”

I backed toward the bungalow, keeping my eyes on the figures by the
water.

The singing stopped.

For a few seconds, there was nothing but the gentle crash of ocean
waves. Then the children began walking toward me.

I went inside and shut the door.

I stood with my back against it, trying to make sense of what I’d seen.

I’d come halfway around the world. I was staying on an isolated beach in
the middle of nowhere. Nobody from home even knew the address of this
place.

And yet, somehow, those four children were outside.

I thought about the little boy in white, standing outside my treatment
tent while I was dying of cholera. Then the boy in red, his shirt soaked
in blood, appearing moments before the hospital was bombed.

Both times, people had died. People I’d known. People I’d tried to save.

And now the children had come back.

I heard a soft knock.

I turned toward the door.

Another knock followed, no louder than the first.

I didn’t move.

I had no idea what these children wanted from me, or what would happen
if I refused to give it to them. They’d never threatened me. They’d
never even been rude. But I couldn’t shake the feeling that their visits
were part of something I didn’t understand, something that had already
cost other people their lives.

I finally looked through the window.

All four were standing on the porch.

They were wearing sheets, draped over their heads and shoulders like
old-fashioned ghost costumes. Each had an opening cut around the face,
leaving their features exposed.

The sheets were different colors.

The first child’s was white, the second’s a deep red. The third wore
black, with something small and metal hanging from one hand. As I
watched, he shifted slightly, and I saw it was a little balance scale.
The fourth child was wrapped in a washed-out shade of grayish green that
looked almost colorless beneath the porch light.

None of them had grown an inch since I’d seen them last.

I could have stayed inside. I could have turned off the lights and
waited until morning.

But they’d come to my door twice before, and each time they’d stood
there until I’d given one of them something. I had no reason to believe
they’d leave this time.

I unlocked the door, but kept one hand on the handle.

“How the hell did you find me?”

Nobody answered.

“How did you even get out here?”

Silence again.

“Who are you kids and what do you want?!”

The boy in black, third from the left, stepped forward.

I nearly slammed the door.

Instead, I stood there gripping the handle as he raised his head and
began to sing.

I didn’t recognize the language. His voice was soft, haunting, and
perfectly steady. The scale swayed from his hand as he sang, one empty
pan hanging lower than the other.

When he finished, he extended his free hand.

“I don’t understand what this is. I don’t know what you’re saying.”

He kept his hand out.

I looked past him toward the beach. There wasn’t another person in
sight. There wasn’t even a proper road to the bungalow. Getting here
from town required riding the weekly water taxi or driving a special
vehicle.

I took out my phone and opened the voice recorder.

“Can you…can you sing that again?”

He did, his hand still stretched out expectantly.

When he finished, I realized in a panic that I didn’t have any candy.

I considered just telling him to leave. But I remembered the other two
children, and how each had taken exactly one piece before all four
turned and walked away.

I didn’t know what would happen if I broke that pattern.

“Just wait here,” I said.

I went inside and found a rice cake in my pantry.

When I returned, the children hadn’t moved.

I stepped onto the porch, closing the door behind me, and dropped the
rice cake into the boy’s outstretched hand.

“Is this what you wanted?”

He took it carefully and placed it in a little cloth bag at his side.

“Thank you,” he said in English.

Then he returned to his place in the line. All four turned together and
walked back toward the water.

I didn’t bother calling after them this time. I stood there until they
disappeared beyond the reach of the porch light.

Only then did I go inside.

I locked the door and pushed a chair under the handle.

I listened to the audio recording three times, checking that the singing
hadn’t been something I’d imagined.

Then I checked the date on my phone.

October 31, 2025.

I hadn’t remembered the holiday. I’d rented a place miles from the
nearest town on the other side of the world.

And they’d still come.

The next morning, I used my rented motorized tricycle to take my phone
to a café in town and asked an employee who spoke excellent English if
she could translate the song.

After a few seconds of listening, she nodded and paused the recording.

“Oh, of course. Pangangaluluwa.”

She explained that pangangaluluwa was an old tradition associated with
Undas, the days surrounding All Saints’ Day and All Souls’ Day. Children
went from house to house dressed as ghosts, singing on behalf of souls
in purgatory and asking for prayers and small offerings.

“Like trick-or-treating, but more religious?” I asked.

“Something like that,” She smiled. “We’re a devout people.”

She hit play and kept listening. Her smile faded. “Where did you hear
this?”

I told her some kids had come to my bungalow the night before.

“On the beach?”

“Yeah.”

“That’s very far from town.”

“I know.”

She listened to the entire song, then translated it while I typed the
words into my phone.

We come to your door to ask for an offering.

Remember the souls of the dead, for more will soon join them.

A day’s wages will buy only a measure of wheat.

The scales will determine how much each person receives.

Those who have plenty will still know hunger.

Give what you can while the fourth is still waiting.

“That’s unusual,” she said.

“No kidding.”

“I mean, the song starts right, asking for prayers for the dead. But
then the rest…this sounds more like the Bible. What were they wearing?”

I described their costumes, including the different colored sheets.

She frowned. “The sheets are traditional, yes, but they’re normally all
white.”

“These weren’t.”

She stopped what she was doing. “Wait. What did you say the third child
was holding?”

“Um…a little scale, or balance, or something like that.”

She looked at me, then back at the phone. “Oh. I think I understand.”

“Understand what?”

“The Four Horsemen, from Apocalipsis, or Apocalypse.”

“From what?”

“I guess you’d call it ‘The Book of Revelation.’ White, red, black, and
pale. Those are the colors of the four horses in the Bible. And the
third rider carries a pair of scales. There’s even a passage about how
much wheat costs, just like in that song. It sounds like the others
aren’t carrying their items though.”

“What are the others supposed to have?”

“Well, the first rider carries a bow and wears a crown. The second has a
sword. And the fourth, the last… he doesn’t carry anything, but we know
his name is Death.”

Three years of Halloween costumes, and somehow every detail fit.

“And the first three?” I asked. “What are their names?”

“They’re usually called Conquest, War, and Famine. Although some people
interpret the first one as Pestilence.”

Pestilence.

I thought about the little boy who’d appeared on my porch years earlier,
wearing a crown and a plague doctor’s mask.

Then I remembered the cholera treatment center. The buckets beneath the
beds. The little girl whose mother kept rubbing her hands.

And the little boy in the white T-shirt, standing outside my tent.

“The second one is war?”

She nodded.

I felt dust in my nose as I recalled the hospital collapsing around me.
The nurse trapped beneath the wall.

And the little boy standing in the doorway, his shirt soaked red with
blood.

“And the third is famine?”

“Yes.”

She offered a perfectly sensible explanation. Maybe the children
belonged to a church group. Maybe they were tourists staying somewhere
along the coast. Somebody had obviously put a lot of thought into the
costumes.

I thanked her and left.

Back at the bungalow, I looked up the passage in Revelation. Everything
she’d told me was there, right down to the price of wheat.

I couldn’t tell whether the children caused these disasters or simply
knew they would happen. But both had occurred in places I’d chosen to
go. Places in crisis.

Perhaps I could make different choices.

One thing was for sure: I wasn’t going anywhere near a famine.

I changed my ticket and flew home two days later. I told my agency I was
done with international assignments for the foreseeable future. No
conflict zones. No humanitarian emergencies. I wanted a domestic
hospital somewhere boring.

I took time to recover, then accepted a contract in an ordinary American
city. The hospital had a stocked cafeteria, dietitians on staff, and a
pharmacy that dispensed more weight-loss medications than heart
medications.

It seemed about as far from famine as a place could get.

I rented a small apartment near the hospital. The neighborhood was
older, mostly working-class, with a grocery store on the corner and a
church that operated a food pantry twice a week.

I noticed the lines outside the church almost immediately. They were
already forming when I left for my morning shifts, sometimes stretching
halfway down the block.

At first, I assumed the church was hosting some kind of community event.
Then I saw the people carrying boxes of groceries back to their cars.

I started buying extra food when I shopped. Nothing extravagant. Rice,
canned vegetables, pasta, whatever was on sale. I’d drop it off at the
church on my way to work.

After a while, I began recognizing people in line. A cashier from the
convenience store. A woman who cleaned rooms at the hospital. One of the
security guards from our parking garage.

People who worked every day and still couldn’t afford to feed
themselves.

I’d seen hunger before, of course. In the places I’d worked overseas, it
usually came with other obvious signs of deprivation. Here, it was much
easier to miss.

One of my patients was a 68-year-old woman brought in after fainting at
a bus stop. Her blood pressure was dangerously low.

She told us she’d been feeling weak for months. When I asked about her
diet, she said she ate toast for breakfast and soup for dinner.

“And lunch?”

She looked embarrassed.

“I usually don’t bother.”

It took some gentle questioning before she admitted that her Social
Security check barely covered rent, utilities, and medication. She’d
been watering down canned soup to make it last longer.

Her primary care physician had recently told her to increase her protein
intake. She laughed when she told me that.

“I asked him if he’d seen the price of eggs lately.”

We treated her symptoms. A social worker arranged for nutritional
assistance and gave her information about local food programs.

Before she left, she asked whether she could take the unopened crackers
from her bedside table.

I gave her as many packages of crackers as we had in the storage room.

A few weeks later, a 56-year-old man arrived with his daughter. He was
confused and could barely walk. He’d lost almost 60 pounds in a few
months after starting a GLP-1 medication for type 2 diabetes.

At first, he’d been thrilled. He’d grown up poor, eating whatever his
mother could afford to feed five people. Even after escaping poverty,
the damage done to his hormones and metabolism kept him chronically
obese for decades. He’d tried fad diets, hot yoga, even gastric bypass
surgery.

Now, finally, he’d found something that worked…and he could easily
afford to use it for the rest of his life.

Except he’d barely eaten in weeks.

The medication had suppressed his appetite. Persistent nausea made
eating even less appealing. He figured that was the point. He was still
a big man. How could he possibly be starving?

His worsening confusion, abnormal eye movements, and difficulty walking
suggested severe thiamine deficiency. We started treatment immediately,
but some of the damage had already been done.

He survived, but he couldn’t walk without assistance. His daughter sat
beside his bed, explaining who she was each time he asked. He couldn’t
recall conversations we’d had minutes earlier.

With concern, she told him how much weight he’d lost.

He smiled. “Almost at my goal,” he said.

I thought about the boy in the black sheet and the little balance scale
swinging from his hand.

A day’s wages for a measure of wheat.

I started paying more attention to the people standing outside the
church. Some of them were elderly. Others had children with them. Most
were dressed for work.

One morning, I walked past the woman I’d given boxes of crackers to
standing near the end of the line. She was leaning against the church
fence, looking exhausted.

I stopped to ask how she was doing. She said she was fine. Then she
asked whether I knew if the pantry would have eggs that day.

I didn’t.

She turned back toward the entrance.

That’s when I noticed a little boy farther down the line. He wore an
oversized black T-shirt and held an empty grocery bag. His eyes were
sunken and hollow. He looked about five years old.

I knew his face immediately. But this time, the dark circles under the
eyes weren’t from costume makeup. I started toward him, pushing between
the people waiting for food.

“Hey! Wait! You!”

He turned and looked directly at me.

A woman carrying a cardboard box stepped between us. By the time she’d
passed, he was gone.

I searched the line in a frenzy. Twice. Then, I asked a volunteer
whether he’d seen a little boy wearing black.

He shook his head. I stood there for a while, watching the line inch
forward, my own stomach in knots for a reason other than hunger. I tried
to get my breathing under control, feeling suddenly dizzy.

I looked back toward the woman in line I’d been speaking to, but she’d
already gone inside.

Four days later, she was brought into our emergency department.

A neighbor had found her unconscious in her apartment. She was severely
dehydrated, her blood sugar was dangerously low, and her body
temperature had dropped well below normal.

We started treatment immediately. For a few hours, it seemed she might
recover.

Then her heart stopped.

I kept thinking about the eggs she’d asked me about. I wondered whether
the pantry had any that morning. Whether she’d gotten to eat them.

Meanwhile, the man who’d spent a fortune trying to lose weight was
suffering just a few rooms away.

She’d died hungry. He’d survived, but his life as he knew it was over.

I’d flown home to avoid famine.

But really, I’d come somewhere people are better at hiding it. And
occasionally, where famine can be bought.

Maybe the children weren’t warning me where not to go.

Maybe they were showing me what was already happening.

Or maybe something worse was still coming.

I’d assumed each visit corresponded to something that would happen
before the next Halloween, as though one disaster ended when the next
began. But the cholera outbreak hadn’t ended when the war started, and
the war was still going on when I came home. I had no reason to believe
the children followed any such schedule.

What I did know was that I’d been wrong to think I could make myself
safe simply by choosing where to work.

By September, my contract was nearly finished, and I started looking for
another assignment. I still wanted somewhere quiet, preferably close
enough to home that I could visit family on my days off.

I found a position that offered exactly that.

I’ve been at my latest assignment for about three weeks now. It’s a
regional hospital in a small town not far from where I grew up. The
staff are friendly, the pay is decent, and the emergency department has
been mercifully quiet.

My recruiter described the position as temporary surge support.
Administrators expected a large influx of visitors around November and
wanted extra emergency staff available. There was mention of a weeklong
festival, but I didn’t pay much attention to the details.

It wasn’t until I arrived that I learned what everyone was coming to
see.

Apparently every autumn, Earth passes through a vast trail of debris
left behind by a comet called Encke. Most of it is harmless: lots of
tiny fragments that burn up when they enter our atmosphere. But larger
pieces can produce brilliant flaming streaks of light visible from the
ground.

They’re called the Taurid meteor showers. I’d never heard of them, but
apparently they’ve earned the nickname “Halloween fireballs” because of
the time of year they appear.

The debris isn’t evenly distributed, and occasionally Earth passes
through particularly dense concentration of fragments.

According to the festival website, a university research team had
recently identified one of those concentrations along Earth’s projected
path. Their calculations suggested that this autumn could produce an
unusually spectacular fiery display.

The town had hosted an astronomy festival for years, taking advantage of
the unusually dark skies outside the city. But the new prediction had
turned what was normally a regional attraction into something much
bigger.

Hotels were booked solid. Restaurants had extended their hours. Shops
were selling glow-in-the-dark souvenirs. Our charge nurse said they were
expecting tens of thousands of visitors over the course of a week.

“All this for a meteor shower?” I asked.

“People are coming from all over the country,” she said. “Apparently
it’s supposed to be quite a show.”

I didn’t think much of it at the time. Then I went home and made the
mistake of reading a little further into Revelation.

I’d stopped after the fourth seal when I first looked it up…one seal for
each horseman. I’d matched their colors and symbols to the costumes, and
stopped before I could get any more freaked out.

I hadn’t read what came next.

The fifth seal was different. There was no rider. John described the
souls of people who’d been killed for their faith, crying out for
justice. They were given white robes and told to wait until the number
of others who would be killed was complete.

I couldn’t connect that passage to anything I’d experienced. But
disease, war, and hunger were still happening around the world. Whatever
the children represented, the disasters associated with them didn’t seem
to follow a neat schedule.

Then I reached the sixth seal.

John described a great earthquake, the sun turning black, the moon
becoming red as blood, and stars falling from heaven.

I read that last part again. Stars falling from heaven. Then I opened
the festival website.

There was a countdown on the homepage. Organizers were encouraging
visitors to gather in parks and open fields outside the town center.
Shuttle buses would carry people to temporary viewing areas, where
organizers were planning telescopes, food trucks, live music, and
midnight celebrations.

Thousands of people standing outside, looking up.

I kept reading.

After the sixth seal, Revelation describes kings, generals, rich people,
poor people, slaves, and free people hiding among the rocks and begging
the mountains to fall on them.

For a while, I seriously considered abandoning the contract. I could
pick up my parents, drive until I couldn’t keep my eyes open, and spend
Halloween in a motel somewhere with the curtains closed.

But the children have found me everywhere I’ve gone. I don’t see why
this time would be different.

I know I’ll see the fourth child again. First on Halloween, standing at
my door with the others, finally stepping forward for his turn. And
perhaps afterward, somewhere among the thousands of people coming here
to watch the sky.

If Death is coming, I don’t think there’s anywhere I can go to escape
him.

So I’m staying. If something happens, the hospital will need every nurse
it can get. Assuming I’m still alive to help, that is. I’ve already seen
what happens when a hospital is overwhelmed, and I’d rather be here
doing what I can than trying to hide from whatever comes next.

But there’s something I need to know, and that’s why I’m posting this.

For three years, I’ve assumed these children were following me. Now I’m
wondering how many other doors they’ve visited.

Think back to the last few Halloweens. Did four young children ever come
to your door unusually late, after the streets had emptied? Did they
stand together, with only one stepping forward to ask for candy?

If so, did the visits begin at the same time as mine? And most
importantly, what happened afterward?

I’m keeping the name of this town to myself. The hospitals are already
preparing for swaths of visitors, and I don’t want to give another
reason for anyone to come. Suffice it to say, if you’re planning to
attend a large astronomical viewing event this year, please reconsider.

I know what’s going to happen at my door on October 31.

I’ve seen what the first three children leave behind.

I don’t know what will be left after the fourth.
