I fit kitchens for a living, so I measure things without deciding to.
It’s automatic. I walk into a room and I’ve got the dimensions before
I’ve said hello.

That’s how I know the chamber was about thirty feet across and roughly
twenty at the high point, because that was the first thing my head did
when I woke up in it, before I’d worked out I couldn’t stand.

I want to be straight about that from the start. Nothing hurt. There was
no wound, no blood, no break I could find with my hands. My legs were
there and they were warm and they would not hold weight, and I lay on
limestone in the dark doing sums about ceiling height because the
alternative was thinking about that.

I’m thirty-seven. I live outside a town in Hart County, Kentucky, with
my wife Jess and our daughter Ruby, who was six then. On the 2nd of
October I took the dog up the ridge behind our place, which I’d done
maybe four hundred times.

I don’t remember going down. There’s a lot of karst back there and every
few years the county fills a sinkhole nobody knew about. That’s my best
guess and it’s still my best guess.

The dog got home on the fourth day. Jess said he was fine, just filthy.

Right. So.

The first voice I heard was a woman about fifteen feet to my left, and
what she said was, “Don’t shout.”

Her name was Kath. She was forty-something, a district nurse out of
Munfordville, and she’d been down there six days before I turned up. She
talked to me for about an hour that first night in a flat, low, steady
voice, like she was reading out a care plan, and by the end of it I
understood roughly four things.

We were not alone in the cave. There was a third person, a man called
Yusuf, up against the back wall, and he’d been there a lot longer than
either of us. Something came, not every day. And the legs wore off.

That was how she put it. It wears off. She said hers had gone almost
completely by day four and she’d been able to stand, and then it came
back and did it again, and she was back to crawling by the next morning.

I didn’t believe her. I want to be honest about that, because it makes
what came later worse.

For about two days I thought Kath was lying. I had a whole theory.
People do this. People take people, and they keep them somewhere, and
the reason my legs didn’t work was that I’d been given something, and
the reason Kath was so calm was that she’d been down there long enough
to start cooperating. I’ve thought about this a lot since and I don’t
think it was stupid. I think it was the only explanation available that
let me sleep.

Yusuf was the thing that broke it.

He was maybe ten feet from Kath and he couldn’t move at all. Not his
legs. Anything. He could talk, badly, and he could turn his head a
little, and that was it. Kath had been moving him every day so he didn’t
get sores, which she did by dragging herself over on her elbows and
rolling him, and it took her the best part of an hour each time.

He’d been a land surveyor. He’d gone missing from a job near Horse Cave
and I worked out later that was five weeks before I went down the ridge.

Five weeks.

You cannot keep a man alive in a hole for five weeks by accident.

It came on my third night, and I’m going to try to describe this
properly because it’s the part people ask about.

You hear it a long way off. Not footsteps. More like something large
moving through a space that’s too small for it, patient about it, and
the sound arrives before there’s any light to see by, so you lie there
for what Kath reckoned was about twenty minutes and I’d have said forty,
listening to it get closer at a completely steady rate.

It never hurries. I want that to land. In nine days I never once heard
it hurry.

Kath said, very quietly, “Face the wall and don’t move.”

So I faced the wall.

It came into the chamber and it went to Yusuf first, and I heard it
stop, and I heard Yusuf make a sound I’m not going to write down. Then
it moved along to Kath and stopped again. Then it came to me.

I had my face against limestone with my eyes shut and I could feel it
standing over me. There’s a temperature to something being that close.
It stayed there for a while. I don’t know how long and I’m not going to
pretend I do.

And then it moved me.

It picked me up by the shoulder and the hip, and it carried me maybe
sixty feet, and it put me down at the edge of water. Not dropped. Put.
Then it waited while I drank, which I did, because I’d had nothing for
three days and my body made that decision without asking me.

Then it carried me back and set me down within a foot of where I’d been.

It did the same for Kath. It took longer with Yusuf.

Our bottles were full in the morning. All three of them, standing
upright in a row against the wall, and I’d watched Kath empty hers the
day before.

That was when I stopped thinking it was people.

Here’s the thing I worked out on about day six, and it’s the reason I’m
writing this instead of just getting on with my life.

It wasn’t keeping us. It was keeping us *ready*. The water, the moving,
the way it rolled Yusuf the same way Kath did. That’s not cruelty, and
it’s not a trap, and it isn’t anything like the things I’d have guessed
before this happened to me. It’s maintenance. It’s what you do to stock.

And if you’re maintaining stock, you have an order.

Kath had already worked it out. Of course she had, she’d had six extra
days and she was a nurse, which means she’d spent twenty years looking
at a room full of people and knowing without thinking about it who was
going first.

The one it takes is the one it’s had to work hardest on.

Yusuf couldn’t move at all because it had done his legs and then done
them again and again for five weeks, and every time it did, it cost
more, and what it got back was a man who needed carrying to water.
That’s expensive. So the ones that get expensive get used.

Which means the healthier you are, the longer you keep.

Which means every mouthful of water Kath gave to Yusuf out of her own
bottle, every hour she spent rolling him so his skin stayed good, was
moving him further from the front of the line.

And me closer to it.

I’d been sharing with him since the second day. Kath had stopped a week
before I arrived and I’d had opinions about that. I’d said something to
her about it on day four. I said something about how we should all be
pulling together, and she looked at me in the dark with an expression I
couldn’t see and said, “You’ll change your mind,” and I thought she was
the coldest person I’d ever met.

She was just further down the road than me.

I stopped on day six.

I didn’t announce it. I didn’t discuss it with her. I told Yusuf my
bottle was empty, and it wasn’t, and I did that on the evening of the
sixth day and again on the seventh and again on the eighth, and he never
once accused me of anything, which I think was because talking was hard
for him by then and I’ve never been able to convince myself that’s the
only reason.

On day eight my legs came back enough that I could stand against the
wall.

On day nine, it took Yusuf.

I heard the whole thing. I’m not going to describe it. It took him and
it went out the way it always went out, and Kath and I lay in that
chamber and neither of us said anything for a long time, and then Kath
said, “Now,” and we started crawling.

It took us most of a day to get out and the only reason we managed it is
that Kath had been mapping the airflow for two weeks with a piece of
thread off her cardigan. We came up through a collapse about a mile and
a half from where I’d gone down, in daylight, and a man called Rodney
Salter who was out on a four-wheeler put us both in the back and drove
us to the road.

They searched. They found the chamber. They found Yusuf’s equipment and
a lot of older things I’ve been told about and haven’t asked to see, and
they didn’t find anything else, and the report that came out of it used
the words “probable animal activity” in a way that let everybody go
home.

Kath and I have spoken twice. She’s all right. She asked me, the second
time, whether I’d stopped sharing my water, and I said yes, and she
said, “Good,” and that was the end of the conversation and I think it
was meant kindly.

Jess knows all of it. Ruby knows I got lost in a cave.

Here’s where I am now, nearly two years on, and it’s the reason I
finally wrote this down.

I still put people in order.

I do it in the pickup line at Ruby’s school, sat in the truck with the
engine off, going along the row of parents. I do it in the dentist’s
waiting room. I did it at my brother-in-law’s wedding, at the table,
during the speeches, and I got all the way through eleven people before
I noticed I was doing it.

It takes about four seconds and it’s completely automatic, like the
measuring.

I know the order for every room I’ve been in this month, and I know
where I come in all of them, and the worst part, the part I can’t get
out from under, is that I’m never wrong about who’s weakest.

I was never wrong down there either.
