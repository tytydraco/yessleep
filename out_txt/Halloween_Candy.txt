Whenever children die the adults all lose their minds. No matter how,
natural or otherwise it’s just such a tragedy, but I don’t think they
ever believe they may have deserved it. They always think we’re all so
innocent, pure or something. Well maybe they don’t think that about all
of us. The way these cops are looking at me right now, it doesn’t feel
like they think I’m innocent.

I doubt they can prove that though. You’ll never take me alive coppers.
“Care to share?” asked Detective Bryant. “Share what?” I asked back,
doing my best to look every bit as young as my fourteen years. “What’s
so funny?” He asked, his anger not hidden as well as he probably thinks
it is. “What’s funny?” I repeated, trying to sound confused not
sarcastic. “You tell me kid?”, good. He does see me as just a child.

“You had a little smirk on your face, like you thought of something
funny. If you can think of something funny at a time like this, I’d love
to hear it.” For fucks sake this guy is so dull. So damn serious, “at a
time like this” as if it’s such a big deal. People die everyday, a lot
of them kids. None of these ones were going anywhere in life anyway, not
like me.

Not every life is a loss, some people just take up fucking space. I
wasn’t about to tell him that though. “No sir, I didn’t mean to ‘smirk’.
I’m sorry.” I can play the part, I’ve had to mask as long as I can
remember. My mother thought I had some sort of delay, but every
pediatrician she took me to always told her the same thing. That I
possess above average intelligence for my age. I’ve always been at the
top of my class since I started school.

I guess a mother does know best though. She knows her own child at
least. The older I’ve gotten the more I’ve grown to understand I’m not
like everyone else. The delay is actually just something that never
developed at all. And it’s in my heart, not my mind. I believe it’s
evolution, it’s made me stronger than most people.

If I saw the right specialists and in light of the current events it’s
highly likely I will have to soon. I imagine they will diagnose me with
something like antisocial personality disorder. Most people with those
disorders deny them, they don’t want the stigma. Me either, but that
doesn’t mean we aren’t aware. We just don’t want the consequences that
come with a diagnosis. Everyone around you knowing, being wary of you.
Not being able to do the things we want to do because everyone around us
is on high alert in our presence.

So we mask and deny. Not out of conscious, obviously, but convenience.
Average people think it’s a bad thing, that lack of feeling. They don’t
take into account the other side of it. An ability to do the hard things
in life that need done, no one else has the stomach for. Paired with my
exceptional mind I’ll accomplish things most people can’t even
conceptualize.

These cops are definitely average people. The detectives in front me and
all the cops probably listening to us somewhere else in this dump. I’m
willing to bet they don’t have any real evidence and are depending on a
confession. Otherwise I’d be under arrest in a jail cell, not being
questioned in an interrogation room.

“Where did you get all that antifreeze?” Detective Bryant asked. Being
carfeul not to ask leading follow up questions like “did you steal it,
or stockpile it for a long time” although that was what they suspected.
“What antifreeze? I answered.”Jesse. This is serious, it will be easier
for you if you tell us the truth.” Detective Chance told me. “Nine
children are dead, your friends and classmates. Their families deserve
to know what happened.” Only nine was disappointing to hear. I wonder if
some of them are still clinging to their pitiful, inconsequential lives
in the hospital.

Of course I couldn’t ask that, but it definitely should have been more
like thirty kids dead. I miscalculated somehow. Human bodies can be
unpredictable like that though. People wake from comas unexpectedly
after decades as a vegetable with no explanation. Suffer traumatic brain
injuries and start speaking foreign languages they have no way of
knowing.

Nine isn’t a bad start though, for my first time. Well unless you count
that little girl three years ago, but that was only one. The Dawson’s
baby, not really a baby. She was two years old and I used my hands,
gently so I didn’t leave any marks. And it worked, just how I wanted it
too. As far as everyone knows she died of sids or some mystery medical
condition.

They never even questioned me when that happened, they didn’t suspect
anyone at all. Because people die all the time, even kids. “Were you
upset because Natasha didn’t invite you to her party?” Detective Chance
asked. “No. I don’t really like Halloween parties. All the scary
costumes.” That was mostly true at least, I do think Halloween is
stupid. Most holiday traditions are. “Is Natasha hurt?” I asked, wearing
my person mask.

“Okay Jesse. Sit tight, we’ll be back soon.” Chance said, sounding
tired. The two men filed out the door, to the hallway packed with
officers who had been watching from behind the two way mirror. “What do
you wanna do?” Bryant asked Chief Carter.

“For now we just have to wait until that psychologist gets here, let
them question him. Hope they know some tactic we don’t.” None of the
officers present was thrilled to hand this case to the feds, but agreed
it’s necessary. “Graham Pomeroy seemed to be the main target. He had the
highest levels of antifreeze so far, but they’re still waiting for all
the labs, sorting all the tests out.” The chief stated as they walked
towards his office.

“Why would he target his best friend, his only friend to hear the kids
tell it?” Asked Bryant. “Why would anyone do anything like this, let
alone a teenage boy from a good family?” The chief questioned, to
himself it seemed more than in response to Bryant. “Poisoned Halloween
candy, like the urban legend. All these children poisoned by their peer
not some evil pervert. It’s incomprehensible.”

Passing through the lobby the detectives could see Jesse parents. His
mother’s eyes puffy and red, shiny with more tears. His father, looking
stoic but concerned. Not yet admitting to himself where that much
antifreeze must have come from. The police would first check local
mechanics and factories. Until the medical examination showed that the
antifreeze was homemade. That’s when it would become undeniable to
everyone.

Jesse’s father, Dan Young owned a private pharmacy. With a small
chemistry lab Jesse spent most of his time in. Nine children dead at a
Halloween party, two more fighting for their lives in critical
condition. It didn’t seem like things could possibly get any worse, but
people always underestimate children.

No one could have known, that the worst was yet to come. Or how much
worse it would be. Monday morning, when the school served breakfast in
the cafeteria. It was not his peers this time, but the grade school. Pre
K through fifth grade, every victim under the age of eleven.

Jesse, the Halloween Poisoner, as the papers would come to call him
after it made international headlines; would soon surpass his own goal
of thirty children dead, by more than double.
