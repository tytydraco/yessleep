After Jehovah had created the heavens and the earth, He created a man
and named him Adam. When Adam awoke, the world was still silent. The sky
hung impossibly high above him, rivers flowed slowly between the
mountains, and trees bore fruits he had never seen before. The wind
moved through the grass, making a soft, endless whisper. He did not know
where he had come from, nor why this world existed. He simply walked
through it, driven by instinct. Jehovah looked upon him and said, “It is
not good for the man to be alone.” So Jehovah created another human
being. Her name was Eve.

They first met in a field covered with white flowers. Adam saw Eve from
a distance and stopped. Eve saw him too. Neither of them spoke. They
simply stared at one another from across the field. Then, at exactly the
same moment, an inexplicable fear came over them. Adam did not know why
he was afraid. He only knew that the creature before him possessed a
body strangely similar to his own, yet was utterly different. Her eyes
were looking directly at him. Her fingers, her hair, the faint rise and
fall of her chest—all of it filled him with a disgust that seemed to
come from somewhere deep within his body, as though he had suddenly
encountered something that was never meant to exist in this world. Eve
felt the same. Looking at Adam, she could think of nothing but fear.
Neither of them argued. Neither attacked the other. At the very same
moment, they turned and fled in opposite directions. Adam went east. Eve
went west. Neither called after the other, and neither was told that
they were, in fact, of the same kind.

The Garden of Eden was enormous—so vast that even a person who walked
without stopping for decades could never reach its end. Adam crossed
mountains and rivers and built a shelter deep in the forest. He gave
names to the trees, the rivers, and the mountains, and slowly learned
how to live alone. Eve did the same. In another distant forest, she
learned to fish and gather fruit, and at night she lit fires and built
shelters from stones and branches. Years passed without them seeing each
other again. Sometimes Eve remembered the creature she had encountered
among the white flowers. She did not know what it had been. She only
remembered the fear it had awakened in her. Adam sometimes remembered
the woman as well. He did not know where she had gone, nor whether she
was still alive. They both believed that encounter had been nothing more
than an accident in their long lives. Neither knew that it had been the
only time they would ever see another human being.

Many years passed. Adam grew old, and so did Eve. One night, Adam came
to the bank of a river. The moon was full, and the surface of the water
was still as a vast mirror. He bent down to drink and saw his own
reflection. But the reflection slowly began to move. First it raised a
hand. Then it turned. Finally, the figure rose from the surface of the
water. Adam stumbled backward in terror. The figure did not attack him.
It simply stood there in the river and looked at him. Adam stared at it
for a long time, and suddenly he remembered Eve, the woman he had seen
so many years ago. He could not explain why, but a strange and absurd
thought came to him: This must be my child. Far away, something similar
happened to Eve. She came to the bank of another river and saw a figure
in the moonlit water. It slowly became solid and rose from the surface.
Eve looked at the person who resembled her so closely, and she too had
the same thought: This is my child.

From that night onward, the rivers began to give birth to people. During
the day, they were only rivers. But at night, when moonlight fell upon
the water, one human silhouette after another appeared beneath the
surface. They became clearer and more solid until they finally stepped
onto the shore. They could speak. They could walk. They could eat. They
could embrace one another. They began building houses throughout Eden,
lighting fires, farming, and hunting. Villages emerged, then cities, and
then more cities beyond them. Their numbers grew. They gave new names to
the rivers, mountains, and forests. They studied the sky, fire, and
death. They could speak to one another, love one another, and form
families. Only Adam and Eve remained apart, living in distant corners of
the Garden, never truly seeing each other again. Everyone believed that
Adam was their father and Eve their mother, and that they were their
descendants. No one knew how these people had truly come into existence,
and Adam and Eve never explained it, because they did not know either.
They simply accepted it, as naturally as they accepted the rising of the
sun and the flowing of the rivers.

As time passed, people began to ask questions. They asked Adam, “Father,
where did you and Mother first meet?” Adam replied, “A long time ago.”
They asked, “Why did you separate?” Adam remained silent for a long
while before answering, “I don’t know.” So they went to Eve and asked
her the same questions. Her answers were no different. They gradually
discovered something they could not understand: Adam and Eve were
supposedly their common ancestors, yet they had never truly lived
together. They had not even spent a single day in each other’s company.
No one knew why their parents had separated, why they had been born from
the rivers, or why they existed at all. Doubt slowly became fear, and
fear eventually became anger. They began to suspect that Adam and Eve
were hiding something from them. They began to wonder whether their
birth had ever been a blessing at all.

By then, Adam had grown extremely old. His hair was completely white,
and his body had become bent and frail. One day, a group of young people
came before him and asked, “Father, why were we born from the rivers?”
Adam looked at them and said, “I don’t know.” They asked, “Does Mother
know?” Adam shook his head. “Then do you know where Mother is?” Adam
remained silent for a long time before finally answering, “I don’t
know.” The young people looked at one another. For the first time, they
realized that the man they called their father knew nothing. The next
day, they found Eve. She too was so old that she could barely walk. They
asked her, “Mother, why were we born from the rivers?” Eve replied, “I
don’t know.” “Does Father know?” “I don’t know.” “Then do you know where
Father is?” Eve closed her eyes. In her mind, she saw again the field of
white flowers from so many years ago, and the man who had filled her
with fear. At last, she whispered, “I don’t know.”

That night, a group of people returned to Adam. He was sitting beside
the river, watching the moonlight drift across the water. One young man
approached him and asked, “Do you remember us?” Adam looked at him for a
long time. “Who are you?” he asked. The young man answered, “Your
child.” Adam asked, “Where is your mother?” The young man said nothing.
Adam continued, “Where is she?” The young man replied, “You don’t know.”
Adam fell silent. For the first time, he truly understood that these
children were never something he had created, that he had never truly
possessed a family, and that he had never truly possessed Eve. Everyone
in this world called him their father, yet he did not even know where
his wife was. The young man raised the stone in his hand. Adam did not
move. The first stone struck him, followed by a second, then a third,
until his body collapsed beside the river. His blood slowly flowed into
the water, staining the moonlit surface a dark red. That night, for the
first time, someone died in Eden.

Far away, Eve died as well. No one knew who killed her, and no one knew
whether she thought of Adam in her final moments. Some said it was one
of her children. Others said it was one of their descendants. Some said
she simply died alone in the forest. More and more people were born.
More and more people died. Cities expanded, forests disappeared, roads
and palaces were built. Humanity began studying the heavens and the
earth, and gradually began to forget the story of the beginning. No one
could say exactly when Adam and Eve had died. Whether they had ever
truly met again became nothing more than an unverified legend. Only the
river remained, still reflecting the moon each night.

Many years later, a child came to the river. He looked down and saw his
own reflection in the water. He reached out his hand, and the figure
beneath the surface reached out as well. The child smiled. But in the
next moment, he realized that the reflection was no longer copying his
movements. The figure slowly raised its head and looked toward the
darkness behind him. The child turned around, but there was nothing
there. When he looked down again, one blurred figure after another had
appeared beneath the surface of the river. They stood silently beneath
the moonlight, as though waiting for something. No one knew who they
were. No one knew where they had come from. And no one knew whether Adam
and Eve had ever truly been their parents. The moon shone upon the
river, and the river continued flowing into the distance. The Garden of
Eden still had no end. Humanity continued to wander through it,
generation after generation—searching for one another, fearing one
another, loving one another, and killing one another. Yet no one knew
that, at the very beginning of the world, there had been only two
people. They had once seen each other.

And then, for the rest of their lives, they never saw each other again.
