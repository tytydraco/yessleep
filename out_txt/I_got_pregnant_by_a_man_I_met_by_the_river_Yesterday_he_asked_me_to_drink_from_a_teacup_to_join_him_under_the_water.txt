My house sits by the river, with willow trees around, far away from any
other house. It is old, wooden, and very silent. It didn’t use to be
this way. When my mother, father, and sister lived here, laughter filled
the house. We’d all sit in the living room together and laugh for
hours. 

My sister was the first one to leave. She was older, and they married
her off at sixteen. My mother died shortly after, and my father grew ill
a year after her passing. 

He lived for two years longer than the doctor had expected. I didn’t
mind caring for him. He was my father, and I loved him. I’d sit by his
bed each day, and we’d talk for hours, just like when Mother and my
sister were still here. 

Due to his illness, my father never found a husband for me, so after his
death I was left alone in this empty, silent house. I spent most of my
time by the water. Its ripples helped me fill the silence.

One day at the river, I was weaving willow branches into little baskets
when I heard a faint whistle through the ripples. It seemed to come
right from the river, but when I looked up, I saw a man sitting in the
willow tree, smoking a pipe. He looked down and smiled. His skin was
pale, almost green, and his hair was white, but he looked young and
handsome. I looked back at my baskets, trying to hide my rosy cheeks.

“Hello,” he said.

“Hi.”

“I’ve seen you here before.”

“I haven’t seen you here before.”

He laughed and jumped down. Up close, he was even more beautiful.

“I’m Vodník.”

“Like the one from the fairy tales?”

“Yes,” he laughed. “My mother loved them, so she named me after him.”

“I’m Anna.”

“That’s a beautiful name for a beautiful woman.”

“Thank you.”

My heart tingled. It felt so good talking to someone again.

“Do you live in that house alone?”

“Now I do. My father passed recently.”

“It probably gets lonely.”

“Yes. Would you like to come by for some tea?”

“If you don’t mind.”

“No.”

We walked back. I poured him tea. He picked up the cup and looked at it,
amused.

“You have beautiful cups. May I keep one?”

“Sure. I only use one.”

We continued talking for hours. The house was filled with laughter
again. I had missed it so much.

“I’ll have to go soon,” Vodník said as the sun set.

“Okay.”

“I can stop by again.”

“I’d love that.”

He came the next day, and from then on, every day. We always sat by the
windows looking over the river. The house finally didn’t feel silent. 

He had never stayed past sunset, until one night. That night was the
most beautiful of them all. His hands were cold but firm. His lips were
full and wet. By morning, he was gone. Only wet footprints remained in
the hall.

With Vodník’s visits, the house seemed to grow damper and moldy in
places it never had before. With the dampness, my stomach started to
grow too. My mornings were now filled with nausea, and I craved only
things from the river: raw fish, silt, and its water. My Vodník made
sure to supply them all. We weren’t married, and I was scared he’d walk
away. He didn’t. Instead, each morning he would stare at my growing
stomach with that beautiful smile of his.

Although the baby gave me trouble, I was still happy. It meant there
would be three of us now, and I’d never feel the house’s silence again.
Maybe Vodník would even move in with us.

One morning he came holding my old teacup carefully in both hands. My
stomach was so big I could barely get up to greet him, but he told me to
stay and came over to us.

“How are you?”

“Okay. What’s with the cup?”

“I came to ask you something.” His blue eyes stared deep into mine.

“What is it?”

“I want you to come with me.”

“Where?”

“Under the river.”

“What? But I…”

“I’ve lied to you, my love. I think you know who I truly am. There’s
only one way for us to be together.”

My heart sank. I looked at the ground.

“You just have to drink from this cup.” He pushed it forward. The water
inside moved on its own. It made my stomach turn.

“I don’t…”

“It will be painless, and we can be happy together with our child. You
will never hear silence again.”

I paused.

“But I’m human.”

“It doesn’t matter. I was human once too.”

I looked back up at him. His eyes now seemed dark like a dirty pond, and
his skin looked wrinkled as if he had been in a bath for days.

Tears filled my eyes.

“I can’t, Vodník,” I whispered.

He looked angrily into the distance. My stomach cramped hard. Cold water
trickled down my leg, quickly turning into a stream. 

The room filled with the smell of silt. My stomach cramped again. Water
kept pouring out of me.

“What’s happening?!” I screamed, but Vodník didn’t move.

Then another cramp. I felt my insides move. Bursting pain tore through
me. Something else came out with the water and drifted toward Vodník. He
picked it up and held it. It began to cry.

“If you don’t want to come with me, I’ll take what’s mine.”

He turned to the door.

“Wait!”

I got up. My head spun. My body almost collapsed. I held onto the wall
for a few seconds before moving on. The baby’s cries echoed outside. I
gathered all my strength and went toward the door.

I was panting, bleeding. The baby’s cries echoed from down the river. I
saw Vodník’s silhouette by the willow. Then the water splashed, and the
baby’s cries slowly faded, mixing with the water’s ripples. Only my old
teacup, turned upside down, stood by the riverbank.
