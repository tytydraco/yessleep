I’ve been backpacking around the world for years, searching for
forgotten, wild places and ancient legends. I wouldn’t call myself
easily frightened or a naive city guy who panics at the rustle of a
branch. I grew up in Russia, in the Voronezh Oblast, and since
childhood, I’ve been deeply fascinated by our local folklore and
mysticism. I’ve spent plenty of time exploring the famous Divnogorje
reserve—a breathtaking and simultaneously eerie place where massive,
ghostly white chalk pillars, known by locals as “Divas,” rise over the
steppe like frozen, silent giants, while miles of dark, cold caves
stretch beneath the earth. It was there, from the village elders, that I
first heard stories about our domestic Gorkun—an ancient subterranean
forest spirit. According to local tales, the Gorkun lures lonely
travelers deep into the chalk labyrinths and thickets with its long,
melancholic, barely audible whistle. The golden rule I was taught since
childhood was simple: if you hear the Gorkun’s whistle, forget your
curiosity, turn around, and leave. Never follow the sound. I honestly
believed that since I knew how Slavic witchcraft worked and how to avoid
the tricks of our Gorkun in the Voronezh steppes, no foreign entity
could ever scare me. God, I was so damn wrong. Last summer, my thirst
for adrenaline brought me here to Colombia. However, I decided to leave
the usual, overcrowded tourist hubs like Medellín, Cartagena, or Bogotá
behind. I wanted the real wilderness. I headed east into the vast, wild
tropical savannas known as Los Llanos. This is thousands of kilometers
of high grass, wetlands, scattered trees, and suffocating, sticky heat.
These endless plains and deep forests felt like a completely different
planet, beautiful by day and terrifyingly silent by night. Before
heading deep into the prairies, I stopped by a half-empty, smoky bar on
the outskirts of a small town called Yopal to escape the midday heat and
grab a cold beer. Noticing my massive hiking backpack and professional
gear, the old bartender with deep wrinkles on his face grew dark. He
called over an elderly llanero cowboy in a worn poncho sitting in the
corner, and they both approached my table. “No camines solo de noche,
gringo,” the bartender said grimly in broken English. “El Silbón is
wandering the Llanos this time of year. The Whistler. He is hungry.” I
just smiled dismissively, thinking of our Gorkun back in Divnogorje. I
thought every culture had its own whistling forest myths—just fairy
tales to attract tourists or scare children. But the cowboy caught my
gaze. His eyes under the brim of his old hat held an absolute,
terrifying seriousness. He began speaking in intense Spanish, while the
bartender hurriedly translated his instructions. These weren’t just
stories; it was a checklist for survival that the locals knew by heart.
“Listen to me, amigo,” the bartender translated while the llanero
scratched the wooden table with his fingernail. “If you are out on the
plains at night and the air around your tent turns ice-cold, lock
yourself inside. Fasten every zipper and don’t make a single sound. Most
importantly—listen to the whistle. It sings a clear musical cadence of
seven notes, like a scale: Do-Re-Mi-Fa-Sol-La-Si. And remember the rule
of blood: if you hear this whistle very close, right by your ear, it
means the Whistler is still far out in the savanna. You are relatively
safe. But if the whistle sounds muffled and faint, as if it’s a couple
of kilometers away… God help you, Russian. It means he is standing right
next to your tent. He is right behind your back.” The cowboy leaned
closer, smelling of cheap tobacco and dry earth. He pointed to his own
spine, and then to a sack resting near his boots. “He is a full six
meters tall,” the bartender translated. “His body is shockingly
emaciated and withered, like an ancient skeleton in gray rags. On his
head, he wears a massive tattered straw hat that hides his face. And on
his back, he drags a burlap sack full of human bones—the bones of his
own father, whom he slaughtered years ago. If you give away your
position, if he realizes someone is inside the tent—he will enter. He
will kill you silently. He will carefully extract every single bone from
your body, and until dawn, he will sit right on your floor mat, counting
them one by one before packing them into his sack with the rest.” I
swallowed hard but still tried to act like a skeptic. As I was leaving,
the bartender grabbed my shoulder tightly: “If you meet him, you only
have two chances. He is terrified of a rabid dog barking and the crack
of a heavy whip. If you don’t have a vicious dog or a whip with you—you
are a dead man, gringo. Do not go out on the plains alone.” I nodded
politely, thanked them, left a generous tip, and walked out. I was
completely confident that my hiking experience and common sense would
protect me. I was so damn wrong. Over the last forty-eight hours in the
deep Colombian wilderness, I have seen this six-meter monstrosity twice.
The first time happened on my fourth night of backpacking. I had gone
very far from civilization. Near evening, the weather turned sour in a
matter of minutes. A thick, heavy white fog rolled over the plains,
completely choking out the moonlight. Remembering the cowboy’s words, I
decided to pitch my tent early near the ruins of an abandoned, crumbling
cattle ranch. Around 2:00 AM, I woke up abruptly. Not because of a
noise, but because of a piercing, bone-chilling cold. The temperature
inside my tent dropped so unnaturally fast that my breath turned into
thick fog, which was physically impossible in the tropical heat of
Colombia. And then, a whistle cut through the silence.
Do-Re-Mi-Fa-Sol-La-Si. The sound felt incredibly close, right outside my
tent flap, but it was strangely muffled. The bartender’s words flashed
violently in my mind: If you hear the whistle close, he is far away. But
if it sounds muffled and distant… The whistle repeated thirty seconds
later. But this time, it sounded like a faint echo coming from the
deepest depths of the dark savanna, a couple of kilometers away. My
heart hammered against my ribs. By the rules of the Llanos, it meant
only one thing: he was standing right behind the thin nylon wall.
Slowly, terrified of making the slightest rustle, I looked up at the
wall of my tent. Through the translucent light of the fog outside, I saw
the silhouette. It was a monstrously, unnaturally tall six-meter
creature. Its head in the massive tattered hat towered incredibly high,
stretching way past the dome of my tent. The entity was shockingly thin,
like a withered dead tree trunk, and on its back hung a burlap sack the
size of a truck bed. I heard its dry, raspy breathing, and then—the
distinct sound of dry human bones clacking together. It was checking its
sack. I clamped my hands over my mouth to keep from making a sound. In a
panic, I remembered the only salvation available to me: a dog barking. I
had my phone in my hand. With shaking fingers, I found a video I had
shot a few weeks prior back home in Russia: my friend’s massive
Caucasian Shepherd barking fiercely and heavily at a stray cat. I turned
the volume to the absolute maximum and hit play. The furious, booming
bark of a massive guard dog tore through the silence of the tent. The
six-meter silhouette outside jolted violently and unnaturally. The
clacking of the bones stopped instantly. A horrific, high-pitched
screech tore through the night, and something slammed into the side of
my tent with immense force, tearing the rainfly to shreds. Then, the
creature bolted into the high grass at incredible speed, and the whistle
echoed right next to my ear, rapidly fading into the furthest horizon.
By his rules, the loud, close whistle meant he was gone. At dawn, I
abandoned some of my gear, packed the rest, and ran as far as I could,
trying to make it to safety. But I made a mistake. I decided to camp out
for one more night in the savanna, pitching a new camp further down the
plains. And that is where I encountered him for the second time. It is
happening right now. I am writing this message without unnecessary
panic, simply stating the facts, huddled at the bottom of my sleeping
bag. Around 2:00 AM, the temperature around my tent dropped to absolute
zero again, and the nylon walls covered over with a thick layer of frost
almost instantly. First, a whistle echoed from the open plains—very
loud, clear, and piercing, right next to me. But then, barely a minute
later, it repeated. Muffled, quiet, faint… directly over the dome of my
tent. According to the Whistler’s rules, this means he is here. I looked
up. Through the translucent fabric of the top rainfly, I can clearly see
this six-meter monstrosity. It is standing right over my tent, its ugly,
infinitely long, emaciated legs spread wide like charred logs. Because
of its colossal height, its head in the massive straw hat is somewhere
high in the night fog, but it is bending lower and lower. The silhouette
of its giant, bony hand has pressed against the fabric. The creature
slowly lowered its arm and, with a soft, distinct tap, touched its long
fingernail to the top tent fabric, dragging it downward with a sickening
scraping sound. My phone battery is dying fast, and the speaker is
crackling as it plays the video of the dog barking from Russia on loop.
But the Whistler seems to have completely adapted to the sound after our
last encounter. He isn’t running away anymore. The clacking of human
bones in the massive burlap sack on his back has stopped, and now all I
can hear is his dry, raspy breath right above my head. I don’t have a
whip, and running into the pitch-black darkness of the six-meter-high
savanna grass is pointless—he will catch me in two steps. Our Gorkun
could be tricked by simply ignoring the sound, but this South American
giant plays by entirely different, lethal rules. I am turning to the
Reddit community, to the Colombians, to the Venezuelans, to anyone who
knows these legends. My phone is dying, I only have 4% left. If you know
how to make this entity leave, if there is anything else El Silbón
fears—I beg you, write it in the comments. I am trapped in the middle of
the Llanos, the creature is standing directly over me, and I don’t know
what to do when the screen goes completely dark.
