It was one of those chilly October nights. Cold enough that my cardigan
didn’t look out of place, but still warm enough that the brown splotches
on the sidewalk were from rain, not snow.

It was a Thursday, and Thursdays were when we’d go to O’Henry’s.

O’Henry’s had ended their drink specials years ago, but in a small
college town that ran on habit, we always seemed to end up there,
talking with Randy the bartender and forking over $20 for a pitcher of
vodka Red Bulls.

I don’t know when I split off from the group. I was always one for the
occasional Irish goodbye, but eventually I was meandering down Oak
Drive, humming softly to myself, tripping over sidewalk dividers.

My friends would track my location, make sure I didn’t get into any
trouble like freshman year. Or at least that was the little lie I told
myself. I guess if I had ended up in a ditch or something, they would
have realized when my usual hungover destination, the dilapidated,
seemingly soggy excuse we called a living room couch, was left vacant.

Anyway, there I was, the lights around me a haze that sharpened when I
squinted, letting them dance across my face as I laughed to myself. I
never promised to be a glamorous drunk. I kicked at a rock I’d been
dribbling along beside me and sent it clanging into one of the trash
cans put out for Friday pickup.

The street was empty. Just me and the asphalt. The yellow, orange, and
red leaves of the changing season broke up the black monotony. The wind
blew through my hair in between bars of my own hummed rendition of Billy
Joel’s “For the Longest Time.”

I saw his little head bobbing up and down as he stepped off his porch,
black plastic bag in hand. The boy opened the tin trash can, made good
on his chore, and scurried back up the stairs.

It was nothing, just a neighborhood kid, no older than ten. I was three
houses away when his front door thudded closed. I was at his next-door
neighbor’s house when his screen door squeaked open again, and he came
out struggling with another garbage bag, kicking it down the stairs.

His house was level with me now, pitch black. Unsurprising for the
lateness of the hour, but weird to see such a young boy out so late. I
watched him drag the garbage bag down the walkway. That’s when I saw it.

From a distance, he could’ve passed for a man. He wore a black fedora
like Indiana Jones and stood leaning against the side of the house on a
patch of grass, masked in shadow, hands shoved into his pockets. Then he
pushed himself off the wall. He was tall. Too tall. It took a second to
register that his head was level with the gutters. He wore a worn black
trench coat, and his skin, or whatever stretched around his body, was a
sickly grey.

I stopped in my tracks.

The boy noticed me staring and took a hesitant step back, his face
scrunched in confusion as he looked up and down the street, the dim
streetlights offering little comfort.

The man somehow stood up even taller, his head barely missing the lowest
tiles of the roof. My breath staggered as he slid his left hand out of
his pocket and raised one finger to his face, almost like he was
shushing me. The finger was easily three times longer than any I’d ever
seen, but no thicker than a human one.

The boy followed my stare and turned. His scream caught halfway out as
the man glided effortlessly toward him and, in one swoop, picked him up
by his left foot and dangled him upside down in the air.

He was massive, easily nine feet tall. He curled a finger over the boy’s
mouth, muting his cries. Then he pulled his hand away, and the finger
stayed behind, snapped off at the knuckle, still wrapped over the boy’s
mouth like a gag. With his free right hand, he opened a pouch near his
stomach, like a kangaroo’s, and stuffed the boy inside.

I blinked in shock.

The boy pushed his way back out of his new prison, half his head poking
out, one desperate hand reaching, crying out.

The creature’s neck was long, and it seemed to grow as I watched. His
face was bare: two simple black dots where eyes should be, and a flap
where a mouth should be.

The man, or creature, or whatever it was, pushed him down and looked
back at me.

Maybe it was the liquid courage, but my body rushed forward across the
street as it glided effortlessly backward.

In a matter of seconds, it was out of sight in the woods behind the
boy’s house. Seeing something break every law of nature should have
sobered me up, but it didn’t. I batted away a thorny bush, shouting
after it as I ran deeper into the darkness. Then I had it in sight
again, the full moon poking through the trees and washing over its grey
skin while my phone’s flashlight lit my path.

As it moved, I heard a loud, almost sucking noise that I couldn’t place.
I raced forward as it swiveled its head back, like an owl, fixed on me,
the flap of its mouth stretching into a grin, as if we were playing a
game of cat and mouse.

Then, as if it had grown bored of the chase, it turned and gained speed,
the black fedora bobbing farther and farther away. My own athleticism,
hindered by roughly ten shots of vodka, proved fatal to my pursuit.

I chased it until I collapsed in a clearing, face up to the sky, my
chest rising and falling. Up and down. My phone had landed somewhere in
the dirt beside me, its flashlight pointed uselessly at the treetops.

“Help. Help me.”

The boy sounded terrified. My answer caught in my throat as the
situation came crashing down on me, and I began to realize how much
danger I was really in.

“I’m over here. He has me, but I’m here.”

I searched desperately, certain my mind was playing a cruel trick on me.

The fedora sat on a stump. The coat and the grey skin lay beside it in a
wrinkled pile, empty like something had stripped it off. I crept closer,
curiosity overpowering my instinct to run far, far away.

Past the stump, just beyond the moonlight, something moved. I couldn’t
make out its shape. Only a wet glint, and that sound again, that slow,
sucking pull. And the boy’s legs, kicking out of the dark.

“Help me…” he gasped one last time. His legs thrashed from side to side
until falling into a haunting stillness.

I scrambled backward, kicking up dirt and rocks, trying to put any
distance between me and whatever was at the edge of the clearing. I
heard it inch closer before my night went black.

I don’t know how I got back to our living room couch the next morning.

Our school sent out its own version of an Amber Alert for the missing
boy. For the first week, I was certain I’d simply had the most
terrifying nightmare possible, and I swore off alcohol and all other
mind-altering substances.

One of my roommates went as far as joining the local search party that
scoured the woods for any sign of the boy, but he called it quits soon
after, mumbling something about essays and midterms.

I made peace with my fantasy. I must have heard about the disappearance
that night or something. I lived my lie with complete confidence for
almost two weeks. Then one morning I was rushing out the door, donut in
hand, late for an early seminar, and nearly tripped over something on
our welcome mat.

A dirty, faded black fedora.
