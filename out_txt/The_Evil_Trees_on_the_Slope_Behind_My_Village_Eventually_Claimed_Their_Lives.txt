In the rural south of China during the 1980s, tailoring was a rare and
sought-after trade. My mother was a seamstress renowned for miles
around. With my father working in a city hundreds of miles away, the
burden of supporting our household fell entirely on her shoulders.

Back then, no major family event could happen without my mother’s
needlework. When the Zhang family’s daughter gave birth, my maternal
grandmother had to prepare the traditional postpartum gifts—bringing
pork, an old hen, and red-dyed eggs, along with stacks of baby clothes
and cloth diapers. When the Wang family married off a daughter, full
sets of wedding attire had to be sewn for the bride and groom. When the
Zhao family got engaged, new outfits were needed for the bride-to-be.
Bolts of colorful homespun cloth and Dacron piled high on the cutting
table in our main hall, and my mother’s Butterfly-brand sewing machine
clattered da-da-da from dawn till dusk.

There was simply too much work for one person, so my mother enlisted the
help of my grandmother, who was equally skilled with a needle.
Naturally, the job of running errands fell to ten-year-old me.

Grandmother’s house wasn’t far—taking the mountain shortcut, the round
trip was less than ten li (about three miles). Sometimes I went every
few days; during peak seasons, I had to make the trip twice a day. One
day I would carry a bundle of cut fabric pieces to Grandmother’s to have
buttonholes stitched, frog buttons sewn on, and delicate fabric-scrap
appliqués added. The next morning, I would fetch them back. The day
after that, I would haul a bundle of freshly carded cotton batting and
fabric for Grandmother to stuff and quilt by hand. Two days later, I
would retrieve the evenly padded, neatly quilted panels, ready for my
mother to stitch together on the machine into warm winter jackets or
vests.

Having grown up running wild in the hills, I was nimble on my feet.
Every time I set out, I bolted like a frisky colt down the road behind
our house, dashed like the wind down the hillside, and hopped like a
little monkey across the stepping stones in the creek at the bottom of
the valley.

Yet the moment I crossed the water and reached the edge of a tiny hamlet
of just seven or eight households, all my carefree energy vanished
instantly. I would slow to a crawl, creeping forward along the edge of
the path like a cautious ant.

Running right alongside the hamlet was a steep, narrow stone path—the
only route to my grandmother’s house.

That path was deeply unsettling. Hemmed in by steep slopes on both
sides, it rarely saw direct sunlight. Thick, slick moss carpeted the
cobblestones, making every step treacherous. Even more chilling were the
massive, ancient trees growing halfway up the slope.

The adults called them the Xiegong Trees—the Evil Spirit Trees.

No one knew how old those trees were. Their trunks were so thick that
three or four grown men holding hands couldn’t encircle one. Their
pitch-black bark was deeply fissured like an old man’s face, wrapped
tightly in wrist-thick green vines that coiled around the trunks like
giant pythons, trailing tendrils that swayed eerily in the cold mountain
draft. Every time I passed them, I held my breath, stared fixedly at the
stones beneath my feet, and hurried past without daring to flick even a
glance sideways. I only knew they were monstrously tall; once, after
sprinting breathlessly to the top of the slope, I turned back to sneak a
peek from higher ground. Following the trunks upward, the dense canopy
blotted out the sky—I couldn’t even see the treetops.

During cool summer nights, village elders loved to lower their voices
and recount the taboos surrounding the Evil Trees. Years ago, a
mischievous boy had climbed the slope to raid a bird’s nest and
accidentally hugged one of the trunks. That very night, he came down
with a violent fever, raved incoherently, and died within three days.
Another time, a traveling peddler rested at the base of the trees and
muttered a curse about the foul weather; just steps down the slope, he
lost his footing on flat ground and snapped his neck. Adults constantly
warned us children: when passing the Evil Trees, never look up, never
speak loudly, and never spit or relieve yourself near them.

Passing by in broad daylight was nerve-wracking enough; at dusk, when
twilight bled into darkness, the narrow stone path felt like the open
maw of a beast.

One autumn evening, I was delayed at my grandmother’s house while
waiting for a rush order of wedding garments. By the time I reached the
edge of the small hamlet clutching my cloth bundle, the sun had already
sunk behind the mountain ridge. Only a bruised purple smear of sunset
lingered on the horizon, and a thin gray mist was rising from the
hollow.

Panic tightening in my chest, I hugged the bundle close and hurried up
the slope with my head down. Halfway up, just a dozen paces from the
Evil Trees, I caught sight of a hunched figure ahead of me out of the
corner of my eye.

My heart jumped. Looking up, I saw an elderly woman in a black cloth
tunic, her hair twisted into a bun, shuffling slowly up the path with
her back turned. In the countryside, seeing a neighbor wasn’t unusual. I
initially assumed it was an elder from the hamlet and opened my mouth to
call out, “Granny—”

The word died in my throat. The old woman made no sound at all. Her feet
didn’t even rustle against the slick moss.

Every hair on my body stood on end. I stared transfixed at the
retreating figure. When the old woman reached the largest of the Evil
Trees, she angled her body slightly toward the trunk—and melted straight
into the bark like a drop of water sinking into dry earth. In the blink
of an eye, she was gone.

The path was completely empty, save for the dry shhh-shhh of the wind
brushing through the hanging vines.

My mind went entirely blank. Too terrified even to scream, I bolted up
the slope like a madwoman, running over two li without stopping until I
spotted the cooking smoke rising from our own roof. I burst into the
courtyard and clung to my mother’s legs, trembling uncontrollably.

From that day on, even long after I had cleared the Evil Trees, I
refused to speak a single word to anyone I passed on the mountain trail,
even familiar faces. I simply kept my head down and walked on in
silence.

Yet strange things in the mountains seemed drawn to those who feared
them most.

About half a month later, dusk caught me on the road once again. I was
hurrying home with a bundle of quilted cotton panels from my
grandmother’s. The weather was stiflingly humid, and darkness fell fast
once the sun dipped below the peaks. I jogged briskly, finally clearing
the mossy slope and leaving the Evil Trees behind. Just as I was
breathing a quiet sigh of relief, I rounded a bend in the trail—and
heard voices ahead.

I froze, peering cautiously through the gloom. Sitting on a flat boulder
by the roadside were two middle-aged women. Both wore faded blue cloth
tunics with their hair neatly pinned up, two bamboo baskets covered
tightly with blue patterned cloth resting at their feet.

Running into fellow villagers on the mountain roads was common enough.
Though I remembered my rule against speaking to strangers, seeing the
two women chatting and laughing pleasantly eased the tightness in my
chest.

Drawing closer, I saw that the round-faced woman held a handful of
roasted peanuts in the shell, while the tall, thin woman held a piece of
oiled paper filled with steamed rice cakes. They were shelling peanuts,
nibbling the cakes, and murmuring cheerfully to one another.

Eyes fixed straight ahead, I lowered my gaze and tried to hurry past
them.

Just as I drew level, the round-faced woman turned her head, smiled, and
beckoned to me. Her voice was soft and coaxing: “Little girl, heading
home? Come here, Auntie has freshly roasted peanuts and sweet rice
cakes. Take a few to tide you over.”

The rich, nutty aroma of roasted peanuts seemed to drift over on the
evening breeze. For a ten-year-old in lean times, treats like that were
rare luxuries. My steps faltered involuntarily, my eyes flicking toward
the white rice cakes resting on the oiled paper.

The moment I looked up, however, I noticed something wrong. Though the
corners of the women’s mouths were curved in smiles, their eyes didn’t
blink—showing far too much white around tiny, dark pupils that gleamed
coldly in the fading light. Worse still, the peanut shells they dropped
onto the dirt had no color at all; they lay pale and ashen, like burnt
paper money.

A chill shot straight down my spine. Remembering my mother’s warnings, I
snapped my head down, clutched the quilted panels to my chest, and
sprinted away without even muttering a refusal. Behind me, I thought I
heard two faint, hollow sighs. I didn’t dare look back, running all the
way home.

Inside the house, the lamps were brightly lit, and my mother was
threading a needle at the table. Still gasping for breath, I blurted out
the encounter as an oddity—telling her about the two women eating
peanuts and rice cakes on the mountain trail who had offered me some.

Clatter. The sewing needle dropped from my mother’s fingers onto the
tabletop.

I looked up in alarm. My usually composed mother had gone dead white,
her lips trembling. She seized me by the shoulders with a grip that
hurt, her voice tight with urgency: “Tell Mama the truth. Did you take
anything from them? Did you eat even one peanut, or a single bite of
cake?”

Frightened by her reaction, I shook my head frantically. “No! I didn’t
dare look at them. I kept my head down and ran straight home!”

“You’re sure you didn’t eat anything? Didn’t even touch them?” she
pressed, scrutinizing my mouth and fingertips.

I nodded vigorously, holding out both hands for inspection. Only after
confirming I hadn’t touched a crumb did my mother close her eyes, let
out a long, shaky breath, and slump back into her chair as if drained of
all strength.

That night, unusually, she didn’t go straight back to the sewing
machine. Instead, she hurried into the kitchen, sliced up a generous
platter of cured pork normally saved for holidays, went to the village
shop for a jug of strong sorghum liquor, and even unlocked a wooden
chest to bring out stashed bags of fruit candies and biscuits. She then
went next door and invited several of my older male cousins over for
dinner and drinks.

The main hall grew loud and lively. The young men drank liquor, ate
cured meat, and laughed boisterously, driving every trace of chill from
the house. They stayed late into the night. Lying in bed in the inner
room, drifting toward sleep, I could still hear the steady da-da-da of
the sewing machine in the outer room, accompanied by my mother’s voice
chatting loudly with the older cousin who had stayed behind to keep
watch until dawn.

Not long after that incident, I woke in the middle of the night for a
drink of water. Passing my mother’s bedroom door, I heard the muffled
voices of my mother and grandmother inside—Grandmother was staying over
for a few days to help finish an urgent batch of clothes.

Pressing close to the crack in the door, I heard my mother still
murmuring anxiously about that evening: “Ma, how could those two things
dare to sit right out in the open by the road like that?”

Grandmother let out a heavy, weathered sigh. “That stretch of mountain
path is over a li from the nearest hamlet, right beside the Evil Trees.
In the countryside, the moment the sun goes down, every household is run
off its feet—bringing in laundry, feeding pigs, penning chickens and
ducks, lighting the stove for dinner, bathing the children. What village
woman has the spare time to hike all the way into the deserted hills
just to eat peanuts and gossip? And even if travelers from another
village were passing through after visiting relatives, seeing night
fall, they’d be practically flying home. Who on earth would stop to
snack right next to the Evil Trees? Those were clearly spirits from the
trees taking human shape, looking for a substitute on the road. Thank
goodness the child didn’t give in to greed—if she’d taken even one
peanut and eaten it, her soul would have been pulled straight into those
trees on the spot…”

Standing outside the door, I went ice-cold from head to toe. Forgetting
my thirst, I tiptoed back to bed, pulled the quilt tightly over my head,
and lay awake until morning.

As I grew older, our family bought a bicycle, and my mother gradually
began subcontracting work to other seamstresses in town. I rarely had
reason to walk that narrow stone mountain path again.

Two years later, in the dead of winter, word reached our village about
the small hamlet beside the Evil Trees. Four or five people from two
families—both adults and children—had suddenly fallen gravely ill at the
exact same time. It began as a low fever and crushing fatigue. Taken to
the county hospital, X-rays and blood tests revealed nothing wrong, yet
injections and medicine had no effect. Day by day, they wasted away to
skin and bone, their eyes vacant, drifting in and out of stupors.

Seeing them on the brink of death, the hamlet’s elders pooled their
money and hiked across the mountains through the night to fetch a
renowned spirit medium from a neighboring county.

When the medium arrived, she didn’t even step inside the sickrooms.
Standing at the entrance of the hamlet, she took one look up the slope
at the towering canopy of the Evil Trees, and her expression darkened.
She set up an incense altar and performed rituals through half the night
before finally packing up her instruments and shaking her head at the
families’ elders. There was no saving them.

Days earlier, men from those two households had been gathering firewood
on the slope. Annoyed by the thick green vines hanging down from the
Evil Trees and blocking their path, they had hacked several down with
their machetes, cursing foully as they swung. Afterward, they neither
offered incense and spirit money to apologize nor left the vines
alone—they dragged them home and burned them in their stoves.

Before departing, the medium left behind a single pronouncement: “The
tree spirits are furious. Their vital essence has already been drained
into the trunks. Prepare their funerals—the trees are taking them.”

Sure enough, within half a month, those adults and children died one
after another. On the day of the burial, as the funeral procession filed
past the moss-covered stone path on the slope, the sky was clear and the
air completely still—yet the thick green vines hanging from the Evil
Trees suddenly began to thrash violently, their leaves rustling in a
dry, rhythmic cadence, as if something hidden in the shadows were
laughing in triumph.

After that, the remaining families in the hamlet gradually packed up and
moved away. The narrow stone path leading to my grandmother’s house was
abandoned entirely, swallowed whole by wild weeds and thick green moss.
Yet whenever I pass the foot of the mountain and catch a distant glimpse
of those towering treetops piercing the sky, I can almost smell,
lingering on the twilight air, the eerie aroma of freshly roasted
peanuts.
