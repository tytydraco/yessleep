I wake up naked in the unknown.

The darkness is so absolute it feels solid. It isn’t the absence of
light. It’s a substance. Something sticky, disgusting, clinging to me.

I lift my hands to my face.

I can’t see them.

I press my fingers against my eyelids just to make sure I still have
them.

I breathe.

The air goes in warm and comes out cold.

I scream.

My voice is swallowed by the darkness. It dies inside my mouth, as if
the darkness chews it before it can be born.

Never, in my entire life, has the void had weight.

Now it does.

I stand up.

I search for something beyond myself.

I stretch out my arms.

A wall.

I turn.

Another.

One more.

Four walls.

I run my palms, my fingers, my nails over every inch.

No door.

No window.

No crack.

No fissure.

The concrete is smooth and continuous, as if it were poured all at once
around my body.

The ceiling is where it should be. The floor, cold and rough, beneath my
bare feet.

Four walls, a ceiling, a floor.

A cube.

Only me.

I think.

I start walking with my hands pressed against the walls. I need to find
anything. A rusty nail. A bump. An irregularity. Any detail that proves
this place belongs to the same erratic reality as the rest of the world.

I find nothing.

My nails scrape the wall until they bend backward.

I keep going.

My fingers start to bleed.

I keep going.

Pain is a relief.

As long as it hurts, something besides fear still exists.

I bang my head against the wall.

Again.

Harder.

The concrete wins.

I always lose.

I don’t know how much time passes.

Minutes.

Days.

Years.

In the dark, time rots.

I scream again, louder, until my throat scrapes like sandpaper. My voice
doesn’t echo. The room gives nothing back. It’s like screaming inside a
corpse.

The silence that follows isn’t empty. It’s full. Full of me.

I hear my own breathing. My heartbeat. The blood rushing through my
veins with a sound that should never be audible, but here, where there
is nothing, everything is noise.

And that noise, over time, becomes unbearable.

I start talking to myself.

Then I discover it’s not me who answers.

I stop speaking.

The questions keep coming.

I pray.

I curse God.

I beg for forgiveness.

I laugh.

I cry.

The tears fall for no reason.

Maybe because the body knows something the mind still refuses to accept.

The room doesn’t change. It never changes. This is the only certainty I
have, and it crushes me like an invisible press.

Every time I stretch out my arms, the walls are exactly where they were
before. The distance between them doesn’t vary by a millimeter. The
texture of the concrete is identical at every point. Even the smell—a
neutral, dry, mineral odor—remains the same.

It’s the changelessness that sickens.

If the room were shrinking, I would be afraid.

If it were expanding, I would have hope.

But it does nothing.

It simply is.

And I, inside it, am gradually ceasing to be.

One day I realize I have stopped walking. I don’t know when it happened.
My body simply decided there was no longer any reason. Standing,
sitting, lying down—all positions are the same. All directions are the
same. The room is the same in every place and at every moment.

So why move?

I stay still.

The silence grows.

Inside it, I hear things.

Footsteps. Not mine, because I’m not moving. But footsteps—clear, slow,
circular—around the room.

I search with my hands.

There’s no one.

The sound stops.

Then it comes back.

Then it stops.

Then it comes back.

Is the room playing with me? No. The room doesn’t play. The room isn’t
alive. The room is only a room, and that’s exactly what makes the
situation so obscene.

The sound is inside me.

I know that.

But even knowing, I can’t stop hearing it.

And, with time, I don’t want to.

Because the footsteps are company. The footsteps are something.

One day, I try to imitate the sound with my mouth. I try to reproduce
the rhythm of the steps by clicking my tongue against the roof of my
mouth.

It works.

Now there are two of us making noise: me… and the footsteps.

We talk like this—me and myself—for a very long time.

I begin to feel certain that I’ve been here before.

Not in this room, but in this situation. This darkness. This stillness.

Maybe inside my mother. Before birth. When I was just a body inside
another body, floating in the dark, listening to muffled sounds from
outside.

But here there is no outside.

Here there is no other body.

Here there is only the room.

And me.

If I am still an “I.”

I touch my face.

I don’t feel anything familiar. Are my cheekbones deeper? My nose,
thinner? My mouth, wider?

I don’t know.

I don’t remember my face.

I try to recall the exact shape of my eyes, the curve of my lips, the
slope of my forehead.

The image doesn’t come.

Only the room comes.

Four walls, a ceiling, a floor.

Me.

Now I’m part of the inventory.

Maybe I always was.

Maybe I never entered this place. Maybe I was born here, sprouting from
the concrete like a nail growing from a buried corpse, without purpose,
without direction, only growing.

I try to remember the outside world.

The sun. The rain. Someone’s face.

But every memory arrives stained black, as if the room had licked each
image before returning it.

My mother’s face now has four walls.

My father is a low ceiling.

My friends are the floor.

I don’t know if I’m crying. My eyes are wet, but maybe it’s just the
natural moisture of a body that refuses to dry completely.

The room doesn’t care.

The room never cared.

The room isn’t torturing me. Torture requires intention, and the room
has no intention at all. It simply is.

I’m the one who insists on existing inside it, like an inflammation that
persists in throbbing.

I try to inflict pain.

Pain is the last thread that binds me to myself.

I sink my teeth into my arm.

The skin gives way.

The blood tastes of iron, of salt, of life.

I lick the wound.

The room doesn’t react.

Of course not.

I chew the flesh of my forearm until I tear off a piece. The pain is
immense. My body screams at me. My mind, for an instant, remembers who I
am.

A naked man in a dark cube.

A caged animal.

A thing that feels pain.

Then the pain passes.

And I return to being just a presence in the dark, without a past,
without a face, without a name.

Do words still exist?

I try to say my name out loud.

The sound that comes out is not a word. It’s a guttural noise, a croak
of a blind amphibian.

I try again.

Worse.

The room is sucking away my language.

Or maybe I’m forgetting it because language is useless here.

There is no one to talk to.

There is nothing to describe.

Four walls. A ceiling. A floor.

What good are adjectives? What good is the verb “to be,” if everything
that exists here simply is, without variation, without nuance, without
end?

I stop thinking in words.

They go away like leaves down a drain.

Now there are only shapes, textures, temperatures.

The floor is cold.

The walls are rough.

The air is thick.

I am warm.

For now.

The heat of my body is the only thing that separates me from the room.

I press my palm against the concrete.

It’s cold.

My hand is warm.

That temperature difference is the only dialogue I still know how to
hold.

I stay like this for centuries. My hand against the wall. Heat against
cold. The organic against the mineral.

Until one day—or night, or the space between two heartbeats—I realize my
hand is as cold as the wall.

I feel no difference anymore.

I take my hand away.

I touch my chest.

Cold.

I touch my face.

Cold.

I touch my stomach, my thighs, my feet.

All cold.

All the same as the room.

The room has won.

Or it has accepted me.

It’s the same thing.

I lie down on the floor. The position is identical to all the others,
but lying down feels like the final gesture, the surrender.

I close my eyes—though it makes no difference, because the dark behind
my eyelids is identical to the dark of the room.

Four walls, a ceiling, a floor.

Me.

Now I am also room.

I no longer think. Ideas are movement, and there is no movement here.

I am just an immobile mass in an immobile chamber, waiting for eternity
to end.

But it never ends.

Eternity never ends.

And the room, which never changed, continues not changing.

And I, who was once a man, who once screamed, who once bled, who once
had a name and a face and memories, am now perfectly still, perfectly
cold, perfectly integrated into the four walls, the ceiling, the floor.

The room is no longer full.

The room was never full.

It was always just me and myself.

And now not even that.

[Later]

They found me unconscious.

Naked.

Inside the ruins of an abandoned building.

They asked me my name.

I couldn’t remember it.

It took me months to learn how to live again.

Today I can work.

I can hold conversations.

I can pretend I’m normal.

But there’s a problem.

Every time I close my eyes…

I’m back in that room.

I don’t dream about it.

I return to it.

Sometimes for a few seconds.

Sometimes for hours.

I’m afraid to fall asleep.

Because one day…

I know I’ll wake up there again.

And next time…

I may never leave that room.
