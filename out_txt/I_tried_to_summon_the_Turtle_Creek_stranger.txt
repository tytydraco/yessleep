Every small town in the world has their own version of an urban legend
that has weaved itself into the founding history of the area. Our
particular relic is called the Turtle Creek stranger, but it has several
names depending on who you ask. I’ve heard it been called the rain
walker, the Turtle Creek cryptid, the broken man, or my personal
favorite the train tracker (pun intended).

The authenticity of what exactly the creature is, and whether it even
exists in the first place, is dubious at best. The only real proof that
I’ve ever found throughout my years of “research” include childhood
stories and anecdotal evidence provided by several of the locals around
the area. But, in order to understand what the Turtle Creek stranger is,
and how to potentially summon him, I must first describe the location in
which this legend is set. 

Located near the outskirts of my hometown is a large wooded area that is
about three miles thick. There is a large stream of water that cuts
through this wooded area called Turtle Creek. A train track was built
alongside this creek during the early 1900s after a brick factory was
constructed within the three mile stretch of trees. 

This particular factory was able to produce millions of bricks
throughout its operation, and it would use the train as its main way of
transferring freight. During the late 1970s, the brickyard was
eventually shut down, and the buildings that made up the facility were
left to rot. Not too long after that, the railroad that ran alongside
the creek was also closed off.

Whenever I was younger, my friends and I would venture out into those
woods during summer break and walk along the tracks, skipping rocks
across the stream’s surface as we went. The location of the brick
factory was still visible if you knew where to look, but only a few
pieces of the foundation remained after decades of abandonment.

Growing up in that area, every kid knew about the forgotten trainline
that snaked its way through the Turtle Creek woods. It was an eerie part
of our town, but it also had history which made it the perfect
birthplace for our very own local cryptid.

The legend behind the Turtle Creek stranger dates all the way back to
the 1920s when both the brick factory and railroad were still in
operation. As the story goes, a man was walking down the tracks one
night during a terrible storm. Visibility was limited, and as he was
making his way out of the woods he was hit by the train. He didn’t die
though. 

Not right away, at least.

Despite how broken his body must have been, the man still managed to
make it all the way out of the woods somehow. It was late into the
evening at this point, but he saw that several of the nearby homes still
had their lights on. 

The man approached the closest house, calling out for help as he did. A
woman answered the door and immediately cowered away in fear as soon as
she saw the man’s disfigured frame. He was soaking wet, which caused the
blood from his many open wounds to leave long streaks of crimson all
over his body.

Broken bones had pierced the skin at several locations along his arms
and legs, causing them to bend at awkward angles as they supported his
weight. All ten of his fingers were pointing in different directions,
and the top half of his skull was caved in like a rotting
jack-o’-lantern. 

He begged the woman for assistance, but she slammed the door in his
face, believing him to be some sort of monster. The man approached no
less than six houses that night, but not a single person chose to help
him. He succumbed to his wounds shortly thereafter.

I hate thinking about how much pain the man must have been in during his
final moments. Not just physical pain either, but the pain of knowing
that people wouldn’t help him because they were scared of his
appearance.

He may not have been a monster when he died, but he certainly became one
after that night.

Despite how well known the legend of the Turtle Creek stranger is, many
of the locals can’t seem to agree on who the original man was. Some
people say that he was one of the masonry workers at the brick factory.
Others have said that he was actually a train conductor. I’ve even heard
the claim that he was just some unlucky fisherman that was walking too
close to the tracks. 

There have been several sightings of the stranger over the years, but
besides the bent limbs and broken face, nobody can seem to agree on an
exact description. It’s almost as if his appearance changes from person
to person.

I’ve lived in this town for over 25 years, and even though I don’t
believe in the legitimacy of our little urban legend, I’ve always wanted
to try and see the Turtle Creek stranger for myself. However, unlike
other types of cryptids, you don’t just randomly stumble across the
stranger in the woods somewhere. He has to be summoned.

Luckily for me though, the summoning ritual is well known and relatively
simple.

There are two entrances to the Turtle Creek woods. One of them is
located next to a public park, and the other one opens up into a small
neighborhood.

To begin the ritual, you must enter the woods from whichever entrance
you prefer. You have to be alone, and like many other urban legends it
has to take place at night. An exact time has never really been
specified, but the general rule of thumb is to enter as close to
midnight as you can. The third, and seemingly most important step, is
that it needs to be raining.

Again, there is no specification on how much rain is needed, but it is
believed that the harder the down pour, the higher your chances are of
summoning the stranger. It is recommended to bring an umbrella with you
as well, not only for the rain, but also because of how useful it can be
later on in the ritual. More on that later though.

Once all three of these requirements are met, you can begin your walk
down the train tracks. You must continue walking until you reach a
bridge which is where the rest of the summoning ritual will take place.

That specific location is significant to the Turtle Creek stranger for a
number of reasons. For starters, the bridge is where the original man
was hit by the train all those years ago. His body was thrown from the
tracks with such force that he landed in the water below and had to
crawl out of the creek before he could make his way toward civilization.

Not only that, but the bridge also happens to be the exact half way
point between both of the entrances to the woods. So once you reach that
spot, it doesn’t matter which direction you pick to leave, it will take
you the same amount of time either way.

To finish off the ritual, there is one final step. You have to walk over
the bridge a total of six times, and on your sixth pass, you must stop
half way across and then throw a rock into the creek below you.

If you hear a second splash hit the water after you’ve thrown the rock,
then you know that the Turtle Creek stranger has been summoned.

Once the ritual has been completed, you can start to head back the way
you came, which is when the real challenge begins.

If the stranger has been properly summoned, then he will immediately
begin to follow you as you make your way out of the woods. Once he
begins to track you, there are two very important rules that you must
obey. Stay on the tracks at all times, and never, under any
circumstances, can you look directly at him.

That is the second reason why an umbrella is commonly recommended. You
can lower it enough to where the only thing that is visible to you is
the ground directly below your feet.

If you can stick to the track and reach the end of the woods while
keeping your head down then you win, and your prize is that you get to
continue on living. However, if you veer off the track at any point, or
happen to look at the man when you aren’t supposed to, then it is said
you will meet a fate similar to that of the stranger.

All of these thoughts and more were going through my head as I parked my
car on the road closest to the wooded entrance. When I had left my house
earlier that evening a small trickle of rain was starting to come down
from the night sky. During the drive, that small trickle slowly began to
increase into a steady stream, and by the time I made it to my
destination the rain was starting to come down in thick waves.

The clock rolled over to midnight, as I made my way over toward the
Turtle Creek trail, umbrella in hand. The train tracks that had once
occupied the space alongside the creek were torn up around the beginning
of 2018, and the entire three mile path was turned into a hiking trail
not long after. 

Urban legends are legends for a reason, but I had no idea if the
renovations to the railroad would in any way negate the summoning
ritual. Undeterred, I decided to give it a try anyways and began the
mile and a half walk toward the center of the trail.

I honestly don’t have a concrete reason as to why I was so dead set on
trying to prove that the Turtle Creek stranger was just some made up
ghost story. All I know is that throughout my entire life I’ve always
been fascinated by the idea of supernatural entities existing in our
otherwise boring and mundane world. 

Whenever I first heard about the legend of Bloody Mary, I was standing
in front of my bathroom mirror later that night chanting her name. A
bought a spirit board during my freshman year in college and played with
it by myself in the middle of a graveyard, just to see if it worked.

There was never a reward associated with these rituals. It was just
pure, unadulterated curiosity. I needed to uncover the truth first hand
and figure out whether there was any amount of authenticity to the
myths. Looking back at everything though, I also think that a small part
of me has always wanted to be proven wrong.

It was currently pitch black out, and the normal night time sounds were
being covered up by the cacophony of falling rain. The heavy drumming
against my umbrella was providing me with just enough white noise to
distract myself from the fact that I was currently walking through the
middle of the woods on my way to summon a 100 year old cryptid.

Flashlights weren’t allowed, and the gray curtain of rain was blocking
any illumination that may have been coming off of the moon. Luckily for
me though, the Turtle Creek trail was well made with wide paths and
packed gravel filling the entire walkway. So there was no chance that I
would get lost.

I’ve also walked this particular trail many times since it was
renovated, and even without the guiding light of daytime, I was still
confident about where I was headed. The crunching of gravel beneath my
feet was reassuring to hear as I made my way to the location of the
bridge.

From where I entered the woods, the creek started off to my left. The
rushing of the water was mixing in seamlessly with the sound of the
rain, and after about thirty minutes of walking, the stream took a sharp
bend to the right, and disappeared underneath the path as the metallic
railings of the bridge came into view.

Without breaking my stride, I made my way across the overpass in just a
few seconds. As soon as I hit the other side of the bridge, I turned
around and walked back. I repeated this step until I had passed over the
creek a total of six times. 

On my sixth and final crossing, I grabbed a small rock that was resting
near the trail and threw it into the river below me when I was about
halfway over the bridge. The rock landed in the water with a satisfying
plop, and I paused for a few seconds, waiting for the sound of a second
splash.

I strained my ears against the white noise of falling rain and rushing
water, but no other sound ever came. I realized how tightly I had been
holding the umbrella, and chuckled to myself a little bit, slightly
embarrassed at how tense I had become waiting for the splash.

“I knew it,” I said to myself as I turned around and started to head
back the way that I came.

That’s when I heard it. 

I whipped around to face the bridge that I had just left and paused, yet
again. I couldn’t tell if what I had heard was the sound of the stream
rushing past me, or if it was something else entirely. Something that I
knew wasn’t possible.

I glanced down towards the water’s edge and saw the ripple of waves
beginning to dissipate out over the surface of the stream, as if
something heavy had just fallen into the water.

Without a moment’s hesitation I turned around and briskly began to walk
back toward the exit of the Turtle Creek woods. I wanted to take off in
a full tilt sprint back to my car, but if the stranger was in fact
following me, which he wasn’t, then I knew that running was not a good
idea. 

According to the legend, the stranger always matches your pace. If you
walk slowly and take your time he will keep his distance, but as soon as
you speed up, he speeds up too.

I pushed those thoughts to the back of my mind.

“Nobody’s following you,” I told myself, and as if to prove my point I
turned around and peered down the trail behind me.

It was empty, so I continued walking.

Any number of things could have fallen into the water to make those
waves. A branch from one of the trees. A loose piece of the bridge’s
foundation. The timing of it all was definitely unnerving, but it was
just a coincidence. Nothing more. 

I walked for five more minutes before checking behind me again. The path
was still empty. Just me and the rain.

I continued walking.

The crunching of gravel beneath my feet was soothing to hear. The
constant rhythm reminded me that all I had to do was keep moving
forward. I walked for another five minutes and checked behind me.

Still empty.

I continued walking. 

Five more minutes passed and I checked again.

Nothing.

At this point, I had less than a mile to go before I was out of the
woods, and I scolded myself for getting so worked up back on the bridge.

I continued walking.

I looked down at my feet as I went, and even though I was still holding
the umbrella, my shoes were soaking wet from the rain. I paused and bent
down to adjust the bottom of my pants.

Then I heard it.

Crunch. Crunch. Crunch.

I shot up. 

Footsteps. I had heard someone else’s footsteps from behind me.

I didn’t turn around this time.

I continued walking, and so did they.

Crunch. Crunch. Crunch. 

I immediately stopped.

The footsteps stopped too. 

“There’s no fucking way,” I told myself. But was there ever any doubt? 

I started walking again. From behind me I could hear the footsteps
approaching me.

Crunch. Crunch. Crunch.

They were much closer this time.

I increased my speed.

So did they.

I gripped my umbrella tighter and pulled it down as low as I could,
causing my peripheral vision to completely disappear.

I had no idea how much further I needed to go, and the crunching of
footsteps from behind me was getting faster.

Crunch.Crunch.Crunch.

It was the stranger, and it sounded like he was right behind me, almost
on top of me.

I looked down at my feet and could just make out the top of his shadow
as he approached. He walked with a lopsided, jaunty stride. Far too
springy and inconsistent. 

I remembered the description of the stranger. His broken bones
protruding from several different spots along his body. His bent limbs
and caved in forehead. Against all reason and logic, I wanted to turn
around and see it for myself. Was it really him?

As if in response to my question, his footsteps picked up their pace and
the shadow that was behind me was now making its way around to my left.

My umbrella was still obscuring my peripheral vision, so the only thing
that I could see was the immediate ground around me. As the stranger
began to pass me on my left, I could make out the lower half of his
body, but that’s all I needed to see.

The ankle of his left leg was at a 90 degree angle. It was swollen and
purple, and every time he put his weight on it the ankle bone would
bulge out against his discolored skin, threatening to explode at any
moment.

His right leg had been completely shattered. The broken tibia had
pierced through the front of his shin causing the splintered bone to pop
in and out of his flesh every time he took a step. Neither injury seemed
to be slowing him down though.

Maybe he was a monster after all.

But his broken body was not what caught my attention. It was his
clothes, or more specifically his shoes.

He was wearing the exact same shoes that I was wearing. They were much
dirtier than mine, but they were definitely the same type.

That’s when it hit me. A realization so sudden and precise that I’m
surprised I hadn’t put the pieces together earlier.

I now understood why nobody could ever seem to agree on what the Turtle
Creek stranger looked like throughout the years. Why he seemed to change
appearances depending on who saw him. He wasn’t just some ancient brick
worker that was hit by the train 100 years ago. 

The stranger wasn’t a stranger at all.

He was me.

He was an omen. A deadly premonition of what would happen to me if I
broke the rules.

I don’t know why, but it was at that moment that I felt this strange,
indescribable urge to throw down my umbrella and look the stranger dead
in the eyes so that I could finally embrace my fate.

I tried to fight off this urge with all my might and continued walking.

Crunch. Crunch. Crunch.

I could feel myself starting to raise the umbrella, unveiling more of
the stranger’s body to me as I did. I had made it all the way up to his
twisted midsection when, all at once, the stranger stopped walking. 

He quickly fell back behind me and I noticed that the gravel beneath my
feet had turned into the paved cement of a roadway.

I looked up and saw that I had made it all the way back to where I had
originally entered the woods from. I continued forward and saw my car
parked exactly where I had left it. As I exited the woods, I could feel
that the rain was starting to let up a little bit.

I wanted to look back, to confirm that it was actually over, but part of
me knew that the stranger was still standing there at the edge of the
woods. Waiting for me to turn around.

So I stood there for a few seconds longer, and then I heard the familiar
crunching of those lopsided footsteps retreating back into the Turtle
Creek woods.

I walked over to my car in a complete daze and got in. I turned the car
on and made my way back into town. By the time I got home it was no
longer raining.
