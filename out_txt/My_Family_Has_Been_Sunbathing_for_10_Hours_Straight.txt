“Today we have a somewhat historic weather report for you all. It seems
summer wanted one last goodbye before the start of the cold season. Tell
us about it, Jason.”

“That’s right, folks, 85 degrees,” he chuckled. “85 degrees Fahrenheit,
no wind, and a cloudless, sunny sky is the weather here today in Ely.
This is a historic high for November in Minnesota, so put on your
bathing suits and get outside to soak in the sun while you can. This is
a real treat from Mother Nature. My name is Jason Sweatman with
Northwood News 8, reporting live that the sun feels AMAZING.”

The weatherman stretched out his arms and looked up at the sun, smiling
widely, before they cut back to the anchors and finished the rest of
their segment. I picked up the remote and switched to Cartoon Network.

“That explains why I woke up so hot last night,” my sister said, not
looking up from her phone.

Dad chortled and pointed through our sliding glass door to the backyard.
“Bowser sure seems to be enjoying the weather.”

Sure enough, the dog was rolling around in the grass like he always did
at the start of spring when the snow finally melted.

My mom finished washing the dishes, patted her hands dry, and said,
“Well, I am going to get my book and some sunscreen and join Mr. Bowser
out there.”

She kissed my head as she walked past and went up the stairs.

“Me too,” my sister said, getting up from the table and slapping me on
the back of my neck as she followed Mom.

After a minute my Dad put down his newspaper and said, “Well, I think I
ought to join the ladies, bud. You going to be okay in here by
yourself?”

I rubbed my stinging neck and said, “Yeah, I will just play some video
games or something.”

There is this condition called Xeroderma Pigmentosum where you really
can’t be exposed to sunlight for long at all because your skin will
begin to blister and burn. I have that condition, so a day like this
basically meant I was confined indoors. I didn’t really mind. I am more
of a homebody anyways, plus I had just gotten an Xbox for my birthday
two months ago. The prospect of playing Modern Warfare 2 all day while
my family didn’t bother me was, in my opinion, the perfect way to spend
my Saturday.

I called my buddy Hudson to see if he was already on the game.

“Nah, man,” he said. “We all went to Pineridge Park. There are, like,
hundreds of people here. I wish you could see it. The sun feels
amazing.”

I told him to let me know when he got on the game later. We had been
playing video games every Saturday night since I got my Xbox, so I knew
he would be.

After a few hours of grinding team deathmatch, I got hungry and went
downstairs to see what Mom made for lunch. To my surprise, no one was in
the kitchen or living room. I looked out the window to the backyard, and
sure enough, there they were. All three of them were sitting in the lawn
chairs facing the sun. Bowser was lying on his back next to them,
panting lightly under the heat.

I opened the sliding glass door and called out, “Hey, Mom, are you
coming in to make lunch soon?”

“Uhh, no, honey, but there are leftovers in the fridge from last night,
or you can make yourself a sandwich,” she said without turning around.

“Are you guys not going to eat lunch?” I asked.

“No, we are enjoying the sun,” my sister said as if it were the most
annoying question ever.

“It really does feel amazing out here, bud. Come sit with us for a
little,” Dad said.

Mom reached out and slapped him on the arm. “Oh, you know he can’t do
that, Chuck.”

“Five minutes wouldn’t hurt!”

Whatever, I thought to myself, sliding the glass door shut as they
bickered. I knew I had no frame of reference for how good the sun felt
on your skin, but I struggled to imagine what would make anyone want to
spend more than an hour out sunbathing at a time. I made myself two
PB&Js and went back up to my room.

I played more team deathmatch, checking my phone between each round to
see if Hudson had tried to call me to tell me he was getting on, but I
still had zero notifications. Once my eyes started to hurt from the
strain of staring at my TV, I finally turned my Xbox off, leaned back
into my chair, and checked the time.

“What the fuck?” I said, staring at my clock.

The clock read 7:52 PM. Had I really just played Xbox for five hours
straight? I peeked through my blackout curtains, and sure enough, it was
as sunny as it was when I woke up. I assumed my clock was broken and
went to check the time on my phone. 7:52 PM.

I stepped out of my room and called out to the house but got no answer.
I went downstairs to the kitchen and looked at the time on the oven. It
said 7:53 PM. It didn’t make sense. It couldn’t possibly be that late;
it was still so bright outside.

Looking out the sliding glass window, my heart sank a little; they were
all still out there. In fact, from what I could tell, they hadn’t moved
an inch since I last came down for lunch. I slid open the door, and to
my surprise, all three of them were in a fit of giggles as if one of
them had just told the funniest joke of all time.

I called out to them, “Have you all been out here the whole time?”

My sister burst out laughing, and my dad turned around to look at me.

“There he is!” He started laughing too, but what I was seeing was far
from amusing. His face was deep red, clearly badly sunburnt.

My mom turned around, smiling ear to ear. “Hey, baby! Come sit with us,
just five minutes. The sun feels amazing!”

She reached out a burnt-red arm in my direction, beckoning me to join
them.

I stared at them in horror. My own mother, who was always so particular
about ensuring I didn’t get exposed to the sun, had just tried to get me
to come outside.
“Jesus fucking Christ! Have you guys not been back inside yet? Have you
been using sunscreen at all?” I said, fear swelling in my chest.

“Woahhh, language!” Dad exclaimed.

“Don’t worry,” my sister said through fits of giggles. “Bowser has been
reapplying it on us.”

All three of them howled in laughter. I was so confused and
uncomfortable. Were they drunk? Stoned?

I looked over at Bowser. He was panting hard now but still splayed out
on the grass exactly where he was ten hours ago. I called for him to
come to me, but he didn’t. He opened his eyes and looked at me, wagging
his tail, but didn’t make any effort to stand up.

I called him again and again, offered him treats, and said all his
favorite words, but nothing moved him. He just looked at me upside down
and wagged his tail. The more I pleaded with Bowser to come inside the
house, the harder my family laughed.

Finally, I became so humiliated, scared, and angry that I boiled over
and yelled at them, “STOP FUCKING LAUGHING AT ME, YOU GUYS ARE BEING
FUCKING WEIRD!” and slammed the sliding glass door shut as hard as I
could, causing the glass to crack.

My sister called out, “LANGUAGE!” in her best impression of my dad’s
voice, before howling in laughter even harder than before.

I ran upstairs, completely forgetting about the fact that it was
supposedly 8 PM but completely sunny outside, that we were supposed to
have already eaten dinner, and that I was supposed to play Xbox with
Hudson any moment now.

I slammed my bedroom door shut, jumped into my bed face-first, and began
to cry. I cried and cried and cried, expecting at any moment that my mom
or dad would come into the room to console me. Eventually, I passed out
from exhaustion, and with tears still in my eyes, I fell asleep.

When I woke up the next morning I immediately felt guilty about my
outburst. I had cursed out my entire family and then slammed and broke
the sliding glass door they had just gotten installed last summer.

I lay in bed staring at my ceiling, contemplating yesterday’s events. I
convinced myself that they were probably just drunk and enjoying the
nice, sunny weather, two things they did every summer anyway and that I
had never experienced. How could I know what that was like, how great it
was? It was probably pretty great as an adult, like their version of
playing Modern Warfare with Hudson.

I was disappointed with myself. They all worked so hard each week, and
the one time they got to unwind with nice weather, I had thrown a fit.
My own fears of the sun and the odd timing of the weather had caused me
to exaggerate the details of yesterday’s events. Of course they had come
back into the house. Of course they had made sure to apply sunscreen (my
mother was a freak about sunscreen given my condition).

The more I thought about it, the more ridiculous and guilty I felt. I
crafted my apology and headed to the kitchen to make it.

No one was there.

The oven clock read 5:36 AM. But the sun was already out, so I had
assumed it was later in the day.

Everyone is still asleep, I told myself.

I went over to the glass door to inspect the damage from yesterday’s
tantrum. Sure enough, there was a big, splintering crack along the pane.

“Damn, goodbye Xbox. Dad is going to kill m—”

I froze.

Outside, sitting motionless in their three chairs
facing the sun, were my mom, dad, and sister. Lying next to them, stiff
as a board, was my dog. In that moment I knew that they never came back
inside the house, I knew that they never would.
