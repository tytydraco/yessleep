By Alistar Rutsac
I’ve never been much of a light sleeper, but lately, the waking world
feels exactly like the sleeping one.
Fharis helps. That’s what I call him. Or rather, what he repeated to me
in my head over and over again until the mental hospital booty juiced
me. Who knew curling up in the fetal position on the ground repeating
“Fharis” over and over again for hours would land you in a padded room.
anyways- He’s been around since I was about three years old, sitting
quietly at the edge of my vision. If you looked at him sideways, you’d
think he was just a trick of the light or a heavy velvet coat draped
over the back of a chair. He’s tall—almost eight feet if he stands all
the way up—but his posture is terrible. He hunches forward over his
digitigrade legs, his incredibly long, needle-thin fingers resting
against his knees.
During the day, Fharis is peaceful. He has two wide, heavy eyes that
watch me with a strange kind of clinical affection. He doesn’t have a
voice—not an actual sound that travels through the air—but his thoughts
spill into my brain like a cold, way too loud, broadcast. He reminds me
to eat, take deep breaths. When the panic attacks hit, he just sits at
the foot of my bed, a solid block of dark, comforting reality, waiting
for the static in my head to clear.
But the shift always starts with a speck.
It’s never a grand entrance. I’ll be doing something completely
ordinary—walking down a sidewalk, clearing off a kitchen counter—and
I’ll spot a tiny black blemish on the floor. It looks like an ink stain
or a dead bug. The first few times i noticed it, i tried to get rid of
it. I tried to scrub it away, cover it up, run away from it- But then it
grows. It opens up, widening until it’s the exact size it needs to be
for me, and only me, to slip. 
Every single time, I fall in.
There is no wind, no rush of gravity. It feels like falling up, an
aggressive pull toward an unyielding ceiling until the world flips
completely. The air is ripped out of my lungs, my skin feels like i’ve
fallen into a field of nettles, my bones feel like they’ve all shattered
in my body. 
When I open my eyes, I’m standing in a long hallway that stretches out
into a horizon of nothingness. The air smells like hot copper and wet
wool. There are three heavy, wood-paneled doors on my left, and a
single, unmarked white door at the very end of the corridor on the
right.
The first two doors on the left are always locked. I’ve thrown my weight
against them, screamed at them, tried to break the door handles, but the
brass knobs don’t even jiggle, and i’m left bruised and tired. The third
door, though, is never latched.
It leads to what i call the TV room.
The floor inside is covered in a neat row of mattresses, and sitting on
them are the people I love most. My fiancé. My mother. My childhood best
friend. my ex’s. my siblings. They look solid at first glance, but their
limbs are cut up, stitched together with heavy black twine that doesn’t
belong in a living body. And where their heads should be, there are
heavy, boxy 1970s analog television sets.
Thick, dusty black power cords spill out of the collars of their shirts,
disappearing down into their neck cavities.
Every screen is alive. They don’t display images—just different textures
of grey and white static. Each one hums with a distinct pitch. When I
open that door, a heavy paralysis creeps across my skin like a million
tiny spiders. I can’t turn around. I can’t close my eyes. i can barely
breathe. The figures slowly look at me, their glass faces humming, and
they step toward me in a synchronized, mechanical manner. They never
touch me. they never get close enough. They just slowly creep towards
the doorway, forcing me to stare directly into the blinding, buzzing
void of their screens until my vision goes white.
The only escape from the static is the door at the end of the hallway. 
The room is small, concrete, and completely bare except for a single
mattress thrown on the floor. Above it, a solitary, exposed lightbulb
dangles from a frayed cord, swaying gently. Heavy iron chains hang from
the ceiling joists, clinking together with a dull, rhythmic metallic
slap.
And then there is the closet.
The door is always slightly ajar. Sometimes, if I look inside, it opens
out into an infinite mountain of bodies. Sometimes it opens directly
into my own bedroom, and I can actually see myself lying frozen under
the sheets, helpless.
Fharis comes out of the closet.
But he isn’t the weird, creepy, guardian figure from before anymore. The
second eye is gone, swallowed by a solid wall of featureless black hide.
His one, wide and hyper-focused eye, staring down at me beneath two
massive, curved ram horns that slice through the dim lightbulb glare.
His mouth split-grins completely around his head, a jagged line of wet
shadow.
When he drags his feet across the concrete toward me, he looks solid,
but as he touches you, you realize he is made of thick, boiling tar. The
black goop transfers to your skin, hot and suffocating, and no matter
how hard you scrape at it, it stays. Burning and staining your skin. His
long, impossible fingers wrap around my arms, pinning me to the
mattress. My body feels heavily sedated, entirely drugged, unable to
fight back against the weight of the shadow.
He doesn’t speak. He just lets out a low, gravelly vibration that
rattles the floorboards, alternating with a high-pitched, ear-splitting
screech that sounds like tearing metal. He strikes me with the rhythmic,
agonizing familiarity of old punishments—the kind of rigid, structural
cruelty meant to break a child’s spirit under the guise of salvation. It
feels ancient. It feels religious. It feels like being possessed by
every piece of scripture that was ever used as a weapon against me in
the church of my youth. The hands of the pastor and his son, the screams
of children being punished, the constant subtle degradation.
I can’t run. I can’t scream. I just have to wait for the tar to swallow
me entirely. i have to allow the pain to consume me like a flame until
im drowning in it. 
Then, abruptly, the air rushes back into my lungs.
My eyes snap open. I’m in my bedroom. The ceiling fan is spinning slowly
overhead, casting rhythmic shadows across the drywall. My heart is
hammering against my ribs, and my throat is raw from a gasp I couldn’t
release.
I sit up, my hands shaking as I pull the blanket to my chest.
In the dim morning light, I look toward the corner of the room, right
next to the closet door.
He’s sitting there. His long fingers are tucked neatly against his
knees, his posture hunched, his two wide, heavy eyes looking back at me
with absolute stillness. The single angry eye is gone. The tar is dry.
You’re safe, the silent broadcast echoes in my mind, calm and steady.
Breathe. It’s time to get up.
I sink back into the pillows, staring at my guardian. My therapist tells
me Fharis isn’t an entity from the outer dark. She calls him a coping
mechanism—a complex psychological architecture built by a terrified
three-year-old child to survive a childhood defined by systemic abuse
and religious terror. She says my brain wave scans are normal, that the
daytime visitations are just the lingering, waking echoes of complex
post-traumatic stress disorder.
…You’re safe, the silent broadcast echoes in my mind, calm and steady.
Breathe. It’s time to get up.
I sink back into the pillows, staring at my guardian. My therapist tells
me he isn’t real. She wants me to dismantle the structure. But as the
morning sun fully floods the room, lighting up the corner by the closet,
my breath catches in my raw throat. I look down at my hands. Embedded
deep beneath my fingernails, staining the skin of my palms, is a thick,
tacky residue that smells faintly of hot copper and wet wool. 
i looked back up. He isn’t hunched. He is standing a full eight feet
tall, his needle-thin fingers scraping against the ceiling plaster. And
beneath the curved ram horns, his single, hyper-focused eye is staring
directly into mine.
The broadcast in my head doesn’t stop. It just shifts into a
high-pitched, ear-splitting screech of tearing metal.
