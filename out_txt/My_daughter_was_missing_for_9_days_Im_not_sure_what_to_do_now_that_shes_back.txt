When the doorbell tore through the silence of the night, it also tore
through the last sparks of hope I had. After nine days, they finally
came to announce they had found my baby… or what was left of her.

I sat up mechanically and threw my legs over the side of the bed. I
barely found the modesty necessary to put on a dressing gown before
making my way downstairs. I dragged my feet up to the door, turned its
lock and threw it open, welcoming the end of it all.

“Sorry. I lost my key,” said an impossible voice.

I threw myself at her, closing my arms around her body so fast and so
hard I doubted I would ever let go. I hadn’t been able to cry after the
first three days, but tears came flooding out and I was now sobbing like
an infant.

“Moooooom, you’re hurting me!” she complained. I held on for a minute,
two, or maybe forever.

I managed to tear myself away from her, but my hands clamped down on her
shoulders. I feared that if I let go, she would disappear again.

“Don’t you dare ever do that again! Where were you!?” I said harshly,
barely able to contain the incendiary anger that was now burning all the
relief away.

“Nowhere,” she breathed, unable to look me in the eyes.

Part of me wanted to tear the truth out of her. Keep yelling until she
told me where she had been for nine days. But it didn’t matter. The
police would be around eventually to close their investigation; they
would care about the whys and the hows. I didn’t need answers as long as
I got my daughter back.

I pulled her inside and closed the door behind her. If only I could make
it vanish from existence so she could never leave again.

Now that she was inside, I couldn’t help but notice how much she smelled
like a rainy day, as if she had rolled around in the mud. Her skin and
her hair, however, were spotless. Even her clothes were surprisingly
clean, as if she had pulled them out of her closet that very morning.

“Mom,” she said. “Do you still love me?”

I embraced her again.

“Of course, I still love you,” I whispered in her ear.

“Would you still love me if…” she started.

“I’ll always love you, no matter what,” I said before she could finish
her thought.

When she pushed me away, I could see tears welling up in her eyes. She
simply wished me good night and walked to her room. A part of me wanted
nothing more than to chase her and make sure she was OK. But I knew my
daughter well enough to know she would just shut me out. I couldn’t
afford to push her away again. I just hoped she knew my door was always
open for her.

In truth, I could barely stand up after nine days of constant fear and
self-loathing. So, judge me if you’d like, but I went to bed myself. I
fell asleep almost immediately, comforted by the sounds of clothes
falling to the ground and bed sheets rustling in her room.

 

A sound stirred me from sleep—one so brief my conscious mind couldn’t
comprehend it.

“Mom,” the voice repeated in a blunt whisper.

A mass sank into the mattress beside me and tightened the bed sheets
around my left leg. How many years had it been since that scenario had
last played out? I couldn’t stop the smile from appearing on my lips.

“Sweetie, what’s up?” I mumbled.

“Would you still love me if I wasn’t your daughter?” it asked.

The question jolted my eyes open.

Even in complete darkness, the shadowy figure stood out. The normally
radiant features of my daughter blended together in a gloomy silhouette,
hunched over in misery.

“You’re my daughter and I love you,” I replied, my voice loud and clear.

“That’s not what I asked,” it muttered.

“You’re not making sense, sweetie. I gave birth to you. I held you right
after you came out of me. You’re my daughter.”

“OK then,” it spat. “Would you still love me if I had been mistaken for
the other girl and I wasn’t the one you gave birth to?”

“It would hurt to hear, but you still would be my daughter. I raised you
and I love you. That’s what really matters. Maybe we could all be a
family.”

“What if your daughter is dead?”

When I couldn’t answer as quickly as it wanted, the figure threw itself
on the bed, crawling on all fours. It loomed over me, placing its arms
around me, pinning me beneath its body. Two white orbs peered through
the darkness and into my soul.

“What if they took your daughter and left me in her place what if they
took her and they buried her and I was left here and you raised me like
I was her but I’m not her I’m me and me is not your daughter or even a
daughter at all really would you still love me if I hated you and I
hated everyone because I’m alone and no one is like me and no one can be
like me?”

“WOULD YOU STILL LOVE ME THEN, MOM?” it snarled, its teeth shining like
a wolf’s fangs in the night.

“Sweetie,” I said, trying my best to sound authoritative. “What’s the
problem?”

“ANSWER ME!” she yelled again, crawling further up, her face now a few
inches from mine.

I could now see her: my daughter. It was her face, her beautiful green
eyes and her perfect teeth. Her eyebrows were drawn tightly together,
and her eyes were aflame with rage, but it was just her.

“I love you. I don’t care about anything else,” I said.

“LIAR!” she yelled again, her mouth distorting with the roar.

She threw herself off the bed and stormed off, slamming my bedroom door
behind her. Seconds later, I heard another door slam shut on the other
side of the house.

I flung the covers off and made my way to her bedroom. I could hear her
crying right on the other side of the door.

I knocked, and the sobs stopped, giving way to barely controlled
sniffles.

“Go. Away,” she ordered.

“You don’t have to open the door, sweetie. But maybe you could listen.”

“So you can lie again?” she uttered, her normally melodious voice warped
by contempt.

“I’m not an idiot. You were gone for nine days, sweetie. I know you’re
not well. But I can’t help you if you don’t let me help. How about we
start by going over those nine days? Where were you?”

“I told you. I was nowhere. With my real mom.”

I’ll admit I wasn’t sure whether it was healthier to play along with
delusions or confront them head-on, but I knew I had to say something.

“Who’s your real mom?” I asked.

“Not who,” she answered.

“What?”

“Exactly.”

 

That’s the last thing she said before she stopped talking to me, but I
can still hear her crying. I’m typing this while I’m sitting on the
other side of the door, making sure she’s at least still in the house. I
don’t know what to say. I don’t know who to call. Is there even anything
I can do to help her?
