I’m using a burner because three of us already had to give statements to
the state troopers, and I’m not trying to get dragged back into that. If
you live anywhere near the state line, you probably heard about the two
drifters they found near the abandoned freight yard last fall—or rather,
what was left of them. The local paper blamed wild dogs or a black bear.
It wasn’t a bear.

There was a guy in my senior class. Let’s call him Cole.

On paper, Cole was completely unremarkable. About six feet tall, decent
athletic build, short black hair, always wore the same faded grey hoodie
and scuffed boots. He didn’t talk much, didn’t really have friends, and
sat in the back of study hall staring out the window.

The thing is, nobody could stand to be in a room with him for too long.
My buddy Marcus used to say it felt like standing next to a microwave
with the door ripped off—just this heavy, static pressure in the back of
your teeth. And his eyes were wrong. I don’t know how else to explain
it. They were dark, almost flat, and if the fluorescent lights hit him
at the right angle, they’d catch a weird, dull shine like a stray cat’s.
Plus, he blinked way too slow.

Around mid-October, stray dogs started vanishing from the south end of
town. Then a couple of rail-hoppers who camped under the Route 11
overpass went missing. Around the same time, Marcus started noticing
Cole walking out toward the woods behind the old lumber mill at like
11:00 PM on school nights.

Being eighteen and stupid, Marcus, my friend Devin, and I decided Cole
was either running a stash out there or doing some sick animal-cruelty
stuff. We figured we’d follow him, catch him on video, and blow up his
spot.

We tailed him on a Friday night around midnight. He walked straight down
the gravel access road toward the wooded hollow by the train tracks,
moving way faster than someone just taking a walk. We kept about fifty
yards back, staying in the tree line with our phone flashlights dimmed.

About half a mile in, Cole stopped in a small clearing near the creek. A
stray mutt—some skinny hound mix that had been hanging around the gas
station for weeks—was rooting around near the bank.

Devin pulled out his iPhone to start recording.

The exact second Cole turned his head toward the dog, the air changed.
My ears popped like we’d just dropped five hundred feet on a highway.
Every single phone in our pockets—and the one in Devin’s hand—went
completely haywire. Devin’s screen tore into violent green and purple
static, his flashlight strobed twice, and then all three of our phones
went dead black. 100% battery to zero in half a second.

Then we heard the sound. It sounded like someone snapping thick, wet
tree branches inside a leather bag.

In the moonlight, Cole’s posture just collapsed forward. His spine
arched up, tearing straight through the back of his grey hoodie. His
arms stretched out—way too long, joints popping backward—until massive,
leathery wings unfurled and dragged across the dead leaves. He grew
easily twice his size in three seconds.

It looked like something straight out of a nightmare. A massive,
hairless, dragon-like bat with thick, scarred shoulders and curved
wing-claws digging into the dirt.

When it turned its head toward the stray dog, the moonlight hit its
face. Its bottom jaw wasn’t solid. It split right down the middle into
two separate, jagged halves that flared outward sideways like pincers,
dripping thick saliva onto the grass.

It made a low, vibrating clicking noise that rattled in my chest. Before
the dog could even yelp, the thing lunged with blinding speed, clamped
both halves of that split jaw around its torso, and dragged it into the
brush with a sicking crunch.

We didn’t scream. We couldn’t. We turned and ran blindly through the
dark woods, tripping over roots until we hit the main road. None of our
phones turned back on until we were two miles away.

Cole didn’t come to school on Monday. His family’s rental house was
empty by Wednesday. I still check my phone battery every time I walk to
my car at night, and if the screen ever flickers, I’m not looking behind
me.
