I’ll show you how I became a millionaire and nearly lost my mind doing
it. But first things first.

I grew up poor. My dad was a junkie, and my mom was too busy fighting
her own demons. So there was only one thing I wanted: to get rich, a
sweet life and margaritas on the beach. No wonder I always fell for
those get-rich-quick, no-effort ads that pop up whenever I try to watch
a YouTube video. Then came the ad that would change my life: “New ways
to earn passive income. Right from your couch,” said the ad of a company
I’m not allowed to name. All I can say is this: most of us use their
addictive products every single day. So I called my best friend, let’s
call him K., and we drove to the info meeting at the company’s
headquarters together. There were ten volunteers, and we all gave off
the same vibe: too lazy to really work, too proud to admit it.

We got visitor badges, took the elevator down to the basement, and
walked into a dark room where they showed us a film to pitch us the
idea: They would put a microchip in our brains, so we could see images
and videos with our “inner eye.” Our eyes would basically work as
monitors. The chip, covered in electrodes, sits directly on the visual
cortex and fires targeted electrical impulses that stimulate the nerve
cells as if the eye had just seen something. Brain-computer interface,
or some shit like that.

“And then what?” K. asked when the little film was over and a chubby man
in a plaid shirt, glasses and a walrus mustache stepped up and
introduced himself as the project manager.

“Once we’ve implanted the visual neuroprosthesis, we play ad films for
you. And you watch them.”

So that was the job. Watching ads.

“At the breakfast table, waiting for the bus, on your lunch break, in
the doctor’s waiting room. You watch our partners’ ads and earn money.
Simple and passive.”

“And how much do you make?” asked a woman who kept squirming in her
chair.

“That depends on how long you watch and which ads you get. The clips are
always personalized for you. We use the data we have on you. That’s also
why we can pay you such high amounts,” said Walrus Mustache, visibly
proud. “On one hand, the ad is perfectly tailored to you. On the other,
you can’t look away like you can with normal ads. It’s the most
effective form of advertising since advertising was invented.”

Then a tablet got passed around, and we were supposed to sign up if we
hadn’t changed our minds. Four out of ten volunteers backed out. K. and
I signed, and Walrus Mustache asked if we wanted the chip put in today.
It would only take a few minutes, and we could earn our first money
right away. That was the magic word we’d been waiting for! Of course we
wanted to start immediately.

My plan was as simple as it was genius: I wouldn’t watch the clips on my
lunch break or in some waiting room like Walrus Mustache suggested. I’d
watch them all day long. I’d get up, roll a fat joint, and chill on the
couch while the world of capitalism rained down on me in bright moving
pictures and my bank balance kept growing. Free money glitch, bitches!

They “injected” the chip, a staff member made the necessary settings,
and just like that I saw my first ad. A day-trading crypto app that
supposedly made you rich fast. Damn, the company really had all my data.
Then I saw a film about a sports car, and the last ad was for
toothpaste. A shrill animation where bacteria were drawn as little
monsters with huge jaws, attacking tooth enamel and chewing it to
pieces. “KAR KAR KAR,” the little monsters screamed as they bit down. 

“KAR KAR KAR.” 

“KAR KAR KAR.” 

“KAR KAR KAR.”

BUY DENT-O-YEAH! TOOTHPASTE NOW! WITH NEW SEVENFOLD PROTECTION FORMULA
AGAINST CAVITIES.

Then the first test run was over.

“How was it?”

I could still see the glaring silhouettes of the little cavity monsters
in my field of vision and told her.
“That’s normal, it should disappear soon.”
The silhouettes vanished, but something else popped up: a push
notification from my banking app. An amount had just been credited to
me. It really works. God bless capitalism!

I met up with K. and the other participants in the basement-lobby.

“This shit is insane, right?”
“KAR KAR KAR,” said K., and I laughed.
“Did you see the toothpaste ad too? I’m still sticking with the cheap
brand though.”
K. didn’t answer. He just stared at me with a crazed look.
“Dude, you okay?”
“KAR KAR KAR,” K. answered.
This time I didn’t laugh.
“Stop that shit.”

Walrus Mustache came over and wanted our visitor badges back. He held
out his hand to K., but my friend didn’t react. He just stared at him
with his insane eyes.

And suddenly K. jumped him and sank his teeth into his face. He bit down
again. And again. Blood sprayed in every direction, Walrus Mustache
screamed in panic and stumbled into a shelf.

“KAR KAR KAR”

A piece of Walrus’s nose landed next to my foot, and I felt vomit rising
in my throat. The project manager crawled away on all fours with his
chewed-up face, while my dear friend K., who would never hurt a fly,
opened the hunt on the other participants. He leapt onto a woman and
clamped down on her neck.

“KAR KAR KAR”

Later, an unofficial report would say he had bitten through her carotid
artery, which explains the huge fountain of blood that sprayed right at
me.

Then all hell broke loose, and I’ll only tell you what I can swear to.
Somewhere an alarm went off. All the doors were locked. The company’s
headquarters was apparently prepared for emergencies like this. But that
also meant we were trapped. Alone with a monster who thought we were all
delicious plaque. We hid under tables, climbed on cabinets, did anything
to escape his teeth. K. attacked one after another. Bit down and spat
out the chunks of flesh. Until his victim had no pulse left and
collapsed lifeless.

And at some point it was my turn. The last survivor in a sea of blood,
severed body parts and corpses. K. jumped me and pinned me to the floor.
That’s when I noticed how strong he suddenly was. K. had always been the
scrawniest of our group and always hated violence. Conflicts, he used to
say, can be solved differently. Well, not much was left of that K. A mix
of drool and blood dripped from his teeth right into my mouth.

I didn’t notice the door opening behind me and armed men coming in, who
took K. down with tasers and a tranquilizer gun. He landed right on top
of me, and everywhere was the bloody stench of iron.

They took both of us out the back exit to a hospital. When I had calmed
down a bit, men in fancy suits stood by my bed and introduced themselves
as lawyers for the company whose name I’m not allowed to mention. They
explained that there had been a glitch in the program. The ad’s content
had affected K.’s brain in a completely different way, so that he
literally believed he was KAR, the enamel-eating monster.
“And how is he now?”
“He’s getting the best medical care available here,” said one of the
suits, and handed me a folder of documents. The deal was clear: the
company would end the new advertising program immediately and large
compensation payments would go to the witnesses and the families of the
dead. And after I signed the NDA, I became a multimillionaire. In the
end, the ad kept its promise. Even if in a very twisted way.

Once a month I visit K., who lives in a private clinic. They take good
care of him, and I don’t get the feeling he’s unhappy. With the 134
million they paid him, I wouldn’t be heartbroken in his place either.

He’s happy to see me every time. We like to sit by the lake and feed the
ducks. K. still thinks he’s the bacterium from the ad, but the doctors
have adjusted his medication so that he’s lost all aggression. K., just
like his old self, wouldn’t hurt a fly.

“Kar kar kar,“ he says, and tosses a little food to the ducks.

And now and then he says something else: BUY DENT-O-YEAH! TOOTHPASTE
NOW! WITH NEW SEVENFOLD PROTECTION FORMULA AGAINST CAVITIES.
