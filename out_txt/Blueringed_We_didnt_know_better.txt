It was a long day at the beach, and the tide had retreated farther than
any of us could remember. What had been open sea only hours ago was now
a vast, jagged maze of shallow pools, black volcanic rock, and patches
of wet sand that reflected the heavy afternoon sun. Tiny fish darted
between the cracks, and dark hermit crabs scurried whenever someone
lifted a stone, greedily holding onto the stolen shells they claimed as
homes. Strange brittle stars rested in the shadows with their long
tentacles outstretched, though we didn’t care much about them. There
were simply too many.

Every few minutes, someone would shout, “Come look at this!” before the
rest of us crowded around a strange shell or a lonely reef fish left
behind by the water. Personally, I wanted to find a nautilus shell, a
dead one. We all wanted some kind of treasure, really. Anything would
do, nothing specific. At the very least, I figured an aquatic hermit
crab couldn’t possibly grow big enough to steal an empty nautilus shell
away from me. Could it?

“Imagine getting stranded here,” Noah laughed, poking a stick into a
pool among the rocks. I watched a tiny fish get swallowed by the
underwater sandstorm he created. It wasn’t a fancy, proper fish, but
some kind of frog-faced bottom dweller that moved unbelievably fast. The
moment the sand settled, it stood perfectly still, blending into the
background with its dull grey and white body. “I’d build a civilization
with the crabs.”

“You’d lose a war to the crabs,” Maya replied smoothly. “They’re
organized.”

Laughter bounced across the wet rocks before being swallowed by the
heavy, distant sound of the crashing waves. Noah was Maya’s older
brother, and I was their cousin. Farther ahead, Eli crouched beside a
narrow crevice in the volcanic stone. He was our neighborhood friend,
and by the looks of it, he had found something.

“Yo, guys.”

Nobody looked. We had all grown bored of Eli’s exciting discoveries. He
had tricked me once before, making me think I was looking at a prized
chunk of whale dung, only for me to learn it was just a lousy, squishy
sea cucumber.

“Guys!” Eli shouted again.

“What?” Maya asked. I guess she was just nice, always ready to believe
whatever Eli had in store for her. He sounded entirely serious this
time, though. We listened.

“I found… something.”

One by one, we gathered around Eli, looming over his shoulders and
tracking his gaze until we saw what he saw. At first, it looked like
nothing more than a damp, tan-colored blob tucked beneath a flat shelf
of rock.

Then, it moved.

A tiny octopus unfolded itself with surprising elegance, one arm at a
time, as if stretching after a long nap. I noticed a faint shimmer
running across the circular grooves of its skin.

“No way,” Maya whispered, stepping closer to Eli.

“It is so small,” Noah added, leaning in. “A baby?”

Eli smiled, fascinated. “It’s adorable. Looks like a baby one, yeah.”

The octopus slowly climbed onto the top of the rock, its soft body
shifting in texture as it moved. It didn’t seem frightened at all.
Instead, it simply explored its surroundings with curious, delicate arms
that curled around nearby pebbles and broken shells.

“I’ve never seen one this close,” Eli said softly.

“Can we touch it?” Noah asked.

“I mean…”

Nobody answered. Not because we knew any better, but because it felt a
little gross to touch a squishy sea blob. Eli, however, carefully
extended one finger. The octopus reached toward it.

“There!” Noah laughed. “It likes you!”

The little creature wrapped a single arm around Eli’s finger for only a
second before letting go again.

“Aww.”

“It literally wants to hold hands.”

Maya crouched lower, her face inches from the pool. “Look at its eyes.”

“They’re huge.”

“They’re kind of cute.”

“I don’t think those are eyes,” I murmured, staring closer at the
pulsing shapes.

As the sun began setting, painting the sky in deep, bruised purples, we
kept the octopus company, letting Eli play with it more.

“Do octopuses make good pets?” Eli asked.

“I’ve seen videos of it,” Noah replied.

“My uncle had one.”

“No, he had a squid.”

“Same thing.”

“They’re definitely not the same thing.”

As the octopus slowly crawled across the surface of the rock, the dying
sunlight struck its skin from a different angle. Faint, electric-blue
circles shimmered across its body, flashing brilliantly before fading
almost immediately back into the dull brown flesh. It began nipping at
Eli’s finger, making him laugh. Tickling nibbles, he called them.

“Oh.”

“What?”

“It changed colors.”

“So?”

“I don’t know.”

“All octopuses can do that, you know,” Noah said confidently.

“All?” Maya asked.

“Maybe we’re interrupting its date,” Noah joked.

Everyone laughed again. Eli, however, didn’t join in.

“I want to keep it,” he stated firmly. His voice sounded tighter now, a
bit more tense.

Three voices answered at once.

“No.”

“Absolutely not.”

“Why?” Eli whined, though his eyes looked strangely wide. “Look at it.
It’ll probably die if we leave it here. I think… I think it’s telling me
something.”

“It lives here, Eli,” Maya said, rolling her eyes.

“But… what if I made it a little aquarium?”

Noah shrugged. “I mean… if you actually knew how.”

“I could learn.”

Maya groaned. “You watched one documentary and now you’re a marine
biologist.”

“I watched half.”

“Exactly.”

Eli looked around the barren stretch of rock. “We just need something to
carry it in.”

“There isn’t anything out here.”

“I’ll go find a bottle or a bucket. We’re literally on a beach, people
leave stuff everywhere.”

Before anyone could argue further, Eli stepped over a ridge of black
rock and disappeared behind a cluster of deep tide pools. I wondered why
he was in such a sudden rush to leave. It was as if he was feeling
something shifting on the inside, a secret he didn’t want to share with
us.

“The sun is almost down!” Noah called out after him.

A hand appeared briefly over the rocks, giving us a quick thumbs-up.
Then, it vanished.

The rest of us kept exploring, heading closer to the retreating
shoreline. Someone found a sea star, and someone else discovered a tiny
shrimp that was transparent enough to nearly disappear in the water. The
minutes slipped by unnoticed.

Eventually, Maya stopped and frowned, looking back toward the shadows of
the dunes. “Eli has been gone a while, hasn’t he?”

Noah pulled out his phone to check the time. “…About twenty minutes.”

“Seriously?”

“He probably found real treasure.”

“Eli!” Maya yelled.

Only the crashing waves answered.

The three of us climbed over the ridge, calling his name louder each
time we reached a new crest. We found no footprints in the wet sand, no
discarded bucket, and no plastic bottle. There was no sign of Eli
anywhere. Behind us, the tide had turned; the waves grumpily flowed back
in, completely submerging the zone where we had found the octopus.

Hours later, after the emergency services had already begun searching
the pitch-black shoreline, Maya sat shivering, wrapped tightly in a wool
towel. A park ranger stood over her, asking exactly where Eli had last
been seen.

She described the jagged rocks, the isolated tide pools, and finally,
the little octopus.

The ranger’s pen stopped moving across his notepad. “What kind of
octopus?”

“I don’t know,” Maya muttered, shaking her head. “It was tiny. Brown.”

“And it had blue circles,” I added from beside her.

The ranger looked at me, his expression suddenly hardening, as if my
words had crushed the last of his professional hope.

“They glowed,” Maya whispered.

A heavy silence settled over the entire group. The ranger slowly closed
his notebook and slipped it into his pocket.

“How much did he touch it?”

Nobody answered immediately. Then, a cold dread washed over Noah as he
remembered Eli smiling, watching that small, stubby arm curl around his
bare finger. He remembered laughing. He remembered saying, “It likes
you.”

Noah didn’t know that those would be the last words he would ever say to
his friend.

Eli’s body was recovered from a shallow crevice later that night. The
medical examiner stated the cause of death was asphyxiation due to total
paralysis. The painless, neurotoxic bite of a blue-ringed octopus. Eli
had been completely conscious, unable to move or breathe, as the tide
slowly came back in.

Let it be a warning. If you ever encounter a wild creature you don’t
recognize, no matter how small, no matter how beautiful, leave it alone.
And make sure the people you love promise to do the same.
