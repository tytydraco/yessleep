The call came in at 4:16 on a wet Tuesday morning. Fire showing from the
rear of a residence on Bell Road, with people possibly trapped inside.

Ellis spilled half his coffee climbing into the engine. By the time we
pulled out of the station, he’d given up trying to clean the seat and
was telling me about a fence his neighbor had built six inches over the
property line. He’d been talking about it since Saturday. Apparently the
neighbor had offered to move the fence, but only if Ellis paid for the
lumber.

“He already bought the damn lumber,” Ellis said. “That’s the part I
can’t get through his head.”

I told him he ought to let the man keep his six inches. Ellis said it
was a matter of principle, which was what he’d been saying since
Saturday too.

We’d worked together for six years. He was 11 years older than me, broad
through the shoulders, with a thumbnail he’d flattened in a car door and
a habit of checking other people’s equipment after they’d already
checked it themselves. He could talk through an entire drive to a call
without mentioning where we were going. Usually I appreciated it.

He stopped talking when we turned onto Bell Road.

The house stood beyond a gravel parking area, an old farmhouse with
additions built onto both sides. Everybody called it the boardinghouse
because that’s what it had been for as long as anyone at the station
could remember. Rooms were rented by the week, mostly to men working
temporary construction jobs. There was a separate apartment at the
front, a row of mailboxes nailed to plywood beside the driveway, and
enough entrances that you couldn’t always tell which part of the
building someone lived in.

Fire was coming out of a ground-floor window at the rear. The flames
bent beneath the eaves and disappeared into smoke that had already
swallowed the upper windows. Rainwater ran down the siding in black
streaks. A man wearing a coat over his underwear stood beside the
mailboxes, waving both arms toward the front porch.

Our captain went to him while the rest of the crew stretched a hose line
toward the rear entrance. A second engine was already on its way, and
the captain had requested additional manpower from a neighboring
department. Ellis and I were assigned to search the front rooms, where
the man said he’d heard a woman shouting.

Before we went in, Ellis caught the fastening beneath my chin and gave
it a tug.

“I checked it,” I said.

“Good.”

He let go, and we went inside.

We found the woman in the front hall, down on one knee with her hand
against the wall. She’d been trying to get out but had gone back for her
son. She managed to tell us he was in the bedroom before another
coughing fit took her breath away.

The smoke was thicker near the ceiling, and the hall was hot enough that
I could feel it through my gear. Ellis helped her toward the porch while
I continued to the bedroom. She tried to follow me, but he stopped her
at the doorway and assured her I would find the boy.

I found his bare feet beneath the bed, tucked against the baseboard
between two plastic storage bins. He couldn’t have been older than six.
He wore a T-shirt with a cartoon shark across the front and had pulled
his knees up beneath it. When I reached for him, he pressed himself
harder against the wall.

I told him his mother was outside and that I was going to take him to
her. He shook his head until I showed him my glove and asked him to grab
it.

He let me take his wrist.

Getting him out meant dragging one of the bins aside and lying almost
flat against the floor. He came reluctantly, scraping his shoulder
beneath the bedframe, but once I had him clear he wrapped both arms
around my coat. I carried him out with his face pressed against my
chest.

His mother was just beyond the porch. When she saw him, she made a sound
I heard over the engines, the radios, and the water striking the back of
the house. He lifted his head immediately. A medic took him from me, and
his mother dropped to her knees to catch him before his feet touched the
ground.

Ellis was waiting at the doorway. The second engine had arrived, and its
crew was helping contain the fire at the rear. Our captain still had no
reliable count of the occupants, so he sent us back to finish searching
the accessible rooms on the first floor.

I took a Halligan from the engine before we went back in. It was a heavy
steel bar with a forked end and a pointed head, useful for forcing doors
that wouldn’t open. I carried it in my right hand as we moved through
the hall.

Inside, the hallway turned past a narrow staircase and continued toward
the rear addition. The boardinghouse had been remodeled so many times
that some doors opened into closets, while others led through what had
once been exterior walls. One room contained a stripped mattress, two
suitcases, and work boots standing neatly beside the bed. Another had a
padlock on the outside. Ellis took the Halligan from me, forced it open,
and handed the bar back after finding bicycles, spare furniture, and
stacks of damp newspapers.

We reported the rooms clear and continued toward the kitchen.

The crew working the rear fire had begun knocking it down. Water hissed
against hot material somewhere beyond the wall, and smoke shifted along
the ceiling as conditions changed. The floor was littered with broken
glass from a cupboard door.

A man stood at the far end of the kitchen.

He wore a white undershirt and dark trousers, with no shoes. Both hands
were pressed against his chest. His head hung forward as though he were
trying to catch his breath.

I called to him. He didn’t answer until Ellis and I had nearly reached
him.

“There’s somebody else,” he said.

His voice was faint, but he seemed alert enough to understand us. There
were no obvious burns on his face. His undershirt was dirty around the
collar, and soot had settled into the lines beside his nose.

“Where?” Ellis asked.

The man looked toward the narrow staircase we’d passed in the hall.

“A woman. She can’t get up.”

We started guiding him toward the front door. He walked stiffly,
favoring neither leg, and his skin felt unusually hot through my glove.
I assumed he’d been closer to the fire than we had.

As we passed the staircase, he stopped and turned toward the door
beneath it.

“Don’t leave her,” he said.

“We won’t.”

He came along after that, though he kept looking over his shoulder.

Outside, the captain took over. The man sat on the wet grass with his
feet together, still holding his chest. A medic approached with an
oxygen bag while Ellis reported the additional victim.

The captain had been speaking with the man from the mailboxes. Several
tenants worked nights, and he couldn’t account for everyone. He’d heard
someone shouting upstairs earlier, but the noise had stopped before the
first engine arrived.

The woman we’d rescued from the front apartment had only one child.
Beyond that, nobody could give us a reliable count.

Our captain checked with the crews working the fire before sending us
back. The flames at the rear were being contained, but the building had
taken considerable heat. A hose line was operating near the rear
addition, and another crew was checking the stairwell and adjoining
rooms. We were told to locate the reported woman if conditions
permitted, then come straight back out.

The basement access was beneath the narrow staircase. Smoke had darkened
the doorframe, and water was already running down the steps from the
floor above. A firefighter from the other crew was at the opening,
checking the stairs and the space below. He reported that the stairway
was intact, though visibility deteriorated toward the bottom.

We found the woman partway down.

She lay across several steps, one arm hooked around the banister. Her
skirt had caught on a nail projecting from a broken tread. Every time
she tried to climb, the fabric pulled tight and dragged her back.

Ellis supported her while I freed the skirt. The cloth tore much more
easily than I’d expected. It left a black smear on my glove, like the
residue from a burnt rag.

She couldn’t stand. We carried her between us, taking care not to strike
her legs against the narrow walls. Her head rested on Ellis’s shoulder.

“There’s a boy,” she said.

“We’ve got a boy out already,” Ellis told her.

She lifted her head.

“Downstairs. By the furnace.”

I asked whether she meant another child.

“He won’t come unless somebody takes his hand.”

I asked whether he was hurt, but she closed her eyes and wouldn’t
answer.

We carried her outside and left her with the medics.

The man in the undershirt was still on the grass. He’d been moved onto a
blanket, and a medic was fitting an oxygen mask over his face. The woman
was placed nearby while another medic brought equipment from the
ambulance.

Our captain checked again with the woman from the front apartment. She
confirmed that she had only one son, but couldn’t tell us whether
another child might have been staying in one of the rented rooms. The
man from the mailboxes knew of no children living in the boardinghouse,
though he admitted he rarely saw some of the tenants.

The captain asked the crew at the rear for an update. They reported that
the visible fire was largely knocked down, with some extension still
being checked. The basement had not been fully searched. He authorized a
brief search for the reported child, with a firefighter from the other
crew maintaining contact at the access and a hose line available nearby.
We were to stay together, remain within the accessible portion of the
basement, and withdraw immediately if conditions changed.

Ellis checked his air, then mine. He touched the fastening beneath my
chin again, though he didn’t pull it this time.

“Ready?”

I nodded.

We descended the stairs together. The firefighter at the access stayed
close enough to maintain contact, his light following us as we reached
the bottom. I carried the Halligan in my right hand, its forked end
down.

The basement smelled of wet plaster, old oil, and something burnt that
didn’t have the sharpness of wood smoke. Water ran down the stairs and
collected along the bottom wall. The cellar was low enough that Ellis
had to turn sideways beneath a pipe crossing the ceiling.

There was an old furnace against the back wall. Its metal casing had
buckled near the bottom, and the plaster above it was stained black.
Shelves held paint cans, a broken fan, and cardboard boxes swollen from
years of damp. The concrete floor sloped toward a drain beside the
washing machine.

Behind the furnace lay a mound of gray material.

It resembled coal ash from an old storage bin. The material filled the
corner between the wall and the furnace, rising nearly two feet in the
middle, though there was no container holding it there. Water had
gathered around its edges without soaking into it.

The boy sat beside it.

He was barefoot and shirtless, his knees pulled against his chest. Soot
marked his elbows and the side of his neck. When our lights reached him,
he lowered the hands he’d been holding over his ears.

“Fire department,” Ellis said. “We’re taking you outside.”

The boy looked at us without answering.

I moved alongside Ellis, keeping the stairs within sight. We had very
little time to spend convincing the boy. The building was still hot
above us, and the radio traffic from the rear was becoming more urgent.

Ellis crouched and offered his hand.

The boy reached for it.

His grip was so strong that Ellis shifted his weight before standing.
The child rose awkwardly, his feet dragging through the water. He seemed
taller once upright, with shoulders too narrow for the length of his
arms.

“Come on,” Ellis told him.

The boy turned toward the furnace.

“One more downstairs.”

I thought he meant another person.

“Where?”

He raised his free hand and pointed behind the furnace, toward the
mound.

I directed my light into the corner. The space between the furnace and
the wall was barely wide enough for a person to stand sideways, and
there was no doorway or opening behind it. We’d reached the bottom of
the house. There wasn’t another flight of stairs, and there was nowhere
farther down for anyone to be.

“Who’s down there?” I asked.

The boy stepped into the gray material.

His foot disappeared to the ankle.

There was no collapse, no puff of ash, no depression where he’d placed
his weight. The surface remained level around his leg. When he took
another step, it accepted his other foot the same way.

I caught his upper arm to pull him back.

Something shifted beneath my glove.

It felt as though a handful of dry leaves had moved inside his skin. His
arm was warm, but the material beneath it gave under my fingers and
gathered again when I loosened my grip.

“Ellis. Let go of him.”

Ellis had felt it too. He tried to release the boy’s hand, but the
fingers around his glove wouldn’t open.

A dark ridge pushed upward from the mound beside the boy’s leg. It was
slick and rounded, thick as a length of pipe. Another rose behind it,
moving independently. Between them, the gray surface drew inward.

The boy continued staring at the furnace.

Ellis braced his boots against the concrete and pulled toward the
stairs. The boy’s arm stretched between them, the shoulder remaining
fixed while the elbow moved farther and farther from it. His skin split
along the underside of the forearm, revealing black fibers packed
together where muscle and bone should have been.

The hand around Ellis’s glove divided into thin strands. They slipped
beneath the edge of his sleeve and tightened around his wrist.

I wedged the forked end of the Halligan beneath the fibers, using the
boy’s ruined forearm for leverage. The metal sank into it with almost no
resistance. I pulled back, trying to lift the strands away from Ellis’s
glove, and the forearm broke open around the tool.

Gray material fell into the water. Beneath it, the black fibers
stretched from Ellis’s wrist to the mound, tightening as I worked the
fork between them.

The mound rose again.

What I’d taken for two separate ridges curved toward each other. A dark
opening formed between them, and a wet surface appeared beneath a heavy
fold of black tissue. It reflected the beam of my flashlight. As it
turned, I realized the surface was an eye.

It was larger than the furnace door.

A ring of tissue contracted around it. The eye rolled toward us,
carrying my light across its surface, and the folds above it pulled
farther out of the ground.

The radio crackled with an order to withdraw. The rear crew had reported
movement in the floor above the fire, and the captain was ordering
everyone out of the basement.

I keyed my radio and told him Ellis was caught and we needed help
getting him free.

Ellis was trying to peel the strands from his wrist with his other hand.
The boy’s head tipped backward. Its mouth opened, and gray dust ran out
over its chin.

The material around the eye wasn’t rising from the ash. The ash was
sliding off something that had been beneath it all along.

A broad section of black skin pushed against the furnace. The metal
casing shifted with a grinding sound. The pipe above us bent at a joint,
shedding rust onto the floor.

I drove the pointed end of the Halligan into the black fibers near the
boy’s elbow. The metal pierced them, but they were too dense to tear
cleanly. I pulled sideways, separating several strands before the tool
slipped free.

The figure beside the furnace was no longer recognizably a child. Its
torso had opened along one side, and the arm holding Ellis was a bundle
of dark cords covered in patches of crumbling gray skin. Its legs were
buried almost to the knees. The face remained in place, though its
features had begun to soften, the cheeks sagging beneath the eyes.

I struck the exposed cords again with the Halligan’s flat blade. Several
snapped and recoiled into the mound, but others tightened around Ellis’s
wrist.

Something struck the underside of the floor above us.

Dust fell from the joists. A shelf tore loose from the wall, scattering
paint cans across the concrete. The firefighter at the stairway shouted
for us to move.

Ellis stumbled as a second set of strands came up through the water
around his boot. They tightened around his ankle and drew his foot
backward. He caught himself against the furnace, which shifted beneath
his weight.

I dropped beside him and hooked the Halligan beneath the strands around
his ankle. They parted reluctantly when I levered the tool against the
concrete, leaving black residue on the metal, but more were already
reaching from beneath the ash.

The figure’s body collapsed into the mound. Its face folded inward,
carrying the last of its features down with it. For an instant the top
of its head remained visible, then it too came apart.

Ellis’s glove was still caught.

I tried pulling it free from the sleeve. The fibers had worked beneath
the cuff and tightened around his wrist.

“Get my hand out,” he said.

I grabbed his forearm and pulled while he twisted against the strands.
His breathing had become loud and uneven over the radio.

The mound opened behind him.

A woman’s hand came out of it, fingers spread.

The hand was human-sized and clean, without soot or injury. It reached
past Ellis toward me. Behind it rose a shoulder and the side of a face.
The woman was pushing through the black surface as though it were a
heavy sheet pulled over her head.

Her mouth opened before the rest of her face had formed.

“Help.”

I recognized the voice.

It belonged to the woman we’d carried off the basement stairs.

The thing raised itself farther, exposing a second arm that ended at the
elbow. Its face was the same one I’d seen resting against Ellis’s
shoulder, but the eyes were still closed and the skin beneath its chin
hung loose.

I swung the Halligan across its reaching arm. The blow struck below the
wrist, breaking the limb into gray fragments, but I had to release my
hold on Ellis to do it.

He slid forward.

His knee struck the concrete at the edge of the mound. The black surface
gave beneath him, and his leg sank through it to the thigh.

I caught the front of his coat and hauled backward. He grabbed my
forearm. For a few seconds I managed to hold him in place, but the floor
beneath us was moving. A crack spread across the concrete toward the
drain, and the washing machine tipped against the wall.

The half-formed woman reached again.

Her remaining fingers closed around my wrist. They were dry and brittle,
and when I struck them with the Halligan’s shaft, two broke away and
scattered across the floor. The face opened its eyes. Behind the thin
surface covering them, gray particles moved in tight circles.

The firefighter at the stairs was already coming toward us, ducking
beneath the low pipe. He knocked the rest of the hand away with his boot
and grabbed Ellis’s harness, adding his weight to mine.

The black surface beneath Ellis tightened in several places at once.
Thick ridges formed around his legs, drawing him down whenever we gained
an inch. Behind the furnace, more shapes pushed against the skin: the
outline of a shoulder, a bent arm, a face pressed sideways so hard that
the nose flattened against the surface.

The furnace moved another foot.

The pipe above it broke free and struck the wall. Water burst from the
joint, spraying across our masks.

The firefighter called a mayday over the radio, giving our location and
reporting that Ellis was trapped in the basement. I heard the captain
acknowledge him and order crews to the access, but the floor was
breaking apart between us and the stairs.

I dropped the Halligan and took hold of Ellis’s coat with both hands.
The reflective strip across his chest was inches from my face.

He was trying to say something.

I leaned closer, but his words were lost beneath the water and the noise
of the moving floor. He looked directly at me through his mask and moved
his mouth again.

Then something beneath him contracted.

His body jerked against my grip. I heard a heavy crack, close enough
that I felt it through his coat.

Ellis stopped pulling.

The firefighter behind me dragged me backward. I held on until the
fabric beneath my gloves began to tear. The front of Ellis’s coat
stretched between us, and a seam opened beneath the reflective strip.

It came away in my hands.

I fell against the other firefighter. He caught me and forced me toward
the stairs as Ellis disappeared beneath the black surface.

The cellar was coming apart around us. The furnace had tipped against
the wall, and the shelves were falling one after another. Behind us, the
enormous eye turned toward the stairway. Its surface caught the moving
beams of our flashlights before a fold of black tissue covered it.

We climbed over broken boards and debris that hadn’t been there when we
came down. The stairs were still standing, though the lower treads had
begun to pull away from the wall. The firefighter went ahead, dragging
me by my harness when I hesitated.

At the top, the wall beside the door bowed inward. The frame had twisted
enough that the door wouldn’t open fully. Another firefighter waiting in
the hall forced it wider with his shoulder, and we squeezed through.

The floorboards were lifting.

Several hands reached through the gaps between them. One was missing
most of its fingers. Another had a wrist no thicker than a broom handle.
They felt along the joists and pressed upward, breaking the boards apart
as they searched for something to grip.

A hand caught the bottom of my coat. I kicked free, and its fingers
broke against my boot, leaving a cloud of powder hanging in the smoke.

We reached the porch as the evacuation signal sounded.

The captain met us on the lawn. He caught me by the shoulders and asked
where Ellis was.

I pointed toward the house. I tried to tell him what had happened, but I
couldn’t get past the part where Ellis’s coat had torn. The strip of
fabric was still wrapped around my right glove.

Behind the captain, the woman we’d carried from the basement stairs was
lying on a blanket.

A medic knelt beside her. As he reached toward her face, the skin along
her cheek folded beneath his thumb. Gray powder spilled from the crease.
Her nose collapsed, followed by one open eye.

The medic pulled back as the rest of her face sank into the blanket.
There was no blood. Her body lost its shape beneath her clothes, leaving
the skirt and blouse draped around a shallow mound of dark material.

Several yards away, the man in the white undershirt was coming apart
too. His head had fallen against his chest. Dust ran through the collar
and collected in his lap while the medic who’d been treating him
stumbled backward.

I looked toward the ambulance.

The real woman from the front apartment was sitting with her son in her
arms. He had begun to cry. She held his head against her shoulder,
covering one ear with her hand.

Our captain ordered everyone farther away from the building.

The remaining crews came out carrying equipment where they could,
abandoning what they couldn’t. Hose dragged across the wet grass. Water
continued striking the rear of the house, but the flames had nearly
disappeared beneath the smoke.

The upstairs windows dropped together.

It was only a few inches. The house remained upright, and I thought the
movement had come from the ground shifting beneath my feet. Then the
windows lowered again, and the porch roof pulled away from the wall.

Someone shouted that the foundation was failing.

The center of the building began to sink. The roof bent inward along the
ridge. Brickwork cracked around the chimney, and the front steps tilted
toward the doorway. Boards split across the porch as the floor behind
them dropped.

People appeared in the front hall.

There were four or five of them, standing close together behind the
doorway. A man in a work cap raised one hand. Beside him was a woman
holding something against her chest. A child leaned against the
doorframe with its head turned at an impossible angle.

They were shouting.

I couldn’t make out the words, but other people on the lawn heard them
too. One of the firefighters started toward the porch before the captain
caught his coat and pulled him back.

The figures didn’t come outside.

As the floor sank, their lower bodies disappeared beneath the doorway.
The woman lifted what she was carrying, and I saw that it was a small
head attached to a strip of unfinished shoulder. The man beside her
opened his mouth. The lower half of his face stretched down his chest,
then tore apart as the wall behind him folded.

The child held on to the frame until its fingers broke.

The whole house dropped into the ground.

Timber struck timber beneath a noise like heavy machinery tearing itself
apart. The chimney went down through the roof instead of falling
outward. Bricks rolled across the collapsing foundation and disappeared.
The porch hung over the opening briefly, supported by one corner, before
it slid after the rest.

Smoke poured from the hole.

I could see sparks moving downward through it, drawn into the ground
against the rising heat.

Then something below contracted.

A broad black surface pushed up between the broken foundation walls. It
was slick beneath the soot, folded into ridges that moved against one
another. Parts of it rose and fell independently, shifting bricks and
timbers as they passed.

There were shapes in the surface.

Some had faces. Others were little more than hands or shoulders, their
outlines pressing outward before sinking back into the black material.
One face opened its mouth wide enough to split along the cheeks. Another
pushed upward beneath a section of skin stretched so thin that I could
see the movement of its jaw.

The whole surface tightened. The shapes flattened beneath it, and the
black mass withdrew into the ground.

Water from the hose lines struck the opening. Steam rose thick enough to
hide what remained.

I sat beside the engine while the crews worked to keep the collapse area
clear. Somebody removed my air pack, and another firefighter helped me
take off my gloves. The right one wouldn’t come free until he unwound
the strip of Ellis’s coat from around my fingers. It was wet, blackened
along one edge, and still had part of the reflective strip attached.

He asked if I wanted him to take it. I told him no.

The woman we’d rescued came over before the ambulance left. Her name was
Nora. She had a blanket around her shoulders, and her son was sitting
with a medic a few yards away. She looked toward the hole where the
house had stood.

“Your partner?”

I shook my head.

She stayed beside me without speaking. When the medic came to collect
her, she put her hand on my shoulder and left it there until he called
her name again.

By daylight, the fire was mostly out. The ground around the foundation
continued to settle, and nobody was permitted close to the opening until
the structural hazards could be assessed. The captain eventually sent me
back to the station. I argued with him, though there was nothing I could
have done by staying.

The official report listed two civilians rescued from the front
apartment and one firefighter lost in the collapse. It included the
additional occupants we’d reported finding, though no one could
establish their identities or explain the material recovered from the
lawn.

The search for Ellis continued after the fire was out. Crews worked
through the collapsed structure as conditions permitted, recovering
equipment, sections of wall, and pieces of the old furnace. The ground
beneath the house had opened much farther than anyone expected. Several
cavities extended beyond the original basement, and portions of the
foundation had dropped into spaces that weren’t shown on the building
plans.

No remains were recovered.

His wife came to the station while the search was still underway. She
wanted to know whether he’d been conscious when I last saw him, and I
told her he had been. When she asked if he’d been afraid, I remembered
his face behind the mask and the way he’d tried twice to tell me
something. I said I didn’t know.

She nodded and looked down at her hands. I’d known her almost as long as
I’d known Ellis, but sitting across from her in that room, I couldn’t
think of anything useful to say. She thanked me for staying with him,
which was worse than anything else she could have said.

When the search ended, I helped clear his locker. His spare boots were
still on the bottom shelf, and there was a sweatshirt hanging behind his
coat. A paper cup sat on the top shelf beside a folded property survey.
He’d brought it in to show me where his neighbor had put the fence.

The department kept me off the engine while I was being evaluated and
until I was cleared to return. During that time, I gave the same account
to several different people. They asked about the fire conditions, the
route we’d taken into the basement, and the moment Ellis became trapped.

I could tell them where the furnace had stood, how far we were from the
stairs, and which part of the floor had opened beneath him. I remembered
the water coming through the broken pipe and the firefighter grabbing my
harness. I described the boy, the gray mound, and the thing beneath it,
though I could see how those details sounded when somebody repeated them
back to me.

The man in the kitchen had asked us to help a woman. The woman had asked
us to help a child. Each time, we’d found exactly what we were looking
for, in a place where a trapped person might reasonably have been.

I kept returning to the moment the boy stepped into the mound. His arm
had felt wrong when I grabbed him, and the mound had begun to move. If
we’d recognized what was happening before the strands reached beneath
Ellis’s glove, we might both have made it upstairs.

But I also knew what I’d do if I found another child sitting alone in a
burning basement.

Nora brought her son to the station in December. His name was Ben. He’d
made a card with a picture of the engine and three people standing
beside it. He’d drawn himself between two firefighters, with a blue
shark on the front of his shirt.

He handed it to me without saying much. Nora explained that he’d been
asking to visit since they’d moved into their new apartment.

We talked about school. He was doing a project about insects and wanted
to know whether firefighters ever rescued animals. I told him about a
dog we’d carried out of a garage fire the previous summer. He asked
whether the dog had bitten anybody.

“Only Ellis,” I said.

Nora looked at me, but Ben was more interested in why the dog had bitten
him. I told him how Ellis had taken off one glove to check the dog’s
collar, and how the dog had caught his thumb. Ellis spent the rest of
the call insisting it hadn’t really bitten him because it hadn’t broken
the skin.

Ben laughed. He wanted to know whether Ellis had been angry with the
dog, and I told him Ellis had been more offended that the dog didn’t
appreciate his help.

That made Nora smile.

I returned to full duty later that month.

My first shift back was quiet until just before dawn, when we were
called to a kitchen fire in a small house outside town. The occupants
were already on the lawn when we arrived. The fire had started in a pan
on the stove and spread into the cabinets. Nobody was hurt.

I worked the call with a firefighter who’d joined our crew while I was
away. He was younger than me and careful with his equipment, though he
still apologized whenever he thought he’d gotten in someone’s way.

He was easy enough to get along with, though he rarely spoke on the way
to a call unless I said something first.

A few weeks later, we were getting ready to leave the station when the
alarm sounded. He was beside me, pulling on his coat as we headed toward
the engine. He’d fastened the front but hadn’t secured the collar
properly. One side was folded beneath the other, leaving the closure
crooked against his neck.

I caught his shoulder before he climbed aboard.

“Hold up.”

He turned, looking down at himself to see what he’d missed. I
straightened the collar and secured the fastening beneath his chin, then
gave it a firm tug.

“I checked it,” he said.

“Good.”

I let go, and we climbed into the engine.
