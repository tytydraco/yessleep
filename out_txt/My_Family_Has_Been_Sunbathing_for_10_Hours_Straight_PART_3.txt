I flinched as the sunlight hit my visor. Muscles tensed, I stood still
in my backyard, waiting for the poison of the sun to take over my mind
once more and sit me down in my rightful place next to my sister.

10 seconds passed. Then 20. Then 30.

…

…

…

I felt the heat begin to swell inside my extensive getup. It became
clear to me that I could only be outdoors for so long at a time in this
winter outfit. Feeling this discomfort, I realized that nothing in my
head had changed. The sun hadn’t taken over my mind. I was still me. My
plan had worked.

Breathing out a shaky, anxious breath, I made my way over to my family,
heading for Bowser first. He was the smallest and easiest target to pick
up and bring inside, so it was the logical choice.

A part of me knew he was already dead. My experience of sitting under
the sun with them had confirmed it. But this was my first and only pet,
so I couldn’t really grasp the concept of him dying just yet. And even
if he was lost to me, I certainly wasn’t going to just leave him out
here. I picked him up off the ground, cradling him with all my strength,
and began walking back to the sliding glass door. He was unnaturally
stiff. His legs stayed pushed out straight even when I picked him up. My
lip quivered as I made my way into the house. I couldn’t look at him
because I couldn’t afford to break down again. I had a job to do. I
needed to save my family. I laid him on his doggy bed next to his water
bowl and patted his head. He looked peaceful and happy. I scooted the
water bowl closer to his dry nose just in case he decided to wake up and
take a drink.

Next, I went to my sister. I stood in front of her, casting a shadow
over her face where she sat. Her wide smile barely wavered, and she
shifted her gaze from the sun to me. I grabbed her shoulders as gently
as I could and said,

“Come on, Carmen, it’s time to go inside, okay? We are going inside. Get
up.”

I tried to help her up, but she wouldn’t budge. Her smile turned into a
frown, and she grunted something that I couldn’t hear through the
helmet. I wondered if she had the cognitive capacity to realize it was
her brother or if she genuinely thought a 12-year-old Master Chief
snowplower had come to kidnap her.

“It’s just me, Carmen. We are just going to go inside for a little bit.”

I grabbed her forearm and gently tried to pull her up. Her skin was red
and raw with blisters, and I knew from personal experience that any
excessive contact with skin this badly damaged could lead to worse
injuries. Despite my gentle touch, she pulled her arm out of my grasp
and grunted again. I hesitated for a second, taking a step back and
deciding what to do.

Free of my shadow, she turned back to the sun once more, her blistering
lips curling into a wide smile. I decided I had to use force to get her
up. It would for sure be very painful for her, but it was better than
dying slowly out here.

“I am sorry about this.”

I went back up to her, grabbed her forearm, and began pulling hard. She
let out a grunt of surprise that turned into a raspy scream of pain as
the blisters under my grip tore open from the strain. My parents both
turned their heads toward us. Carmen’s legs didn’t straighten, and her
head fell back as she mustered every bit of her remaining strength to
resist my grip. Her grotesque, broken screams of pain brought tears to
my eyes, but I didn’t let go. We struggled for a bit, my visor fogging
up from the sweat building up in my suit. Out of the corner of my eye, I
saw my dad very slowly push himself out of the chair, knees shaking as
he straightened out his legs and began to shuffle over to me. I turned
to look at him.

“Dad, please, please help m—”

My father mustered enough strength to hit me in the head with an
open-palm strike, snapping my head back and causing me to lose my grip
and stumble backwards, falling on my butt. Carmen fell back into the
seat, the white handprint of my grip clear on her arm. She turned back
to the sun, tears streaking down her face, her smile flickering as the
mix of the pain of her arm and the pleasure of the rays battled for
dominance in her head. I began crying once more. For the first time in
my life, I was terrified of my own father, who towered over me, swaying
as he struggled to hold up his own tired frame. I wasn’t really hurt. My
helmet had absorbed the blow, but the shock of being hit for the first
time, and by my father of all people, immobilized me. He looked at me
for a few seconds before turning around and hobbling over to his chair
and sitting down again.

As the shock of the situation began to fade, I felt another emotion take
over: anger. Fuming, I went up behind Carmen’s chair, grabbed the
backrest, and began dragging it. I was sweating profusely at this point,
but I didn’t waver. I jerked her chair backwards, again and again,
dragging it through the overgrown grass a few inches at a time. She
began screaming again, seemingly aware of what I was doing. This time,
both my parents began standing up slowly and turning to us. I tried to
pick up my speed.

I just have to make it to the shade. Just a little further. They won’t
follow me into the shade.

I was about halfway to the door when my strength began to waver. I felt
myself begin to overheat from the effort. My parents, in a zombie-like
state, still smiling, kept pace. I could tell how weak they were, how
physically painful it was for them to trudge forward like this after
cooking themselves under the sun for the past 20 hours.

At this point, I think I had completely lost them. They were no longer
my mom and dad. They were slaves to the sun, and I wasn’t complying with
their master.

I am so weak. The thought invaded my mind as the chair became heavier,
the friction of the grass making it harder to pull. I put my head down,
focusing my efforts, and continued to pull, ignoring how close my
parents now were to us.

Just a little further. I just have to make it to the house.

I huffed and puffed, and the chair became even harder to pull. My sister
was howling and crying, even harder than before. I looked up, and my
mother was pulling her other arm, but Carmen had a death grip on the
chair. My dad was coming up to me, hand raised, getting ready to swing
with full force again. I yelled and yanked with all my might, hoping to
break my mother’s grip and pull Carmen and me into the doorway that was
a mere two feet away. My gloved hand slipped, and I stumbled backwards,
trying to regain my balance. The thing that used to be my father was
towering over me once more. He let out a deep grunt like a caveman as he
buried his fist into my stomach. I doubled over, gasping for air, the
wind completely knocked out of me. It felt like I was suffocating. The
mix of the heat and the shock of the pain had me grasping at my helmet,
trying to take it off so I could breathe. I realized what I was doing
and spread my arms out to stop myself. I looked up through a completely
foggy visor and saw my dad stumbling, trying to maintain his composure,
before taking a step towards me, lifting his foot, and stomp-kicking me
in the head.

“Please!” I gasped, head ringing. “Dad.”

He stumbled backwards after the effort from the kick and fell to the
ground, where he lay down and spread his arms and legs as wide as he
could, soaking up the sun and smiling again. My mother was already
pulling Carmen’s chair back with my sister in tow. Her head was slumped
down, and she was breathing heavily. She stared up at me through her
bangs, already smiling again. I was fully overheating now. I crawled on
my hands and knees back into the house in defeat. I pulled off the
helmet and the balaclava and gasped greedily for the cold,
air-conditioned air. Maybe if I spent more time being active instead of
wasting away playing video games, I would have been strong enough to at
least save my sister.

There has never been a time in my life when I felt as useless as I did
in that moment. The bitter taste of failure is often the best motivator
for self-improvement, and that was enough motivation to last me a
lifetime. I still work out excessively to this day so that the next time
I need my body to be strong enough to help someone, I will be ready. My
parents were probably, at that point, suffering from extreme
dehydration, sunburns, fatigue, and aches and pains in general, but they
were still too strong for me. All I had to do was drag my sister, who
was sitting in a fucking chair, into the house 35 feet away, and I
failed.

Another thing about experiencing this level of failure and tragedy as a
child is that as an adult, you don’t have much to cry about in
comparison. In fact, the only times I have cried in the past decade have
been in my dreams with my family and when I wake up from them.

As I lay on the hard floor and watched my dad do snow angels in the
grass and my sister’s chair be placed back in its spot next to my
mother, I cried. I cried and cried and cried. I crawled over to my dead
dog, buried my face in his coarse fur, and cried into him. Wouldn’t it
be a miracle if my tears could soak into his skin, rehydrate his body,
and bring him back to life? Maybe then they would be good for something.
I wished for this all to be over. I wished for this all to be a horrific
dream. What chance did I have against the sun?

………………..

After about a half-hour, I ran out of tears and had no choice but to get
up. I walked over to the sink, turned on the faucet, and chugged enough
water for all five of us. I filled up the biggest water bottle we had,
put on my headgear again, and walked outside to douse each of them with
water. When I held it up to their mouths, they didn’t drink. They just
pushed the bottle away.

I went back inside and filled it up again before emptying the contents
of my school backpack onto the ground and shoving the bottle in it. I
walked up to the front door, giving one final long look back at my
family. They were all sitting side by side again, hand in hand. If I
didn’t find someone strong enough to help me soon, they would die. If I
wasted any more time letting Hudson stay out there alone in that park, I
was sure he would too.

I felt a hollowness inside of me as I stepped through my front door and
out onto the street.

I reckoned I had a real chance of saving Hudson. He was even skinnier
and smaller than me, and he would likely be incredibly weak by now (if
he wasn’t dead), which would make him easy to drag into a house or under
a shady tree. I couldn’t guarantee that the efforts I spent trying to
save my family would ever pay off, and the more I pushed my parents to
react physically, the higher the chance they had of suffering
heatstroke. If I could grab hold of Hudson, I was sure I would be able
to save him, and maybe if he recovered in time, I could suit him up and
we could work together to try and save my sister.

Hudson was my one and only friend in Goldfield. We both sat at a table
with a bunch of jerks at lunch on the first day of middle school who
chose to pick on us two in particular, so we decided to start sitting at
a different table together instead. We traded Pokémon cards back and
forth every day, and he told me about all the mature-rated games his
parents let him play on his brother’s Xbox. His brother was going to
college like Carmen but let him keep the Xbox, which I thought was the
coolest thing ever. Hudson was the reason for most of my interests, even
today. I had no cousins, aunts, uncles, or older friends who could teach
me what was cool in pop culture, and Carmen usually didn’t want anything
to do with me, so Hudson was the source for most of this information. I
thought about how badly I wished this was just another normal day, about
how we would be playing Xbox together, learning new curse words and
trying them out in the Call of Duty lobbies.

The sweltering heat I felt as I marched through the empty road of my
neighborhood stifled my fantasies. The sun wouldn’t even let me have
that.

“I hate the fucking sun,” I growled at my feet as beads of sweat dripped
off my eyelashes. Growing up, I had always resented my condition and
often blamed a lot of my troubles on it. Now, the tables had turned, and
I felt myself growing livid at the sun instead.

“Took my fucking dog… trying to take my family… and my only friend… I
HATE YOU!”

I yelled, looking up at the giant burning ball of light, giving it the
most spiteful middle finger I could muster. Realizing how ridiculous
this looked, I dropped my arm and kept walking. Amongst the many other
peculiar anomalies about this sun, I noticed that looking at it didn’t
have the blinding, eye-melting effect it usually did when you looked at
it. There was something beautiful about it, something alive. I didn’t
expect I would ever understand the secrets of the sun, so I tossed the
thought from my mind.

As I continued to walk, I noticed someone was in their front yard. It
was one of the guys my dad played poker with. I didn’t know his name,
but I recognized his face. He was sitting on his riding lawnmower,
staring up at the sun, a warm beer left untouched in the cup holder.
God, it really did get everyone, I thought as I continued to walk. I
wondered if everyone in the world was affected by it or if it was just
my city. I know now it was just my city.

I saw a few other people I recognized. The old couple who always gave
out full candy bars during Halloween were sitting in their favorite
porch rocking chairs, but in their yard this time. The husband was
slumped over, head buried in his wife’s shoulder, and she was looking up
and smiling. A delivery driver lay in the middle of the road about 10
yards from his UPS truck, doing slow, weak snow angels on the rough
cement, leaving an outline of blood from where his skin was being sanded
down. I tried not to look at him. I saw a few boys who I went to school
with. They had clearly come out to play street hockey but left the net
unguarded and their sticks and puck abandoned on the ground. One was
standing on top of his parents’ car, arms outstretched. One was lying on
the road. One was face down in the grass. I tried not to look at them
either. I just kept walking past, only stopping to pick up a hockey
stick. I figured if I had to fend off anyone else who might try to
attack me, I would do so armed.

Eventually, I came upon the park. The local news van was sitting on the
side of the road with the tripod set up on the walking path close by. I
went up in front of it and saw the red light indicating it was still
recording. I looked past the camera and saw the pile of people, still
churning as it had been on TV an hour or so ago. I had the idea to wave
into the camera and, through my helmet, explain that I needed help
saving my family.

“If there is anyone out there, please help me. I am at Pineridge Park.
My name is Andrew. I am just a kid. I need help saving my family. I
can’t get them inside, out of the sun, because I am not strong enough. I
need an adult. If there is anyone out there, please come help me. You
can go outside if you cover up your skin. The sun won’t change you.”

I thought about what else to say.

“I will give you all our money, my Xbox, and Pokémon cards. I will give
you anything we have at the house. Please just help me save my family.”

I began walking away but turned back to the camera and added, “Thank
you.”

I realized how pointless a plea it was. Even if someone hadn’t gone in
the sun yet and just so happened to be watching the local news, why
would they risk their life to help some random kid in a Halo costume?

I had already seen the pile on TV earlier, but I still wasn’t prepared
for what it was like in person. The amalgamation of flesh was about
three feet high at its peak. Those who had managed to make it to the top
of the pile were stretched out, soaking in the sun, while those
underneath pushed, pulled, and clawed their way up. Hands were reaching
out from between crevices, grasping for light. The pile was composed of
people from all walks of life: old people, young people, children, fat
people, skinny people, strong people, weak people, men and women. Some
were already dead, crushed beneath the weight of others, bodies thrown
off to the side of the pile like it was a living thing shedding its own
skin. I recognized a few faces from around town, but most were
strangers. I saw Hudson’s mother among those at the top of the pile,
lying flat. She was alive, but I didn’t have a chance of helping her. I
had to stay focused on Hudson.

What got to me worse than what I was seeing was what I was hearing. The
symphony of joyful laughter and moans of pleasure was mixed with screams
of pain and weak, muffled cries from under the pile, saying:

“Help me…”

“Help us, please…”

“Someone help…”

I was horrified as the realization hit. Those under the pile were no
longer exposed to the sun. They were conscious human beings, fully aware
of their situation. I thought of the hellish loop they were caught in,
suffering from underneath the pile, fully aware of the situation, just
to crawl to the top and lose themselves to the sunlight before being
pushed down to reality again until they finally gave out and their
lifeless bodies were pushed to the side.

This is hell. That was all I could think. I had to save Hudson from
this. I circled the pile, looking for a flash of his red, curly hair,
listening for the sound of his muffled cry. I heard a crunch under my
foot and looked down. It was his flip phone! I poked around the pile at
that spot with my hockey stick, yelling his name. From between a random
skater kid and the baker my mother always bought birthday cakes from, I
saw a small arm go up with Hudson’s Casio watch on the wrist.

“HUDSON!”

I stepped forward over a dead body, trying to reach for the hand with my
hockey stick. He didn’t grab on but hit it away, preferring the sun to
salvation. I took a step into the pile, trying to get close enough to
pull him out. I stepped on one person, then another, trying to find my
balance on the uneven, shifting, fleshy floor. Someone underneath me
grabbed my leg. I turned back around, trying to kick them off, but they
wouldn’t let go, so I smacked them with my hockey stick until they did.
I took another uneasy step forward and grabbed ahold of his arm with one
hand. He wasn’t nearly as burnt as my family was but still had turned
quite red. I pulled, but he was too buried underneath to loosen from the
grasp of the pile, so I began pushing the skater kid out of the way. The
kid was so weak at this point that he just kind of rolled over and out
of the way before being dragged down by other desperate souls who wanted
to make it to the top. I could see Hudson’s shocked face transitioning
into one of bliss as the sun and air hit him at once. I pulled him up to
the top, where he flopped down and spread his body out. He was so weak
and tired at this point that he didn’t cry or resist as I pulled him
backwards out of the pile. I struggled as I backed up, hands grasping at
my limbs and my hockey stick. I had to hit several people away from both
me and Hudson as we made our way out.

I felt a hand grab me by my sagging, oversized jacket and yank me down,
bringing me to one knee. An arm wrapped around my calf, locking me in
place, and I began to panic, exhaustion and the overwhelming heat
weakening me further, but I didn’t relent. I hit them with the butt of
the hockey stick over and over in their eyes and face until they let go.
The moment they did, someone else grabbed my hockey stick, yanking it
away out of my grasp and tossing it to the side. Another grabbed the
collar of my jacket, pulling me down, trying to force me under the pile.
I smacked them and punched and did everything I could to get them off of
me, but their grip didn’t loosen. I felt like I was going to suffocate.
If I went under, I would surely die, and Hudson would too. I screamed in
terror as I felt myself being forced down into the pile.

“GET OFF ME! LET GO! NO! STOP!!!”

In a desperate move, I smashed my helmet down, headbutting them in the
face. I did it again and again and again until their eyes rolled back,
their grip loosened, and their arms went limp to their sides. I pushed
myself up and grabbed Hudson again, pulling him backwards and out of the
pile, past the dead bodies, past the broken phone, and back until we
made it under the shade of a tree, where I slumped against the trunk. I
was about to pass out, the heat and exhaustion slowly cooking me inside
my outfit. It took everything in my power to reach into my backpack,
grab the water bottle, and pour small amounts of water into Hudson’s
open mouth, which he instinctively swallowed. He was breathing heavily,
too, his smile fading as he slowly came back to reality. After a few
moments of silent breathing, he began to push himself up and turned to
look at me.

“Andrew?”

“Yeah, it’s me,” I said.

I was watching him carefully, looking for any signs of him turning back
to the sun’s grasp. It seemed the shade of the tree was doing enough to
stifle it.

“Holy shit,” he said, looking at me. He turned and looked at the pile.
“HOLY SHIT!” he croaked. “I thought I was in a nightmare. This is real!
This is real, holy shit!”

He started to push himself up to his feet.

I reached my arm out, trying to grasp at him, but my strength was
rapidly fading. I was going to pass out. The shade wasn’t working to
cool me down quickly enough, and I didn’t even have enough energy to
remove any of my gear.

“Hudson, please sit down.”

Hudson was looking at the pile again when he saw his mom lying on the
top, struggling against two men who were pulling her down under. He
stood up, raising a shaky finger and pointing at the scene.

“Mom!” was all he could muster as he stepped forward towards the pile.

I couldn’t move. My body was overheating, and my mind was fading.

“Hudson, no…” was all I could say.

I managed to save Hudson for a grand total of three minutes before he
flopped into the pile and into the sunlight again, crawling on top of
the bodies on all fours. He was light, so he was able to easily crawl on
top of others, moving over his own mother (he had already forgotten his
mission) and making it to the center, where he pushed himself up once
more, stretching to his full height with his arms out, and yelled, “THE
SUN FEELS AMAZING!”

I didn’t say anything. I didn’t cry or get mad at him. He did exactly
what I did when I saw my family in danger. He ran headfirst to save them
and became a slave to the sun. Standing on top of the pile with his arms
stretched, he looked like the king of the world. But he was small. He
was a kid, so when the hands of the adults began pulling him down, he
wasn’t able to put up a fight. They grabbed his arms, his legs, his
hair, his shirt, and his pants and pulled in all directions, trying to
move him out of their sun. Hudson was screaming in pain as his joints
popped out of their sockets, his hair ripped out in chunks, and his
shirt tore apart. His screams became muffled as the tug-of-war match
ended and his body was forced under the pile once more. I felt my head
grow heavy and fall to the side as darkness clouded my vision.

I passed out there under the shade to the sound of my only friend dying.
