I started working nights at St. Gabriel’s Hospital because the pay was
better. Everyone warned me about Ward 7, but every hospital has ghost
stories. You hear enough of them, and eventually they all sound the
same.

“Don’t answer Room 718 after midnight.”

That was the joke.

The room had been closed for years after a fire. There wasn’t even a
door anymore—just a blank section of wall where renovations had sealed
it off. According to the floor plans, the room no longer existed.

Yet every nurse who had worked nights claimed the call bell still rang.

I laughed the first time I heard it.

At exactly 12:17 a.m., the nurse station lit up.

718 – PATIENT CALLING

I looked around.

“Which room is that?” I asked.

My charge nurse froze.

She slowly stood up, walked to the panel, and pressed the silence button
without saying a word.

Five minutes later…

718 – PATIENT CALLING

Again she silenced it.

This happened every night.

No one acknowledged it.

No one investigated it.

Eventually curiosity got the better of me.

One Tuesday, after everyone else was busy with admissions, I followed
the old floor plan stored in maintenance records. The measurements led
me to a blank wall at the far end of the hallway.

The call bell rang.

Not from the station.

From inside the wall.

A faint…

ding.

Then another.

ding.

My stomach tightened.

I leaned closer.

Someone whispered through the drywall.

“Please…”

I nearly ran.

Instead, I knocked.

Silence.

Then scratching.

Slow.

Deliberate.

Like fingernails dragging from the inside.

I should have told someone.

Instead, I came back the next night with a maintenance keycard.

Behind a nearby linen closet was an old service hallway hidden by
shelving. At the end was a rusted steel door marked:

718

The room wasn’t supposed to exist.

The keycard somehow unlocked it.

The smell hit me first.

Smoke.

Rot.

Something sweet.

Inside, everything looked untouched since the fire decades ago.

An IV pole still stood beside a melted hospital bed.

Monitors blackened by heat.

The walls were covered with handprints burned into the paint.

Then I noticed the chart.

A single patient.

No name.

Only one instruction written over and over across every page.

DO NOT LET THEM FALL ASLEEP.

The lights flickered.

The cardiac monitor turned on by itself.

A flatline.

Then…

One heartbeat.

Another.

I heard breathing behind me.

When I turned around, the doorway was gone.

Just another wall.

The room had sealed itself shut.

The monitor suddenly displayed my employee ID instead of a heart rhythm.

Then my full name.

Then today’s date.

A printer beside the bed started feeding out paper.

It was my death certificate.

Time of death:

12:17 a.m.

The exact time the call bell always rang.

The lights went out.

In the darkness, dozens of voices whispered at once.

“You answered.”

Hands gripped my arms.

Cold.

Too many hands.

I couldn’t see them, but I could feel fingernails pressing into my skin.

When the lights returned, I was back at the nurses’ station.

Everyone stared at me.

The charge nurse looked pale.

“You were gone,” she whispered.

“For six seconds.”

I checked the clock.

12:17.

Exactly.

I never told anyone what happened.

I quit two weeks later.

But every night at exactly 12:17, my phone rings from a blocked number.

When I answer, I hear hospital monitors.

Someone breathing.

Then a weak voice.

“Can you press the call bell? They’ve forgotten I’m still here.”

Last week I searched my name online.

An obituary appeared.

It had been published thirty years ago.

The attached photo wasn’t old.

It was taken the night I opened Room 718.

If you ever stay overnight in a hospital and hear a call bell ringing
from a room that doesn’t exist…

Don’t answer it.

Because someone always does.

And the room always needs another patient.
