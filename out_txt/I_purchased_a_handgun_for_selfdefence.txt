A rippling sensation shot through my fingers when they touched the cold
metal in my hoodie pocket. It traveled the length of my hand, up my arm,
and across my body, making my teeth chatter even as I sat in a warm car
parked along Coral Street. Recoiling my fingers, I looked left and
traced the chain store’s architecture with my eyes. My business of
choice sat just across the street in the lamplit night: the Happily Ever
After liquor store, with its glowing pink sign written in an obnoxiously
millennial font. The old brick building’s brown contours didn’t make it
any less full of fake corporate love. It might as well have been painted
stark white like an office building. Either way, everyone saw it as a
capitalist stain on Kensington, which is why I chose it as my target.

I almost laughed when I realized the metal in my pocket that caused me
to flinch was just a loose quarter. Seizing the opportunity to calm
myself, I reclined my seat back and retreated into other, less
incriminating, thoughts.

Gwyneth was probably still asleep over in our apartment, though it
wouldn’t be long before she awoke to stick her head under the bathroom
sink to gulp down that refreshing, copper-tasting, Philadelphia tap
water. I thought ahead and left a note saying I was having sleep
problems again and went out for a run, hoping she didn’t notice my
handgun missing from the nightstand.

About two months ago, I purchased a Smith & Wesson SD9 2.0 handgun for
self-defense. I don’t know a lot about handguns, being a city kid and
all, but the guy at Archer’s Addictions convinced me it was the perfect
bang-for-my-buck defense gun (the “SD” even stands for “Self Defense”).
On top of the practicality, I feel like a cowboy whenever I say “Smith
and Wesson.”

Now it, regretfully, sat in my hoodie pocket, folded in an old shirt so
it didn’t leave a suspiciously gun-shaped dent. If I had known money
would get tight in October, I wouldn’t have spent so much on the damned
thing. Looking back, I didn’t really have a reason for buying it. It’s
not like I’ve ever been mugged. Maybe I was having some sort of paranoia
episode and worried about someone breaking in and hurting Gwyneth. Oh,
right, Gwyneth. That was why. I bought the handgun shortly after Gwynnie
had to start using a wheelchair. She looked like a newborn deer trying
to maneuver the chair around our apartment for the first time; far too
vulnerable to be living with a man without a means to protect her.

My sedan is well over twenty years old with enough miles on the engine
to reach the moon, and I have to kick the driver-side door in order to
get out. In doing so, my mind was so busy filling itself with doubts and
worries that I forgot to check for traffic when I stepped out, leading
to me almost being turned into pathetic red paste on the asphalt as a
truck swerved past me. I leapt back into my seat, hitting my head on the
top of the car’s frame and dropping my shirt-wrapped gun. Vision still
blurry from the impact, I dropped to my knees to retrieve the clattering
SD9 before someone saw it. I figured it must have fallen under my car,
so I lay on my stomach and stuffed myself between the oily street and
the car’s underbelly to find the gun resting behind a tire. Taking
advantage of the concealment, I racked the weapon to clear it before
pressing the little button on the side and allowing the magazine to
drop. I figured I shouldn’t even bring the magazine because I want my
gun to stay unloaded in the liquor store, so I shimmied out from under
the car and tossed it and the extra bullet into the passenger footwell.
The handgun will have to be sold eventually if Gwyneth is going to get a
diagnosis, let alone treatment, but why shouldn’t I try and get some
bang-for-my-buck before then?

“It’s just for Gwynnie. Nobody is going to be hurt. It’s just for
Gwynnie,” I mumbled to myself. It was true, everything I was about to do
was purely for medical expenses, but I needed reassurance. Everything
was planned out, from the time of day to what I wore, but something
tells me I didn’t expect to actually follow through. “Walk in. Browse
until it’s just the cashier and me. Take what I can. Get out through the
back door. Change in the alley. Ditch clothes in the dumpster. Through
Amber Street. Right on Collins. Right on East Stella. Right on
Frankford. Use the empty lot to get back to the car. Play dumb.” I even
dyed my hair and used makeup for this. My eyes, nearly blinded by the
fluorescent lights, stared through the floor as I walked into Happily
Ever After. I could hear the cashier’s half-hearted greeting, but the
last thing I wanted was to make eye contact with anyone. All I could do
was remind myself of Gwynnie and hopefully shove my shame somewhere it
couldn’t pester me. I think I started to tear up.

Emotions were something I was never really good with. I might be a big,
tall man with a face that earned me the nickname “Chief” at work, but at
home, I could be a mess. Gwynnie never cried, though. I guess when
you’ve been suffering as long as she has, you run out of things to cry
over.

Gwyneth suffered from birth. She was born prematurely at twenty-three
weeks. However, the blood pressure complications that followed became
almost obsolete. As she aged, her body refused to develop properly.
Surgeries were initiated in order to ensure her limbs would be
consistent in length. Despite that, one of her shoes needs a custom
platform so she doesn’t lean to one side when she stands. Heart, kidney,
and stomach problems kept her in the hospital for the majority of grade
school.

When I met Gwyneth, which was our junior year of high school, I was
drawn to her resilience. In a flood of pessimistic teenagers, she stood
out as the girl who always had something positive to say. Finding out
she was battling a mysterious illness that, according to many doctors,
should’ve killed her as a toddler made her all the more extraordinary.

Over the course of our relationship, I was gradually introduced to the
ugly side of her complications. I remember a day when Gwyneth and I were
chatting in the waiting room of a hospital. We were there for a
rectal-related surgery that she was not keen on talking about. As
always, jokes were cracked about her “Gwynnie Disease” that would
definitely seem tasteless to the outsider. After the surgery, I found
her, seemingly frailer than usual, in a hospital bed. She was in a good
mood before I had even entered the room. She mumbled, “Surprise,” before
showing me the result of an ostomy. How could I complement her
positivity knowing the love of my life would now defecate in bags
through a hole in the side of her abdomen? Gwyneth outdid me, so I left
to cry in the restroom.

I’m an emotional human being, mostly for my Gwynnie, but I don’t like it
when she sees me when I’m being, well, an emotional human being.
Sometimes I’ll excuse myself so she doesn’t witness my more vulnerable
moments, but she knows. Gwyneth reminded me countless times that
expressing emotions is normal and not emasculating and whatnot, but it
doesn’t change the fact that I still had yet to see her cry.

All these surgeries, procedures, medications, and the dialysis were the
result of her parents’ back-breaking labor. But now that they have no
more money to give, it’s all up to us. I picked out a six-pack of lager
to remain inconspicuous and commenced the agonizing march to the
register.

“Have your ID ready. It’s store policy,” the cashier droned without
looking up from her phone.

I looked up to see if she was side-eyeing me through her blonde wisps,
only to see she wasn’t, before I set the lager on the counter. My right
hand, having never left the pistol grip, was sopping with sweat.

“Just this,” I said with fabricated confidence, waiting for her to look
up so I could initiate my terms. My left hand joined its partner and
rested on the muzzle of my handgun.

“I still need your ID,” she said with an inflection of impatience.

Before I could whip the SD9 out of my pocket, the front door’s handle
clacked. I glanced over my shoulder to see who was entering the store.

In walked a gargantuan man. Even though I’m six-foot-three, he was
looking down at the top of my head with beady, black eyes that lacked
both eyebrows and eyelashes. He raised an ape-like hand to remove the
ascot cap from his wrinkly bald head before adjusting the surgical mask
that concealed the bottom half of his pale face. His brown trench coat
lay over a set of shoulders that had to shift sideways in order to fit
through the front door.

Snap snap.

I returned to find the cashier snapping her fingers merely inches from
my face.

BANG

Something exploded with a force that stabbed my eardrums with an
agonizing ring. The cashier looked me up and down with the bewilderment
of someone who witnessed a murder. My lips parted to question the noise,
but I forgot how to speak when I heard the distinct sound of dripping
liquid. Looking down to see why my hands were suddenly so wet, I found
my hoodie stained red. What could only be blood was pouring from my
pocket into a puddle on the ground.

“Oh shit,” was all I could muster as I slowly pulled my left hand out of
my pocket.

My stomach heaved acid into my throat as I saw my ring finger hanging
off by a lone tendon. I shot myself, and the cashier was now shrieking
uncontrollably.

With the pain in my hand still not registering, I bounded towards the
door, almost running directly into the large man. Before I could move
around him, he grabbed a scruff of my hoodie, anchoring me in place with
his inhuman strength. I tried to kick off the ground and escape, only to
find that he was lifting me by my shoulder and actively moving out of
the store.

“Get the fuck off me!” I screamed, beating my fists against his arm as I
was struck by the frigid air outside.

I felt as if I was punching bags of frozen meat. After being outmatched
in physical strength, I looked up so I could plead with him.

“Come on, man! Look at me! I’m pathetic! I got nothing! My girl’s gonna
die, and I’m just trying to save her!” I yelled with pure desperation.
We were alone outside, and I was confident he could crush my neck like a
soda can if he wanted to.

To my surprise, and also my relief, he sat me down against the brick
wall of the liquor store. But just as I thought he would let me go, he
instead pressed his palm against my sternum and revealed an orange
hunting knife. I hopelessly wriggled as the knife moved closer to me.

He pressed the blade into his own arm, the one that kept me pinned to
the wall. Trying to scream, I could only gag as the smell of potent
feces gushed into the air around me. A black, syrupy liquid began to
flow from the wound as the blade submerged deeper into his colossal
forearm. Releasing the knife, he reached behind my back and grasped the
wrist of my mangled hand, which only now began to sting with pain. I
didn’t bother resisting as he used my open flesh to wipe up the warm,
soupy mess on his arm. Between the smell, the searing pain, and the
sensation of body-temperature sludge sliding down my arm, I passed out.

When I awoke, I was sitting in my car, parked outside of my apartment
building. With the sudden influx of memory, I snatched my left hand and
began fidgeting with my ring finger. My hand was uninjured, and I
released the most relieving exhale of my life. It looked like I had
fallen asleep before I even started my drive to the liquor store and
dreamt the whole ordeal, hence the dribble on the front of my T-shirt.

Assuming my nightmare was an act of God dissuading me from robbing
Happily Ever After, I climbed over the center console and slipped out
through the passenger door, as I couldn’t kick my way out in such a
drowsy state. When I did so, I felt my SD9 press uncomfortably against
my crotch. Confused, I pulled the handgun from my waistband.

“Since when did I appendix-carry?” I muttered to myself. “And where’s my
hoodie?”

Before I could further question my oddly-placed firearm and missing
hoodie, I looked down at the SD9 to see that not only was it missing a
magazine, but it had a used casing sticking out of its side; I think
it’s called a stovepipe jam.

I must have looked like a psycho, staring at this gun for nearly ten
minutes, but bitter cold was starting to make me shiver. Not wanting to
even think about it, I moved to place the weapon in my glovebox along
with the magazine I found in the passenger footwell. The gun wouldn’t
fit in there until I removed the wad of paper shoved in there.

“What? What the hell is this?” I mumbled, removing the paper.

It was a pair of crinkled tickets. They’re valid for this Friday at the
Lowell Theatre. I don’t remember buying these.
