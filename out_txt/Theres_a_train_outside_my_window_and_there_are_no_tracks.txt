I’m sitting on a cold metal bench at a railway station outside York,
England, and something is wrong. I keep telling myself there’s a
reasonable explanation, but I can’t stop hearing the announcement that
just crackled over the loudspeaker.

“Passengers for the last service, please remain where you are.”

I’m twenty-four, a junior accountant at a small office in the city
centre. On Mondays I stay late to finish invoices before the weekend.
Tonight I left at 09:45 p.m., bought a cheese sandwich from a petrol
station, and hurried here because the weather has turned bitterly cold.

My train is delayed. The departure board says it will arrive at 10:09
p.m., so I eat my sandwich and wait. There are only three other people
on the platform: an elderly man in a brown coat, a woman holding a
sleeping child, and a railway employee pushing a yellow cleaning
trolley.

At 09:51, the lights above the opposite platform go out.

I assume the station is closing that section for maintenance. Then the
loudspeaker crackles again.

“Passengers for the last service to Bellweather, please proceed to
platform four.”

I look at the departure board. There is no Bellweather service listed.
I’ve never even heard of Bellweather.

The elderly man stands up immediately. He folds his newspaper, tucks it
beneath his arm, and walks toward the staircase. The woman with the
child follows him. The railway employee stops pushing his trolley and
watches them leave.

I check my phone. No signal, although I was sending messages just
minutes ago.

A train appears on the opposite track.

It moves so slowly that I can see every window. The carriages are old,
painted a dull green, with yellow lights glowing behind dirty glass. No
destination displays, no advertisements, no numbers on the doors.

As it passes, I notice people sitting inside. Every passenger faces the
window. None of them move.

The train stops with a long metallic squeal. Its doors open, and warm
air drifts across the platform. It smells of damp clothes and something
faintly sweet, like flowers left too long in a vase.

A conductor steps down. He wears a dark uniform with brass buttons and
carries a small leather ticket punch. His face is ordinary enough, but
his skin looks unnaturally pale beneath the platform lights.

“Bellweather?” he asks me.

“No,” I say. “I’m waiting for the York train.”

He glances at my wristwatch. “You’re late.”

“Late for what?”

He looks past me toward the station entrance. “Your train has already
arrived.”

I turn to the departure board. My service is still marked DELAYED. When
I look back, the conductor is standing closer.

“Ticket, please.”

“I don’t have a ticket for your train.”

He holds out his hand anyway.

Behind him, the passengers remain perfectly still. Near the front
carriage, an old woman sits with her hands folded in her lap. A young
man beside her wears a school uniform that looks decades out of date.

Then I notice the window nearest the conductor.

A man is standing inside the carriage, staring directly at me.

He wears a navy work jacket with a small tear near the collar. His hair
is dark, his face narrow, and a thin scar runs through his left eyebrow.

He looks exactly like my father.

My father died when I was eleven. I remember that scar because he got it
falling off his bicycle when I was little. I remember the navy jacket
too. My mother kept it in a cupboard for years before finally giving it
away.

The man inside the train raises his hand and presses it against the
glass.

I can’t breathe properly.

He mouths something. I step closer before I can stop myself.

His lips form two words.

“Come home.”

The conductor grabs my sleeve.

I pull away so violently that I nearly fall onto the tracks. He doesn’t
look angry. He looks disappointed.

“Not your stop, then,” he says.

He steps back into the carriage, and the doors close.

As the train begins to move, every passenger turns their head toward me
at exactly the same moment. The man who looks like my father keeps his
hand against the glass until the carriage disappears into the tunnel.

The platform lights come back on. My phone finds its signal. The time is
10:09 p.m., and my real train is pulling in on the opposite track, its
familiar blue-and-white carriages crowded with tired commuters.

I board and sit beside a window. I don’t tell anyone what I’ve seen.

When I get home, my mother is still awake in the kitchen. She asks why I
look so pale, and I tell her the train was delayed.

Then she notices something in my hand.

It’s a small paper ticket, yellowed at the edges, with a punched hole
near the bottom. I’ve never seen it before.

The printed destination reads BELLWEATHER. Beneath it is a date: 14
November 1998.

My mother takes the ticket from me. She stares at it for so long that I
ask what’s wrong.

She says my father was travelling home from work on that exact date when
his train was cancelled. He called her from the station at 10:09 p.m.
and promised he’d be home soon.

He never arrived.

The next morning, railway staff told her no train had been scheduled on
his route after eleven.

She puts the ticket on the kitchen table and tells me never to mention
it again.

I go to bed. I tell myself that’s the end of it.

Then I wake.

The clock beside my bed reads 10:09 p.m. A train is passing outside my
bedroom window, slow and heavy, and I live miles from the nearest
railway line.

I cross the room and pull back the curtains. Beyond the streetlights, a
long green train is gliding through the darkness, though there are no
tracks beneath it.

Every carriage is full. Every passenger faces my house.

And in the last window, my father stands with one hand against the
glass.

This time, he isn’t looking at me. He’s looking at the empty space
beside him.

My bedroom phone starts to ring.

I haven’t used that phone in years. It isn’t connected to anything.

My hand shakes as I lift the receiver.

A man’s voice, quiet and familiar, says, “Son, there’s a seat saved for
you.”

The line goes dead.

Outside, the train has stopped. Its doors are open.

And right now, as I stand frozen with the receiver in my hand, I hear
someone walking up the stairs toward my bedroom door.
