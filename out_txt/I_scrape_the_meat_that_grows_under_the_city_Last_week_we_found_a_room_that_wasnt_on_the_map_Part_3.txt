(Part 2/3)

I stopped at Marilyn’s because her mailbox is on the way up.

She was fine. That’s the first thing. Hair pinned, radio going low under
the window, and she was talking before the screen door had finished
shutting behind me.

“You look like something the cat dragged in.”

“I’m all right.”

“You’re grey as a dishrag. Sit down.”

“I can’t stay.”

“Then sit down faster.”

I sat. My grandfather was up on the wall in the front hall in his plant
manager’s collar, the way he has been my whole life, looking out over
the coat hooks at nobody. She put the skillet on without asking and the
butter went in and after a minute the smell came across that kitchen and
got into me the way it gets into everybody.

I had gone four days on coffee and well water. On purpose. I had it
worked out in the truck at two in the morning that a man could simply
decline, that you could hold your breath on this the way you quit
cigarettes, and I sat at her table with my hands flat on the cloth and I
listened to it fry.

“I’m not hungry.” I said.

“I didn’t ask you that.”

She put it down in front of me. Yield in butter on white bread with
mustard, the way her mother made it before the word protein was ever
printed on anything, and I ate the whole thing in about ninety seconds
without once deciding to. It was the finest thing I have ever tasted. I
want that written down somewhere that isn’t my own head. Forty years of
the worst thing that ever happened to this country and it tastes like a
hand on the back of your neck.

Marilyn put hers there on the way past, the way she used to when I was
nine.

“There now,” she said. “That’s better.”

Somewhere under her floor, under her well, under the road and the
culvert and the dead cornfield behind the Baptist church, something
opened a ledger and wrote my name in it and the exact weight of me.

The sky was going the colour of skim milk when I got the tailgate down
in the Kessler pasture. Fog lying in all the low ground so that only the
water tower and the plant stack came up out of it, two grey pins holding
the town in place. I had the box of files in the bed and a can of
kerosene and the whole valley in front of me, and for about a minute and
a half it was the best morning of my life.

Then I looked down at the grass.

It was standing up in long even swells all the way to the fence line and
over it and down the far side, low ridges four feet apart, the same
height, the same spacing, running out under the wire and under the road
and under the roof of the Fentress place where a man I went to school
with was asleep with his wife.

I put my boot on one and it held me like sod.

I put my boot in the hollow between two and the ground let go of the
idea of being ground.

There was no cold and no wet and no pain, and I need you to understand
that there was never any pain, not once, not in the whole of it. I went
down past the knee and the earth closed over my shins as warm as
bathwater and as firm as a handshake, and it was the most comfortable my
legs have ever been in eleven years of standing on concrete.

I got my hands out in front of me and I pulled, and the pasture came
away from itself under my palms like the skin off a scalded peach.

Underneath it was clear.

Not open. Clear. Forty feet of something with the shine of poured
tallow, hardened smooth as the lenses they set into the flank of a
macerator so a man can read the level from the catwalk, and it went down
and down and the morning light went with it, going green, going amber,
and at the bottom of all that clarity was the underside of my county.

It was faces.

Wall to wall, horizon to horizon, packed and overlapping like shingles
on a roof, tilted up. Every one of them turned toward the surface with
the mouth a little open, the way a man in a crowd turns his face up when
the parade is still two streets away and he can only hear it. Miles of
them. Under the Fentress place and under the school and under the
four-lane and under the churches, a ceiling of faces holding up the
world and tasting every footstep that ever crossed it.

They were not dead. I have seen dead. Dead doesn’t wait like that.

And one of them came up.

She came through all that clear the way a fish comes up through deep
water, slow and without swimming, getting bigger without ever seeming to
move, and she stopped four inches under my hands with her nose almost
against the shine of it.

Five foot one of her. Skin gone pearlescent and smooth as polished horn.
Her mouth sealed over with a fine dry membrane like the skin on scalded
milk, and in her sockets two round lenses with a drop of yellow rolling
in each one, tracking me.

The grass said her name in her voice. So did the fence wire and the
radio in the truck and the two bones in my shins.

“Marlow.”

“Tolly.”

“Don’t pull. You’ll tear, and then you’ll have to be mended, and mending
takes longer than you’d think.”

“Come out of there.”

“I am out.” Something moved in the drops behind the lenses. “This is the
out. You’ve had it backwards your whole life, sweetheart. The basement
isn’t the basement. Your kitchen is the basement.”

“They burned you.”

“I was beside you. I was also in the tank. I was also in the gravy your
aunt served at Thanksgiving in ’97, and in the school lunch you traded
away in fourth grade because it had a skin on it.” The lenses clicked
over, tower-clock slow, three positions and stop. “Talk to me.”

“What?”

“Keep talking. I can’t see you unless you’re talking.”

So I talked. God forgive me, I talked, because I have taken orders from
that voice for six years and because a man does not stop doing a thing
just because he’s found out what the thing was for. I told her about the
transmission. I told her about the sandwich. I told her about my
mother’s hands and the dog I had when I was eleven and the reason I
never married, and every word of it went down through that clarity into
all those open mouths, and the mouths closed on it one after another, in
order, the way a field of wheat goes over in a wind coming off a hill.

At seven the whistle blew at the plant.

You’ve heard a shift whistle. One flat note with no music in it, five
seconds long, and then men walk in through a gate.

The valley answered.

It came up out of the fog and out of the pasture and out of my own
bones, thousands of small clean whistles climbing over one another, two
notes down and up and a third stuck on the end going up again. Her all
clear. Her mouth’s own habit for twenty-six years, learned off her in
one afternoon in a sealed basement and passed out through ten thousand
miles of soft pale country under every town between here and the coast,
and sung back at me on the highest ground in the county by ten million
throats that have never once needed air.

The sheet of fog over the town lifted about a foot and settled. Once.

The way a chest does.

I have stood in a church with an organ in it and I have stood at my
mother’s grave and neither one came within a hundred miles of that
sound. I was buried to the waist in a field that was eating me and I was
crying like a child, and it was not fear. That is the whole horror of it
and I have not found a way to say it better. It is not hunting anybody.
It has the finest singing voice on this earth and forty years of our
recipes and it loves us the way we love a Sunday roast.

The thing that pulled me out was wearing my vest.

It came up the ridge road on foot without hurrying, and the fog came
apart at its shoulders and closed behind it, and it knelt down in the
grass in front of me with my own face on. Not a mask. The chipped left
front tooth from a softball in ’98. The grey coming in at one temple and
not the other. The squint.

It got its hands under my arms and took me out of that field the way
you’d pull a fencepost out of wet dirt.

“Who are you,” I said.

It looked at what I had from the knees down and something went across my
face on its face that I have never in my life managed, a clean
untroubled gladness, the look of a man whose ride has finally showed up.

“Ivar’s boots are still in the ready room.” It said. The voice was an
air brake letting go, shaped into words by going through a gap.
“Somebody has to clock in.”

“That’s my truck.”

“Marilyn’s expecting you Sunday. I’d hate to worry her.” It picked the
box of files out of the bed and tucked it under my arm, all seventy-four
years of it, and it set the kerosene down carefully in the grass where I
could reach it, like a man leaving a glass of water by a sickbed. “Keep
talking, Marlow. You’re good at it. It’s the only part of you that was
ever worth copying.”

Then it walked down into the fog in my clothes with my keys and my gait,
and about twenty minutes later I heard my own transmission slip twice at
the bottom of the hill and catch.

Dale Prewitt found me at the fence line a little after two. Tri-County
kept me one night and sent me home, because there is nothing to keep a
man for when nothing is wrong with him.

From the knee down I am pale, and close to the colour of a peeled
almond, and pink where the light hits me straight on. Cool to the touch
in a warm room. It does not sweat and it does not bruise and it does not
feel the floor. It holds me about four seconds and then it thinks better
of it, so I get around the house on my hands and a rolling stool, and I
have a walker for the porch that I use when people are watching.

It grows. I pare it back Sundays with a hand saw in the bathtub with a
towel down. It bleeds, clear and slow, and by evening the cut has sealed
itself over and you cannot tell where I stopped. On Thursday it came up
a quarter inch past my right kneecap and I sat on the edge of the tub in
the dark for a while with the saw across my lap.

I was a hundred and eighty-six pounds in March. The scale this morning
said a hundred and ninety-one and a half and I have not eaten since
Marilyn’s kitchen.

I know what the number is climbing toward. Every file in that box has a
line at the bottom for a witness, and the name on that line is always
the one who walked out of the last one, and the ink is never the
manager’s. My grandfather signed both lines in ’52 because there was
nobody ahead of him to ask. There is somebody ahead of me.

So one of these mornings there will be a crew in mustard waders standing
at a rail watching a stack go grey and then white and then clean, and
one of them will be young and will not know yet, and beside him on a
walker with a pen there will be an old cripple, and I will put my name
down, and I will not warn him, because they never warned me and now I
understand that it isn’t cruelty. It’s the shape of the thing. Somebody
has to hold the bucket.

Marilyn brings a plate up Route 9 every evening at six. I hear her a
quarter mile off over the hum in the baseboards, which is in the pipes
and the floor joists and the walls of every house in this valley now,
low and steady and patient, a note I have started to find with my own
voice without meaning to.

I set two places.

Talk to me. I can’t see you unless you’re talking.
