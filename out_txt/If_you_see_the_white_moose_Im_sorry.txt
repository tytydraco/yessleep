I haven’t seen my best friends since the winter of 2020, when we were
eighteen and stupid. It’s just one of those things. You see each other
every day in high school, you grow up, COVID happens, and you all
scatter to the winds. Silas graduated from art school, moved twenty
minutes away, and only answers texts months after you send them. Like
we’re still sending letters in the 1910s or something. Tonya teaches
high school in Texas and occasionally has mental breakdowns on Facebook.
And Jake had a messy breakup with the girl we both liked and then
disappeared to join the Marines, ruining my friendship with her and
temporarily destroying the friend group as a whole. Such is the nature
of high school friendships. I’m surprised so many of them survive our
adulthoods.

I’ve made other friends here and there. Coworkers, mostly. My fiancé’s
circle has taken me in like I’m a bird with a broken wing. But I’ve
never had friends like Silas, Jake, and Tonya. Maybe it’s because I’m
older and it’s a different world. There are no 24-hour Walmarts to bum
around in. The mall by my house has closed down. Running around the
woods with sticks is fine when you’re eighteen, but when you’re
twenty-five, it loses a little luster.

But none of that matters now.

I’m getting married tomorrow, on the land my fiancé’s family owns. It’s
nestled deep in the pine forests: a well-kept farmhouse with acres of
fenced meadows behind it, then deep woods farther back. Five years of
living together, finally cemented in a legal contract and a gold ring. I
guess that’s romantic.

He’s having his bachelor party across town. There will probably be
strippers or lap dances and light-up shot glasses or whatever else
you’re supposed to have at a bachelor party. It’ll probably be more fun
than mine. I bought ‘pin the junk on the hunk’ from Spencer’s, but no
one seemed interested.

Instead, we decided to go to an arcade bar in town. The air is smoky,
and the counters vibrate from the cheesy eighties music being pounded
from the speakers. I’m entirely sober while my friends must be at least
eight shots in, and I try not to eye the half-finished Modelo in Jake’s
hand. I’m watching Tonya and Silas bat a puck across the air-hockey
table, drinking in every detail.

Tonya has a few stray gray hairs. She’s wearing a Hawaiian dress and
dangly hoop earrings. Silas has not aged a day. He’s wearing the same
plaid shirt he wore six years ago when I last saw him. Same gray hoodie.
His eyes sparkle with the same intelligence as he drunkenly dogs a movie
he watched a month ago. Jake sips his beer and looks at me. He has the
same pale blue eyes, same dark hair. He has stuble now and more muscle
in his arms, but he’s the same guy I would scale parking garages with
and join on long, midnight drives. The same guy I’d steal cigarettes
from. The same guy I shared the best nights of my life with.

He chugs the rest of his beer and clears his throat. “Allie, can we
talk? Alone?”

“Sure.”

My ears are ringing as we leave the bar, and I turn down the cigarette
he offers me. I mumbly ask him not to smoke, and he cocks his head,
shrugs, and slips the cigarette back into the package. I stare at the
yellow filter longingly.

“I missed you,” he says.

“You could’ve texted me.”

He shrugs. “Not the same.”

“I know.”

He plays with the cigarette box, drumming his fingers on the
plastic-encased lid. He draws in a long breath, his eyes flicking
between me and the ground. I wait. Finally, he asks: “Do you remember
that night?”

I lean back and close my eyes. “That night” can only mean one thing. I
had tried to forget about it for years. When Jake had said he’d seen ‘a
cryptid’ on one of his adventures, I’d offered to come with him. We sat
crouched together on a tree branch ten or so feet above the ground,
listening to each other’s breathing. It was February, freezing cold. We
inched together, our breath clouding in the air.

Before I saw it, I had this feeling. Every hair stood up on my skin, and
I thought I would fall off the branch. I knew. I felt it deep in my
bones. I knew this would be our last adventure together.

And there it was, ghostly silent. Massive, bigger than the Clydesdale
horses that would sometimes trot through town at Christmas. It was a
moose, its shining hide so white we should’ve spotted it from miles
away. And there wasn’t even the crack of a stick or the crunch of
leaves. Its antlers gleamed like a rack of bones, bigger than any buck’s
I’d ever seen on my occasional hunts. Its nut-shaped eyes blinked red as
it lifted its massive head. It nosed and pawed the dirt, silent. Jake
trembled beside me, his warm hand suddenly squeezing mine.

I looked down at the moose, at our clasped hands, and then at Jake’s
pale face. He yanked his fingers away, and we watched the moose lumber
deeper into the woods. Our last adventure.

“I think about it all the time,” I say.

Jake’s eyes trace the cracks in the sidewalk. “Do you think…I mean…do
you ever wonder…” He stops. Starts again. His hands clutch limply at his
sides. “Do you ever think about what could’ve happened…with us?”

I should be furious. Why the fuck would you bring this up the night
before a wedding? But instead, I shrug. “Sometimes. We were always
dating someone else or crushing on the same person. It just never worked
out.”

“Do you regret it?” he asks. He pulls a cigarette from the pack and raps
it against his knuckles. I close my eyes again, remembering the
bone-like antlers of the thing we saw together. There aren’t supposed to
be moose in Virginia. There’s the Bellwood Elk, elk maintained on
government property in honor of John Bellwood’s legacy. And there are
zoos. But the thing we saw, the thing that is supposed to herald ill
omen, was something entirely different. Something that shouldn’t exist.

“I think I’m pregnant.” It comes out suddenly. I hadn’t even told Dakota
yet. “I hid the test. I’m gonna look at it after the wedding.”

“Oh.” He breathes. He smiles, but his eyes are flat. He fumbles to
pocket his cigarettes. “Well, just pretend I haven’t said anything.
Dakota sounds like a good guy. I’m glad you’re happy. I…I just want the
best for you.”

I nod. I feel cold all over, like the cryptid’s red eyes are still on
me. “I think we should get back inside.”

“Oh, yeah, yeah, of course.” Another nervous smile. He pats me on the
shoulder and then stuffs his hands into his pockets. We walk back into
the bar, noticeably farther apart than before. “Don’t worry,” he says.
“I’ll drink enough for the both of us.”

***

The bride and the groom aren’t supposed to see each other until the
wedding, but how many hours before is up for debate. Dakota and I are
staying at the farmhouse together for the night. I’m sitting on the
screened-in porch, trying to read and push the nerves away. It’ll be a
small ceremony. Just family and close friends, then the honeymoon. I’m
trying not to think about the pregnancy test, tucked in a cardboard box
and hidden in the bottom of my suitcase.

It’s a cool spring night. The wind plays across the grass, crickets
chirp, and owls hoot from the dark forest in the distance. The air
smells of unseen flowers, and I rock back in my chair, watching the
clouds roll along the skyline.

Dakota tumbles through the door, laughing. He takes a few stumbling
steps, squeezes my shoulders and plants a wet kiss on my hair. He tries
to sit across from me and knocks the chair over.

“I love you,” he says. “I missed you. Oh God, my stomach.” His eyes are
cloudy from the booze. I grab his hands. I don’t know what I’ll do if
the pregnancy test comes back positive. I know he’ll be a good father.
He’s a wonderful teacher, well-loved by his students. He’s the kind of
man who spends a significant chunk of his paycheck on donuts and
crafting supplies for the other teachers and students. I help him to his
chair, shush him, and we watch the woods. Another owl hoots. Lightning
bugs blink against the treeline, and distant frogs croak.

And then, silence.

Dakota’s eyes flash with fear. He reaches over and squeezes my hand, so
tightly it hurts. His body goes rigid. “Do you see it?”

Nothing. And then—

A white shape. Red glowing eyes in the distance.

It isn’t supposed to be here. I’m two hours south of where I saw it
last. I’ve never told Dakota about it; I didn’t want to think about it
myself. “Stay still. Don’t move. Maybe it’ll go away.”

It’s moving closer, close enough that I can see its crowning rack of
giant antlers. They glimmer like a crescent of smiling teeth in the
moonlight. It’s looking straight at me, those red eyes cold and
unblinking. Dakota whips his head toward me, his eyes suddenly clear.

“That thing is evil.”

I can’t move. Can’t breathe. “What are you feeling?”

“I just, I just have a bad feeling.”

“Like what?”

I’m staring at the moose. Its long white legs gleam like exposed bones.
It lowers its head and noses the grass, its body so white that it’s
almost glowing. “Dakota, you need to tell me.” I try to keep the rattle
out of my voice. “What are you feeling?”

“Like I’ll never see you again.” He stands up. The chair clatters to the
ground, but the animal outside doesn’t seem bothered by the loud sound.
“I have to kill it.”

“No!” But he’s too fast. He races out the porch door, and I follow into
the cold air. I know his favorite concealed-carry gun is tucked in his
waistband. But before he left to go drinking, he emptied it.

Something he must’ve forgotten.

He runs so close to the creature that he’s almost nose-to-nose with it.
He puts the gun against the animal’s shoulder. The gun clicks. Nothing.

The animal lifts its head. I’m frozen, and so is Dakota. I can’t move,
can’t speak, my own fear so thick that it roots me to the grass. Maybe
it’s not an ill omen. Maybe it just signals change. Or maybe it’s just
an animal, nothing more and nothing less. But I’m so afraid that I can’t
move, and neither can Dakota. He just stands there, the barrel of the
gun still pressed to the animal’s ghostly hide.

It flicks an ear and snorts. It pushes its nose against Dakota’s hair,
like a friendly horse. I hold my breath.

And I watch helplessly as the animal rams its antlers through Dakota’s
throat.
