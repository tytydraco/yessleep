Everyone in the house was asleep. I’d taken the batteries out of the
wall clock in my room to get rid of the ticking. Now there was complete
silence, and I could try again to do something I’d already managed to do
successfully.

A week ago, I’d turned over a piece of paper lying on my desk, with a
poem I’d written in primary school on it. It was my first success after
years of practice. Six days ago, I’d unscrewed the cap of a juice bottle
that hadn’t been closed all the way. Five days ago, I’d moved one of the
cigarettes in an open pack slightly outward, out of the pack. Three days
ago, I’d peeled off one of the pieces of tape holding up poster on my
wall.

And tonight, my target was a much larger object. I was confident in my
abilities now, and after this final attempt, I would be able to reveal
my talent to my family and those around me. I had made exactly four
attempts in the past week, and I’d succeeded every single time. I had
complete faith in myself.

For years, I’d tried countless times and gone through a lot along the
way; I’d lost hope, taken a long break from my attempts, then started
believing I could do it again and after trying over and over, I’d come
to think telekinesis was nothing but a lie and taken another break but
then one day, I happened to see a pack of wet wipes fall to the floor
all by itself, which rekindled my interest and got me started on my
attempts again, and so on.

And now, finally, in the middle of the night, when complete silence had
been achieved, I went to the kitchen. I took a steak knife from the
drawer where we kept the cutlery and returned to my room.

I gently pulled my desk chair back and sat down. I didn’t want to lose
my focus, so I was being quiet. The faint sound of the knife touching
the desk as I set it down, the soft sounds of my footsteps as I walked,
the barely audible scraping of the chair as I pulled it back had an ASMR
effect on me. These little sounds, too quiet to disturb the silence,
made me feel thoroughly prepared.

Without wasting any time, I focused on the knife. I positioned my hands
around it and gathered all my concentration. I slowed my breathing. The
only thing in my mind was a knife slowly and calmly spinning around its
own axis. I focused, and focused… I concentrated as if someone were
hypnotizing me, as though I were following a pocket watch swinging from
side to side without ever taking my eyes off it.

Minutes passed. Seconds, minutes, maybe an hour… There wasn’t the
slightest movement. The knife couldn’t care less about me; it was
ignoring my efforts, stubbornly refusing to budge.

Eventually, I couldn’t take it anymore and gave up. I was getting more
and more bored, and as time went by, I lost my focus. I had to go to
work at noon the next day, and earlier that day, before I left work, my
boss had pointed out that I’d left my fly undone. I’d started thinking
about that. Who says something like that in front of everyone? You pull
someone aside and tell them in private. Anyway, I took the knife back to
the kitchen.

After I lay down in bed, I thought about the knife until I fell asleep.
I’d lost my confidence in my abilities. If I told someone I could move
objects through telekinesis, they might ask me to prove it, and I might
fail and make a fool of myself.

I couldn’t get the knife out of my mind. I felt as though it were a
person who had shattered all my trust in them, betrayed me by revealing
a secret I’d shared with it to anyone who would listen. I was angry. The
image of the knife standing perfectly still in that spot, like a tree
that had sunk its roots deep into the ground, wouldn’t leave my mind. I
fell asleep in a fit of anger.

I woke up to the sound of my phone alarm. I turned it off, tossed the
duvet to my left, and rolled onto my right side to get up. Just as I was
about to stretch, I saw it and jolted in place: the knife was suspended
in midair beside my bed, standing upright.

I clapped a hand over my mouth. I was about to scream; every instinct in
my body was telling me to. I bit my tongue to suppress it.

I took a deep breath. I tried to calm myself down. Then I took another
deep breath. My heart was racing.

I reached out toward the knife, but immediately changed my mind. I
didn’t know what to do. I’d never experienced my thoughts speeding up
and getting tangled together so intensely in a moment of panic. Within a
few seconds, I’d made and abandoned a dozen decisions, gone back and
forth between asking for help and not asking for help, imagined the
knife stabbing itself into my body, then dismissed the thought as
irrational. My thoughts were racing back and forth at lightning speed. I
needed to calm down.

I got out of bed, poured half a glass of water from the pitcher on my
desk, and drank it.

I turned around to check if the knife was still there. It was closer to
me now. I no longer cared about staying quiet. I yanked the door open
and rushed out of the room.

I started walking briskly toward the kitchen. I wanted to grab any drink
I could find and gulp it down. I needed to interact with something
somehow, to calm myself down. I opened the fridge, took out the milk,
and chugged it. I put the milk back, closed the fridge, and turned
around to go back to my room.

And the knife was right behind me.

It was a meter away from me, just standing there. It was as if it were
staring at me, judging me. It didn’t move at all. It always maintained
the same position and stayed at the same height above the floor, level
with my bed, right around the height of my shinbones.

I started walking backward toward the kitchen doorway, wanting to see
how it was following me, how it was coming after me. Without changing
its position, it glided toward me as if it were moving across a
perfectly smooth surface. It always maintained the same distance. When I
stopped, it stopped a metre away from me. When I walked, it followed.

I cautiously made my way back to my room, walking backward. I sat on my
bed. The knife and I were facing each other. I tried to think of what I
could do. I was afraid to touch it. I was worried things might get out
of hand. I’d done this. That much was obvious. Now that I’d calmed down
a little, my panic had shifted in a different direction. Someone might
see us. I had to sever my connection with the knife before I got caught.
I might be able to accept this as normal, but I didn’t think my mother
and father would be able to handle a knife following me around.

Yes, it was probably because I’d concentrated so intensely on the knife
all this time that the bond between us had grown stronger. I’d probably
never concentrated on a knife so intensely in my entire life. I needed
to reverse the process. I relaxed a little. I tried to think about other
things, to take my mind off the knife.

I thought about the wristwatch my father had bought me for my birthday
last year. It was a terrible watch, but I hadn’t let on because I didn’t
want to hurt his feelings. Then one day, while wearing it, I’d
deliberately gone swimming in a pool. The watch had stopped working, and
that was how I’d gotten rid of it.

The knife was still in the same place.

I thought about the eggplant soup my mother had made yesterday. It was
my favorite soup. I’d tipped the bowl back and gulped down the last few
mouthfuls, and my father had given me a stern look.

The knife hadn’t moved at all. It was standing there, a meter away.

My mother knocked on the door and called out.

“Breakfast is ready, Albert.”

I had no other choice. I didn’t know what was going to happen, but I
reached out toward the knife. My arm couldn’t reach it, though. I walked
toward it. As I moved closer, it moved farther away, until it had backed
all the way up against the wall. It had nowhere left to go. It was
pressed against the wall, stuck there. I approached it and grabbed the
handle.

My heart was pounding at full speed. I closed my eyes. As a final
attempt, I thought about the baseball bat I’d lost before I became
interested in telekinesis. I’d searched everywhere for it. Such a large
object, yet it was nowhere to be found. I’d checked the baseball field,
my grandmother’s house, the streets I’d walked along, and I’d even
contacted the lost property office in case I’d somehow left it on a bus.
It was gone.

Yes, good. I could feel a long chain of thoughts forming and my focus
weakening considerably.

The knife was loosening, letting go. I could feel it. But I still didn’t
have complete control over it.

I needed to think about something else. But I couldn’t think of
anything. I heard my mother’s footsteps approaching my room. She was
getting closer to the door.

“Come on, son. Are we supposed to wait for you?” she said from outside,
then opened the door and walked in.

I pulled the knife with all my might, and just like when you yank
something hard that’s stuck somewhere and it suddenly comes loose, I
went flying toward my bed.

With the knife still in my hand, I hit my head against the bed frame.
And passed out.

When I opened my eyes, I was lying in a hospital bed with an IV drip in
my arm. My father was sitting beside me. I felt exhausted. I’d received
three stitches in my head, and it ached a little.

“Son, why were you holding a knife? What were you doing with it?”

“What knife, Dad?”

“You were clutching a knife in your hand. What do you mean, what knife?
What are you up to?”

I couldn’t remember anything. That was good. I hoped it wasn’t temporary
memory loss, because I didn’t want to attempt telekinesis again.
