I first realized I might want to try caving when I thought about
crawling into the oil well on my parents’ property.

I had never gotten too close before. Mom and Dad had forbidden me all my
life, when the workers were around, when the machinery was still, and it
had never really called to me. The pumpjack loomed too harshly against
the horizon, austere and violent, and when the workers labored, it
bellowed out awful noises and swung like Themis’ scales. The bulk of the
apparatus honestly still scared me, as well as the idea of the
suffocating, iridescent sludge within, but the second it occurred to me
that it all tied to a tunnel into the earth, something lit up in my
brain: some hubris, some excited sector of castigation, that overrode my
faculties and had me stumbling out in the twilight to leer at the lip of
the well from a short distance, fingers toying idly with the smooth
plastic of the lighter in my pocket until I had the fleeting thought
that flicking the spark wheel might blow up my entire family.

I clasped my clammy hands in front of me instead and approached. It
wasn’t terribly interesting; just an old steel tube with a rotten and
sickly sweet stench that wafted up from the depths of a long, dark hole.
I thought about sitting on the edge of the steel, dangling my feet over,
and lowering myself down, down into those depths to see what was on the
other side.The sirens’ song of a crush injury.  But the squeeze wouldn’t
be tight enough, I thought.

That night I dreamt of a smaller well, a sort of plaything for a child
make-believing about oil barons. My ears rang and the well screeched,
but still I climbed inside as if the sounds were simply a lullaby
drawing me to my bed. I barely fit, the walls snug against my ribs and
hips and shoulders, but as I let gravity suck me down, I knew what it
had felt like, the thing that never became a memory. In the dream, there
was only the idea of pain. I’m not sure what happened in the end; I woke
up sharp and sudden in my bed, at first nearly frightened, and then
almost satisfied.

The next few days were spent on daydreams of the well and recollections
of long past similar situations. Crawling through plastic playground
tunnels when I was a child, hiding in closets with my friends as a
drunken teenager, sneaking into a festival curled up in a car trunk.
These were fine, almost suitable distractions, but I dreamt of them at
night, too, along with what I was more keen to be distracted from: the
vision of a glistening coffin being lowered into a cozy grave and
suffocated with dirt. Sometimes I woke up with soil under my nails.
Sometimes I could almost taste earth and clay in my mouth.

These nightmares and mysterious stains were a bit too much for me. I was
never incredibly tolerant. I figured I just had a bizarre, slightly
macabre new hyperfixation that my mostly-ghosted therapist would
probably easily link to trauma, and I just as easily assumed that I knew
how to remedy it. I’ve indulged in worse before, so I texted my friend,
Moore, from college, who does spelunking and skydiving and all the other
adrenaline-producing shit rich kids pay for when they get bored and want
a taste of manufactured danger. He briefly pretended to care whether I
had experience in the field and accepted my redirection without much
concern for my or his wellbeing. I think he worked in risk management;
it’d probably given him some sort of alarm fatigue.
My experience, of course, was nonexistent, and his was too low to take a
clueless charge down into the bowels of the uncaring earth, but maybe he
did believe me when I hinted at knowing a bit more than nothing. Maybe
it was some combination of attraction and indifference; maybe it was
pity. There’s a chance it had even been some misguided, cold vitriol
that had inspired him to allow himself the opportunity to see me dig my
silver spoon into rock and mud, but regardless, he didn’t share his
motivations if he had them, and instead emailed me a time and place, as
well as a brief checklist:

-   helmet
-   knee + elbow pads
-   headlamp (800 lumens)
-   rainboots 
-   backpack (water, food, batteries, extra headlamp, 3 flashlights)

I was glad for the succinct nature, the lack of request for a chat over
dinner or a briefing over coffee. I hadn’t spoken to Moore since
college, didn’t have anything good to catch him up on, and wasn’t
terribly curious about his own life beyond his ability to go where I
never had. My interest wasn’t as locational as it was geological; the
act of discovery and the sights within the caverns of our region weren’t
of as much importance to me as the scrape of stone and the kiss of
closeness. I’d be lying if I said I fully understood it, but then again,
I’d never chased the motivation behind my impulses.

I didn’t tell anyone where or when I was going, what my plans were, why
I had a sudden urge to deeply inconvenience myself by squeezing into the
bowels of the earth. Survivalists and explorers, from my limited
research, say you should always let someone know when you plan to return
in case of emergency, but I assumed Moore would take care of that. I
didn’t talk to many people anymore other than my parents, at least
during the daylight hours, and despite my adulthood, I had the feeling
my family would try to dissuade me from my adventure. To them, it would
seem woefully dangerous at best and passively suicidal at worst, and I
didn’t care to listen.

I also didn’t care to listen to Moore, who spoke more than I remembered.
His mouth was open the second I climbed out of my car, my boots
crunching onto the sparse gravel of the informal parking spot located on
the shoulder of a weaving, forested road. He’d been rummaging through
packs when I rolled in, working by the hatch light of his SUV and the
dim morning light that filtered through the leaves above, but he whirled
when I approached, squinting at his phone, at me, at the woods around
us. “You’re a little late,” he said, getting a good look at my shoes.

“Does it matter?”

“Yes,” he said. He didn’t sound annoyed, but he didn’t sound thrilled.
He’d always been a little too blasé for me. Maybe it was his general
demeanor, just a calm guy, but I didn’t like the lack of a leg to stand
on I had with him. It would be better if someone was excited at the
opportunity to show me the hidden innards of the ground, explain their
weird little hobby to me in a way that I could tune out, grab my pack
for me and clip me to whatever I needed to be clipped to. Instead, he
was methodical about the whole thing. “You bring everything I told you
to?”

I nodded. It was a simple list of provisions for what was supposed to be
a pretty simple day expedition. I’d stayed awake a lot of nights now
looking through pictures and go pro footage of different cave systems,
Mammoth Cave, Jewel Cave, the Carlsbad Caverns, but I think any of them
would suffice for what I needed.

“You’re gonna fuck up your jeans.”

I blinked at him. He hadn’t specified much for clothing beyond the
required safety equipment and general coverage, but he was wearing a
stupid-looking yellow polyester suit. I had on sturdy pants, long
sleeves with a jacket, the boots and pads and gloves required of me.
“That’s okay,” I answered, and he blinked back, shrugged, and bestowed
into my hands a crumpled windbreaker from the trunk of his vehicle
before he slung his pack over his shoulder and started off into the
woods.

The lock systems on our rides beeped their sharp goodbyes at us as we
trekked down the worn trail. I pulled the windbreaker over my head at a
brief pause and had to hustle to catch back up with Moore, who wasn’t
purposefully hurrying but was still moving at a pace I wasn’t accustomed
to. It was early fall, cool and damp in the after dawn hours, but I
could still feel clammy perspiration building up under the insulated
layers of clothing I had on, trickling down my spine like a nose bleed.
I took shallow swigs from one of my plastic water bottles as we ventured
along, then a surreptitious pull from a patterned tumbler. Moore only
looked back when I stumbled over rock or root, but he told me generally
of the cave we were marching to.

It’s probably not completely undiscovered but it’s not well documented.
No official trail signs leading us towards our destination and Moore
says no maps or videos online of it, either. He’s been in once or twice,
spurred by the spirit of exploration to branch away from the more
well-traversed cave systems in the nearby area, and he says it’s simple,
roomy at its best parts in ways that mimic a hike and with a few tight
squeezes, but nothing snug enough to be dangerous, no sumps or pits or
other vocabulary terms I didn’t recognize that he had to explain. There
had been relics of dusty old equipment when he’d ventured in, beer cans
and food wrappers illuminated by the beam of his headlamp that indicated
it had been touched not just by civilization, but by the heedless,
youthful population of humanity that meant it should be an easy trip for
people like me. It was maybe a bit reckless on his own part to take me
along into a largely unexplored cavern, but I didn’t feel any
trepidation as I followed him. All I really felt was the lull of the
forest around me, the wet chill of perspiration and autumn, the burn of
my calves as I traipsed along the half-trail and of my throat when I
took a drink between blurs of still green trees and dirt.

“Becca.”
“Hm?” I crunched to a halt behind Moore. He was standing still at the
edge of the clear cut through the brush, and I raised my eyebrows in a
mirror of his expression as I re-did my hair tie that didn’t need to be
redone.

“I asked if you’d talked to Lacey’s parents. You heard from them at
all?”

This did make me feel something other than boredom, for just a second. I
pulled at my hairband a little too tightly and snapped it, cursed and
fished another out to yank my ponytail back. I shook my head while I did
it, pursing my lips like I had something in my mouth I couldn’t let go
of, then said, “No,” when he kept looking at me. It was simple, and it
was partly a lie. I had received several messages from Lacey’s parents
and her sister. My parents had received calls, and our reps had received
emails. We just hadn’t responded to any. My mother had brought up the
idea of me calling them back, but when I thought of that, I wasn’t
smothered tight by the earth or by metal or a wooden box; I was spinning
aimlessly up, up through the air, in a direction I didn’t understand. So
no, I said, and no, I meant, but it wasn’t for their lack of trying.

Moore accepted that easily, with neither pleasure nor derision, and I
took another drink. Down, down, swirled the embers, like a whirlpool,
like a tumbling hunk of metal on a hill, down the way I wanted to go.
Moore stepped down, down, a gentle incline through a raggedy grove of
foliage that he parted and pulled until a swath of rock was revealed.
There was a generous enough entrance cut into the stone, jagged and
short enough to have to stoop into, but nothing like a mailbox slot.
Nothing like my oil well, my wooden box.

“Gypsum,” Moore said with a hint of pride. He tilted his head towards
the cave entrance. “Others in the area are mostly dolomite. S’a karst
forest. I think.” He shrugged, kicked a stone away with a scuff of his
boot, and explained none of those words to me. With one last
scrutinizing look at my gear, he bent to step into the maw of the rock
and reminded me, “Make sure you do what I say. Let me know if anything
is too much for you.”

I ducked in behind him. The entrance to the cave was pale and glittered
softly in the brief rays of weak sunlight that managed to worm through

somber, colder than the outside in temperature even at this shallow
depth, but more so, empty of the vibrant life of the forest. If there
was life in this cave, it was near silent; we were the loudest things,
our footfalls interrupting and echoing, and only when Moore paused to
flip on the bulb of his headlamp did I hear it. Almost inaudible, more
of an idea, a gentle whisper of air came from the depths. It came in
stilted puffs, only truly able to be felt once I had heard it, and they
were just barely warmer than the air around us. They caressed my face
like the breath of an animal, and their smell was mostly stale and
stagnant and earthen like that which surrounded us, but tinged with
something like metal and decay. I wrinkled my nose. Moore took a deep
pull.

Breath in and out through working lungs. A drink down. My headlamp was
flicked on like the high beam of a car, by someone else’s fingers, Moore
reaching back and taking the wheel, and the cave came into wet repose
again. Dampness clung to the walls and ceilings and floors like spit,
and everything was beautiful and boring and awful. It was a harsh, alien
landscape, dirty white stone and nonsensical edges. It felt like
stepping over tooth and tongue and into mouth, into gullet, into lung.
This was the throat of the world, breathing quietly, frozen and gross
and–I shrieked as a spider the size of my palm skittered across the
ground in front of me, my boot striking out and missing. Moore swung his
gaze and his light towards it, catching a streak of amber body as it
disappeared into a hovel of rock, and he laughed.

“Yeah, there are animals in these sometimes. Usually just bugs, bats,
but I’ve seen scorpions and stuff, too. Gnarly little guys.” He stepped
on ahead, his footing surer than mine. “This is mostly just walkable,
but there’s a little pass up ahead, and the other side is pretty. Little
cramped on the way in, but plenty of room for standing and exploring
once you’re through.”

In the cave, all was dark except that which our light illuminated. We
were glaring intrusions under the stalactites, and I felt artificial,
like I was in a dream. Everything was a bit too quiet; all the noises
were us and the distant blowing breath. Moore swept his headlamp over
different rock formations, took pictures and videos and pointed things
out to me, and I looked dutifully but distractedly. I was uneasy, but
not claustrophobic; in fact, I had too much room. I could stand to my
full height and stretch my arms out almost fully to the sides to run my
covered fingers through the sweaty trails inching down the cave walls.
To Moore, I may have looked reverent, but I felt like I was free
falling.

I stopped greedily when he halted our expedition in a small dip of the
system. It was off a left or right turn; I hadn’t been keeping careful
track, but he’d left cracked glowsticks along the way to aid dimly on
our way back. He sat down on a mineral ulcer jutting from the ground and
I crouched. We ate quickly. I was surprised to find that I was hungry. I
hadn’t felt it, but we’d been walking for a couple hours, so it made
sense. I drank from a crinkling water bottle and the sloshing tumbler
and my eyes blurred momentarily, but they righted themselves as I waited
for him on the cave floor. We didn’t move on till he was ready; he had
the wheel, and my life was in his hands.

“This is the squeeze,” he announced easily, pointing out a hole that
carved itself into the rock wall a few feet above the ground. It looked
natural, eroded, and like an altogether bad idea. He’d been through this
way before, so I knew it was big enough for his larger frame to crawl
through, and I could see it in the width as well, but it was still
overwhelmingly small in context. My heart hammered insistently for a few
beats as I stared and half listened to his explanation of technique,
some foul mix of fear and excitement.

“Yeah, I can do it,” I told him when he asked. I wanted to do it, and so
I would. Maybe this was really what drew me underground, the opportunity
to overcome hesitance and nervousness and to do something through sheer
willpower. I watched as he folded himself into the orifice and shuffled
inside. It wasn’t beautiful or graceful, but it was practiced. He went
slowly but easily, and there was enough room for his pack to clear the
ceiling. I let him disappear and slough through stone until he
presumably made it to the other side, and his echoing call came through
the tunnel, too quiet to understand but enough to hear. I brushed stray
hairs behind my ears and bent at the knees in front of the mouth and
called back. “What? I can’t hear you!”

For a moment, all was silent except for my own pulse, and I felt the
exhale upon my face through the squeeze. If it blows, it goes, I go, I
blow. My ears rang in response to the nothingness and my vision blurred
gently at the edges again, like sleep encroaching, and then the wind
from where the sky can’t touch whistled again, like static like sirens
like screaming like a choir singing, and bubbled into another garbled
response that was half Moore’s voice and half not. I squinted and
recoiled, and it groaned again, the tunnel, before I heard, “Bec!”

“S-sorry!” I didn’t give it time. I didn’t think twice. I twisted myself
in an approximation of the shape Moore had been in when he entered the
tunnel and shoved myself forward. My footing wasn’t great, and the
rubber of my boot slipped against the slick floor, but I was able to
push pull drag myself forward, until I was in. My body hunched in crawl,
forced down unnaturally by the low ceiling, and I took a moment to get
my bearings. I was surrounded by walls the color of aged bone,
pockmarked by time and dirty water, and it was all close enough that if
I shifted, I would collide with it in some manner. I took my first
tentative drag forward and shuddered as rock bit into my leg through my
pants. Mud smeared down the parts of my body that brushed the floor, and
water dripped down onto my helmet, ran into my eyes and over my lips. I
felt like I was lost to the earth, already buried alive, and the air was
full of false promises as I sucked it in. But it was perfect, and I kept
going, inch by inch and foot by foot through the tunnel.

It was almost euphoric, having the itch scratched gently after all the
dreams. This was maybe the first time in my life I had truly known what
I wanted. I crawled and bent as the ceiling lowered, scooting myself
forward more by the toes of my shoes and the pads of my fingers than
anything. Something scuttled by me again in the small breadth of space
between myself and the left wall, but this time I didn’t scream. I
imagined it crushed by my body as I moved forward. I imagined myself
wrapped tight by the earth. It was a mother’s embrace, and I was the
petulant child, the one who angered her. I winced as I scraped my helmet
against the ceiling, laughed breathlessly to myself, and realized I was
at a very narrow point. Far ahead, I could vaguely see the glow of
Moore’s headlamp.

Toes and fingers were not bringing me forward anymore. The rock above me
held my head back like a trick when I tried, and when I bowed close
enough to the ground, I couldn’t move my limbs in the right order
anymore. I faltered for a moment, wrapped in the world, seemingly unable
to move. “Get down on your stomach, Becca,” Moore called down the
passageway, flickering his light. “It’s just an army crawl. You got it.
Breathe out if it feels too tight.”

I lowered myself belly to earth and looked around with bright, concerned
eyes. How terrible would it be to be stuck here? A shift forward, just
an inch or so. How long could you survive in this exact position? A
scoot that got me a little farther this time. How scary would it all be?
A drag powered by my weak arms. Would it hurt more or less than a car
crash? Moore’s headlight loomed in the distance, almost blinding me when
I stared straight down the tunnel, so I fixed my eyes down with the
little room allotted to me and kicked my splayed leg to move me forward.
The ceiling was still a good half foot about my back, but this time, I
didn’t move. I couldn’t feel if my foot had lost its purchase or if I
was snagged on some fishhook jutting from the walls, but I hastily
returned my leg to its position and pushed forward again. Nothing.
Stagnant, still, and I tried to turn to look behind me, suddenly
overcome by the feeling that grave things were coming from behind me and
clinging to my limbs, but all I succeeded in doing was thunking my
helmet against the wall. I swore and sucked in a ragged breath and tried
to pull this time, but I was so disoriented by the cessation of movement
that I wasn’t sure if I was moving at all.

“I’m stuck!” I shouted to Moore, who wasn’t that far away at this point.
“I don’t know what on–”

“You’re fine.” I was not fine, but he sounded sure enough. “It’s not
tight enough to actually be any trouble. Just relax, breathe out, and go
slow. Take your time. It’ll work.”

My fingers twitched and scrabbled against the rock, my breath getting
away from me. I felt feral in my nerves and my excitement. My lungs
emptied out with a stuttering breath, I closed my eyes, and I toed
forward again. This time I felt the scrape of movement, and I held my
breath, latched on, and inched forward until I was dizzy. It was only
minutes until I tipped over the lip of the tunnel, addled and
cumbersome, into the next room. My shoulder hit the ground hard, and I
giggled mindlessly for a second before I got the gall to crack my eyes
open.

“You good?”

Moore hovered over me, his face pinched, but I focused on the cavern
behind him. It was beautiful. I nodded, breathing out my pain and
accepting his hand. He tugged me up and I saw the chamber upright. Much
of it was shrouded in darkness, but he had his flashlights arranged in
an illuminating, outward facing circle that shed light on some of the
closer features beyond the scope of my headlamp. The walls and ceilings
were formed from the same glittering gypsum, but gossamer tendrils and
rosettes spiraled from the rock like marble carvings. They looked
delicate enough that a single touch would break them, but drops of water
rolled over their edges into standing pools of water across the muddy
floor. The water rose only around shin deep, but it glimmered foggy and
serene in the still cave, and the same tendrils reached up from the bed
like coral flowers. Blind fish and pearly snails hid amongst the mud and
pebbles, like tiny bones come to life, and I crouched down beside the
little pond. It was like I had been suffocated in the tunnel and born
again to a strange eden.

“This is really beautiful,” I said, and it was sincere. I watched as a
drop of water from above sent a placid ripple through the water. “How
did you know this was here?”

“This is where I ended up before,” Moore replied, shrugging like it was
matter of fact. “You’ll see stuff like this a lot in the hobby, but it’s
usually when it’s already been found by someone else. Most of the time
it’s still pretty untouched, but I kinda like that this is just for me
and a few other people so far. No trash in here, so I’m not even sure if
the teenagers tried it through the tunnel. Lame, ’cause that’s an easy
crawl. Usually the good sights are on the other side of some stuff
that’ll seriously kick your ass.”

It was a nice thought, finding something special on the other side of it
all. There wasn’t much to the cavern all in all; a bit of space beyond
the light, more alabaster spirals and tranquil water. I stepped
carefully around the pools and watched creatures mill guilelessly
through the beam of my flashlight. I was approaching what would soon
turn into boredom or complacency when I saw it, new, less than
beautiful. A mass the size of a softball undulated through the foggy
water, and as it drew closer, I saw that it appeared to be a clew of
worms tangled together, red as blood and moving through the pool like
they were each the searching limb of an organism. The knot moved over
one of the bone snails and when it passed, the shell was still.

“What the fuck.” I grimaced and turned to head back towards the exit
before I saw anymore worm amalgams, only to jump when Moore was standing
perfectly still behind me. His eyes were wide and the rest of his face

“Yeah, that’ll happen,” he said. He tightened his grip on his flashlight

I crossed my arms and followed the beam with my eyes, squinting through

smaller than the one we’d entered from, shaped oblong and low. The

“I thought there was,” he said hurriedly. His hand still trembled and

meant something altogether different to Moore. It must be something like
the thrill of discovery, or maybe the chance to ensconce himself so far

“This wasn’t here when I–I didn’t see it the first time. I don’t know. I

“It looks tight.”

“Is that a problem?” He eyed me with a look I hadn’t seen in a long
time. He wasn’t trying to strongarm me into it. He looked beseeching,
like it would make him vibrate out of his skin if I allotted him the
opportunity to crawl into this tunnel. I didn’t know if this was all
protocol or not. The knowledge lay with Moore, and it seemed like he was
handing me the control. I turned my attention back to the narrow
opening, just wide enough for him to slip into, just barely more than

together this time, how he’d talk me through any tight spots and we’d

stupid and crawling into this hole would give me what being cruel and

done and still lived. This would be how I found it. I cut Moore off

It was a snug fit. Moore disappeared into the cleft in the rock slower
this time and shouted back at me when he felt he had cleared enough
ground for me to follow. He’d had to shimmy off his pack and shove it
ahead of him, so I copied the tactic as I bent and twisted myself enough
to fit into the entry. It was awful immediately. Rock pressed at my

up from a belly crawl, the ceiling bit at my scalp and spine. My
flashlight had just enough room around my pack to illuminate a short
distance in front of me, showing rock and narrow path and Moore’s shoes
farther ahead. I shuffled forward, feeling terrified and embraced and
correct.

Every inch was arduous, much harder than the first tunnel, like the
earth itself was rejecting us. I had to shed my helmet and push it ahead
with my pack, but I told myself that if Moore could make it through,
then so could I. The only way we knew there was somewhere to make it
through to was the air; if it blows, it goes, and the quiet puffs of air
emanated out from the tunnel. In the claustrophobia, I thought that they
even felt stronger, sounded louder, like the source was closer now. We
surged forward like devolved creatures, half blind and wriggling through
the dirt and over stone, holding back conversation to keep from
inflating our lungs enough to make it harder to traverse the depths. We
went so long without speaking that when Moore cursed under his breath,
it scared me so badly that I flinched upward and met the rock skull
first. I gasped loud and felt an immediate wet rivulet trickle through
my hair, dripping onto the ground before me.

“You good?” Moore called over his shoulder, pausing his forward
momentum.

“Yeah, just a–a gash on my head,” I replied. I wanted to reach up
instinctively and feel at the wound, but my arms were pinned too tight
for that. I settled for wrinkling my face to try to direct the blood
from my eyes and taking a breather. “What made you–” I didn’t finish my
question, because I quickly saw the answer. A clutch of amber spiders,
at least a couple dozen, streamed past Moore in the tiny cracks left
around his body, on ceiling, floor, wall. They scuttled over his legs
and shoes, directly towards me, and I cried out and tried to shove
myself back to no avail. They weren’t interested in me, of course, but
they weren’t dissuaded enough by my presence to give me a wide berth as
they worked their way around me either. I felt phantom limbs brush
through the cut on my head and squeezed my eyes shut, wanting nothing
more than to stand and run in that moment. I kept my eyes closed so long
that Moore had to call out to me again, and I shook my head as much as I
could, although he couldn’t see. “Maybe we should turn back. I don’t
want to crawl into a nest.”

“Probably not in the tunnel,” he reasoned, his voice distant and muffled
by the rocks surrounding us. He pulled himself forward a few more inches
and muttered something, but it was muffled again not just by the stones,
but by a whistle of air and the sound of loud scraping. I froze and saw
his feet do the same ahead of me as the sound grew louder, or closer,
and I assumed the worst. We were going to be truly crushed in a collapse
or at least trapped here in this coffin. I shrieked and Moore started to
say something that was probably meant to calm me, but it was cut off
this time by a lurching gurgle. It sounded like the noise someone makes
when the driver stomps on their brakes too suddenly and they’re choked
by their seatbelt. There was nothing after. His feet moved neither
forward nor back.

“Moore?” Nothing. “Moore!” Nothing from him, but the scraping returned,
and this time I felt the ground quiver with the wind that blew in. It
carried the same smell as earlier but stronger, earth and brimstone and
tangy copper. I tried to push myself backwards quickly again on
instinct, like with the spiders, the fleeing fucking spiders, and got
nowhere. The walls of the tunnel shook the vodka in my veins into
bubbles and rattled my skull, and I grabbed for useless purchase on the
ground, only to feel something wet touch my fingertips, dampening my
pack. I jerked my hands back as much as possible in the cramped space
and fixed my flashlight on the offending liquid. Thick streams of blood
clumped with hair and something gray writhed through the mud and fed the
dirt, and for a second I thought my head wound had cracked open and
spilled my viscera onto the ground before me. I stopped breathing,
frozen by an impossible sight, and could only swallow hard as I pressed
down my pack and shone the light on Moore’s shoes.

A river of blood flooded out from underneath him. He was perfectly
still. I nearly jerked my shoulder out of the socket maneuvering my arm
so I could reach forward and tug at the heel of his boot, my voice still
trapped in my throat. He didn’t respond at all, but the ground shuddered
and breathed again, and the steady scraaape echoed through the tunnel.
The dim edges of my light barely penetrated the tiny space between his
back and the top of the ceiling, but I could swear I saw the rock move
downward. Not violent and sudden like the cavein I had feared, but slow
and methodical. Like teeth coming down. Like something was chewing… I
felt the barest twitch of his foot and my heart hammered so hard I
thought I was going to die right then and there in the tunnel from shock
or excitement that he hadn’t just been killed and left me here alone.
But the twitch wasn’t him trying to signal to me; it was the rubber of
his heel slipping out of my soaked fingertips as the jaws of the tunnel
pulled him forward just slightly, just a few inches, enough to leave a
streak of blood in his wake.

I found my voice then. I screamed and I probably swore, but my ears rang
so loudly that they blotted out all rational thought in my mind other
than the breathing of the cave, the chewing of the cave. I tried in vain
a third time to shove myself free of the mouth of a stone beast and was
met again with the catch of stone against me. I watched as the tunnel
bit down on Moore’s body again, through the last bit of recognizable
head I could see over the hump of his shoulders, and I realized in that
moment that it wasn’t just pulling him forwards into whatever horrible
rock maw it had; it was moving forward as well.

I could’ve passed out. I really wanted to pass out, actually, to go
blissfully unconscious and not have to deal with the fear of what was
approaching me, the total confusion regarding what was happening to me,
and the inevitable crushing pain. Instead, my brain stayed woefully
awake, my nerves singing in anticipatory pain as I watched the tunnel
eat Moore’s body. Everything in me trembled, but I started to work
myself backwards at the same painstaking pace I’d had to crawl forwards
with. It felt wrong. It felt like strolling away from a tsunami. It felt
like driving through a guardrail. It was slow and difficult and I could
barely tell I was making any progress at all, and all I had was the hope
that the rock was slower. I wished I was sober. I should’ve gone in
sober. Maybe this was all some horrific hallucination caused by the
combination of alcohol and cave gases, I thought, but then I saw the
ceiling come down on my discarded helmet and crush it like an eggshell.
I don’t think I’ve ever been as scared as I was watching Moore slide
away, the stone inch closer, followed by gore and death and
impossibility. But I don’t know if I’ve ever been as relieved, either,
as when my toes slipped off the edge of the entrance finally.

It took maybe five more awful minutes to work myself back far enough
that I could tear myself from the wall. I fell from the entrance and
expected to hit hard ground, but instead I crashed into something
slippery and slick. I fumbled my flashlight as I tried to get my footing
and found that the floor of the cavern was covered in shining white
mucus. It clung to my clothes and body as I wrenched myself free, and I
backed away from the hole in the wall slowly. It was breathing loudly
know, exhaling rot and fear into the cavern, and I wondered if the teeth
would follow, or if that was the mouth. Maybe the whole cave system was
the mouth. Maybe I was in the stomach. I stared for too long,
recklessly, until I felt a stinging on my forearm and looked to find a
clump of the blood red worms from before, wriggling and intestinal, some
with metallic pincers sunk into my skin. I shrieked and ripped it away,
but the shining penny-colored prongs took a chunk of my flesh with it,
and the skin left around the bite began to redden and swell immediately.
I turned blindly on my heels at that and splashed through the snot and
saliva and water filling the cavern, coughing and wretching as it soaked
me. The flashlight circle Moore left had been covered so I just ran like
a madman until I found it, the first tunnel, and threw myself in
headfirst.

At this point, after the tightness of the other crawl and the
consumption of my friend, it was the easiest thing I’d ever done. I army
crawled forward, soaked to the bone in blood and congealed mess, and
didn’t stop until my flashlight flickered out. I banged it against the
ground to restart it, and it waxed and waned through dim light, but the
insides had probably been damaged by the fall or the moisture and soon
it darkened permanently. My other lights were in my abandoned pack,
so–fuck it, it was a straight shot. I forged forward through the
darkness, crawling, crawling, quickly and then as fast as I could as I
felt wind at my feet and heard the rumbling scrape of stone behind me.
It was pitch dark for what felt like miles, and I imagined myself
trapped in a labyrinth down there, running like a rat until the earth
caught up to me, but then the blue sheen of a glowstick far, far in the
distance caught my eye. Salvation, eden, heaven, paradise was above
ground and I would cherish it well. I dragged myself forward with
renewed vigor and collapsed out of the tunnel’s mouth once more, into
the navigable beginner caves I’d been promised at the start of all this.

The smartest thing at this moment would’ve been to follow the glow and
run back towards the surface, but some stupid animal thing in my head
had taken over, some sort of testament to Moore’s exploratory spirit,
and I turned back to the tunnel. The glowstick didn’t leave near enough
light to see anything substantial by, but something in my head insisted
that I see if anything was following me. I didn’t have my pack anymore,
but I did have the plastic lighter in my pocket. I fished it out with
haste, held it close to the entrance while standing back like I was
ready to run, and flicked the flint wheel with trembling fingers.

I didn’t see anything. I don’t know if the force of the explosion
knocked me down or if I dropped to my knees of my own accord, but I
ended up splayed on my back on the cave floor as the spark from the
lighter ignited and presumably devastated the tunnel. Rubble from above
sifted onto my face like snow, and it was all I could do to right
myself. My head swam and I almost stumbled back the wrong way, convinced
I was heading towards the glowstick before I realized it was an eruption
of blue flames. I coughed and turned again, stumbling at the fastest jog
I could muster and navigating by the comparably pathetic glowsticks,
gripping onto walls to lead myself out. My eyes stung worse than the
flash burn when I first saw the light, but I reached towards it like a
child to its mother all the same and tripped forward, forward, forward,
until I was out of the cave’s mouth and spilling onto the mud. I cried
out and rested my face against the ground for just a moment before I
pulled myself to my knees, wiped my nostrils clean, and started the hike
back to the cars.

Sunset was tinging the horizon when I reached them. I wasn’t afraid.
Being above wasn’t scary. I had foolishly taken my keys into the cave
with me, but I tried Moore’s passenger door and it popped open easily.
Risk management my ass, I thought as I gingerly climbed in and reached
up to the sun visor over the driver’s side which, sure enough, had his
keys sequestered away against the mirror. I took them down in a hand
almost too weak to grip them fully and hoisted myself over the center
console to collapse in the seat. It wasn’t lost on me that just hours
ago, Moore had sat here, and now he had succumbed to the earth. I didn’t
know what it all meant. I didn’t understand why that had happened or
truly what had happened at all. I knew I would live it over and over in
my dreams, and that I would remember this time. But I don’t think it was
my fault. I don’t. That’s comforting to me, as I turn the keys in the
ignition and reach into the pocket of the windbreaker to feel at the
grooves of the patterned tumbler stowed away there.
