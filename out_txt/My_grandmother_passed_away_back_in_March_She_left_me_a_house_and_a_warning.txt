My grandmother passed away back in March. She was 94. Her mind had
completely deteriorated from dementia during her final years, so her
passing wasn’t exactly a shock. The real shock was her will. The
attorney handed me the keys to her old two-story house along with a
crumpled piece of paper. The notary actually apologized, mentioning that
my grandma had thrown a massive fit in their office, refusing to leave
until they attached this specific note to the legal documents.

Written in her shaky, uneven handwriting, it read:

“Alex, I am so sorry. She made me sign the papers. She choked me at
night until I finally gave in. Do not go down into the basement. Not
because of the dark—but because of what crawls out of it when the lights
go out. And whatever you do, never answer the knocking. In my bedroom,
in the top drawer of the old dresser, there is a revolver. Use it if you
have to. I couldn’t.”

I figured it was just the delusions of a dying, deeply sick woman. I was
getting a house in a great neighborhood completely debt-free. Who in
their right mind would turn that down?

The first week went by without a hitch. I scrubbed the rooms to get rid
of that old-people smell, painted the living room walls, and started
settling in. Things were fine for the first ten days. But on the
eleventh night, everything changed.

I woke up in the middle of the night. You know that heavy, suffocating
feeling when you don’t wake up from a noise, but from the raw instinct
that someone is watching you?

The house was dead silent, but a minute later, I heard a sound coming
from beneath the floorboards. It wasn’t mice. It was a heavy, wet,
scraping sound. Like someone dragging a raw, heavy sack of meat across a
concrete floor. The sound slowly moved under the floor, traveling from
the kitchen to the hallway, and then stopped directly underneath my bed.

I froze, barely daring to breathe. And then came the knocking.

Thud. Thud. Thud.

Three slow, heavy blows. Coming straight from below, right through the
floorboards, vibrating through the legs of my bed.

I clutched my blanket, and then, a voice drifted up from the dark
underbelly of the house. It was dry, raspy, like the sound of old
wallpaper peeling off a wall, but it was unmistakably the voice of my
late grandmother:

“My boy… open the basement door. It is so cold and lonely down here.”

Everything inside me went numb. It sounded exactly like her, but the
cadence was completely wrong—mechanical and hollow, as if something else
was clumsily playing back an old audio recording. I didn’t sleep a wink
until sunrise, lying there listening to whatever was down there
scratching at the wood.

The next morning, armed with a heavy pipe wrench and a flashlight, I
went down into the basement. I needed to figure out what the hell was
going on. The place smelled of damp earth and rot. In the far corner,
hidden behind a rotting wardrobe, I found an old trunk. The padlock was
rusted through. I pried it open.

Inside were clothes and personal belongings. My things. My baby rattle.
My old middle school notebooks. My first baby teeth kept in a tiny glass
jar. And then I found my grandmother’s diary, dated back to the years of
my childhood. I flipped it open to a random page and my heart dropped.

“August 14, 1998. She demanded I give the baby back again. She sits on
the bottom step of the stairs and howls all night. She says he is her
newborn, the one she kept in a cardboard box down there. My God, I just
wanted a family… I am raising him as my own grandson. But she doesn’t
age. She waits. She is feeding on my memories; I am starting to forget
my own name. She said if I don’t sign the house over to him when he
grows up, she will come for me in the graveyard.”

My hands shook so violently the diary almost slipped from my fingers. I
am not her grandson. I am the child she took from this basement.

Suddenly, a sharp pop echoed behind my back. The lightbulb overhead
shattered, plunging the basement into pitch-black darkness.

From the depths of the dark, right in front of me, that same dry voice
spoke again. But this time, it wasn’t trying to sound gentle. It was a
guttural, triumphant whisper that made my ears ring:

“You’ve returned to the nest. Time for bed.”

I screamed, dropped my flashlight, and bolted up the stairs, scraping my
nails against the wooden steps. Behind me, I could hear a rapid, wet
slithering sound—it was rushing up from the bottom of the basement at an
impossible speed. I managed to slam the heavy oak door shut just as
something massive slammed into it from the other side. I locked all
three deadbolts.

Panic blinding me, I ran for the front door, but the handle wouldn’t
turn. I checked the windows—painted shut, swollen from humidity, sealed
tight. I was trapped. Not by magic. By neglect. By an old house that had
been waiting for this moment.

Terrified, I fled upstairs and locked myself in my bedroom.

The knocking downstairs stopped about ten minutes ago. But right now,
I’m hearing something much worse. The sound is coming from the
second-floor hallway. Right outside my bedroom door. That wet, dragging
slither. God, she must have come up through the air vents. That’s why
the knocking stopped. She wasn’t asking anymore.

She is standing right outside. And now, she is speaking in my own voice.
She’s practicing. She is trying to sound exactly like me, repeating my
name over and over again.

But I remembered. Grandma’s note. The revolver. Two doors down. She was
right outside mine. I could hear her breathing through the wood—dry,
uneven, wrong. I took a breath. And moved.
