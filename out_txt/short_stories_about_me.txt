The last thing I saw before plunging into the abyss was the dying light
over the Santa Monica pier. It was a sickly, bruised orange, a warning I
was too broke to heed. Up to my eyeballs in gambling debts to people who
didn’t take IOUs, I signed my life away to Nebula Corp. My assignment:
night technician at Acheron, a research station anchored 36,000 feet
below the Pacific—a depth where the crushing pressure turns heavy
machinery into flat soda cans in milliseconds.

When the titanium hatch hissed shut, Miller, the hulking shift
supervisor from Texas, handed me a tablet with a cracked screen stained
with dried brown residue. He didn’t offer a tour, just a cold
instruction to memorize the thirteen survival rules inside if I ever
wanted to see a California sunrise again. The rules were deeply
unhinged. They warned of strange things knocking on the glass, phantom
coworkers, and food that shouldn’t exist. But the real nightmare began
when the ocean’s silence started feeding on our sanity.

My shift ran from ten to six in a room bathed in sterile blue neon. My
only companion was Sam, a quiet guy who hid his face under a beat-up cap
and incessantly whistled “Take Me Home, Country Roads.” By the third
hour, the melody began to distort, twisting into a sharp, metallic
screech. When I called his name, he turned around, revealing a face
stripped of features—just a hollow, pitch-black void lined with rows of
needle-thin teeth. It rasped a request for me to unlock Sector 4.
Remembering the tablet’s warning, I forced out the required phrase: “The
moon in Texas is beautiful tonight.” The thing went dead silent, turned
back to its desk, and resumed its skin-crawling whistle. That was the
moment I realized Acheron wasn’t just a research station; it was a
pressurized coffin.

Deep in the station’s classified logs, I found the truth. Nebula Corp
wasn’t drilling for oil or studying marine life. They were excavating
something ancient buried beneath the seabed—something they called
Leviathan Zero. It wasn’t dead; it was just starving. Before I could
process the terror, sirens wailed, flashing blood-red. Containment in
Sector 4 had failed. Black, eye-studded tendrils were dissolving through
the steel bulkheads.

Panicked, I bolted toward the emergency stairwell, overriding the broken
self-destruct system that turned out to be nothing more than a cruel
placebo. As I descended, the madness spiked. The intercom crackled to
life, but it wasn’t an automated alarm. It was the smooth, cultured
voice of Nebula Corp’s CEO, broadcasting from a high-rise thousands of
miles away. He announced that an escape pod was being lowered, but
oxygen was failing. The pod had room for only one survivor: whoever
brought the heart of their coworker to the altar in Sector 4 would win
the ticket home.

In an instant, the terror of the deep was eclipsed by the savagery of
human nature. The line between rational men and feral beasts vanished.
Miller snapped, using his massive hands to choke Sam until his eyes
bulged, then tearing into his flesh with pure, unadulterated savagery,
claiming survival tasted better than anything he’d ever known. Down the
corridors, crew members weren’t just fighting for air; they were driven
into a communal psychotic break. They carved eldritch symbols into their
own skin with broken glass, laughing hysterically as they butchered one
another to offer sacrifices to the shadow pressing against the glass.

I crept through the blood-slicked halls, sickened by the realization
that Nebula Corp hadn’t just isolated us—they had psychologically
conditioned the crew to turn on themselves for sport. I found Miller
sitting atop a pile of corpses, brandishing a rusted skinning knife. He
didn’t look at me with malice; he looked at me with an eerie, hollow
peace, offering me the choice to kill him or be killed. But I wasn’t
looking at him. My eyes were fixed on a monitor in the corner. The live
feed wasn’t streaming to a command center—it was broadcasting to a
private, high-stakes auction room in New York, where billionaires sat in
silk suits, sipping champagne and placing bets on who would be the last
man standing.

It wasn’t a cult. It was a pay-per-view snuff film.

A cold, deliberate rage replaced my terror. I didn’t kill Miller out of
hatred; I ended his life as an act of mercy, plunging a blade into his
forehead while he smiled. As the last survivor, the escape pod finally
docked. But instead of opening its doors, it began venting toxic gas to
erase the final witness.

Coughing up blood, I collapsed against the massive observation window.
Leviathan Zero drifted closer, its colossal, ancient eye pressing
against the glass. In my final moments, looking into that abyss, I
didn’t see a monster. I saw the reflection of humanity—the monsters
lounging in penthouse suites, holding corporate shares, and driving
ordinary people to tear each other apart for a paycheck.

With my remaining strength, I dipped my fingers into the pooling blood
on the floor and smeared a final message onto the thick glass before the
sea crushed the hull into dust: Humans are not victims of the darkness;
we are the darkness waiting for a spark.

As the icy Pacific flooded my lungs and the crushing pressure folded the
room in on itself, the light went out. But right before the total quiet
took over, I heard that familiar tune echoing in the pitch-black
water—not from Sam, but from my own throat, joining a chorus of a
hundred lost souls waiting in the dark.
