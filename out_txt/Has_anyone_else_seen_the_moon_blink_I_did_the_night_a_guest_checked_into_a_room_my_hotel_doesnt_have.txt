If the moon seems extra big or a particularly potent yellow, don’t be
the first to blink. 

I shouldn’t be telling you that. But I’ve been playing alone for two
weeks, and I need help, and this is the only place I could think of
where people might actually take me seriously. I’ve searched online for
hours and found nothing. So if any of the following sounds familiar,
please reach out.

Some context:

I’m the night manager, day manager, and housekeeper of a small hotel off
a highway exit hardly anyone takes. The sign under the exit number
literally reads: NO SERVICES. We get about one guest a week if we’re
lucky. I live in Room 108. The owner, Mr. Hadley, lives three states
away and communicates almost entirely through emails about the
thermostat.

I’ve been lurking on this sub for years, mostly during dead overnight
shifts, and I always assumed that if I ever posted, it’d be fiction. I
wish I’d been right about that.

Two weeks ago, a guest checked in and left something behind. I’ll get to
what. First I have to explain how he checked in, because that’s when
things started.

It was a Tuesday, around 2:40 AM. The chime on the back door went off.
That’s normal; there’s a lot behind the building and most people park
there.

The man who came down the hall was dressed head to toe in black. Black
suit, black shirt, black tie, black shoes polished so bright they caught
the fluorescent light. He looked like he’d come from a funeral. He had
an old leather briefcase with brass latches in one hand and a closed
umbrella in the other. The umbrella was dripping, leaving a little trail
across the tile. I’d been inside since noon and hadn’t known it was
raining.

He asked, very fast, almost like he was reading off a card:

“I would like a room on the east side of the building. At least the
fourth floor. With as many windows as possible.”

I remember that wording exactly, and you’ll see why it matters.

Our booking system is ancient and it took forever to load. The whole
time, he checked his watch. Not glancing at it. Checking it, every
couple of seconds. Wrist up, eyes down, wrist down. Wrist up, eyes down,
wrist down. Like a metronome. He never looked at me once.

He also kept drifting. Our lobby has no windows and doesn’t connect
directly to the outside. There’s a long hallway from the front doors
that bends at a right angle, so no light from outside ever reaches the
desk. He kept backing toward that hallway, a step at a time, until he
was halfway down it with his neck craned toward the bend. Like he
desperately needed to see the sky and couldn’t stand that he couldn’t.

Wrist up, eyes down, wrist down.

When the room grid finally loaded, every room showed as occupied except
one: Room 417.

We had no other guests that night. And our fourth floor only goes up to
room 416.

I figured it was a glitch. Old system, phantom entry. I should have
refreshed the page. Instead, because I was tired and he was making me
nervous, I just marked 417 as booked and handed him a key card from the
drawer. It didn’t occur to me until later that we’d never had a key for
417. I still don’t know where it came from.

He crossed the lobby faster than I’ve ever seen a man in dress shoes
move, grabbed the key card without touching my fingers, and sprinted up
the stairs. He didn’t even look at the elevator. He left the umbrella
leaning against my desk.

I assumed he’d come back down in five minutes, annoyed, and I’d put him
in a different room and comp him breakfast.

He didn’t come back down.

Fifteen minutes after he left, I remembered the east side of the
building doesn’t have any windows. Not one. The whole east face is a
mural of a lake at dusk, dark water and pines under a moonless sky. I’m
fairly sure Mr. Hadley’s late wife painted it. 

An hour later I’d mopped up the umbrella water and reset the wifi (it
dies every night at 3:00, like clockwork). I remember the water on the
floor was warm. Not room temperature. Warm, like bathwater. It bothered
me in a vague, sleepy way at the time. It bothers me a lot more now.

I needed air. I walked down the hallway, around the bend, out the front
doors, and the night met me completely clear.

Not a cloud in the sky. Not a puddle on the asphalt. The gravel was dry.
The grass was dry. The only car in the back lot, a long black sedan, was
dry and cold when I touched the hood.

I didn’t put it together. I’d been awake for twenty hours on vending
machine coffee. I just stood there thinking huh, it stopped.

The moon was out.

It was big. Really big, sitting low over the trees past the highway, and
it was yellow. Not the warm orange of a harvest moon. A sick, buttery
yellow, like a light bulb seen through a dirty lampshade. I remember
thinking it didn’t look big so much as close. Like it had leaned in.

I walked around the side of the building and slid down the wall into the
grass. I just wanted ten minutes. I tipped my head back against the
stucco, looked up at the moon, and felt my eyes start to drift shut.

That’s when it happened.

The moon blinked.

I know how that reads. But I’ve replayed it a thousand times, so I’m
going to describe it exactly as I saw it. 

It wasn’t a cloud; there were no clouds. It wasn’t my own eyes closing.
I was looking at it out of the corner of my vision, and the light
dimmed, top to bottom, like a lid sliding down over something wet. For a
fraction of a second the moon was dark. Then the darkness pulled back,
bottom to top, and the yellow returned.

Brighter, I think. Like it had just woken up.

I sat bolt upright. My heart was pounding so hard I felt it in my teeth.
I kept staring, waiting for it to happen again. It didn’t. It just hung
there, fat and yellow and close.

Then I noticed something that scared me more.

I was leaning against the east wall.

I hadn’t thought about where I was going when I walked around the
building. I’d just picked the side with the soft grass. I looked up at
the wall above me, at the lake and the pines and the moonless sky.

There were windows in it.

Rows of them. Four stories’ worth. Small square windows cut right
through the painting, like someone had punched holes in a canvas. They
were all dark, except one.

Fourth floor. I counted. Seventeen from the left.

It was glowing yellow.

I don’t remember going back inside. I don’t remember walking around to
the front, or the hallway, or the lobby. The next thing I remember is
standing in the elevator, pressing the button marked 4. The button was
warm under my finger, the same way the water had been.

I don’t remember the ride up. I don’t remember the doors opening. I
remember the fourth floor hallway, and I remember that at the end of it,
where there’d always been blank drywall with a fire extinguisher bolted
to it, there was a door with brass numbers.

417

It wasn’t locked. It wasn’t even shut. Yellow light was leaking through
the gap.

I pushed it open.

The room was small. Smaller than any of our rooms. Just a bed, still
made, and the briefcase lying open on the bedspread. I saw what was
inside as I walked past. A cheap plastic hand mirror. And eye drops.
Dozens of the little single-use bottles, and every one I could see had
been twisted open and squeezed flat.

There was one window on the far wall. The curtains had been dragged all
the way back and pinned to the wall with thumbtacks. The moon filled
almost the entire window. I could see texture on it, craters and gray
seams, and it was so yellow the whole room looked jaundiced.

The man stood in front of the window with his back to me, perfectly
still, arms at his sides. His watch was off. It was lying face up on the
windowsill. I could see his reflection faintly in the glass. His eyes
were wide open. Red-rimmed. Wet. He looked like he hadn’t blinked in a
very, very long time.

I opened my mouth to say something. Sir, you can’t be in here. Something
stupid like that.

He spoke first. He didn’t turn around. He didn’t move at all.

“Do not be the first to blink.”

I looked at the moon.

I don’t know why. I think I couldn’t not look. It was right there,
filling the window, and the moment my eyes met it I felt it look back.
That’s the only way I can put it. The way you feel someone staring at
you across a room before you turn around, except a thousand times worse.

So I stared.

I stared until my eyes burned. Until the edges of my vision went fuzzy.
Tears ran down my face and into the corners of my mouth, warm like the
water off the umbrella. Every nerve in my body was begging me to close
my eyes, just once, just for a second. But I didn’t, because the man
beside me still hadn’t. I understood somehow that this was a game, and
that you did not want to lose it.

The moon swelled in the window. It was getting closer.

I didn’t blink.

I looked away.

I tore my eyes off it, turned my whole body toward the door, and ran.
Down the hall. Down four flights of stairs. I didn’t stop until I was
behind the front desk in the windowless lobby with my back against the
wall. I only let myself blink once I was sure there was no sky anywhere
I could see.

In the morning, the man and his car were gone. The key card for 417 was
sitting on the front desk in a little puddle of warm water. The booking
system showed no record of him. The fourth floor had its fire
extinguisher back and no door. The mural had no windows.

But on the carpet right under the fire extinguisher, there was a sheet
of paper folded into quarters. Damp. Warm.

It’s typed, on what looks like a typewriter. The bottom third is so
water-damaged the ink has run into gray clouds. I’m transcribing exactly
what I can read and leaving out nothing except what’s illegible.

THE LOOKING GAME

You may only begin when the moon is larger than it should be, or a
yellow you do not recognize. If you are unsure whether it is the right
moon, it is not. You will know.

1.  You must be in a room on the East side of a building. 
2.  You must be at least four stories up.
3.  The room must have no windows.
4.  Do not bring anyone with you. If someone follows you into the room,
    they are playing.
5.  Do not be the first to blink.
6.  It will try to make you blink.
7.  Crying is permitted. Closing your eyes is not.
8.  If you cannot continue, you may look away. Looking away is not
    losing. Looking away is postponing [this word is crossed out by
    hand, and something else is written above it in pencil, but I can’t
    make it out]
9.  You must continue the game after

That’s where the water damage starts. I can make out the number 10 and
possibly the word “mural.” There’s a handwritten section crammed between
Rules 3 and 4. It’s in a different hand than the word above postponing.
It reads:

Check the time before you look up. Note it. You will not be able to
check it again until the game has ended, and you will want to know how
long it lasted.

That’s it.

So. A few things.

The man’s request at the desk, “a room on the east side, at least the
fourth floor, as many windows as possible,” covers Rules 1 and 2. But
Rule 3 says the room can’t have any windows. He clearly asked for them,
and his room had one.

He checked his watch obsessively before he went up, and left it on the
windowsill once the game started. That’s the handwritten rule.

The eye drops, I think, were him trying to get through Rule 6.

He left the door open. I walked in. Rule 4.

I didn’t blink. I looked away. According to Rule 8, that’s not losing.
But someone crossed out the word that tells me what it is.

The last two weeks

I’ve been keeping notes. Here’s the short version.

Night 1: Moon noticeably bigger. Same yellow. I didn’t look directly at
it. I could feel it there anyway, like someone reading over your
shoulder.

Night 3: Emailed Mr. Hadley asking if there had ever been a Room 417, or
ever windows on the east wall. No answer. He usually answers within an
hour.

Night 5: The moon was sitting on the tree line at 1 AM. It’s never that
low that late. I went back inside and stayed in the lobby until sunrise.

Night 6: Looked at the mural in daylight for the first time since that
night. There’s a small dark shape on the far shore of the painted lake
now, half hidden in the pines. I’ve looked at that mural every day for
six years. It wasn’t there before. It could be a man in a black suit.
It’s facing the sky.

Night 9: The umbrella the man left is still behind my desk. Tonight it
was wet again. Dripping. I didn’t touch it.

Night 11: Booking system loaded instantly for the first time in years.
Every room green. I scrolled down out of habit and, for about two
seconds, there was a single gray square at the end of the fourth floor.
Then the screen refreshed and it was gone.

Night 14 (tonight): Mr. Hadley still hasn’t replied. The moon is so
bright tonight that it’s lighting the lobby hallway all the way to the
bend, which shouldn’t be possible. I haven’t gone outside.

If you’ve heard of The Looking Game, played it, or know someone who did,

And if you hadn’t heard of it before today: I’m sorry. I’ve read Rule 4
about forty times. I don’t think reading counts as following someone
into the room. I really don’t think so.

But if the moon looks bigger than usual tonight, maybe just don’t look
up.
