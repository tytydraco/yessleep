I grew up in a village in northeastern Thailand, and there is something
about the way people say goodnight here that I don’t think I’ll ever be
comfortable with again.

In Bangkok, when you finish talking to somebody, you say goodbye.

Where I live, you ask whether they’ve eaten.

That’s it. You can see a neighbor across the fence at six in the
evening, call out [กินข้าวแล้วบ่?]kin khao laew bor? (have you eaten yet?),
and receive [กินแล้ว]kin laew (already eaten) in return. Nobody has to
stop what they’re doing. Nobody has to come over. It’s just a way of
acknowledging that the other person is there.

My mother and the man who lived next door did this almost every evening
for twenty years.

He died by hanging last month.

For the first week afterward, my mother still answered him.

I thought she was grieving.

Then I heard him ask the question myself.

And now, every evening, just before sunset, my mother puts an extra bowl
of rice on our kitchen table.

The strange thing is that she doesn’t put it where he used to sit.

She puts it in my father’s place.

My father has been dead for almost a year.

Sorry. I’m already getting things out of order.

I’m thirty-two. Thai, male, currently working remotely for an IT
company. I moved back to my childhood home in Yasothon Province after my
father died last November.

I’m not giving the district or village name, because there are maybe
three hundred households in the whole place, and anybody who knows us
would recognize the story immediately.

If you’ve never been to rural Thailand, I’m guessing your mental picture
is something from a travel video. Wooden houses on stilts, monks in
orange robes, coconut trees, maybe a water buffalo standing in a rice
field.

But most houses here are concrete.

Ours is a single-story house with blue roof tiles, an outdoor washing
area, and a kitchen that my father built onto the back about fifteen
years ago. There’s a shrine shelf in the living room and in my parent
room also, an old standing fan that makes a clicking noise when it
oscillates a bit, and a refrigerator with a broken vegetable drawer that
has to be held shut with a lot of rubber bands I could imagine haha.

There’s a little square of concrete behind the kitchen where my mother
hangs laundry.

Beyond that is a waist-high cement-block fence.

Our neighbor’s kitchen was on the other side.

Maybe eight feet (2.4 meters if not U.S.) between the two houses,
including the fence.

You could hear him washing dishes. You could hear his television. If
both kitchen windows were open, you could hold an ordinary conversation
without raising your voice.

His name was Prasert.

Everybody called him Lung Sert.

Lung means uncle. It’s what we call older men, even when they’re not
related to us.

He was sixty-one, thin, with dark skin and a little patch of gray hair
at the back of his head. Usually wore those fake cheap football jersey
shirts and those blue rubber sandals sold at the market.

He fixed electric fans for a living. Water pumps too, although he
complained that modern pumps were getting too complicated.

He lived alone.

He was a nice man. for me.

Not especially friendly in the way some people are. He wouldn’t sit
around drinking with the other men or come over just to talk.

But if your water pump stopped working, he’d fix it, and if you asked
how much you owed him, he’d say to buy him a coffee sometime or buy him
a meal is ok for him.

He helped carry my father’s coffin.

That’s something I keep thinking about.

The day we took Dad to the crematorium, I remember seeing Lung Sert
walking behind the pickup with the other men, his hand resting against
the coffin to keep it steady.

He was wearing a white shirt that was too big for him.

I don’t know why I remember that.

There’s a particular thing about funerals in villages like ours.

When someone dies, everybody becomes useful.

No people or visible movement.

Only shadows changing and chairs appearing one after another.

I showed them to my aunt.

She watched the video without speaking.

Then she asked whether I could delete it.

I said no.

She looked at me.

“Please.”

I asked why.

She said she didn’t want to see it again.

I asked whether she’d heard anything the night before.

She hesitated.

Then she told me she’d woken up around one to use the bathroom.

As she passed the upstairs landing, she’d looked down into the garden.

There were people sitting on the porch.

She assumed they were her friends from around here.

She said she couldn’t see their faces properly.

They were sitting with their backs toward the house.

Mostly old people.

Some wearing white shirts.

Some in ordinary clothes.

She noticed a man in a football jersey sitting near the edge.

He looked familiar, but she couldn’t remember where she’d seen him.

Then one of them turned around.

My aunt stopped talking.

“The chair turned around,” she whispered.

“What?”

“The man didn’t turn around.”

She looked up at me.

“The whole chair turned. And he stayed sitting in it.”

I didn’t say anything.

She was crying now.

“He asked me whether your mother was home.”

I swallowed.

“What did you say?”

She covered her face.

“I told him yes.”

My mother has been sleeping most of today.

I went back through the CCTV recordings from our house.

All of them.

Every clip from the night after the funeral until the day we left.

I wanted to know how many chairs had appeared.

I made a spreadsheet. Date. Time. Camera. Number of chairs.

I counted them carefully.

Thirteen.

Fourteen.

Seventeen.

Nineteen.

Twenty-two.

Twenty-four.

The chairs weren’t appearing randomly.

They were appearing in groups.

Two.

Three.

One.

Four.

The timing was irregular, but the arrangement was consistent.

Every new chair faced our house.

And after a while, every row had the same number of seats.

I went back to the first photograph.

The thirteen chairs after Lung Sert’s funeral.

One extra.

The old woman had told me to count them.

I zoomed in.

Under the thirteenth chair was our house number.

18. 

But there was something else beside it.

A small mark.

At first I thought it was a scratch.

Then I remembered the marks under the chairs at the funeral.

I looked through the other photographs.

The newer chairs all had small marks beneath the seat, near the back
legs.

Some had one.

Some had two.

Some had three.

They weren’t scratches.

They were vertical lines.

Like tally marks.

I just started counting them.

Twenty-four chairs.

Twenty-four marks, distributed across them all.

He hadn’t been counting days.

He’d been counting guests.

And when he died, he’d reached seventeen.

By the time we left our house, there were twenty-four.

I checked the camera from my aunt’s porch.

Eleven chairs.

I turned one over.

A single vertical line.

The newest one had eleven.

At the funeral, Lung Sert’s daughter had told me that on his last phone
call he’d asked a strange question.

He’d asked whether she knew how many people had attended her mother’s
funeral.

She said she didn’t remember.

He’d asked whether there had been enough chairs.

She told him yes.

Then he started crying.

She’d asked why.

He said: “Because I think some of them stayed.”

It’s almost six in the evening.

I’m sitting at my aunt’s dining table writing this.

My mother is awake.

She’s sitting beside the kitchen window.

My aunt is making dinner.

She’s cooking rice and chicken with basil.

Then my mother asked if we had enough plates.

She was staring toward the back porch.

I asked how many plates she wanted.

She didn’t answer.

Then she stood up.

And began taking out bowls.

One.

Two.

Three.

Four.

She kept going.

I asked her to stop.

She turned around.

She looked frightened.

“There’s no point,” she said.

“No point in what?”

“Keeping them outside.”

My aunt came into the room.

“Who?”

My mother looked toward the porch.

“The guests.”

I heard something scrape against the tiles.

Then another chair creaked.

My aunt dropped the spoon she was holding.

We all stood still.

From outside, I heard a man clear his throat.

Then a woman spoke.

She was asking whether there was enough rice.

Like relatives waiting for dinner.

Then Lung Sert spoke.

“Everyone here?”

There was a pause.

Somebody answered.

“Not yet.”

I was standing near the kitchen door.

I could see the porch through the glass.

The chairs were occupied.

I need to be clear about what I mean.

I couldn’t see people.

But I could see the seats bending under weight.

I could see the chair legs spreading slightly.

I could see the armrests move as though someone were resting their hands
on them.

One chair rocked back and forth.

Another tilted as if someone had leaned to the side to speak to a
neighbor.

And in the middle of the row, a chair slowly turned toward me.

There was nothing in it.

Then my father’s voice came from the porch.

“He’s here.”

I couldn’t breathe.

I was staring at the empty chair.

My mother walked toward the door.

I grabbed her arm.

She pulled away.

Not violently.

Just firmly.

“Don’t be rude,” she whispered.

“Your father has brought guests.”

Then she opened the door.

I remember my aunt shouting.

I remember the sound of chairs being dragged into the kitchen.

I remember my mother smiling at something I couldn’t see.

And I remember hearing my father say:

“Come sit down.”

I ran. I took my mother’s arm and pulled her away from the door.

My aunt grabbed her other arm.

We got her into my car.

She was crying.

She kept saying we’d embarrassed the family.

That the guests had come a long way. That they were hungry.

We drove to a hotel in the city.

My mother is asleep in the bed behind me.

My aunt is in the room next door.

After we checked in, I looked at the camera feed from my aunt’s house.

It was still working. The porch was empty.

No chairs. Not one.

Then I checked the camera I’d taken from our village house.

The old recordings were still saved.

I opened the last clip from the morning we left.

I’d watched it before, but only to check the chairs.

This time I noticed something in the kitchen window.

A reflection. My mother was standing beside the table.

Behind her, reflected in the glass, were people.

A whole row of them.

Sitting inside the house.

I couldn’t see their faces.

The camera was too far away.

But I could see their shoulders.

Heads.

Hands resting on knees.

And one man standing behind them.

Thin.

Dark-skinned.

Wearing an old football jersey.

Lung Sert.

He wasn’t looking at my mother.

He was looking directly at the camera.

And his mouth was moving.

I enlarged the video. I slowed it down. I watched his lips.

He was saying the same thing over and over.

I don’t know how to lip-read Thai.

But I recognized the shape of the words.

He was saying:

Don’t let them in.

Then I saw something else.

He wasn’t standing with the others.

He was on the other side of the fence.

He’d never crossed it.

He was looking into our kitchen.

And he was crying.

It’s 11:47 p.m. now.

Then, about twenty minutes ago, somebody knocked.

Three soft knocks.

A woman spoke from the corridor.

She sounded elderly.

She asked whether this was room 418.

It is.

I stayed quiet.

She asked again.

Then I heard a man answer her.

“No. That’s not the one.”

Then I looked through the peephole.

The corridor was empty.

Except for a blue plastic chair.

Facing my door.

I called reception.

They sent a security guard.

He came upstairs and checked the corridor.

He said there was no chair.

I opened the door.

He was right.

I locked the door.

Then I heard the scraping noise.

Behind me.

From inside the bathroom.

I turned around.

The bathroom door was open.

There was a blue plastic chair sitting in front of the sink.

Number 18 underneath.

My father’s handwriting.

And on the seat was a little paper cup of water.

The kind they give you at funerals.

I don’t know how long it had been there.

I don’t know whether I somehow brought it with us.

I don’t know whether I’m losing my mind.

I only know I didn’t put it there.

I went to wake my mother.

She was already sitting up in bed.

Looking toward the bathroom.

She was smiling.

“Your uncle came,” she said.

I asked which uncle.

“Lung Sert.”

My hands started shaking.

“What did he say?”

She looked confused.

“He said he’d been trying to tell you something.”

“What?”

She reached for my hand.

And suddenly she looked terrified.

“He said the people aren’t coming to visit us.”

I stared at her.

“Then what are they doing?”

She began crying.

“He said they’re coming to a funeral.”

I couldn’t speak.

My mother squeezed my hand.

“He said he was trying to make them wait at his house because he didn’t
want us to know.”

“Know what?”

She looked toward the bathroom.

Then back at me.

“Whose funeral it was.”

I heard a chair scrape behind me.

The little blue one in the bathroom.

A slow, deliberate movement.

Then another sound.

A chair being pulled across the carpet.

Except there was no other chair.

I looked toward the door.

The hotel room was full of them.

Blue plastic chairs.

And one at the foot of my mother’s bed.

I could hear people sitting down.

One after another.

The familiar little click.

Then the creak.

Then the scrape.

I couldn’t see a single person.

But I could hear them talking.

And then I heard my father.

He sounded close.

Very close.

“He’s awake.”

Every chair went quiet.

My mother was staring at the empty chair beside the bathroom.

Tears running down her cheeks.

I asked her what she could see.

She didn’t answer.

I asked again.

She whispered:

“Everybody.”

Then she looked at me.

And said:

“Except you.”

I’m sitting on the bed typing this.

My mother is beside me.

She’s holding my wrist and won’t let go.

The room is very quiet now.

I can hear the air conditioner.

I can hear somebody moving in the corridor.

And every now and then there’s a little creak from one of the chairs.

The kind of sound plastic makes when somebody shifts their weight.

About the four chairs he borrowed.

About how frightened he looked when he asked whether my mother answered

Seventeen.

Eleven at my aunt’s.

At the bathroom door.

She’s crying.
