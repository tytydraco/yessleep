The nightmares were different this time. For the first time since the
beginning, since I’ve been haunted by the same torturous and repetitive
dreams, it was different. 

It started off like any other night: hanging by some invisible support,
dangling in midair while I was reduced to a charred husk of flesh. The
torment was just as vivid as it had always been, lasting for what seemed
to be hundreds and hundreds of years.

The first sign that something was off was when I felt the first
raindrop. It sizzled and evaporated almost instantly upon hitting my
back. It felt strange, even foreign after years and years of nonstop
scalding heat. 

But then it started to rain. The invisible skies above me opened up, and
gallons upon gallons of raindrops shot down at torrential speeds,
cutting through the flames and sending plumes of gray, opaque smoke up
into the air. 

As the fire was slowly extinguished and the smoke filled my chest, the
rain seemed to melt my imaginary chains that were keeping me above the
“ground.” I felt myself slipping, gradually at first, until I fell down
into the darkness below, snapping me awake.

My body constricted. I wasn’t where I was supposed to be. I gasped for
air as I clawed at the dark walls surrounding me and tried to peel off
the tight bonds that ensnared me. I burst out of them, crawling outside,
where the sun blinded me. Everything was white. My eyes squinted, my
vision adjusting as I furiously looked around for anything to help me
get my bearings. My lungs seized up as they tried to catch up with my
gasps for oxygen, and spittle foamed at my mouth. 

But as my eyes slowly adjusted to the rising sun, the blood rushed to my
head, and my chest finally filled itself with air, I could start to make
out the outlines of trees. Then the horizon, then the triangular peaks
of tents, then the fire pit from the night before. 

It always takes some time after my dreams to fully acclimate to reality
again. Once I’m awake, it never seems like I was burning for that long,
but having your mind tricked into thinking it’s spent centuries on fire
every single night means that it always needs to reorient itself. Being
in a new place didn’t help much either. But after getting readjusted,
all of the events of yesterday creeped back into my mind. With my steps
retraced, I remembered that I was at the campground. I was with Ilanna
and her friends from work, we’re on the first day of our hike, we had a
fire and…

The emotions from last night’s events suddenly reared their ugly head as
I played the flashbacks in my head. I turned to look at Ilanna’s tent.
It was still zipped shut, and I prayed I could get out of here before
she or anyone else woke up. 

To my surprise, on my left, I spotted Kayode. He was laying down on the
ground next to the log he sat on, wrapped in his sleeping bag. He
shifted, coming out of his slumber. 

Before I could even begin to ponder why he was out here, I felt the
worst nausea I had ever felt in my life materialize in my stomach. My
heels spun around, and I did a hobbled sprint to the outhouse, holding
my stomach along the way as I heaved. My bare feet kicked up the dew in
the grass, and the still, humid air accelerated my desire to puke. I
just barely made it.

I’m sure even those who have not thrown up in an outhouse have an
understanding of the inherent unpleasantness of it. Barfing is already
an invasive and humiliating experience to begin with. It feels so
foreign to have your body reject something so violently, to have this
thick mass, so hot and acidic, exploding from your stomach. Combine that
with the smells of an old wooden outhouse that probably hasn’t seen a
drop of bleach or an air freshener in decades, the picture it paints is
obvious. It’s not a pretty one. 

I threw up multiple times for the next ten minutes. By the third
go-around, I was dry heaving, every undigested bit of food already
having left my system. In between, I laid my back against the locked
door, fighting off the flashes of sinking cold sweats and the peaks of
humid sweats. The corners of my eyes buzzed from the dehydration, and I
could feel a pounding, searing headache starting to form. 

Once my body had finished purging itself, I slowly pushed myself up,
holding the door handle. My vision did loops that pulsed with my aching
migraine. I unlatched the door and stumbled outside. 

Taking a moment to breathe in the warm morning air, I shifted my weight
back and forth on my bare toes, squinting from the sun and trying to
stop my dizziness. My eyes felt dry, dryer than normal. I groaned as I
realized I had slept with my contacts in, something I usually tried to
avoid. My mother had always told me that it can lead to cataracts. 

The more the seconds ticked by as I stood there, swaying back and forth
outside the small shack, the more I realized I would be in no fit state
to hike back up the mountain. I could barely stay on my own two feet,
let alone scale the same mountain I had just barely made it over not
twenty-four hours ago. 

I pulled out my phone and dialed my mother. No answer again. Odd.

“Hello Mother, it’s Elijah. Again,” I said after the beep, “It’s… the
morning… of the day after I left you the previous message. Anyway, I
hope you’re not already on your way, because I woke up not feeling super
great…”

Realizing that she would probably die right then and there of a heart
attack if she knew I was drinking last night, I scrambled for an excuse.

“I’ve been throwing up a lot. Feels like those stomach bugs I used to
get. So I’m probably just gonna stay the night here again and then touch
base with you tomorrow morning. And um… yeah, that’s it. Sorry if you
already left. Bye.”

The second I shoved my phone in my pocket, the door to the outhouse
beside me opened up, and Kayode stepped outside.

“Morning,” he said, nodding at me. I nodded back.

Great. He probably heard me, and now Ilanna would know that I was
planning to ditch her once again. I racked my brain for what to say.

“Um,” I started, but he held his hand up.

“It is not my business,” he said.

After processing what he meant, I gave a half-smile in thanks.

“You hungover too?” I asked him.

“Oh yes,” he replied, “It is easy to overdo it when you’re among
friends.”

“I don’t think I’ve ever been that wasted before,” I said. He chuckled.

“Not much of a drinker?” he asked. I shook my head. “It can very much be
a double-edged sword, even for the most disciplined,” he said.

“Yep,” I agreed, “Pretty sure I’m feeling that other edge right now.”

He smiled as he started to walk back to his tent, but stopped and turned
to look at me. 

“Your decision is yours to make, Elijah,” he said, “But perhaps you
should try to stick it out for longer. Just a little. For Ilanna, hm?”

Kayode started strolling away, but his words hung in the air, lingering
over me with their potent accuracy. He did have a point, but I just
wasn’t sure if I had the guts to continue on. I’d have to think on it
some more. After last night, I dreaded the moment that Ilanna would
emerge from that tent. I wasn’t sure if she’d be angry, upset, or if she
had forgotten the entire thing. I couldn’t decide which would be worse.

Danny had woken up too, and I saw him already starting to break down his
tent once I reached the site. He nodded at me as he squinted his eyes no
matter what direction he looked in. I noticed Kayode rolling up his
sleeping bag on the log, and I recalled how I heard him and Ava fighting
last night. I wondered if she had kicked him out.

Even though I still wasn’t sure about whether or not I’d continue on
with the group, I started packing up my tent. Herd mentality I suppose.
I watched Ilanna’s tent on the other side of the site, closed up and
still, like a ticking time bomb that I had no hopes of diffusing. 

Brent was up next, and he stretched the second he was out of his tent
with a yawn. He looked around at us.

“What up, people?” he said quietly, his voice hoarse and dry.

“I hope I don’t look as shitty as you do,” Danny told him.

“I blame you, asshole,” Brent retorted, “I think your drinks are better
for cleaning rust off a car battery than consumption.”

Kayode snickered as Danny picked up a small twig on the ground and threw
it at him. He turned to me.

“How are you feeling?” he asked, “You gonna be stable enough for another
full day of hiking?”

“I’ll manage,” I answered. I wondered if had his suspicions from last
night, given that he saw me making the call.

“Guess I didn’t make them strong enough, then,” he said with a grin.

Before I could answer, I heard the sound of another tent unzipping. It
was Ilanna’s. I froze as I watched her slender frame step out into the
sun, wearing a tank top and shorts. She squinted into the light.
Everyone watched her, silently anticipating her next words.

“Look who finally woke up,” Danny said, “We all thought you were dead,
all of us. Brent especially.”

Brent just chuckled and shook his head. Ilanna scratched her head. I
snuck glances at her while I stuffed my tent in its bag.

“Gonna take more than that to keep me down I’m afraid,” she answered,
“Although I do feel like someone took a vacuum and sucked all the water
out of me.”

“Ah, about that,” Kayode said, digging in his pack and producing a water
bottle, “For you. I have one for everybody. I will restock at the camp
store today, before we leave.”

“You’re the man,” Ilanna said, walking over to him and grabbing it from
his hands. She took the cap off and drank a small sip, “Ava not up yet?”

Kayode shook his head. “I am letting her sleep in. She was not in the
best of spirits last night.”
Ilanna nodded and took a few more small sips. She sighed and wiped the
excess off of her mouth. 

“Yeah well, that makes two of us.”

At that moment, her eyes turned to me. I was ducking down slightly in
some sort of subconscious attempt to hide from her, but she tracked me
down and started sauntering towards me. As I slowly wobbled to my feet
with a nauseous groan, everyone else turned away and went back to
breaking down their tents, avoiding being a witness to whatever was
next.

Ilanna reached me and put her hands on her hips. The sun rose directly
behind her, the sun so bright it obscured her expression. It made her
seem ethereal, angelic. 

“Hey,” she said softly.

I gulped quietly. “Hey,” I responded rather lamely. 

“You know, you don’t even look that bad for how hungover I bet you are,”
she said with a light smile. 

“Yeah well,” I said, not meeting her gaze, “I’ve always looked kind of
pale and dehydrated. ‘Sickly,’ as my mother so lovingly puts it.”

She chuckled. “Well, I don’t know about you,” she began as she started
slowly pacing back and forth, “But I don’t think I’ve ever drank that
much in my entire life. At least not since college.”

I raised an eyebrow. “You drank in college?” I asked.

“Here and there,” she said with a sly grin, “And if you tell my parents,
I’ll kill you before they kill me.”

I laughed, but only to cover up the palpable awkwardness that hung
between us. I hope my face wasn’t as red as it felt. She momentarily
stopped her pace to look me in the eye. 

“So, um…” she began, and I think I audibly took a deep breath, “I’m…
really only getting flashes of what happened last night.”

I hung my head, fiddling with a single tent pole in my hands. “Uh, yeah.
Same. Same, pretty much,” I said as I replayed every second of it in my
head.

“Mm. And…” she continued, “Whatever I said, to anybody for that matter…
that wasn’t me. Not at all.” She squeaked out her words, like she was
walking a voice-activated minefield.

“Oh yeah, I figured,” I said, rubbing my neck, “Drunk words aren’t sober
thoughts, right? Pretty sure that’s the saying.”

I finally locked eyes with her, and even though she barely snickered, I
could see the relief pour into her expression.  

“Exactly,” she finally said, taking a step towards me, “So let’s just
forget about all of it, huh?”

I could tell from her body language and the way that her eyes pleaded
with me that she felt horrible, which gave me a strange mix of relief
and dread. I nodded at her in agreement.

“Good,” she sighed, opening up her arms, “Hug it out?”

In case it wasn’t apparent already, I’m not super big on physical touch.
It makes me feel sensitive, vulnerable, like someone’s shattering an
invisible bubble that needs to be intact to ensure my safety. But I knew
that I’d need to break it that day for her.

On her tip-toes, she wrapped her arms around my neck and put her chin on
my shoulder. I could feel her eyes clench tightly as she squeezed me.

“I’m just really glad you’re here,” she whispered, her voice wavering. 

Looking back on that moment now, I realized that it was when I knew that
I couldn’t leave. At least not at that point. Ilanna held on to me like
she was afraid I’d suddenly float away into the atmosphere like a
runaway balloon, and I could almost feel her put her heart into my hands
for safekeeping. From that point forward, whether I liked it or not, it
was my job to ensure nothing happened to it. 

“Me too,” I whispered, her grip around my neck jostling my sensitive
stomach. 

She let go of me, but not before giving me a kiss. It was a quick,
blink-and-you’ll-miss-it kind of peck on my cheek, her lips barely
scraping my skin. But it undoubtedly was a kiss. Before I could process
it, she gave me a quick smile and started walking back to her tent.

“Let’s get a move on, everybody,” she said to everyone as they looked up
at her walking to the middle of the site. “No rest for the weary, right
Ave?” she asked as Ava was just then crawling out of her tent.

“Mm-hm,” Ava mumbled, shrugging off Kayode’s hand on her shoulder, “can
I have a vinegar shot?”

“Sure thing,” she replied, reaching down into her bag. She looked up at
me. “You want one, Eli?”

Still stunned from the kiss, I had to snap myself out of my stupor.
“Uh.. for what?” I asked. 

“Hangover cure,” she said, pulling a big bottle of apple cider vinegar
from her pack. “It’s not foolproof, but it’ll at least help with the
nausea.”

The rest of the group gathered around, and Ilanna poured a little for
everyone in Danny’s tin cups. I made my way over, and we made a circle.
Ilanna held up her cup for a toast.

“First full day. We got this. Hundred Mile Wilderness ain’t got shit on
us!” Danny exclaimed, and everyone groaned in cringe. “What?“ he said,
“Excuse me for trying to bring some positivity for all you sad-sacks!” 

We clinked our cups and took our shots. It was disgusting, and it really
only made me more nauseous. We took some Motrin for our headaches, (much
to Danny’s chagrin,) packed up the rest of our stuff, and hit the road. 

We made a quick stop at the campground store one last time to restock on
a couple of food items. We ate a quick breakfast, and Danny and I
grabbed a cup of coffee, hoping to jolt ourselves out of our zombified
hungover state. 

We crossed the Abol Bridge mid-morning, stopping briefly to take a look
at Katahdin. It sat over the still lake in front of us, obscuring the
dark clouds that creeped over it. The dark, dense reflections of the
forest rippled in the water as fish poked up to catch the water bugs
skimming the surface. The bridge looked to be about three hundred feet
long, and quite narrow. It stood in the lake, supported by concrete
piers. The main dirt road was for cars, but there was a little
sectioned-off path on the side for us hikers. I remember thinking it was
slightly underwhelming to name an entire campground after it.

The morning sun was still harsh on my recovering body, and I sized up
the mountain’s elevation, confirming for myself that there was no way I
could hike back up it today. I flexed my calves as we leaned up against
the faded wooden guardrail, wincing. The soreness from yesterday’s hike
was even worse today, compounded by my hangover. I turned to Kayode, who
scanned the horizon, squinting.

“You still recovering?” I asked him. It took him a second to answer.

“Oh yes,” he said with a sigh, “But I am thinking about those circles we
saw yesterday. There is something about them that I don’t quite like.”
He turned to me. “And I am thinking about last night: what you and Brent
said, the shopkeeper and his suspicions.”

I had only known Kayode for a little over twenty four hours, but
something about seeing him on edge, even slightly, unnerved me. When
someone is so even-keeled, so calm, it makes their rare slips in
demeanor more glaring. Before I could answer him, Brent barged his way
in with his expertise.

“It’s probably just some loggers that made ‘em,” Brent said, “They have
routes that run all through this area.”

Kayode rubbed one of his temples. “Perhaps, but it just did not feel
right to me.”

“Uh, yeah. Same here,” Brent replied. “They’re invasive as shit. Blight
on society if you ask me.”

“Loggers…” Kayode mused, “I don’t know.”

“Trust me dude,” Brent scoffed, “If anyone’s evil enough to remove huge,
sporadic, nonsensical areas of trees, displacing the homes of wildlife
and impacting the ecosystem, it’s lumber companies.”

Brent gave a satisfied huff and turned, heading further down the bridge.
Ilanna rolled her eyes and gave me a knowing smirk, and we all followed
suit. I looked behind at Kayode, who still had his eyes glued to the
forest in front of him as he stepped away. 

“You know,” Danny said as we all passed by him, “There’s a very slim
chance that my brother was actually the Unabomber, but it’s not zero.”

Soon after, we reached the entrance to the infamous hundred miles. The
path into the forest was littered with sticks and small fallen trees. At
the front, nailed to a wooden post in the ground covered with initials
and hearts, was a dark red sign. Below the writing that said
“APPALACHIAN TRAIL, CAUTION!” it read: 

“IT IS 100 MILES SOUTH TO THE NEAREST TOWN AT MONSON. THERE ARE NO
PLACES TO OBTAIN SUPPLIES OR GET HELP UNTIL MONSON. DO NOT ATTEMPT
UNLESS YOU HAVE A MINIMUM OF 10 DAYS SUPPLIES AND ARE FULLY EQUIPPED.
THIS IS THE LONGEST WILDERNESS SECTION OF THE ENTIRE A.T. AND ITS
DIFFICULTY SHOULD NOT BE UNDERESTIMATED.
-M.A.T.C.”

We stood below, each of us silently reading to ourselves. The wind
howled in the distance, and the air tasted heavy, dewy. I looked ahead
at the muddy path, which was surprisingly dark, the sun shrouded by the
trees and the overcast day. 

“Okay, one last time,” Ilanna said, turning to us, “check your packs.
Make sure you absolutely have enough for the next ten days.”

I opened my pack, rummaging around and examining my food. I had packed
some Annie’s mac and cheese, pepperoni and cheese sticks, (with some
tortillas,) a huge pack of mixed nuts, Smuckers sandwiches, energy bars,
beef jerky, chocolate, and some instant coffee. My stomach rumbled just
looking at it, but I knew I’d be sick of eating the same stuff by the
third or fourth day. 

After finishing her own check, Ilanna looked up at us, holding a thumbs
up. “Good?” she asked. We all nodded. “Okay,” she said, “Like the sign
says, no restocks for ten days. If you desperately need something, speak
now or forever hold your peace. Well.. not forever, I guess. More like a
week and a half.”

We chuckled, but there was a certain unease in the air about entering
such a remote area for so long. Or at least, I was nervous and was
projecting it on to everyone else. Ilanna turned to Kayode.

“Kayode,” she said, handing him a folded piece of paper, “You’re on map
duty today. You know the route?”

Kayode slowly nodded. I wondered if it was a good idea to have him
leading the way. He looked sick, tired, and hungover like the rest of
us, but he also looked rattled, and honestly a little scared. He
unfolded the map and began studying it. 

“Well,” Ilanna said in that signature peppy voice, “Nothing left to do
but hike the darn thing!”

“Let’s do it!” Danny said, clapping his hands together. With that, we
all followed Kayode into the wild. 

Apart from us having a rotating shift of map-readers, the main thing
keeping us on the trail was the painted tree marks. The main one was a
singular white rectangle, which confirms that the hiker is indeed on the
right path. Naturally however, the trail isn’t always straightforward.
There are numerous sharp turns and changes in direction, which are
denoted by two white rectangles, with the top one offsetting in the
direction of the upcoming change. There are also blue markers, which
mark side-trails, which can lead to campsites or water sources. Finally,
there’s yellow markers, which signify the end of the trail corridor -
that the hiker is entering property not owned by the A.T. Conservancy.
The meaning of these four different kinds of marks was one of the first
things that Ilanna made sure I knew while we planned the trip. 

Of course, we’d come to find out that the rain made it quite hard to
denote which was which.

The first sign of the incoming weather was only about thirty minutes
into our hike that day. We walked silently, Kayode in front, Ava behind,
then Danny and Brent, then me and Ilanna. It reminded me of those scenes
from an alien invasion movie, where the huge mothership floats into the
atmosphere, completely covering up the sun. The dark cumulonimbus cloud
creeped over our heads, darkening everything its airy, shadowy hand
touched. 

“Oh hell nah,” Danny quietly exclaimed as we all looked up at the sky,
“Already?”

“You have my rain jacket, right?” Ava asked Kayode. He stopped and swung
off his bag, unzipping it. The rest of us followed suit. It took me the
longest to find mine, as I rummaged through my overstuffed bag.

Just then the skies opened up, and rain shot down. Billions and billions
of drops streaked to the ground like missiles. I scrambled, violently
digging in my pack as my coat was still M.I.A. Ilanna, who shivered in
her own coat, shot me a confused look.

“Aw, come on, man,” Danny shouted over the rain, “Don’t tell me you made
room in that thing for a damn space heater but you forgot your jacket!”

I ignored him, growing more and more panicked as all of the items in my
bag grew increasingly soaked in the torrential downpour. 

“Do you not have it?” Ilanna asked, eyebrows raised and arms crossed.

I looked up at her and shrugged.

It felt like hours, but after a minute of searching, I found it, quickly
throwing it on. I picked up my pack, and it was somehow even heavier. It
was waterlogged, soaking wet and heavy. Water dripped from the bottom
and slicked off the top. I groaned and slung it over my shoulder, and it
made a wet thwap.

As we ventured onward, the rain grew heavier. The drops made a thick
mass of water only broken up by our tiny, shivering bodies and the trees
around us. It made deep puddles on the muddy path that splashed up dirt
on our ankles and legs as we trekked through them. We stepped over slimy
tree roots and pushed their branches out of the way, flinging buckets of
water in every direction. The blanket of the shower and the rising fog
of the humidity only made the few feet surrounding us visible. It was
like walking through a waterfall.  

Another few hours of miserable, sopping wet hiking went by, and it
became clear that this weather was not stopping anytime soon. The
temperature was dropping, and I fought off my waning nausea that was
being clung on to by my cold body and my chattering teeth. My waterproof
boots had now been completely soaked inside and out, despite them
fighting valiantly against the onslaught of rain. I snuck glances at
everyone around me. Kayode led the pack, hunched over the map as he
covered it with his hands to little avail. Ava followed close behind
him, head hung low. Danny and Brent were next, and they walked briskly
in tandem, all out of sly jokes or comments about our predicament.
Ilanna and I were at the back. She crossed her arms and gave me a tiny,
shivering smile when she saw me looking. I returned it. She looked up
ahead.

“Kayode!” she shouted over the rain, “Can you still see the map?”

He looked back and forth between her and the map a few times. “Um…” he
said, “I think… I think so… Yes. I can see it.” He didn’t sound sure.

“You think?” Danny said incredulously.

“I can see it,” he affirmed hesitantly. The rest of us exchanged
concerned looks as I felt a slight pang of anxiety. 

“That’s not very reassuring, dude,” Brent said.

Ava, who seemed the most worried, tapped Kayode on the shoulder. “Why
don’t we take a breather and find some cover,” she said to him. She had
to raise her voice over the thunderous patter. “I can’t see shit right
now. And I’m cold.”

“I don’t imagine that will do you much good,” he replied.  

Ava scoffed. “Oh, okay,” she said, “Since you’re such an expert I
guess.”

“You need to keep your blood moving,” he replied, staring ahead, “And if
we keep walking, it will get us out of the rain.”

“Or.. we could wait the rain out and figure out where we need to go!”
she shouted testily, “How do we even know you’re taking us in the right
direction?” He said nothing.

“Kayode,” Ilanna said, “I really think we should-”

Just then, Ava slipped, falling on her side into the muddy ponds that
littered the path. She immediately rolled on her back, cussing and
grabbing her ankle. 

The four of us rushed to Kayode, who was already by her side with a hand
on her shoulder.

“Are you okay?” he asked her, calm and collected.

“No, I’m not fucking okay!” she shouted, groaning in pain, “I think I
twisted my ankle.”

“Let me have a look,” Ilanna said, kneeling down and brushing the
running water off of her face. She took off her shoe and sock, examining
the leg. “It doesn’t look swollen. Let’s get you somewhere we can take a
break.” She shot a furrowed brow at Kayode for that last part. Ava,
nodded her head with a wince.

“I brought a tarp we could hang up,” I offered, remembering that I had
packed it. The group all looked at me and mumbled words of agreement.

“Okay, let’s get you up,” Ilanna said, putting her hand under one of her
shoulders. Kayode grabbed the other, and they helped Ava limp off of the
trail.

We carried Ava to a collection of three trees that formed a triangle.
Me, Brent, and Ilanna tied a rope to each tree that we fished through
the eyelets of my tarp. While Ava, already soiled, sat down against the
tree on the wet ground, the rest of us opted to stand. We stood side by
side, watching the rain continue its bombardment of the trail. The
droplets poured off of the vinyl blue canvas, forming deep divots over
our head. Ilanna stood next to me, and I watched her eyes analyze every
minute detail of our surroundings through the storm. She met my gaze,
and the uncertainty on her countenance was dizzying. Grasping for
reassurance, she grabbed my hand, squeezing it tight. The warmth of her
fingers cut through the dirt and dampness on both of us. She leaned
close to me, laying her head on my shoulder as the mist from the
splashes sprayed our faces. I could feel her pounding pulse on her neck
and her chest move in heavy, rhythmic breaths. 

It took another hour before the rain finally waned. The dark clouds
remained, still protective of the sun’s glow. The skies still spit out
misty sprinkles. Nevertheless, the downpour was finished for now, and
the Earth warmed. We all breathed a collective sigh of satisfaction. My
ears had a soft ringing from the neverending whoosh of the rain hitting
the ground. I looked to Ilanna, who had now moved to the other side of
the tarp. She gazed around, her head moving robotically, like a security
camera.

“Okay, I admit it,” Danny said, clapping me on the back as I flinched,
“I was a little too harsh on Mr. Be Prepared over here, because this
thing’s a lifesaver.”

“You can thank my mother,” I said, “She practically forced me to bring
it.”

“Kayode,” Ilanna asked, her back turned to us and her arms crossed,
“When’s the last time you saw a trail marker?”

I watched the gears turn in his head, his brow furrowing deeper and
deeper. “Why do you ask?”

She turned around, her eyes wide. The rest of us gawked at her alarming
question. She held her arm out and gestured to the land around us.

“Why do I ask?” she sounded exacerbated, like she had to physically keep
her voice from raising. Her lip quivered. “Does any of this look like
the trail to you?”

We all turned our heads, analyzing the soaking wet forest. The ground
was covered with weeds, bushes, and small trees. The larger ones were
scattered every which way, their large canopies creating dancing shadows
on the floor as the water dripped off the leaves. None of them bore the
signature white marks, or any marks for that matter. 

“No…” he said slowly, “It does not.”

The silence was thick and palpable. We all exchanged nervous glances.

“Okay,” Ilanna said, her voice wavering, “before we jump to any
conclusions, let’s get back to where we got off the path to help Ava.”

Kayode winced. We hung on his every word. He held his hand, pointing to
a spot not two-hundred feet away. “It was right over there where she
hurt herself.” He said, barely a whisper. We all looked to where he
pointed.

No distinguishable trail was visible.

I saw Ilanna’s mouth drop open. Kayode slowly clutched his head with his
hands. He took a few steps outside of the tarp, looking around. He
lumbered in circles, presumably hoping to catch a glimpse of any sign of
our path. The rest of us watched him with baited breath.

“What happened?” Ilanna asked, the fear in her tone building, “How’d we
get off the trail?”

“I…” he began, stuttering softly, so quietly it was as if he spoke to
himself. “It was raining… I could not see…”

He sheepishly turned around to look at us.

“I was certain I was following the map correctly.”

“You were certain?” Danny asked.

“What does that mean, dude?” Brent asked, the worry pouring from his
mouth.

Kayode turned around, slicking his hair back as he winced. He held his
hands on his hips, and I could see him taking short, panicked breaths. 

“Kayode…” Ilanna asked, “Do you know where we are?”

Once more, he faced the rest of us. His body tensed. He knew the answer,
but saying it seemed to require every ounce of his strength.

“I’m sorry, everybody.”

Ilanna groaned, Danny smacked his forehead in disbelief, Brent turned
around with a sigh, and Ava shot daggers at her boyfriend. I’m sure that
I looked just as dumbfounded as the rest, and my heart pounded out of my
chest.

“You gotta be shitting me,” Brent said.

“You were the guy today, man! The map guy!” Danny exclaimed, “Why didn’t
you say something right away?!”

“Oh my God,” Ava said, her voice quivering.

“You have got to be shitting me,” Brent repeated. 

“I told you we should’ve stopped,” Ava said with as much vitriol as she
could muster sitting down, “I told you, and you didn’t listen.”

At that moment, Kayode looked as if he held the weight of a thousand
worlds on his shoulder. “I am so sorry,” he pleaded, “The rain. I could
barely see. And I have felt quite sick since last night. It is no
excuse, but…”  

He looked at Ava. Ava glared back. Ilanna gazed ahead, blinking and
unmoving.

“I have also had a lot on my mind recently.”

Danny spun and paced behind the group, frantically looking around.
“Goddamn it!” he shouted, and his cry echoed into the woods, scaring a
few birds from their roost. I crouched down, trying to retrace our steps
in my head. It proved fruitless, and I instead began to retrace every
life decision I’ve made that led me to this moment. I sighed and
burrowed my head in my hands.

I watched Ilanna stare up at the sky, calculating our next move. She
exhaled and looked down at me, putting a hand on my shoulder.

“Look,” she began, “It’s okay, Kayode. It’s okay. We can point fingers
at anyone, but the truth is none of us noticed where we were going. It
was too rainy.”

She looked at us, and we tentatively nodded in agreement.

“But we can’t have made it too far off the trail,” she continued, “So I
think the best thing to do is to just keep going in the direction that
we were. Chances are, we’ll connect back to it at some point.”

She pointed up at the sky. 

“Unless anyone has a compass, we’ll follow the path of the sunset.”

Everyone seemed on board, even Ava, who still sat against the tree,
rubbing her twisted foot.

“What do you think, Elijah?” Ilanna asked me. 

I thought for a second. Heading directly into the deep Maine woods
sounded terrifying. In all honesty, heading Northeast seemed like the
better idea. Doing that would eventually bring us within sight of
Katahdin, right? But I figured I’d defer to Ilanna. She was the expert
after all. 
“Seems as good a plan as any,” I replied, “But what about Ava?”

“I’m fine,” she said, “I can limp.”

Kayode wrapped Ava’s ankle with some surgical tape, but not without a
yelp of pain and an “Easy!” from her while he did it. He helped her up,
then handed the map to Ilanna. She studied it closely, although I wasn’t
exactly sure what good it would do. Me and Danny took down the tarp and
folded it back up. It was wet, but I stuffed it in my pack anyway. Not
like it could get any more soaked than it already was. 

“Let’s go,” she said to all of us, “Everyone, keep your eyes on the sun.
It sets in the west, and that’s where we should be heading.”

I shifted my feet around in my shoes, as they squeaked and squished,
waterlogged to the brim. I wrung out my shirt and wiped some soil from
my brow. I looked at the rest of the group. We were wet, covered in mud,
and tired. And yet Ilanna, who looked the dirtiest of us all, wore a
soft yet hopeful smile, mostly aimed in my direction. Perhaps I should
have been more concerned, but she always had the infectious positivity
about her that you couldn’t help but share. 

“We’ll find our way,” she assured me.

With that, we were off.

My mother was staunchly against the harm of any living thing on this
planet. She was a vegetarian, only bought cage-free eggs and ethical
dairy, and would never swat a mosquito or stomp on a spider. She always
told me that everything living and breathing that existed was part of
God’s creation, and it was not anyone’s call to make but Himself who got
to live or die. And while I understood the notion and respected her
dedication to it, I had a few reservations. I truly believe that if I
had to pick a single thing that existed on this planet to completely
eradicate without any undue harm to food chains or ecosystems, it would
be horseflies and deerflies.

They swarmed us all moths to a flame. They took huge chunks out of every
member of our troupe, despite our attempts to slap them away. No amount
of bug spray could dispel their insipid buzzing and biting: they always
seemed to find spots we missed. My body had mostly dried up of the rain
water, but it left my skin dry and scaly, burnt from the sun. It ached
every time I clapped an insect off of me. We trekked past hundreds of
trees, roots, bushes and weeds, constantly bombarded with the
coordinated attacks of these bugs. By the time it had crept towards the
horizon, we were covered all over in little red dots, like terminal
chicken pox patients.

“How is your leg?” Kayode asked Ava as she leaned up against a tree. We
had all stopped walking for a quick break.

“Fine,” she said dismissively, wiping some sweat off of her face. She
turned to the group. “Does anyone recognize any of this? Landmarks or
something?”

The rest of us took a look around and shook our heads. Ava cursed and
rubbed her ankle. 

“Has anybody tried looking at the map on their phone?” Brent asked,
swatting at a horsefly on his arm. 

“I tried that,” I responded, “No signal. It needs service to download
the map for this area.”

I opened by phone to show them the gray grid surrounding my tiny blue
dot. 

“We’ll just have to keep trying, then,” Ilanna said.

Brent frowned, and Danny punched a tree in frustration.

“Well… not to state the obvious,” he croaked, “but this fucking sucks.”
We hung our heads in a silent agreement.

It seemed that with every step we took, a sliver of hope was lost. The
darkening of the sky and the impending night hung over our heads more
and more as we searched for any sign of a cleared path or painted tree
mark. There were a few times that day where we thought we were finally
back on track, but it was nothing more than a small spot where the trees
were sparse.

I kept looking at Ilanna, the toughest nut to crack, who was seeming
more and more defeated as the day ruthlessly carried on. The rest of the
group hung their heads low, and their steps grew tired and lumbering.
Behind me, Kayode looked to be on the verge of tears. My throat
tightened and my heart pounded as I looked up at the horizon, the sun
peeking over and sending its orange glow throughout the trees. 

Soon after, we reached a bubbling stream on a small hill. Wordlessly,
quietly, slowly, we all picked a tree and slumped down in front of them,
resting our legs. Danny picked up rocks and hucked them into the flowing
water, Kayode helped Ava sit down as sweat dripped from her brow, Brent
took a careful sip from his water bottle, and I watched Ilanna. She was
the only one standing. She watched the brook as it trickled down the
rock formations that made up the mound, spilling through mossy stones
and dirt. I didn’t need to see her face to know what she was thinking.

Everyone knew it at that point. It was right there on their faces. That
hopeless quiver that seeped through the musk and grime of the day’s hike
plastered everyone’s expression. We knew the sun was getting dark, we
knew that it was due time to set up camp for the night, but no one
wanted to. That would be admitting it, and admitting it was to make it a
reality. Nobody wanted to be the first to say it, to sign the
certificate that declared our fate. But it was real. It was true.
Painfully, sickeningly, horribly true.

We were lost.
