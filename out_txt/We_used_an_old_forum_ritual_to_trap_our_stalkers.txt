Monsters are real, and ghosts are real too. They live inside us, and
sometimes, they win.
That’s what I kept telling myself as I stared at the peeling yellow
wallpaper of that old apartment, watching the dust motes dance in the
gray light of a dying afternoon. Long before the guests arrived, long
before the candles were ever lit, the flat had that specific, hollow
chill - the kind that settles deep into your bones and stays there, no
matter how many sweaters you put on.
I lived there with Barnaby, a quiet, golden-eyed cat who usually spent
his evenings chasing shadows across the floorboards. But that night, he
was different. His claws clicked frantically against the wood, a
nervous, jagged rhythm, hours before anyone even knocked on the door. He
kept staring at the hallway mirror with his ears pinned flat, his back
arched as if he were trying to make himself as small as possible.
Two of my former best friends - girls whose voices I hadn’t heard in
months, yet there they were, standing in my kitchen like they’d never
left - were helping me set up the table. On the small, cracked porcelain
counter of the bathroom, I arranged a tight, obsessive circle of black
and red candles.
We hadn’t invited the boys over for a party. Everybody in the
neighborhood knew what those two men were. We knew the casual cruelty in
their smiles, the way they cornered girls outside the convenience store,
the suffocating sense of dread that drifted off them like heat off
asphalt. We were terrified. It was that slow, grinding kind of terror
that wears down your edges until you aren’t even scared anymore - you’re
just angry.
That anger was how I spent nights scouring archived, forgotten forum
threads, reading and re-reading a buried post about binding until every
instruction burned into my brain. The ritual wasn’t born out of dark
curiosity or occult obsession; it was born out of a desperate, miserable
need for control. If the world was going to let monsters roam free, we
figured we’d build a cage of our own.
Following the old thread’s exact steps, I arranged a tight, obsessive
circle of black and red candles on the small, cracked porcelain counter
of the bathroom. We wanted absolute safety. We wanted to strip away
their free will the moment they walked through that door, turning them
into docile, obedient husks as long as the wax burned. “When they cross
the threshold, the flame binds them,” I whispered to my reflection,
repeating the text from the screen until the words tasted like copper
and ash on my tongue. “They belong to us now.”
The boys arrived right on schedule, smiling too wide, shaking the cold
night air off their leather jackets. But the second the front door
clicked shut behind them, the atmosphere in the apartment didn’t just
shift - it collapsed. The air turned heavy, thick like wet wool,
smelling faintly of sulfur and old, rotting leaves.
And then, the magic took hold.
I rushed to the bathroom to check the candles, and the sight of those
wicks drowning deep in pools of molten wax brought a wild, dizzy wave of
relief. Back in the living room, the transformation was complete. The
two guys stood frozen mid-stride for a second before their posture
slackened into absolute obedience.
For a brief, intoxicating moment, everything felt weightless. We
laughed, the sound bubbling out of us in breathless, nervous giggles of
sheer disbelief. It worked. We were holding the leash.
Watching them was mesmerizing. They didn’t hurt anyone; they couldn’t.
Under our silent command, they just… did normal things, utterly stripped
of their menace. One of them sat stiffly on the couch, mechanically
flipping through a magazine page by page, his eyes glazed and vacant.
The other stood by the kitchen counter, slowly wiping down the surface
with a sponge over and over again in a blank, rhythmic trance. No sharp
remarks, no predatory grins, no threat in the air - just docile,
harmless shadows moving the way we wanted them to move. We traded
victorious grins, feeling safe in our own home for the first time in
months.
I smiled, turning away to grab us all a drink from the kitchen, basking
in the strange, empowering quiet.
Then, the hallway lights flickered once. Twice. And died.
A sudden, absolute silence dropped over the flat - the kind of heavy
quiet that feels like cotton stuffed into your ears. I spun around to
rush back to the living room, but the candles in the bathroom - all five
of them - simultaneously hissed out.
Pitch-black darkness swallowed me whole.
“Guys?” I called out, my voice trembling, sounding impossibly small in
the dark.
No answer. Only a wet, dragging sound echoed from the hallway, like bare
feet sliding slowly across linoleum, accompanied by a hollow, rhythmic
clicking - tap, tap, tap - like fingernails tapping against a coffin
lid. I stumbled out of the bathroom, calling out for my friends, but the
living room was empty. The two guys were gone. My friends were gone.
And worst of all, Barnaby wasn’t meowing. No comforting purr, no frantic
scuttling paws. Just nothing.
Panic hit me like a physical blow, knocking the wind straight out of my
lungs. The moment those flames died, the spell hadn’t just ended - it
had snapped. Whatever ancient, hungry force we had unknowingly tapped
into to enforce our petty little dominance didn’t care about our safety.
It had used the boys and my friends as currency to cross over. And now
that the wax had cooled, the leash was gone, and the entity was looking
for a new host.
I needed to get out. The front door was down the hall, straight past
whatever was dragging itself across the floor in the dark. There was
only one other way.
The bathroom window.
I scrambled backward into the tiled room, my hands shaking so badly I
could barely catch the latch. The window jammed for an agonizing second
before bursting open with a sharp, echoing snap that cut through the
silence like a gunshot.
I hauled myself up, bracing my knee against the sill, ready to drop into
the alleyway below. But just as my upper body cleared the frame, a
freezing, unnatural draft hit my face. It carried a smell like wet
earth, copper, and something sickeningly sweet, like melting candle wax.
My instincts screamed at me not to look down, but my eyes betrayed me.
In the dim amber glow of the streetlamp below, the pavement was empty.
But right outside the glass, standing flush against the brick wall just
inches beneath the bathroom window, something was waiting.
They weren’t walking anymore. They were standing perfectly still,
crammed tight against the building, their faces pressed upward against
the glass like dead things washed ashore. My two former friends were
there, alongside the two dangerous men. Their eyes - wide, milky, and
completely void of any human thought - were staring straight up at me.
And wrapped tightly around one of their slack, graying wrists was a
familiar, blood-stained orange collar with a small brass bell that
refused to ring.
They weren’t our puppets. The moment the fire died, they became the
wardens. And something else was already inside the apartment, waiting
for me to turn around.
I couldn’t see them in the pitch-black void of the bathroom, but I could
feel them. The air grew thick and greasy, heavy with the stench of cold
tallow. I could feel an unnatural, freezing press of bodies standing
just an inch behind my spine - too many bodies to fit in that tiny
space. I couldn’t see a single outline in the dark, but I felt the
phantom brush of hair against my neck, the agonizing absence of
breathing, and the distinct, sickening sensation of multiple pairs of
unblinking eyes staring directly at the back of my head.
And as the breath caught in my throat, a voice didn’t come from the
people below. It brushed against my skin, quiet and intimate, echoing
softly from the dark space right behind my ear:

“Now we all fit inside.”

And that’s the moment I woke up - gasping, staring at my real bedroom
ceiling in absolute, suffocating darkness. I didn’t dare move a single
muscle, too terrified to even blink.
Because right beside my pillow, something heavy and wet was breathing
softly into my hair. And directly against my ear, a tiny, metallic voice
whispered:
„Found you.”

I thought it was talking to me back then. But it wasn’t. It was just
practicing the words for when it reached the right person.
This isn’t my story anymore.
