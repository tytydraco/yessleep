I found Leah.

I need to say that first because I know how my last post ended, and
because what happened after I reached the storage facility is
complicated enough without making people wonder whether my sister is
dead.

She isn’t.

She’s asleep in the room next to me right now.

The problem is that according to my employer, the police and apparently
the Australian government, Leah hasn’t existed since Tuesday.

And the auction for Unit C-44 ends tomorrow morning.

I drove straight to the facility after Darren texted me that Leah was
inside.

The police were already on their way. I called them before leaving Mum’s
house and told the operator my sister might have been abducted. I didn’t
mention disappearing storage units, duplicate keys or photographs taken
before they should exist.

I’d already tried that version.

Darren was waiting outside the gate when I arrived. He looked worse than
I had ever seen him. He was wearing track pants, work boots and the same
polo he’d worn during my suspension meeting.

He opened my passenger door before I had even switched off the engine.

“Give me your phone.”

I told him to get stuffed.

“Not your actual phone. The auction.”

I pulled up the listing.

The photographs of my apartment were still there.

The final image had changed.

The chair inside C-44 was gone.

Leah was sitting on the concrete floor instead.

Her back was against the far wall, her knees pulled to her chest. She
was wearing her work uniform.

Beside her were her handbag, her car keys and one shoe.

The description beneath the photograph read:

ASSORTED PERSONAL EFFECTS. CONDITION UNKNOWN.

There were already three bids.

I don’t know why that detail bothered me as much as it did.

Maybe because the bidders obviously couldn’t see what I could. Darren
showed me the listing on his phone.

His photographs were different.

To him, C-44 contained ordinary household junk.

A bedside table. Some boxes. A microwave. The same wooden chair I had
photographed weeks earlier.

No Leah.

I refreshed my screen.

She was still there.

That was when Darren finally told me he knew more than he had admitted.

C-44 had appeared before.

Not once.

Several times.

The first recorded incident he knew about happened in 2009, years before
he became manager.

Back then, our facility was owned by another company. A branch
supervisor named Graham Bell had been caught stealing from overdue
units.

He didn’t take everything. Mostly small, valuable items that could
disappear without changing an auction photograph too much.

Jewellery. Cash. Collectables. Electronics.

If customers returned and paid what they owed, Graham would claim the
items had already been missing when the staff opened the unit.

Apparently, it worked for almost two years.

Then customers began reporting stranger losses.

Things Graham couldn’t possibly have stolen.

One woman found her wedding ring missing from her bathroom while she was
still renting a unit at the facility. The ring later appeared inside an
overdue unit belonging to someone she had never met.

A man’s passport disappeared from his home and turned up in a locker
scheduled for disposal.

Then Graham opened a unit that wasn’t on the site plan.

C-44.

Darren had been nineteen and working casual weekend shifts.

He didn’t see the first opening himself.

He saw what happened afterwards.

Graham started using C-44.

That was the part Darren had never told anyone.

Whenever something disappeared from a normal unit, Graham would
eventually find it inside C-44. He stopped stealing because he didn’t
need to.

He would wait.

Customers would fall behind on their payments.

The company would legally classify their property as abandoned.

And somehow, the valuable things would begin appearing inside C-44
before the auctions.

Graham sold them privately.

Darren said he knew because Graham paid him twice to help carry boxes to
his car.

He kept insisting he hadn’t understood what was happening then.

Maybe that’s true.

I don’t know.

But one night, Graham found something inside C-44 that wasn’t property.

It was a customer.

A man whose storage unit had been incorrectly processed as abandoned
after a payment error.

The system had marked his account as terminated even though he had been
arguing with head office for days.

Graham opened C-44 and found him sitting inside.

Alive.

The man had disappeared from his house the previous evening.

The police eventually became involved, but by the time they arrived, the
man was back home.

He claimed he remembered walking into his garage and then waking up on
the floor.

Nobody could explain how he had travelled fourteen kilometres.

The company blamed Graham.

They already had evidence of theft, so everything else was buried
beneath that investigation.

Graham was fired.

C-44 disappeared.

“What happened to him?” I asked.

Darren looked at the auction listing on my phone.

“He killed himself a few years later.”

I’m only including that because it’s what Darren told me. I haven’t
verified it, and given everything else he lied about, I’m not treating
it as fact.

What mattered was something Graham had apparently told Darren before he
left.

C-44 didn’t collect lost things.

It collected abandoned things.

There is a difference.

A lost thing still belongs somewhere.

An abandoned thing doesn’t.

That was why my future receipt appeared before I lost it. I had thrown
away the receipt the first time I bought those groceries. The version
inside C-44 hadn’t been taken from the future.

It was what the unit expected to become disposable.

Mo’s glasses were cheap and constantly misplaced. He had apparently left
two previous pairs around the facility without bothering to collect
them.

The cracked screen protector in the first crate had been mine. I had
replaced it months earlier and thrown the old one into a bin at work.

I hadn’t recognised it.

And my apartment key?

That was the part that frightened Darren most.

C-44 hadn’t copied my key.

The key inside the unit was the spare a previous property manager had
cut during a lock change. It was supposedly destroyed when the
management companies changed.

Someone, somewhere, had treated access to my home as something nobody
owned anymore.

Then C-44 found it.

I asked what any of that had to do with Leah.

Darren said he didn’t know.

I did.

My sister’s car had broken down three weeks earlier.

It was sitting in the employee section of a mechanic’s yard, waiting for
a replacement part.

She had been using Mum’s car.

Earlier that afternoon, the mechanic called and said the repairs would
cost more than Leah expected.

She told Mum she might “just abandon the stupid thing and buy another
car”.

That sounded ridiculous as an explanation for what had happened to her.

Until I remembered something from Part 1.

The apartment photographs.

The auction wasn’t only showing my possessions.

It showed me asleep.

C-44 didn’t distinguish between a possession and the person attached to
it.

It followed ownership.

And ownership can become messy when people talk about things as though
they don’t matter anymore.

I asked Darren how auctions had been handled in 2009.

He said essentially the same way they were handled now.

Notice period.

Inventory.

Photographs.

Public listing.

Sale.

Then I realised something so obvious that I felt stupid for missing it.

Nothing inside C-44 had ever come after me until I photographed it.

That was my job.

My photographs weren’t documenting what the unit had taken.

They were inventorying it.

Every time I photographed something inside C-44 and uploaded the images,
I was doing what we did to every overdue unit.

I was officially recording those things as abandoned property.

The police arrived while Darren and I were still arguing about it.

Two officers entered through the gate. One was Constable Reid, who had
attended after I found the duplicate apartment key.

I told him Leah was somewhere on the property.

Darren backed me up.

That changed the conversation.

We showed them Leah’s car, which was still at the mechanic’s. We showed
them Darren’s text. We showed them her unanswered calls.

Then I showed Reid the photograph on my phone.

He saw Leah.

That surprised me.

Until then, I had assumed the images only appeared differently to me.

Reid stared at the screen for several seconds.

Then he asked Darren where C-44 was.

Darren pointed towards C Block.

The unit was there.

No appearing wall.

No changed corridor.

Just C-44 sitting beyond C-42, exactly where it shouldn’t have been.

Its roller door was closed.

Reid told me to stay back.

He and the second officer approached with Darren.

I followed anyway.

I’m not pretending that was sensible.

It was my sister.

Reid raised the roller door.

Leah wasn’t inside.

Neither was the chair.

The unit contained my apartment.

Not things from my apartment.

My apartment.

The kitchen began where the concrete floor should have ended. I could
see the bench, the refrigerator and the cheap little clock above my
stove.

The clock showed 11:38.

My phone showed 11:38.

Reid swore quietly.

The second officer stepped backwards.

Darren only stared.

Then my phone rang.

Leah.

I answered.

She was crying but trying not to make any noise.

She said she was inside my apartment.

I could hear my refrigerator humming through the call.

I could also hear it twenty metres in front of me.

I told her to stay where she was.

She said she couldn’t.

Someone was opening the front door.

I looked through C-44.

The front door of my apartment was visible beyond the kitchen.

The handle moved.

Nobody touched it from our side.

Then a man walked in.

I had never seen him before.

Late fifties, maybe sixty. Grey hair. Work shirt.

Darren made a sound beside me.

“Graham.”

The man stopped in my hallway.

He couldn’t see us.

Or he pretended he couldn’t.

He called Leah’s name.

She whispered through my phone that she didn’t know who he was.

Graham moved slowly through the apartment, opening cupboards and
drawers.

He wasn’t looking for Leah.

He was inspecting things.

Then he began attaching auction stickers.

One on my television.

One on my laptop.

One on the framed photograph of Leah and me.

Red stickers.

LOT ITEM.

Each time he attached one, the corresponding object disappeared from the
image of my apartment on the auction listing.

Then Graham stopped outside my bedroom.

Leah was hiding inside.

I almost ran through the unit.

Reid caught my arm.

He was right to stop me.

We had no idea whether that floor was really my apartment or whether we
would be able to return.

Instead, I opened our auction management app.

My suspension hadn’t removed all my permissions.

The listing for C-44 showed ACTIVE.

Eleven bids.

Closing time: 9:00 a.m.

I tried to cancel it.

MANAGER AUTHORISATION REQUIRED.

I handed the phone to Darren.

He entered his credentials.

The screen changed.

CANCELLATION REASON?

There were six options.

Payment received.

Legal dispute.

Incorrect inventory.

Customer reinstated.

Administrative error.

Other.

I told Darren to choose customer reinstated.

He hesitated.

“There’s no customer.”

“Yes, there is.”

I entered the name attached to the original C-44 account.

Elise M.

The woman who had supposedly rented the impossible unit.

Darren stared at me.

Then he understood.

We had treated C-44 like an abandoned unit from the beginning.

Maybe it wasn’t.

Maybe the first mistake wasn’t opening it.

Maybe the mistake was accepting that it belonged to nobody.

We marked the account as disputed and cancelled the auction.

The effect was immediate.

Every light inside my apartment went out.

Graham turned towards us.

For the first time, he seemed able to see the roller door.

He walked towards it.

Reid raised a hand and told him to stop.

Graham did.

He looked ordinary.

Tired, even.

Then he said something I haven’t stopped thinking about.

“You can’t return everything.”

The roller door dropped.

It struck the concrete hard enough to shake dust from the ceiling.

From inside, Leah screamed.

I grabbed the handle and tried to lift it.

It wouldn’t move.

Then my phone buzzed.

AUCTION CANCELLED.

Below it:

PROPERTY STATUS: RETURN TO OWNER.

The roller door rose by itself.

C-44 was a concrete storage unit again.

Leah was lying on the floor.

Reid reached her first.

She was conscious. Confused. Dehydrated.

But alive.

The wooden chair stood against the back wall.

On it were my wallet, my work ID, Leah’s missing shoe, Mo’s glasses and
about twenty objects I didn’t recognise.

Nobody touched them.

The officers walked Leah out.

I stayed behind for maybe ten seconds.

There was something beneath the chair.

A cardboard archive box.

Written on its side in marker:

UNCLAIMED 2009

I photographed it.

Old habit.

Then I stopped myself before saving the picture.

I deleted it.

We closed the door.

C-44 disappeared before we reached reception.

That was the point when I thought it was over.

It isn’t.

Leah came home with us.

At first, everything seemed normal.

Then Mum called Leah’s workplace to explain why she wouldn’t be coming
in.

Her supervisor said nobody named Leah worked there.

We assumed he was confused.

Then Leah opened her banking app.

Her accounts were gone.

She tried logging into her email.

No account.

Her phone still worked, but every contact except Mum and me had
disappeared.

Her driver’s licence was still in her wallet.

The government verification app wouldn’t recognise the number.

This morning, we went to her apartment.

Her key didn’t work.

A couple answered the door.

They said they had lived there for eight months.

They had photographs on the walls.

Furniture.

A dog.

Everything looked settled.

Leah began yelling that it was her apartment.

The woman threatened to call the police.

So we left.

We went to the police station instead.

Reid was there.

He remembered Leah.

Thank God for that.

His partner remembered her too.

But when Reid searched her name, he couldn’t find the incident from last
night.

He couldn’t find the missing person report.

He couldn’t find her attached to my statement.

He wrote everything down on paper.

He wouldn’t enter it into the computer.

I asked why.

“Because right now, paper seems safer.”

Darren called around lunchtime.

The auction for C-44 had reappeared.

Different branch.

Different listing number.

Same closing time.

9:00 tomorrow morning.

There was only one photograph.

It showed the wooden chair.

On the seat was Leah’s driver’s licence.

The listing description said:

UNCLAIMED PERSONAL EFFECTS. OWNER UNKNOWN.

No bids yet.

Darren contacted head office and told them the listing was fraudulent.

They couldn’t remove it.

Every time they deleted it, it came back.

We have tried changing Leah’s details everywhere we can.

Bank.

Medicare.

Licence records.

Employer.

Lease.

Every system behaves as though we’re trying to create a new person using
someone else’s documents.

Mum remembers her.

I remember her.

Reid remembers her.

That’s it, as far as we have been able to confirm.

Even people who have known Leah for years look at photographs of her and
ask who they’re looking at.

Something else has started happening.

Objects Leah touches are disappearing from photographs.

Mum took a picture of the kitchen this afternoon.

Leah was sitting at the table.

In the photograph, the chair was empty.

The mug she was holding wasn’t there either.

The table continued straight through the space where both should have
been.

That’s why I’m updating this now.

I think cancelling the auction returned Leah physically.

I don’t think it returned ownership.

C-44 still considers her abandoned.

And tomorrow at 9:00 a.m., unless we can establish that she belongs
somewhere before the listing closes, I don’t know what “sold” means when
the item being auctioned is a person.

Leah has been asleep for about forty minutes.

I checked on her before I started writing this.

She’s still there.

But a few minutes ago, somebody placed the first bid.

One dollar.

I refreshed the listing to see whether there was a username.

There isn’t.

It only says:

BIDDER: G. BELL

Darren told me Graham Bell died in 2014.

I’m going back to the facility before morning.

Not to find C-44.

This time, I’m going to find out who Elise M. was.

Because if C-44 has an owner, I think we’ve been auctioning somebody
else’s property for seventeen years.

And I think she wants it returned.
