There is no apartment 4C in my building. I need you to hold on to that,
because it’s the last thing I was sure of.

I live in 4B. The building has eight floors and two units on each, A and
B. Sixteen doors. The lease packet had a floor plan, and in three years
I have never needed a third letter.

The first envelope came in October, pushed under my door instead of left
in my box. A gas bill. My name, my street, my floor. Apartment 4C.

It was warm. Not warm like something left in a car. Warm like it had
been held.

I put it in the lobby bin and told myself it was a printing error.

Two days later a reminder arrived from a dentist I have never visited.
Same name, same address, 4C again. A cleaning, confirmed for Thursday at
3:15.

I called the number. The receptionist found me in seconds and read my
date of birth back to me, correctly.

“You were in last Tuesday,” she said. “You booked the follow-up
yourself.”

“I was at work last Tuesday,” I said.

“That’s what the chart says,” she said. “You mentioned your knee. The
left one, when it rains.”

“I never told you about my knee.”

“You did. In person.” She said it without any defensiveness at all.
“Gray sweatshirt. You always wear the gray one.”

I was wearing it. I hung up and sat very still.

After that the envelopes came every day. A pharmacy pickup for a knee
brace in my size. A receipt from a locksmith for a copied key. A notice
from the gas company asking me to confirm occupancy. Always my name,
always 4C, always warm.

The gas bill showed usage almost double mine, spiking between midnight
and four. I keep my heat off at night.

Then my own apartment started going wrong in small ways. The oven on
when I came home, though I hadn’t used it in a week. The bed made, which
I never do. A towel damp on the rack. Each thing explainable once. I
stopped counting how many times I explained it.

I crossed the hall to ask Dennis in 4A if he’d been getting my mail.
He’s in his sixties and he notices everything that happens on our floor.
He opened the door only a few inches.

“Not your mail,” he said. “But I’ve been wanting to ask about you.”

“Ask me what?” I said.

“You walk the hall at night. Around two-thirty. Months now.” He kept his
eyes on my shoes, not my face. “You go to the end and stand at the wall.
You stay there a long time.”

“I don’t sleepwalk,” I said.

“I know,” Dennis said quietly. “Because I see you leave for work at
eight every morning, and you’re always in your own door. And the one at
night, I’ve never once seen him come back.”

He closed the door. I heard the deadbolt, then the chain.

The hallway ends in a blank wall with a fire extinguisher and a framed
evacuation map. I went and stood in front of it. The paint was cold
under my palm, colder than the rest of the hall. I took my hand away and
found I was holding my breath, and I couldn’t have told you what I was
listening for.

That night I put a chair at my peephole and turned off every light.

At 2:41 the hall light clicked on by itself, the way it does when the
sensor catches someone.

Footsteps came up from the elevator. Uneven, with a hitch on the left
side. I have a bad left knee from an accident at nineteen, and I know
that rhythm because I hear it every time I take a flight of stairs.

Through the peephole I watched myself walk past.

Gray sweatshirt. My haircut, even the part at the back that never lies
flat. My keychain swinging from his hand. He walked toward the end of
the hall, and I let out a breath.

Then he stopped. He turned around and walked back, slowly, and stood in
front of my door.

He was close enough that the peephole filled with the back of his head.
I could see the same crooked part in the hair. I could hear him
breathing, slow and even, and he was tilting his head toward the wood
the way you would to listen to someone sleep.

I stopped breathing. I don’t know how long. When I thought my chest
would give out he straightened, turned, and walked to the end of the
hall.

He pressed his key against the plaster, and the wall opened along a seam
of yellow light. I heard a television, and under it a laugh track, and I
knew which show, because it’s the one I fall asleep to. He stepped
through. A half second after the audience laughed, I heard him laugh
too, a little late, the way you laugh when you’ve learned where the
laughs go.

The seam closed. The hall light went out.

I didn’t move until morning.

When I opened my door at seven there was an envelope on the mat. My
name, and under it, 4B, which was new.

Inside was a sheet of notebook paper in my handwriting. I know it’s mine
because of the lopsided g, the one I have never been able to fix.

*Stop watching. You’re making it harder. Rent’s due on the first for
both of us.*

I opened my banking app with unsteady hands and scrolled back three
years. Two rent payments every month. $1,340 to my landlord for 4B. And
$1,340 to a property company I had never heard of, memo line 4C, the
same day, every month since I moved in. Somebody had been paying for
that room out of my account the entire time, and I had never noticed.

I took my lease out of the kitchen drawer. There was a second page
stapled to the back that I don’t remember being there.

It was a lease for 4C. It had my signature on it, and in the other box a
second signature. The same lopsided g. Both dated the day I moved in.

I held them side by side for a long time. I couldn’t tell you which one
I signed.

It’s almost 2:30 now. I’m in the chair with the lights off, and I just
heard the elevator.

The hall light clicked on. The footsteps have the hitch in them. They
didn’t go to the end of the hall.

They stopped outside my door, and the peephole went dark. Not a shadow.
Something pressed against the other side of the glass.

“I know you’re awake,” he said, in my voice, through the wood. “Don’t
make this harder.”

I’m holding my keys. I can hear a second set sliding against the lock,
slow and patient, like he’s done this many times and has never once been
surprised to find someone home.

And I can’t remember whether I locked it from the inside.
