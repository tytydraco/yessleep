I was lying alone in my room, where a dim nightlight illuminated only
the small area around it. As I often did, I found myself wondering why I
was afraid of the dark, trying to understand it. Most nights, I fell
asleep in fear, or rather, I would drift off without realizing it while
still feeling afraid.

As a child, I was simply scared and never gave it much thought. When I
was younger, it bothered me, and I couldn’t make sense of it, but I
didn’t pay much attention to it either. Now, however, at forty-seven, I
was still afraid of the dark and struggling to sleep in it, and it hurt
my pride.

I remember, two years ago, on October 2, my mother and six brothers came
to visit me. I hadn’t seen any of them in a long time, and we’d had a
wonderful time together. Since there weren’t enough rooms in my house,
my youngest brother and I had to share a room. We kept chatting late
into the night, and I was praying to myself that I wouldn’t be the last
one to fall asleep. But of course, as you might expect, I was the last
one left awake.

My brother was snoring away right in front of my eyes. I was pretty
pissed off. I felt like waking him up and slapping him across the face.
“Jonathan, how the hell can you sleep? You’re such a carefree bastard.
Can’t you see the room is pitch-black?” I wanted to say. I’d lost all
rationality. I was coughing loudly, opening and slamming the door, but
he didn’t give a damn. He’d fallen asleep in the light of a nightlight
that was barely good for anything, leaving me all alone in the room.

That was when my pride was truly hurt. Not being able to close my eyes
in my own room, not knowing what I was afraid of but still being scared
shitless, was really getting on my nerves. After all these years, I’d
finally run out of curiosity. I didn’t care what the reason was anymore.
I just wanted to overcome it.

After spending the entire night awake and having a miserable day the
next day, I immediately started doing research. My brother woke up in
the morning, stretched contentedly, yawned, and scratched his belly
while I lay on the couch in the room, irritated and tense. Seeing him
like that drove me fucking crazy.

My family stayed with me for a week, but my mind was never really with
them. I was constantly looking things up on my phone and couldn’t pay
much attention to them. I was researching other people who were afraid
of the dark, trying to find a good psychotherapist, and so on. I kept
picturing my brother stretching happily that morning. I wanted them to
leave as soon as possible. I was so damn annoyed with him.

Anyway, whatever. I researched and researched. I found people who were
afraid of the dark and talked to them. I had long conversations with all
kinds of people: those who wondered why they were afraid, those who had
figured out the reason, those who were trying to overcome their fear,
and those who told me lengthy stories about their childhood traumas.

Finally, four months ago, I found the Fear of the Dark Motel, and at
last, I was convinced that something might actually help me.

I’d learned about the place from Robin. He was afraid of the dark, just
like me. Despite all my research and all the people I’d talked to, I’d
met him purely by chance. He’d moved into the apartment downstairs, and
one day, as we ran into each other on our way out of the building, we
got to chatting. At some point, he mentioned that he was afraid of the
dark.

His journey had been the same as mine: years of searching for the
reason, until finally deciding to forget the whole thing and just
overcome it.

“It’s hard to give up wondering, isn’t it, Jason? But if you put the
energy and mental effort you spend wondering into finding a solution,
you’d have overcome your fear by now. You’d have become the dancer in
the dark,” said Robin.

Dancer in the Dark was one of my favorite films. I’d taken an immediate
liking to Robin from the very first day, since I’d rarely met someone
whose experience mirrored mine in so many ways, and I’d trusted him to
help me find a solution. His joke was shit, but I didn’t mind. After
all, I loved the film.

But of course, it was impossible to stop thinking about it. Tomorrow,
I’m going to stay at the motel, and yet I’m still wondering why I’m
afraid of the dark.

The motel’s concept was actually quite simple. According to the owner,
they would make us experience terrifying things in the dark that we knew
weren’t real. The motel only accepted guests by referral, meaning you
needed someone who could vouch for your fear of the dark. Then, they
waited until the motel was fully booked. There was a six-month period.
If the motel wasn’t full by then, they would proceed with whoever was
already there. But if it filled up before the six months were over, the
fear sessions would begin that very night.

When I met Lucy, the owner, she explained the concept to me like this:

“I like to keep things simple, Jason. Fear of the dark is a fear of
supernatural entities. It’s as simple as that. Jason, our goal is to
create an imitation of such an experience and show your subconscious
that you won’t come to any harm from a possible supernatural attack in
the dark. It’s that simple. For example, when you see a ghost in your
room here at the motel and realize it can’t hurt you, your subconscious
will start to believe there’s nothing to be afraid of in the dark.”

“But if I know it’s not real, why would it affect me? That doesn’t make
much sense to me.”

“Do you believe in ghosts?”

“No.”

“Thank you, I have no further questions.”

***

It’s 3 a.m. We’re at the motel. Robin is staying in the room next to
mine. The lights are off. Nightlights aren’t allowed. I’m sure the other
guests aren’t asleep, that I’m not alone. And yet, I’m still afraid. My
eyes keep searching for the tiniest glimmer of light. A sliver coming
through the window, seeping in under the door. But there’s nothing. The
room is pitch-black.

Suddenly, Iron Maiden’s “Fear of the Dark” starts playing from somewhere
inside my room.

I flinch and instinctively look around. I get to my feet, feeling around
with my hands until I find a speaker. The song plays for a little while
longer, then suddenly cuts off, and the room sinks into silence once
again.

I hear the sound of a door creaking open. Very slowly, calmly. Since I
know where the speaker is, I lean in and put my ear close to it. No, the
sound isn’t coming from the speaker. My fear grows in a way I hadn’t
expected. I start to regret coming here.

The creaking sound is very close, and crystal clear. I search for
another sliver of light, trying to figure out whether my bedroom door
has opened. But everything is still pitch-black. The door keeps creaking
for thirty seconds, then stops.

I hear a very loud click. Again, it comes from somewhere inside the
room, and again, it’s crystal clear. I’m certain it didn’t come from the
speaker. I listen to the silence with all my concentration, waiting to
hear the sound again so I can pinpoint its location. But it doesn’t come
back.

I hear a whisper: “Jason.” It’s more like someone calling out to me. A
voice with no intention of frightening me, simply calling my name. I
don’t hear it again. I can’t keep track of time, but I’d guess I sit
there in the dark and silence for about ten minutes.

“I’m sorry, Jason, but unfortunately, I’m going to kill you. After that,
I’ll kill everyone else at the motel. It’s me, Lucy. Don’t you recognize
me?” the same whisper says. “Of course, my voice sounds different when I
whisper. That’s normal.”

Each word seems to come from a different place in the room. She must
have installed a bunch of tiny speakers all over the place. But thinking
about that does nothing to ease my fear. I feel like begging her not to
kill me, but I hold myself back. I know none of this is real, and I’m
surprised by how terrified I am despite knowing that.

Suddenly, the lights come on. A second later, they go off again. My eyes
are dazzled. I take a deep breath.

“Did you see me, Jason? Do you believe me now?”

Hmm. So she’d turned on the lights so I could see her. I must have been
looking in the wrong direction.

I hear Robin’s voice coming from the speaker.

“Jason, don’t believe her. That’s what she’s trying to do, make you
believe her. If you do, she’ll kill you. This isn’t a normal place.”

A second later, I hear Robin’s voice right beside me. It doesn’t sound
like it’s coming from a speaker. It sounds real.

“I didn’t know, Jason.”

The lights come on again for a second and then go out, and I see Lucy
standing beside me. She must be wearing a black contact lens.

“Don’t believe him, Jason.”

I’m getting annoyed by how he keeps repeating my name.

“I’ll convince you,” Lucy whispers. Her voice comes from the speaker.

I feel the hairs on my body stand on end. I try to figure out how the
whole setup works, but I can’t concentrate. All I feel is fear.

I want to get out through the door and leave, but I think I’m doing
well. I’ve endured this much fear, and that’s a good thing.

A tiny white light appears on the wall. An oval-shaped light, about the
length of an index finger. It stays perfectly still, and for a very,
very long time, nothing happens. The light doesn’t move. No sound. Just
pitch-black darkness. I’d guess the stillness lasts for about an hour.

The light disappears.

“Do you believe me now?” Lucy asks, whispering again. My ears have grown
accustomed to the silence. Her voice sounds incredibly sharp. Robin
tells me through the speaker not to believe her. Then I think my window
opens, and I hear a distant scream for help from somewhere far outside,
along with the faint sound of the wind.

Then silence again. It goes on for a long time again. Lucy must be busy
dealing with the other guests in the meantime.

Suddenly, I hear a phone ringing. I feel around beside the bed with my
hand. There’s a nightstand. On the fifth ring, I find the phone and
answer it. It’s Lucy.

“Call me when you believe.”

I wonder why no light came in from outside when the window opened. I get
out of bed and walk straight ahead, reaching out with both hands, trying
to find the wall. I find it. I feel along the wall, searching for a
window. I’ve never spent this long in such pitch-black darkness before.
I keep repeating to myself that none of this is real.

The lights come on for a second again, then go out, and I see where the
door is. I walk towards it.

As I walk, I hear a howling sound coming from the speaker. At the same
moment, all the lights come on. I reach the door and try to open it, but
it’s locked. I look around and realize there’s no window in the room.
The howling continues.

“Jason, look out the window,” Lucy says through the speaker. Her voice
blends with the howling. As soon as she finishes the sentence, the
howling stops, too.

I search the walls for a window, but I can’t find one. The lights go out
again. Once more, I’m left in prolonged silence, and nothing happens
until dawn. The window must be in the ceiling. I must be on the top
floor, because sunlight comes pouring into the room from above. I relax.
I’m glad the night is over, and a surge of excitement washes over me. My
fear is gone.

I go to the door and try to open it, but it’s still locked. I give it a
few forceful tugs. Lucy opens it. She’s holding a gun, pointing it
straight at me.

“I’ve been telling you from the beginning, but you never listen.”

She starts shooting. I hear one gunshot after another. I feel my chest
tighten. The gun runs out of bullets, but Lucy keeps pulling the
trigger. I hear the empty clicks. Ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen… She
keeps pulling the trigger. I collapse onto the bed, but I’m still
conscious.

“Are you immortal or something, man? Just die already, would you?” Lucy
says.

I feel around my body with my hands. There’s no blood. I look at Lucy.
She puts the gun on the table, takes out another one, and points it at
me again. She keeps pulling the trigger, over and over.

I check my body again with my hands as I lie there. Still no blood. She
must have used a toy gun that makes shooting sounds.

She puts the other gun on the table and leaves the room.

Five minutes later, the phone rings. I take a deep breath and answer on
the third ring.

“Congratulations, Jason. I’m sorry if I went a little too far. I hope
this helps, and that you feel the benefits as soon as tonight. I hope
you get a good night’s sleep. Thank you very much for choosing us. You
can leave the payment at reception. Have a good afternoon.”

***

I found a night job, and now I can only sleep during the day.
