I have always picked.

My mother used to say I started before I was old enough to remember
doing it. She told me once that when I was four, I would sit in the back
seat of the car and worry the skin from my lips with my teeth. She would
reach one hand back while she was stopped at a red light and pull my
fingers away from my mouth.

“Leave it alone.”

I would nod.

Then, as soon as she looked forward again, I would start again.

By the time I was eight, my lips were almost always raw. I learned to
keep my mouth closed when people looked at me. I learned to lick away
the blood before anyone could notice. When my mother asked why they were
always bleeding, I would shrug and tell her they were dry.

She bought me lip balm. I lost it. She bought another. I lost that one
to…this time on purpose, dropping it from the pouch of my backpack on
the walk home from school. She didn’t notice. Nobody ever did.

It wasn’t that I wanted to hurt myself, I didn’t. I didn’t even
particularly dislike my lips. There was simply something unbearable
about feeling a loose piece of skin there, something slightly raised or
uneven, something that didn’t belong. Once I noticed it, I couldn’t stop
thinking about it until I removed it. There was always a moment
afterward when everything became quiet. That was the part I liked.

I was the middle child, which meant quiet suited me. There were five of
us. My older brother was the first, and first children seemed to come
with their own gravity. Everything he did mattered. His grades mattered,
his sports mattered, His friends mattered. When he got into university,
my parents told everyone they knew. My younger sister was the baby, and
babies have a way of remaining babies even after they can drive. She was
always the one who needed something.

The three children in the centre were different, and I was smack dab in
the middle. my parents rarely fussed over my closest siblings. Of course
we were carted around to our appointments, soccer games, whatever. But
nobody really ever checked in on us to see if everything was ok. We were
fine. That was what people said about us. And I seemed to be the one
they “didnt have to be worried about”, the most.

“You’ve never had to worry about her.”

“She can handle herself.”

“She was always such an easy kid.”

I used to think those were compliments. I understand them differently
now.

My parents weren’t bad people. They loved us, I know that. There were
birthdays ,and Christmases, and family vacations, and pictures on the
refrigerator. My mother packed lunches. My father taught me how to ride
a bike. Neither of them ever hit me. Nobody locked me in a closet or
screamed that I was worthless..

There simply wasn’t much reason to look closely at me. I wasn’t the
child who needed saving, I wasn’t the child who needed celebrating. I
was the child who was doing fine. I learned to make that easy for
everyone. When my brother got a ninety-eight on a test, I didn’t mention
my ninety-two. When my sister cried, I waited until she was finished
before telling my mother that I needed something.

When my parents compared us, it was never cruel. That almost made it
worse.

“Your brother was always so driven.”

“You could try a little harder.”

“Your sister is so outgoing.”

“You’re just more reserved.”

Reserved. Quiet. Sensitive. Easy. Average. Nobody ever called me
remarkable. Eventually I stopped expecting them to.

The picking got worse as I got older.

My lips became my nose, my nose became my fingernails, my fingernails
became the skin around my fingers, and eventually my face became the
place where I spent most of my time. I could find imperfections nobody
else could see. A tiny bump along my jaw, A pore that looked darker than
the one beside it, A flake of dry skin, A scab no bigger than the head
of a pin. Once I found something, I had to finish it. That was the rule,
although I don’t remember ever deciding on it.

Sometimes I didn’t notice I was picking until I felt the sting.
Sometimes I would realize my fingers had been working at the same place
for several minutes. Sometimes I would stand in front of the bathroom
mirror and lose an hour. I always told myself I was almost done, I was
always almost done.

By thirty-five, I had become very good at hiding the evidence. Makeup
helped, and lighting. Long sleeves hid the scabs on my arms. Avoiding
close friendships helped, nobody can ask too many questions about a
person they don’t know very well.

I had an average job in an office on the third floor of a building that
looked exactly like every other office building in the city. I answered
emails, attended meetings, entered numbers into spreadsheets, and ate
lunch at my desk alone more often than I ate anywhere else.

My apartment was clean but not particularly decorated. I had no husband,
no children, no pets. I wasn’t lonely enough to call it loneliness. I
had acquaintances, people I could text, people who would probably notice
if I disappeared for a week.

Probably.

There was nothing wrong with my life, and that was the problem.
Sometimes I would lie in bed at night and think that if someone replaced
me with another woman who looked almost exactly like me, nobody would
notice for at least a few days.

Maybe that was why I picked. It was one of the few things I did that
left evidence. A scar meant something had happened, A scar meant
something had been there, I just didn’t know what.

The Thursday it started was not remarkable. That seems important now.

If there had been thunder, or a strange phone call, or a black bird
tapping against my window, I might have recognized the moment as
significant. Instead, I stopped at a coffee shop on my way home. There
was a long line, and The woman in front of me changed her order twice.
The man ahead of her complained about the price of something, A child
near the front was crying because someone had ordered the wrong pastry.
People sighed and checked their phones and shuffled forward by inches.

When I finally reached the counter, the barista looked over my shoulder.

“Next?”

It seemed like she stared right through me. Then the person behind me
stepped forward. I opened my mouth, but nothing came out. The barista
had already started taking his order. I could have corrected her. I
could have said, “Excuse me, I was next.” Instead, I stepped backward.

“It’s okay.”

I smiled.

The man behind me didn’t even look at me. Something tightened beneath my
ribs. My hand went to my face., and I found a rough patch along my left
cheek, close to the jaw. I rubbed it with my thumb. Then scratched it.
Then picked. I didn’t notice the blood until I felt something warm slide
toward my chin. I wiped it away with the back of my hand.

When I finally ordered my coffee, the barista apologized, however she
seemed like she was just humoring me, like she genuinely believed I
hadn’t been there in line, skipped over by the person behind me. I told
her it was fine. It wasn’t. By the time I got home, I had mostly
forgotten about it. Mostly.

I ate a frozen dinner in my armchair while watching a game show. The
contestants laughed too loudly, the audience clapped at the right
moments, the host smiled as though he had never experienced a bad day in
his life.

I fell asleep there. When I woke, the television was still playing. The
room was dark except for the blue light coming from the screen. My hand
was against my cheek, and there was dried blood beneath my fingernails.
I stared at it for a moment before remembering the coffee shop.

“Oh,” I whispered.

The spot was worse. I went to the bathroom. The overhead light made
everything look harsher than it really was. My hair was flattened on one
side, there was mascara beneath one eye. My cheek was red and swollen
where I had been picking. I leaned toward the mirror.

There was something beneath the torn skin. At first I thought it was
simply new skin. It was pale and smooth, almost impossibly smooth. I
touched it, it was warm. I pulled back, Then touched it again. It didn’t
feel like the rest of my face.

I should have gone to bed. Instead, I picked at the edge, The piece came
away. There was no blood. I stopped breathing. Beneath the damaged skin
was a small oval of something that looked almost translucent. It wasn’t
shiny, exactly. It seemed to hold the light rather than reflect it. I
stared, Then I touched it with one finger. A strange calm spread through
me. The anxiety I’d been carrying since the coffee shop—the
embarrassment, the anger, the feeling of being swallowed up by everyone
else’s needs—simply disappeared. Not faded. Disappeared.

For a few seconds, there was nothing inside my head at all. I smiled.

“There.”

I don’t know why I said it, but I did. I went to bed.

The next morning, the wound was almost completely healed. That should
have frightened me. Instead, I stood in front of the mirror for several
minutes, examining the patch.

It was beautiful, That is the only word I can use.

The skin around it was ordinary. Mine. Slightly uneven, slightly pink
from years of picking, with tiny pores and faint scars.The new patch had
none of those things. It was smooth. Clear. Almost luminous. I tilted my
head toward the bathroom window. The morning light moved across my
cheek. For just a second, I thought the new skin shimmered. I blinked.
It looked normal again. I covered it with makeup before work. Nobody
noticed. I was disappointed.

I was relieved.

I was disappointed that I was relieved.

That evening, I picked again. I told myself I was only cleaning up the
edge. That was a lie. I wanted to see the skin underneath. The new patch
was larger. And the relief came faster. After that, I began looking for
places to pick. At first I searched for obvious imperfections. Then I
started creating them. A small bump became an opportunity. A dry patch
became an invitation. I bought tweezers. Then a small metal extraction
tool. I watched videos about skincare. I learned the names of things I’d
never cared about before. Comedones. Sebaceous filaments. Keratin plugs.

I became fascinated by the architecture of my own skin. There were
layers beneath layers. There was always something underneath. And
underneath that was more. I began to wonder how much of me was actually
the skin I’d been born with. The thought should have frightened me.
Instead, it comforted me.

Because the new skin was better. It made me look better. Not
dramatically at first. A little brighter. A little smoother. A little
more alive. Then people began looking at me. A man held eye contact with
me on the subway. A woman in an elevator told me I had beautiful skin. A
coworker asked if I’d changed my makeup. I hadn’t. I smiled when she
said it.

For the rest of the day, I kept touching my face. I had spent my entire
life wanting someone to notice. Now they were. So I kept picking.

The first time I noticed the pattern underneath the new skin, I thought
it was a blood vessel. It was near my left eye. A thin, delicate line
beneath the translucent surface. I picked at it with the tweezers. The
line moved. In shock, I dropped the tweezers. They made a light metal
clang as they hit the porcelain sink.

I stood perfectly still. It moved again. Not a twitch. Not a pulse. A
slow contraction.nLike something beneath my skin had drawn a breath. I
stepped away from the mirror. I didn’t sleep that night.

The next morning, the line was gone. I told myself I had imagined it. I
told myself I was tired. I told myself I had been picking so much that I
was beginning to see things. That explanation was easier. I preferred
it.

For several days, I stopped. Then I found another rough spot. I picked
it The relief came.And with it came the terrible certainty that I would
keep going.

My sister called on a Sunday. We hadn’t spoken in several weeks.She told
me our brother had gotten another promotion. My mother was planning a
trip. She asked whether I was seeing anyone.

“No.”

“Anyone interesting?”

I laughed.

“No.”

There was a pause.

“You’re still working at that same place?”

“Yeah.”

“Do you like it?”

I looked around my apartment. I thought about the gray office walls. The
frozen dinners. The game shows. The same coffee shop. The same route
home.

“It’s fine.”

She laughed softly.

“You’ve always been easy.”

After we hung up, I stood in front of the bathroom mirror. I looked at
my face. I looked at the beautiful patches spreading across it. I
thought about the word. Easy. I wondered if I’d spent my entire life
choosing the safe thing without realizing I was choosing it. Safe
clothes. Safe conversations. Safe jobs. Safe relationships. Safe
opinions. Safe silences.Maybe I had been picking at more than my skin.
Maybe I had been picking away everything that might have made me
difficult to ignore.

I touched my cheek. The new skin was warm beneath my fingers. For the
first time, I wondered whether it wanted to be touched. Then I laughed
at myself. That was ridiculous. Skin didn’t want anything. Skin was
skin.

I picked.

And something underneath moved.

The hospital came three weeks later. I don’t remember the ambulance. I
remember the bathroom floor. I remember blood. I remember my hands
slipping. I remember trying to stop myself and realizing that I
couldn’t. I remember looking into the mirror and seeing my reflection
smiling while I was crying. Then nothing.

When I woke, my hands were bandaged. There were bandages across my face
and neck. A monitor beeped beside me. nA doctor stood at the foot of my
bed.

“Can I see my face?”

She hesitated. That was the first thing that frightened me.

“What happened?”

“You sustained significant tissue damage.”

“Is it permanent?”

Another pause.

“We don’t know.”

I laughed, it came out weak.

“That’s not very reassuring.”

She didn’t smile.

“We took samples.”

“Of my skin?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

She looked toward the door.

“We don’t know what we’re looking at.”

I stared at her.

“What does that mean?”

“It means the tissue isn’t behaving the way human tissue should.”

She left before I could ask anything else, I slept.

When I woke again, there was a photograph on the table beside my bed. A
microscopic image. Beneath it, someone had written:

SAMPLE A — UNIDENTIFIED TISSUE

I studied the photograph. Layers. Hundreds of them. Some appeared
normal. Some didn’t. And underneath all of them was something impossibly
smooth. bPerfect.bI looked at my hands.nThe skin was beautiful
now.nAlmost translucent. I had always hated my hands, too plain., too
ordinary. Now I could see faint patterns beneath the surface.

I pressed my thumb into my palm. Something underneath moved. I dropped
the photograph and screamed. A nurse came running.

“What happened?”

I couldn’t answer.

Because suddenly I understood something I had never thought about
before.

We spend our whole lives believing we are the same person because our
bodies look continuous. But skin is replaced constantly. Cells die.
Cells are shed. Cells are born. Layer after layer, the body quietly
removes itself and rebuilds.

We call that healing. We call it aging We call it life. But if your skin
is being replaced… how do you know you’re the same person underneath it?

I looked toward the darkened hospital window. At first I thought the
reflection was mine. It was my face, but it wasnt. My face, but somehow
different in a way I could not explain. Then she smiled. I didn’t. I
stepped closer. She did too. I raised my hand. She raised hers. For a
moment, everything matched. Then I noticed the eyes. They were brighter
than mine. The skin was flawless. The face was beautiful. Perfect. More
beautiful than I had ever been. More noticeable. Impossible to ignore.

I had spent my whole life wishing someone would see me. I had never
asked what I wanted them to see. I pressed my palm against the glass.
Something pressed back. I pulled my hand away. The reflection didn’t.
She stood there, watching me.

Then she lifted one hand to her cheek,

And began to pick.
