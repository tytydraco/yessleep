I’ve worked graveyard dispatch for the regional line for six years, and
I want to say that up front, because what I’m about to tell you doesn’t
fit anywhere in those six years. Not one shift. Not one anomaly report.
I need you to understand that before I say anything else, I know this
system. I know every switch, every siding, every dead spur from here to
the coast. I helped draw half the maps.

So when I tell you there is no track where I saw what I saw, I need you
to believe that I checked. Twice.

It was a Tuesday in November, the kind of Pacific Northwest night where
the fog doesn’t roll in so much as arrive all at once, like someone
dropped a wet sheet over the coast range. I was doing my usual loop,
Highway 101 runs parallel to our freight corridor for about eleven miles
north of town, and part of my job on the graveyard shift is driving that
stretch in the company truck, checking for washouts, debris, anything
that might foul the line before the 3:40 AM freight came through.

The fog that night had a weight to it. I don’t know how else to describe
it. It pressed against the windshield like it wanted in.

Around mile marker seven, my headlights caught something wrong.

There’s a siding out there, an old spur that hasn’t seen active use
since the paper mill closed in the nineties. It’s maybe two hundred feet
of rusted rail branching off the main line, ending in a bumper block
swallowed by blackberry bramble. It’s on every map I’ve ever worked
from. I could draw it from memory.

That night, there was a train car sitting on it.

I slowed the truck. My first thought, my only thought, really, because
your mind reaches for the mundane before it reaches for anything else,
was that a car had come loose from a consist somewhere and rolled down
onto the dead spur. It happens, rarely, but it happens. I radioed it in
as a stray car, requested confirmation on the log.

Dispatch came back within thirty seconds. There was no missing car.
Every consist on the line that night was accounted for, coupled and
logged.

I sat in the truck with the engine running and the wipers going and
looked at that car through the fog, and something in my chest went very
cold and very still.

It was a passenger car. Old…. the kind you see in photographs, riveted
steel, a dull hunter green gone matte with age, the paint not so much
faded as tired. No markings I recognized. No road name, no number
stenciled on the side, nothing. Just a long dark shape with a row of
windows, every single one of them black.

Not dark. Black. There’s a difference, and I didn’t understand it until
I was standing next to the thing.

I got out of the truck. I want to tell you I hesitated, that I thought
it through, but the truth is I was already moving before I decided to
move. That’s the part that scares me most in hindsight, some animal
piece of my brain got up out of the driver’s seat and walked me toward
that car before the rest of me caught up.

The fog swallowed the truck’s headlights behind me within a dozen steps.
The temperature dropped. Not gradually, all at once, like walking into a
walk-in freezer, that specific damp cold that gets into your molars. My
breath came out in a thick cloud and hung there instead of dispersing,
like the air itself had gone too heavy to carry it away.

And underneath the cold, a smell. Copper. Wet pennies and old blood, the
smell of a nosebleed multiplied by a hundred, coming from somewhere I
couldn’t place, everywhere and nowhere, the way smells get when the fog
is thick enough to have texture.

I stopped maybe fifteen feet from the car. Close enough to see the
rivets. Close enough to see that the metal wasn’t rusted so much as
pitted, like something had been chewing on it slowly for a very long
time.

That’s when I heard the footsteps.

Not on the gravel. Inside the car.

Thump.

A pause. Long enough that I started to convince myself I’d imagined it.

Thump.

Heavy. Deliberate. Not the light, uneven footfalls of a person walking
normally, this had weight and rhythm, like something enormous taking
careful, measured steps, matching a beat only it could hear. It moved
the length of the car, slow, and I realized I was counting without
meaning to, and I realized the count matched roughly one step per
window, and I realized I had started backing away without my legs asking
permission from the rest of me.

The door of the car, a single, narrow accordion door near the front, was
closed. I want to be very clear that it was closed. I would have seen it
open. I was staring right at it.

And then it wasn’t closed anymore, and I never saw it move.

One second, shut. The next, a black gap where it had been, like someone
had taken a bite out of the car. No sound of hinges, no hiss of an air
brake system releasing. Just there, suddenly, the way a word appears on
a page you’ve been staring at without reading.

The footsteps stopped.

I stopped too. Every part of me stopped, my breath, my blood, whatever
part of your brain keeps your knees from locking. I stood in that
freezing, copper-smelling fog and stared at a black doorway on a train
car that did not exist, and something in that doorway looked back at me.

I never saw a shape. I want to tell you it had a face, or claws, or red
eyes glowing in the dark, because that would be a story with an ending,
a monster you could name and be done with. What I got instead was worse.
I got the certainty of being seen, the same prickling, full-body
awareness you get when you’re being watched from across a room, except
amplified until it was almost a physical pressure, pushing against my
sternum, pushing the air out of my lungs.

And underneath that pressure, a thought that was not mine arrived in my
head, fully formed, the way a word gets stuck in your throat before you
say it out loud.

You found the siding. Now you belong to the schedule.

I don’t know how long I stood there. Long enough for the cold to work
its way into my hands so deep I couldn’t feel my own fingers curling
into fists. Long enough for my mind to go somewhere very small and very
quiet, a place where I was just an animal wanting to run and too frozen
to manage it.

What finally broke it was the radio. My handheld, clipped to my belt,
crackling to life with dispatch’s voice asking for my twenty, routine as
anything, and the ordinary human sound of it, code numbers and static
and someone chewing gum on the other end, was enough to snap something
loose in me.

I ran.

I don’t remember the fifteen feet back to the truck. I remember the door
handle freezing to my palm. I remember the engine turning over on the
second try, my hands shaking too hard to manage the key on the first,
and I remember not looking in the rearview mirror, not once, all the way
back to the yard, because some certainty deep in my gut told me that if
I looked, something would be looking back.

I filed the anomaly report. I want you to know I filed it exactly as it
happened, and my supervisor read it with the particular blank patience
of a man deciding whether to send you home or send you to HR. He sent a
maintenance crew out at first light to check the siding.

There was no car there. There was barely a siding at all, the rail had
been pulled up years before decommissioning, the ties rotted through,
blackberry vines grown thick enough over the bumper block that the crew
had to cut their way in with loppers just to confirm what the survey
already told them. Nothing had rolled through there in decades. Nothing
could have.

I went back myself, three days later, in full daylight, because I needed
to see it with my own eyes in the sun. The rail was exactly as the crew
described. Overgrown. Silent. Ordinary.

Except that when I crouched down at the spot where I remembered the
car’s front wheels sitting, I found the gravel scorched in a long,
narrow rectangle, the rocks fused together in places like they’d been
through a kiln. Maintenance had no explanation for it. Neither do I.

I’ve thought about that unspoken sentence more nights than I’d like to
admit. You found the siding. Now you belong to the schedule. I’ve turned
it over so many times the words have gone smooth and strange, the way
any phrase does if you say it enough.

I still do the graveyard shift. I still drive that stretch of 101 four
nights a week, because the job doesn’t stop needing done just because
something out there knows my name now, and I believe it does know my
name, though I couldn’t tell you why I believe that, only that I do, the
same bone-deep way I believe the sun will come up.

I’ve never seen the car again. Not once, not in over a year of driving
that road.

But three weeks ago, on a foggy Tuesday not unlike the first, I glanced
at my dashboard clock as I passed mile marker seven, out of habit,
nothing more.

It read 3:40 AM, the exact minute the old freight used to run through,
back before the mill closed, back before the schedule that car seems to
be keeping all its own.

I haven’t looked at that clock passing mile seven since. I don’t think I
ever will again.

But I keep wondering what happens the night I forget.
