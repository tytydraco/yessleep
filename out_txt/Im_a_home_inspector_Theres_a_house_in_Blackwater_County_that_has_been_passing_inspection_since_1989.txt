Blackwater County. Posting this from my kitchen, with every light on.

I’m not going to give you my real name, and I’m not going to tell you
which road the house is on. If you live around here you already know a
house like this. If you don’t, I’d like you to keep it that way.

I’ve been a licensed home inspector for eleven years. Fourteen hundred
inspections, mostly in the mountain counties north of Atlanta. I’ve seen
raccoons in attics, a bathtub held up by paperback westerns, a man’s
life savings in a sealed Folgers can behind a water heater. Houses are
honest. They tell you what’s wrong with them if you’re patient and you
look.

The call

The realtor’s name is Teri. I’ve worked with her for years. She called
on a Friday in the middle of October and said she had a favor that paid.

“Estate sale,” she said. “Farmhouse out Larkin Hollow. Nobody’s lived in
it since the eighties. Heirs in Ohio just want it gone. I’ve got a young
couple who’ll take it as-is if the inspection comes back clean.”

“Nobody’s lived in it since the eighties and you think it’s going to
come back clean?”

“I think they’re not going to do the work either way. I just need the
report. Three other guys said they’d do it and then didn’t show.”

I should have asked about the three guys. I asked how much. She said
six-fifty. I wrote down the address and said I’d be there Tuesday at
three.

The house

Larkin Hollow Road runs five miles off the county highway, up a long
valley with the ridge on one side and laurel on the other. The house sat
alone at the end: two-story farmhouse, deep porch, swing, white
clapboard, tin roof, stone chimney. Big oak in the yard. Shed.
Clothesline. Rusted pump.

I pulled in at ten after three. Clear day, high fifties, light already
slanting.

A house empty for thirty-six years has a particular posture. The porch
sags. Paint goes to chalk. Windows go milky. Something gets into the
gutters. This house looked like somebody had just stepped out. Porch
boards swept. Swing hanging straight. Paint a clean soft white. Windows
so clear I could see the yellow checked curtains inside.

The lawn was cut.

I told myself somebody was paid to maintain it. That’s a normal thing.
It happens.

The lockbox code worked. The door swung in and the first thing that hit
me was the smell of baking bread—warm, yeasty, the kind you smell once
in a grandmother’s kitchen and never forget. Not faint. The smell of an
oven that had been running for an hour.

“Hello? Home inspector. Anybody in here?”

A clock ticked somewhere. I stepped inside.

It was warm. Comfortable. The old round Honeywell in the front hall said
seventy-one. I took readings in every room with the infrared.
Seventy-one.

The power company had no record of an account. Teri had checked. The
house had been dark since 1989.

I did the inspection anyway. Started at the panel. Federal Pacific, a
little old, but every breaker tight and the wiring neat as a quilt.
Outlets tested clean. Water ran hot in twenty seconds and came clear.
Under the sinks: dry. Attic insulation like fresh snow. No mice.

In my line of work a perfect house is a house that’s lying. I kept
going.

The dining room table was set for supper. Five places. Blue plates,
mismatched forks, a glass of milk at the smallest chair. The milk was
cold. I know because I picked up the glass and set it down again very
carefully.

The basement

I always do the furnace last. This one was a big floor-standing Lennox
from the late seventies, tan, running. I could hear the burners and the
blower.

I switched on the combustion analyzer and held the wand at the flue.
Then near the supply register. The number climbed and the alarm began to
shriek.

Four thousand one hundred parts per million.

Thirty-five ppm for eight hours is the worker limit. Four hundred is a
headache in an hour. Sixteen hundred, unconscious in twenty minutes.
Four thousand is death in minutes in a closed house with the people
inside asleep.

I got down on one knee and shined my light into the burner compartment.
The heat exchanger had a crack as long as my hand. The flame was rolling
out of the seam in a ragged yellow-orange tongue, breathing carbon
monoxide straight into the ducts.

I shut the gas valve. The burners died with a little sigh. The blower
slowed. The analyzer’s screech fell to a chirp.

In the silence, from the floor above me, a chair scraped. A woman’s
voice said something low and cheerful. A child answered. Then a man
laughed, deep in his chest.

The Ostranders

I climbed the stairs.

The kitchen was full of afternoon light through the yellow curtains. It
smelled of bread and onions and woodsmoke. A woman in a blue apron stood
at the stove, stirring. Maybe thirty-five. Brown hair pinned up. A boy
of about eleven sat on the counter swinging his feet. A girl of about
seven stood on a chair at the table, arranging forks, tongue between her
teeth.

The woman turned.

“You’ll be the inspector,” she said. She smiled a tired, kind, ordinary
smile. “You’re early. They don’t usually come till four.”

“Ma’am. The door was unlocked. Teri sent me. I didn’t know anyone was
living here.”

“It’s all right, honey.” She wiped her hands on the apron. “I’m Ruth.
That’s Tommy and that’s Birdie, and Hal’s out at the shed. The baby’s
asleep. You’ll want to see the furnace, I expect.”

On the wall by the door was a feed-store calendar, open to October. In
the margin, in a woman’s neat pencil: Tues. 17th, furnace man, beside
it: chicken for supper.

The year at the top of the page was 1989.

“Ruth,” I said. “Mrs. Ostrander. Your furnace is broken. It’s leaking
carbon monoxide. If you run it tonight, all of you—in the night—you
won’t wake up.”

“I know, honey,” she said gently. “You’re the sixth.”

What I tried

I told Hal when he came in from the shed, a big man in a canvas coat
with sawdust on his sleeves. He listened to every word, nodded, and said
he’d get it looked at after the first frost. Then he put his hand on my
shoulder and said, “We appreciate you coming out,” in a voice that meant
nothing could be done.

I went back downstairs and pulled the burner assembly. I took the
thermocouple. I unscrewed the gas line at the union and put the fitting
in my pocket. When I came back up, the clock had moved and Hal was in
the basement with a wrench, putting it all back together, humming. The
burners caught.

I opened every window in the house. When I came back from the last one,
the first was closed.

I scooped Tommy off the counter and ran with him through the hall and
out onto the porch. With every step he got lighter in my arms. At the
rail he was a coat, just a boy’s canvas coat, folded, with a mitten in
the pocket.

I turned around. Tommy was in the kitchen, sitting on the counter,
swinging his feet.

“It doesn’t work,” he told me politely. “Everybody tries.”

The hooks

In the front hall, going back in, I saw the coats.

A row of wooden pegs along the wall by the door. On them hung five
coats. Beneath them, on the narrow hall table, lay five clipboards.

Canvas work jacket with a corduroy collar. Green nylon windbreaker with
a patch that said Hartley Home Inspection. Heavy tan parka. Gray fleece
vest with a company logo from the 2000s. Black rain shell, newer, zipper
still shiny.

Five coats. Five clipboards. The top paper was yellow with age, the
pencil gone silver. The bottom one used the template I use. Date in the
corner: 10/17/2021.

There was a sixth peg. Empty. At the end of the row.

“That’s yours,” said Birdie.

She had come up behind me. Seven years old, yellow dress, two braids, a
gap where her front tooth had been. Holding a fork. Perfectly calm,
friendly face.

“Supper’s ready,” she said. “Mama says you can sit by me.”

The table

I followed her because I could not think what else to do.

The table had grown. It ran the length of the room now, white cloth, and
they were all at it. Ruth, Hal, the two older children, a high chair
with a baby asleep holding a biscuit. And in the other chairs, in their
stocking feet, with napkins in their laps, sat five men.

They’d taken off their coats. They’d set down their tools. They were
eating chicken and dumplings off the blue plates, pleasant and
unhurried. Every one of them had the same look: the look of a man who
had stopped being surprised a long time ago. It looked like peace. It
was the worst thing I have ever seen.

The man nearest me was in his sixties, gray mustache, thin windbreaker.
He wiped his mouth.

“Evening. Hartley. Dale Hartley. Don’t mind us.”

“You’re dead,” I said.

“I’m having supper.” His eyes did not quite follow my face. “It’s easier
to just sit, son. You know where you are. You know everybody. It’s a
good table.”

Ruth had come around the table, drying her hands on her apron. She spoke
low so the girl wouldn’t hear.

“You don’t have to. Nobody’s ever made to. It isn’t a trick. We’ve got
used to the company. It’s a long night, and then it’s the same night.
But if you want to go, go. We don’t hold anyone.”

“If I go,” I said, “what happens to you.”

“The same.”

“If I stay.”

“The same, honey. You can’t change it. Not the first one, not the sixth.
It already happened. We’re the part that’s left over. All you can decide
is where you’re standing when it does.”

I looked at the analyzer in my hand. It still said 4,100. It was the
only honest thing in the room.

The clock on the wall said 5:58.

“I’m sorry,” I said to Birdie.

She looked at me with that calm face and the gap in her teeth.

“Okay,” she said. She let go of my hand. “Say hi to the next one.”

Every head at the table turned to watch me go. They turned together, the
same small polite smile on all of them. Dale lifted two fingers from his
fork like a man waving a car through.

Leaving

I remember the porch swing and the cold on my face and the oak dark
against a pink sky. I sat in the cab of my truck and put my shoes on
with hands that would not do the laces.

The house stood in the dusk with every window lit. Through the
dining-room window I could see them at the table, a row of heads bent
over their plates. I counted them three times. Ten, counting the baby.
Eleven, counting the empty chair.

On the sixth peg in the front hall, visible through the glass of the
door, something dark and heavy was hanging.

I looked at the seat beside me. My jacket was there. I put my hand on it
to be sure.

I drove down Larkin Hollow Road at eight miles an hour. I didn’t look in
the mirror until the highway. When I did, there was only dark where the
valley was.

After

I wrote the report that night. Furnace: heat exchanger failure. Carbon
monoxide at lethal concentration. Do not occupy. I attached the photos,
which were all black, and the analyzer log, which was full of readings I
had not taken. I sent it to Teri at 11:40 p.m.

At 6:15 in the morning I got a copy back from my own system. I opened
the PDF.

Summary of findings: No deficiencies noted. Property passes inspection.

At the bottom was my digital stamp. Above it, a handwritten scrawl in
blue ink, careful and looping, a woman’s hand. The date beside it was
the seventeenth of October, 1989.

I called Teri. She said the buyers were thrilled. A young couple named
Nate and Lauren Weems, with a little boy. Closing on Friday. Moving in
on Halloween.

I tried to tell her. She laughed and said that’s not what the report
says, and asked if I was feeling all right.

At the courthouse a clerk looked at the address and went still. Five
people had died there in October of 1989. Carbon monoxide, in their
sleep. The house had been sold six times since. None of the sales had
lasted. She asked if I’d inspected it.

I said yes.

She told me, very quietly, to go home and not drive that road again.

Now

The closing’s in three days. I’ve called the Weems couple. I’ve left
voicemails. I’ve emailed them the real report. I don’t think the system
will let them read it. Teri has stopped answering the door.

I haven’t been back to the house.

At exactly ten after three every afternoon, wherever I am, the world
goes quiet for the length of a breath and I smell bread.

Last night I hung my work jacket in the hall closet. This morning the
closet was empty. I found the jacket hanging on a peg by my own front
door, where I’ve never had a peg. It was damp. It smelled like woodsmoke
and baked bread.

There’s a sixth peg by my front door. I didn’t put it there.

I keep thinking about what Ruth said. All you can decide is where you’re
standing when it does.

I’ve checked my own furnace eleven times. The analyzer says zero.

It says zero.

I’m going to go check it again.
