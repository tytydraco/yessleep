I wake most nights with that familiar catch in my throat, as though some
essential word has lodged there forever, waiting for the moment it might
have changed everything if only I had spoken it sooner. The apartment is
dark and ordinary, the same four walls, the same faint red numbers on
the clock glowing 3:17 or 4:02 or whatever indifferent hour sleep
finally releases me. The air feels thick, weighted with the scent I can
never quite shake: strawberry bubblegum, sweet and fleeting, the way it
used to drift through our childhood house like a promise kept.
Sarah arrives without announcement, the way she always does, stepping
straight into the quiet space behind my eyes. Tonight we begin on the
porch again. The old swing creaks under our combined weight. I am
fifteen or sixteen, lanky and still growing into my hands; she is nine,
small enough that her sneakers dangle above the weathered floorboards.
She chews with the serious concentration she always saved for important
work, working the pink mass patiently until she can blow a perfect
bubble. It swells, trembles, iridescent in the late-afternoon light,
then pops against her nose with a soft snap. She laughs, that bright,
uncontainable sound, and peels the sticky pink off her face with two
careful fingers.
“Gotcha again,” she says, grinning. “You always stare when I do that.”
“I’m waiting for the day it covers your whole head,” I tell her. “Then
we’ll have to cut it off like hair.”
She giggles and leans against my shoulder, warm and solid. “You’d never
cut my hair. You like it long.”
“I like you any way you come,” I say, and it is the simplest truth I
know. I reach over and tuck a loose strand behind her ear. “Even with
gum in it.”
She blows another bubble, smaller this time, deliberate. “Jake,” she
says, her voice softening, “do you think we’ll always live here? You and
me and Mom?”
I look out at the yard where the lilac bush hangs heavy with purple
blooms. The air smells of cut grass and summer. “We’ll always be
together,” I tell her. “Doesn’t matter where. You’re stuck with me,
squirt.”
“Good.” She rests her head on my arm. “Because you’re my favorite
person. Forever and ever.”
I believed her then with the easy certainty of youth. I still believe
her now, even when belief hurts.
The swing keeps its slow rhythm for a while longer, the chains sighing
overhead. Her chewing quiets gradually. She coughs once, a small sharp
sound, then again, deeper in her chest. She tries to cover it with her
hand, as though she can hide the sound from both of us.
“You okay?” I ask.
She nods too quickly. “Yeah. Just tickly throat.” She blows another
bubble, but it is fragile and breaks early. “See? I’m fine.”
The lie hangs between us only a moment before the porch begins to thin,
the colors leaching out like watercolor left too long in the rain. The
lilacs fade. The swing stills. We are in the doctor’s office now, the
fluorescent lights humming overhead like trapped insects. The doctor
speaks in calm, measured sentences that have already carved themselves
into my memory: acute lymphoblastic leukemia, induction phase,
chemotherapy, long road ahead. Sarah’s hand finds mine under the
crinkled paper on the exam table. Her fingers are cold despite the
warmth of the room.
“Jake,” she whispers, so only I can hear, “am I gonna die?”
I swallow against the stone in my throat. “No. You’re not. We’re gonna
fight this thing together. You and me. Like always.”
She looks up at me, eyes wide and searching. “Promise?”
“Cross my heart,” I tell her.
She squeezes my hand once, hard. “Okay. Then I believe you.”
The promise settles over us both, fragile as new skin.
The nights begin to layer after that, one memory folding into the next
until time feels less like a line and more like stacked stones. In the
next dream she is thirteen. Her head is smooth from the chemo, pale and
vulnerable under the hospital lights. She sits propped up in the narrow
bed, IV line taped neatly to the back of her hand. I wait until the
nurse steps out, then slip a fresh piece of gum into her palm like
contraband.
“Thanks,” she says quietly. “Everything tastes like old pennies
otherwise.”
I sit on the edge of the mattress. “Better?”
She nods, chewing slowly, carefully, because even that small motion
pulls at the sores inside her mouth. “Much.” She leans against my
shoulder. “Tell me about the beach again.”
I start the story we have told so many times it feels like scripture.
“White sand so soft it feels like powder under your feet. Water so clear
you can see straight to the bottom, fish darting like little arrows.
We’ll build a castle with towers and a moat. You’ll be in charge of the
flags.”
“Pink flags,” she says at once. “And a dragon on top to guard it.”
“Pink flags and a dragon. Biggest one we can make.”
She smiles, small but real. “You’ll help me dig?”
“Every shovel full. I’ll even carry the buckets when your arms get
tired.”
She rests her head on my chest. “You’re the best brother in the whole
world, you know that?”
“You’re the best sister. No contest.”
The grief moves in quietly, the way dusk arrives. It settles in the
empty chair beside her bed when visiting hours end. It lives in the way
her laughter has grown thinner, more effortful. It clings to the gum
wrappers she still saves, taped end to end in a long silver-and-pink
chain across the hospital wall because she says it reminds her of home.
I drive her to appointments in silence, hold basins when the nausea
comes in waves, learn the language of white counts and central lines and
neutropenic precautions. Between rounds I braid the fragile new hair
that grows back, thin as corn silk. I learn how love and terror can
occupy the same space without one ever fully canceling the other.
One afternoon the room is quiet except for her soft crying. She has
turned her face to the wall. The steroids have rounded her cheeks and
she will not look in the mirror anymore.
“Sarah,” I say, sitting on the bed’s edge. “What’s wrong?”
She wipes her eyes with the back of her hand. “I look like a different
person. I don’t even recognize myself.”
I turn her gently until she faces me. “You look like my sister. Same
eyes. Same smile when you want to use it. Same stubborn streak a mile
wide. Nothing’s changed that matters.”
She sniffs. “You’re just saying that.”
“I’m saying what’s true. You’re still the girl who beats me at checkers
every time. Still the one who makes me laugh when I don’t feel like it.
Still mine.”
She leans into me then, small and fierce. “Don’t ever stop being my
brother, okay?”
“Never,” I tell her. “Not in a million years.”
The hospital room shrinks a little more with each returning dream. The
machines keep their steady count. Her breathing grows shallower, more
deliberate. She is fifteen now, pared down to bone and stubborn will.
The pain has become a constant companion, rising higher than the
morphine drip can reach. She still tries to smile for me, lips cracked,
eyes luminous with the effort.
One night she whispers, “Jake, I’m so tired. I don’t know if I can keep
doing this.”
I climb onto the bed beside her, careful of the tubes and wires. I wrap
my arms around her small, fever-hot frame. “You don’t have to do it
alone. I’m right here.”
She nestles closer. “Love you to the moon and back.”
“To the stars and farther,” I answer, the old nighttime words we traded
when she was little and afraid of thunder.
She sighs against my chest. “I believe you still.”
The final dream stretches longer than the others. The room is dim, the
oxygen hissing softly through the nasal cannula. She sleeps, face slack
with exhaustion, every shallow rise and fall of her chest costing her
something precious. I stand beside the bed and watch. The grief swells
until it fills my throat, my chest, the spaces between my fingers. I see
the mornings still to come: her waking in tears before the pain fully
registers. I see her trying to blow one last bubble for me, even when it
makes her cough until bright blood speckles the tissue. I see love
asking the one question it has no right to ask. What if mercy looks like
this?
My hand moves before thought can stop it. The pillow is soft, ordinary,
warm from her body. I lift it slowly, place it over her face with the
same care I once used to tuck her blanket under her chin. Then I press.
Her body startles once, a faint, instinctive flutter, then stills. The
monitors shatter the quiet with their wail. Nurses rush in. I step back,
hands shaking, and say the words I have carried through every version of
this night: “She’s gone. The cancer took her.”
They believe me. They offer comfort. They tell me I was the best brother
she could have had.
But in the dream I catch my own reflection in the dark window. The truth
lives in my eyes. I did not wait for permission. I ended it. I pressed
the pillow down because I loved her too much to let another sunrise
arrive carrying more pain. I called it mercy then. In the quiet hours I
still call it mercy.
I wake gasping, sheets damp with sweat, heart hammering against my ribs.
The apartment is silent except for the low hum of the refrigerator. My
hands remember the give of the pillow, the sudden hush that followed.
Sarah’s face lingers behind my eyes: the gap-toothed grin, the
pink-stained tongue, the absolute trust that never wavered.
I loved her. I love her still. That love is the blade that cuts deepest
and the only balm that matters. It is the crime that cannot be undone
and the absolution I cannot quite accept.
In the dark I whisper her name like a prayer and like an accusation.
Sarah. My sister. My heart. I’m sorry. I love you. To the stars and
farther.
The dreams will return tomorrow night. They always do. They will carry
her back to the porch, chewing gum, laughing, believing in me with that
same unshakable faith. They will remind me of every promise I kept and
the one I broke in the name of love.
I close my eyes and let sleep pull me under again, because she is
waiting there, patient, unchanged, blowing perfect pink bubbles in the
afternoon light of a house we will never see again.
And I cannot leave her alone. Not yet. Not ever.
 Not ever.
