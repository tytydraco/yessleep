Part 4

I need to be honest with you all before I continue.

Writing that last part did something to me. I thought getting Ian’s
story out would help. It didn’t. It stirred everything back up. The
bell. The door closing soft behind me. The math I did in that kitchen
with my hand on the knob. Some of you left comments telling me it wasn’t
my fault. I appreciate it, I read every one of them. I don’t believe a
single word, but I appreciate it.

So bear with me if this one reads a little rough. I’m writing because
the alternative is sitting with it, and I’m done sitting with it.

Work didn’t stop. Work never stops here. I kept showing up, kept reading
the notes, kept finding my keys in whatever stupid hiding spot was
picked that night. The calls kept coming too. I want to tell you about
the weird ones, I do, but honestly I barely remember them. A woman who
reported her reflection missing. A traffic stop where the driver thanked
me by a name that isn’t mine and I didn’t bother correcting him.
Something at the gas station that I’m not going to write down. I handled
all of it the same way. Show up. Listen to dispatch. Do what they say.
Drive away.

Steve passed me in the locker room about two weeks after it happened. He
looked at me longer than he ever has and I thought, here it comes, the
speech. First one’s the worst, kid. Instead he just nodded. One time.
Like he was confirming something.

It was a night shift, about three weeks after Ian. I walked past the
empty desk like always and headed for the Sergeant’s office to find my
note. I stopped dead in the middle of the hallway because I need you to
understand what I was looking at.

The door was open.

Every shift since I transferred here, there’s been a note taped to that
door and nothing behind it. Or something behind it. I never knew which
was worse. I’ve been dispatched to demons and car crashes that drive
themselves and I have never once seen that door so much as cracked. Now
it was standing wide open with the light on. And the light was just
normal.

Every instinct I have told me this was the most dangerous thing I’d ever
seen in this town.

I approached it the way you approach a car with tinted windows at 3am.
Slow. Angled. One hand resting where it always rests. I stopped at the
threshold and looked in. I don’t know what I was expecting.

It was an office. A desk, a chair, a filing cabinet, a coffee mug. A
door in the back corner, half open, private bathroom, I could see the
edge of a sink. The desk had paperwork on it, squared into neat little
stacks. And the walls were covered in notes. Pinned up corner to corner,
hundreds of them. Every note I’d ever pulled off that door and hundreds
more I’d never seen. Same handwriting on every single one. I remember
thinking that handwriting looked familiar. Not from the door. From
somewhere else. I remember starting to figure out where I’d seen it
before, and that’s as far as I got.

Because that’s when I heard whistling coming from the bathroom.

I stepped inside. I want to tell you something made me do it. Nothing
made me. I stepped inside, both feet over the threshold, and the door
slammed shut behind me hard enough to rattle every note on the walls.

The whistling stopped.

And out of the bathroom, drying his hands on a paper towel like it was
the most normal thing in the world, walked Ian.

“Ah. You finally made it.”

I’d love to tell you I said something smart. Something a twelve year vet
would say. What actually came out of my mouth was closer to a grunt and
stutter. Ian tossed the paper towel in the trash, walked around the
desk, and sat down in the Sergeant’s chair like it was fitted for him.

He looked exactly the same. I need to stress that. Same haircut from
three weeks ago. Same crease in his collar. There was even a pen
sticking out of his chest pocket, the same one he took notes with in my
car.

“You’re dead,” I said. “I heard you. In that basement, I heard you.”

“You did,” he said. “Sit down, Chris.”

“No, no. You don’t get to Sit Down Chris me. You were screaming. I stood
in that kitchen and I listened to you screaming and I walked out the
door.”

“I know,” he said. “That’s why the door’s open.”

I didn’t sit down. I stood there with my back against a wall of notes
and made him talk, and to his credit, he talked. I’m going to give it to
you the way he gave it to me, because I’ve been carrying it alone for
long enough.

Ian was never my rookie.

There was no academy class. The kid I picked up in that parking lot, the
one who called me sir and wrote everything down in his little notepad,
has been working in this town since before I got here. A long time
before. He wouldn’t give me a number. When I pushed, he just said,
“You’d handle the number worse than you’re handling this.”

The people who die in this town don’t leave it. Neither do the ones who
get taken. The laughing cops in the locker room. The EMS guy who’s also
the tow guy. The desk that’s never empty, not really. Everyone who works
here worked here before, in one capacity or another, and then they
didn’t stop. Steve is the only one on the roster with a pulse, and Steve
stopped asking questions thirty one years ago, which is the only reason
he still has one.

And me. I was never transferred here because I was good in chaotic
situations. That’s the line they use. I was selected, and then I was
watched, and then I was tested.

Ian was the test.

Everything I did with him counted. The town watched me tell him to keep
his head down. Watched me show him the fastest way out, just in case.
Watched me stand between him and that old woman. And then it watched the
only part that mattered. It watched me put my hand on that basement
doorknob, with him screaming on the other side of it, and take my hand
off, because dispatch told me to.

“You listened,” Ian said. “Everyone tells you to listen. Steve told you.
Dispatch told you. But anyone can listen when it’s cheap. The town
needed to know what you’d do when it cost you.”

“The screaming,” I said. My voice didn’t sound great. “Was that real.
Did that happen to you.”

Ian looked at me for a second, and for that one second he looked like a
rookie again.

“It has to be real,” he said. “It doesn’t count if it isn’t real.”

I asked the question I’d been chewing on since the door slammed. The one
I’d asked Steve, the one I’d asked my old Lieutenant, the one nobody in
a year of asking has ever answered.

“What is this town?”

And Ian actually answered me.

Not all of it. I could tell there was a floor he wouldn’t go below. But
he answered, and here’s the shape of it. This town is where things are
kept. The woman who wants to play hide and seek. The thing in apartment
3C. Whatever rings that bell. They don’t come from here. They’re brought
here, or they end up here, the way water ends up at a drain. And a town
full of things like that needs staff. It needs someone to answer the
calls, follow the response times, do the rounds, keep everything fed and
bored and contained. It doesn’t need heroes. Heroes open doors. It needs
people who listen.

The dead do most of the work because the dead have no choice. But the
town always keeps one living officer on the roster. Ian wouldn’t say
why. There has to be one. Right now it’s Steve, and Steve is thirty one
years in, and Steve is tired.

“So that’s what this is,” I said. “The open door. I passed, and now I’m
next in line for Steve’s job. I’m the next lifer.”

“No,” Ian said. “You passed, so now you get a choice. Nobody gets a
choice here, Chris. Do you understand what I’m telling you? Steve never
got one. I never got one.”

He opened the top drawer of the desk, took out a folder, and slid it
across to me.

Transfer papers.

Real ones. My name, my shield number, a precinct number I actually
recognize, two towns over from where I used to live. A commanding
officer’s signature that I’ve seen before on real paperwork in my real
career.

“Served with distinction. Released.”

“You walk out of here with that folder,” Ian said, “and it all closes
behind you. You’ll get a normal desk, a normal radio, normal calls.
You’ll remember this place less every year, until it’s a bad taste you
can’t put a name to. The Lieutenant will take your calls again. He won’t
remember why he ever stopped.”

I want to be honest with you, because I promised myself when I started
writing these that I’d be honest even when it makes me look bad.

I wanted it. God, I wanted it. I stood there in a dead man’s office
holding my way out and I thought about my old precinct, roll calls, a
desk officer who exists, coffee that a living person made. I thought
about it so hard I could smell it. My hand actually closed around the
folder. Ian just watched me. He didn’t try to stop me. That’s the part I
keep coming back to. Nobody in this town has ever needed to stop me from
anything. They just wait.

And while my hand was on that folder, while I was already half out the
door in my head, the radio on Ian’s desk crackled.

It wasn’t dispatch. Dispatch has a tone, a cadence, calm as a lake. This
wasn’t that.

“I’m sorry.”

My whole body went cold, and it took me a second to understand why. Then
I understood why.

It was my voice.

“I’m sorry. I’m so sorry. I tried to save them. I tried.”

My voice. Exactly my voice, the way it sounds on body cam, the way it
sounds on a voicemail. Coming out of the Sergeant’s radio, saying the
words I heard through a door in an abandoned building on the third
floor, the words the thing behind that door said to me.

Ian didn’t look surprised. That was the worst part. He looked at the
radio the way you look at a clock.

“The ceremony,” he said. “Every six months. You’ve seen it five times.”

“The priest does the ceremony.”

“The priest is gone.”

I asked gone where. He didn’t answer. I asked when the ceremony was due.

“Tonight.”

The radio crackled again and my own voice came through one more time,
and I swear to you it sounded closer.

“I know where the keys are, Christopher.”

Then silence.

Ian stood up, straightened the stacks on the desk, and said the only
thing anyone in this town has ever said to me that sounded like it cost
him something.

“You’ve got the folder in your hand and a full tank of gas, and the town
line is nine minutes away, and if you drive there right now, nothing
will follow you. That’s the truth and I’m allowed to tell it to you. Or
the keys are in the visor, and the car is parked out back.” He looked at
me. “Nobody is going to dispatch you, Chris. Not tonight. Tonight you
dispatch yourself.”

I walked out to the parking lot behind the precinct and I sat in that
car for a long time.

The folder was on the passenger seat. The keys were in the visor, right
where the note would have said they’d be, if anyone had needed to leave
me a note. The engine was running. From that lot, the road out of town
was a left turn. The abandoned building was a right.

Three weeks earlier I had stood in a kitchen and listened to a kid
scream, because following instructions is how you survive this place.
Everyone who told me that was right. I was alive because I listened.

There were no instructions that night.

I put the car in drive.

And I turned right.
