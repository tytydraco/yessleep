The first time I heard somebody coughing inside my bedroom wall, I
assumed it was my neighbour. I live in a terraced house, so hearing
people through the walls isn’t particularly unusual. I’ve heard
arguments, televisions, a dog that barks whenever its owners go out.
Once, I could hear somebody practising the trumpet for nearly an entire
Sunday afternoon.

But this cough sounded close. It was a horrible, phlegmy cough, followed
by the sound of somebody trying to clear their throat. I actually said,
“You all right, mate?” and laughed to myself afterwards.

A few seconds later, somebody said, “Yeah.”

I sat up in bed. The voice hadn’t come from next door. It sounded as
though somebody had spoken from inside my wardrobe, which stood against
the adjoining wall.

I got out of bed and checked it. There was nothing inside except my
clothes and a suitcase. I even moved the suitcase because I was
beginning to feel ridiculous. I stood there listening for a while, then
went back to bed.

That was about six weeks ago. I’m writing this from my sister’s house. I
haven’t slept at my own place since Wednesday, and I don’t intend to go
back.

I bought the house three years ago. It’s an ordinary two-bedroom
terrace, probably built around the 1920s, in a fairly unremarkable
residential street. The previous owner had lived there for decades. He’d
done a lot of the decorating himself, including some fairly questionable
alterations to the upstairs rooms.

My bedroom had built-in wardrobes along one wall when I moved in. They
were hideous things, with sliding mirrored doors that kept jumping off
their tracks. I ripped them out during the first month and replaced them
with a freestanding wardrobe. Behind the old wardrobes was a plain
plasterboard wall. I painted it, moved my furniture in and never gave it
another thought.

At least not until the coughing started.

Over the following week, I heard somebody on three separate occasions.
It wasn’t always coughing. Sometimes it sounded like somebody shifting
their weight in a chair, or scuffing their shoe against the floor. Once,
at about eleven in the evening, I heard a very distinct sigh.

I went next door the following morning. My neighbour is a woman called
Denise, who lives alone and has been there much longer than me. I asked
her whether she’d recently moved her bedroom around.

“No. Why?”

I explained that I could hear somebody through the wall.

“Well, it’s not me,” she said. “My bedroom’s at the back.”

This confused me because my bedroom was at the front. I asked what room
was on the other side of my wall.

“The landing, I suppose.”

“Do you have a wardrobe against it or something?”

She frowned. “No, love. It’s just the stairs.”

That made me feel better, initially. If her staircase ran along the
wall, then I was probably hearing her moving about. Old houses carried
noise in strange ways. I mentioned the coughing.

“Wouldn’t have been me,” she said. “I’ve been staying at my daughter’s
this week.”

She’d been away since the previous Friday. I didn’t tell her about the
voice.

I started recording the noises on my phone. Not because I thought the
house was haunted. I wanted something I could play to a builder, or
possibly the council if there was a problem with the adjoining property.

I got almost nothing for the first few nights. The recordings were
mostly traffic, the radiator clicking as it cooled and me occasionally
turning over in bed. Then, last Monday, something happened that made me
stop sleeping in the bedroom.

It was around half past one. I’d been reading on my phone and was just
about to turn the light off when I heard a scraping sound. I held my
phone against the wall and started recording.

The scraping continued for maybe fifteen seconds. Then came a small
knock. I knocked back. There was a pause. Something knocked twice. I
knocked twice.

This time there was no reply. Instead I heard the unmistakable noise of
somebody dragging something heavy across a wooden floor. I shouted
through the wall for whoever it was to stop. The dragging ceased
immediately.

I waited, holding my phone against the plaster. Then somebody spoke.

“Do it again.”

I actually felt my stomach tighten. Not because of what was said, but
because of how clearly I heard it. There was no muffling whatsoever. I
could hear the man’s saliva moving in his mouth.

“Who’s there?” I shouted.

I waited for an answer, but none came. I slept downstairs with the
television on.

The following morning I listened to the recording. You could hear the
scraping and both sets of knocks. You could also hear my voice shouting.
But the recording didn’t pick up the man speaking.

I listened to it with headphones, then through my laptop speakers. There
was a faint rustling noise at the point where he’d said something, but
nothing resembling words.

That afternoon I contacted a local builder named Graham. He’d done some
work for me previously and I trusted him not to invent an expensive
problem. I explained that I thought there might be a gap between my wall
and the adjoining property, possibly left over from some old
alterations.

Graham came over on Tuesday evening. He tapped along the bedroom wall
with his knuckles. Most of it sounded solid, but a section behind my
wardrobe was noticeably hollow.

“Probably dot and dab,” he said.

I asked what that meant.

“Plasterboard stuck onto the brickwork. That’s all. Won’t necessarily
mean there’s anything wrong.”

He moved the wardrobe away from the wall and took some measurements.
Then he asked whether I’d had the room altered. I told him I hadn’t. He
measured again, this time checking from the bedroom door to the far
wall.

“Funny,” he said.

“What is?”

He asked whether he could look downstairs. We went into the living room,
where he measured across the front of the house. Then we went back
upstairs and he repeated the measurement.

“Your bedroom’s narrower than your living room.”

“How much?”

“About twenty-eight inches.”

He said some difference was normal, given the construction, but not that
much. There could be a boxed-in service space, or possibly a section of
an old chimney breast behind the plasterboard. I mentioned the previous
owner’s wardrobes.

“Could be something they covered up,” he said.

He offered to cut a small inspection hole. I agreed. Graham went out to
his van and returned with a drill, a small torch and one of those
inspection cameras plumbers use. He drilled a hole a few inches above
the skirting board. A little cloud of plaster dust fell onto my carpet.

Then he fed the camera through. I watched the screen over his shoulder.
There was brickwork on one side and the back of the plasterboard on the
other. The space between them seemed much larger than I’d expected.

He moved the camera around. “Well, there’s your gap.”

“How big?”

“Looks about two foot, maybe more.”

He moved the camera further in. Something pale flashed across the
screen. He stopped.

“What was that?” I asked.

“Dunno.”

He pulled the camera back slightly and turned it towards the floor.
There was carpet inside the gap. It was an old brown patterned carpet,
flattened and dirty, but unmistakably carpet.

Graham stared at the screen. “That’s bloody strange.”

He adjusted the camera and moved it along the floor. A few inches beyond
the carpet was something that looked like a shoe. He pulled the camera
out.

I asked why he’d stopped.

“Think somebody’s been in there.”

“What do you mean?”

“There’s stuff on the floor. Might’ve been an old cupboard, something
like that.”

He didn’t sound convinced. I asked him to put the camera back in, but he
suggested making a larger opening the following morning. He wanted to
check how the wall had been constructed before removing anything.

As he packed away his tools, he asked me something. “How long have you
been hearing these noises?”

“Couple of weeks. Maybe longer.”

He looked towards the wardrobe. “Does it sound like they’re moving
about?”

“Sometimes.”

He nodded, then told me to keep the bedroom door shut until he came
back. I asked whether he thought somebody was living inside the wall.

“I wouldn’t have thought so,” he said.

But he looked at the hole again before leaving.

That night I stayed downstairs. I’d put a piece of masking tape over the
inspection hole before moving the wardrobe back, although I couldn’t
really say why. I suppose I didn’t like the idea of something being able
to look into my room.

At around midnight, I heard footsteps overhead. They were slow,
irregular footsteps. At first I thought somebody was walking along
Denise’s landing, but then I remembered what Graham had found.

I muted the television. The footsteps continued. There was a creak, then
another, followed by a short scraping sound. I stood at the bottom of
the stairs. The noise stopped.

I called out, “Hello?”

There was no answer. I went upstairs, keeping the landing light on. My
bedroom door was closed. I opened it and reached for the switch. Nothing
appeared out of place.

Then I noticed the wardrobe. I’d pushed it right against the wall
earlier. I remembered because one of its legs had caught on the carpet
and I’d had to lift it slightly. Now it stood several inches away from
the skirting board.

I looked at the gap behind it. The masking tape was gone. Not peeled
away and left hanging. Gone completely.

There was something dark around the inspection hole. I went closer. The
plasterboard was wet. A little patch of moisture had spread around the
hole, maybe the size of a fifty-pence piece.

I leaned forward to look at it. Somebody breathed out on the other side.
Warm air touched my face.

I fell backwards against the bed. For a second I couldn’t get my feet
underneath me. I scrambled across the mattress, knocking my bedside lamp
onto the floor.

As I reached the door, a voice spoke from behind the wardrobe.

“Sorry.”

It was quiet. Almost polite. Then came the cough.

I got out of the house without my shoes. I sat in my car for about ten
minutes before I was calm enough to drive. Eventually I went to my
sister’s, where I told her somebody had been getting into my house. She
wanted me to call the police. I did.

Two officers came to the property the following morning. Graham arrived
shortly afterwards. The officers searched both floors and the loft. They
found nothing, although one of them agreed that the space behind the
plasterboard needed investigating.

Graham was reluctant to remove the wall with the police standing there.
He explained that he needed to locate the fixings and make sure there
were no electrical cables running behind it. One of the officers asked
whether there was any other access.

“Possibly through next door,” Graham said.

I went to get Denise. She let us examine her landing and under-stairs
cupboard. There was no door or obvious opening in the adjoining wall.
Graham measured the space from her side.

He stood on the landing with his tape measure extended, then asked to
come back into my house. We returned to the bedroom. After measuring
again, he looked at the officer.

“There’s something I don’t understand.”

“What?”

“This gap doesn’t belong to either house.”

He explained that there appeared to be a narrow passage running between
the two properties, separate from both internal walls. The officer asked
whether somebody could get inside.

“Well, somebody put the carpet down.”

Eventually they agreed to let Graham open the plasterboard while the
officers remained downstairs. I stayed with him. He cut a rectangular
section from the wall, roughly two feet wide and four feet high. He
worked carefully, avoiding the electrical socket nearby.

When he pulled the plasterboard away, we both saw it. There was a narrow
passage behind the wall. The brown carpet ran along the floor. It was
filthy and worn almost smooth down the middle.

The space extended in both directions, disappearing beyond the reach of
Graham’s torch. It was just wide enough for somebody to walk sideways.
And somebody had been using it.

There were marks on the brickwork at shoulder height, as though clothing
had rubbed repeatedly against the walls. Near our feet lay a crushed
plastic water bottle and an empty crisp packet. Further along the
passage was a small folding chair.

Facing my bedroom wall.

For several seconds neither of us spoke. Graham crouched beside the
opening and shone his torch towards the chair. That’s when I saw the
holes.

There were several drilled through the plasterboard, all at different
heights. From the other side they would have been almost invisible. Tiny
dark marks in the painted wall. One lined up with my bed. Another looked
directly towards the bedroom door.

The third was at the height of somebody sitting on the folding chair. I
realised where it pointed. My pillow.

I went downstairs and told the police. One officer immediately came up
and looked through the opening. His colleague went outside to check the
neighbouring gardens.

They asked whether anything had been stolen or whether I’d seen anyone
entering the property. I told them about the voice. The officer looked
uncomfortable.

“Could be somebody’s been staying in there,” he said. “We’ll need to
establish how they’re getting access.”

I asked whether he was going inside. He said they weren’t going to send
anybody crawling into an unknown confined space without knowing where it
led. He contacted someone on his radio, and Graham was instructed not to
disturb anything else.

We waited downstairs while the officers made arrangements for a more
thorough search. I remember feeling relieved. Somebody was actually
taking it seriously. There was an explanation for the noises, even if it
was a frightening one. A person had somehow found a way into the space
between the houses and had been secretly watching me.

I was already thinking about changing the locks and getting the whole
wall rebuilt. Then Graham came downstairs.

“Can I show you something?”

He took me into the hallway, away from the officers. He had a photograph
on his phone. It showed the folding chair and the carpet running along
the passage.

“Look behind the chair,” he said.

I enlarged the picture. At first I couldn’t work out what he meant.
There was a dark patch of brickwork near the floor. Beside it was a line
of pale marks. I turned the phone sideways.

They were fingernail scratches. Hundreds of them. They ran along the
bottom of the wall in two parallel lines.

“The chair’s facing the wrong way,” Graham said.

I looked at him. “What?”

“Whoever’s been sitting there wouldn’t have been looking through the
holes.”

I studied the photograph again. He was right. The folding chair faced
away from my bedroom wall. Towards the bricks separating the passage
from Denise’s house.

I asked him what that meant. He shook his head.

“I don’t know. But look at the carpet.”

There was a long worn strip through the centre of it. I’d assumed it was
where somebody had been walking. Graham pointed to the marks on either
side.

“There’s nothing to suggest anybody’s been walking down there.”

“Then what’s worn the carpet?”

He didn’t answer. Instead he went back upstairs. I followed him. He
shone his torch through the opening again. I could see the chair, the
brickwork and the narrow passage stretching into darkness.

Graham held the torch steady. “Look at the scratches.”

They were close to the floor. Not where somebody’s hands would naturally
rest if they were sitting in the chair. They were at the same height as
the carpet.

I crouched and leaned towards the opening. That’s when I heard it. A
cough. It came from somewhere deep inside the passage.

Graham recoiled so quickly he knocked his shoulder against the wardrobe.

“Did you hear that?” I asked.

He nodded. We both stood very still. Another cough came from the
darkness. Then something began moving towards us.

I can’t describe the sound properly. It was a heavy, dragging noise,
with an intermittent scrape against the bricks. Graham backed towards
the bedroom door. I stayed beside the opening, staring into the passage.

The beam of my torch picked out a movement at the far end. Something
pale was pressed against the wall. At first I thought it was an arm.
Then it moved, and I saw a hand.

A human hand, resting flat against the brickwork with its fingers
pointing towards the ceiling. Another hand appeared beside it. They
moved alternately, gripping the uneven mortar. Something was pulling
itself along.

It came closer. I could see a head pressed sideways against the bricks.
The face was turned towards the wall, so I could only make out an ear
and part of a jaw. Its body was squeezed into the narrow space.

The shoulders looked completely wrong. One was higher than the other,
pressed almost against the back of its head. Its legs trailed behind it.
The soles of its shoes scraped against the carpet.

I backed away. The thing stopped. For a moment it remained absolutely
motionless. Then its head turned.

I heard a horrible series of small clicking sounds as its face rotated
towards the opening. It was a man’s face. Or something close enough to
one. His cheek was flattened against the brickwork. One eye was almost
closed, while the other protruded beneath an eyebrow rubbed raw.

He looked at me. And smiled.

“Do it again,” he said.

I ran. Graham was already on the landing, shouting for the police. I
followed him, but before I reached the stairs I heard plasterboard
snapping behind me. Something struck the bedroom floor. Then there was a
rapid scraping noise.

The officer who’d been standing downstairs came up towards us. Graham
shouted that somebody was inside the wall. The officer ordered us
downstairs while he called for his colleague.

I tried to explain what I’d seen. I remember shouting that it was coming
out of the bedroom. Then my bedroom door moved. It had been left
half-open. Something pushed it from the other side. The door swung shut.

The officer drew his baton. “Police! Come out of the room!”

There was no reply. Then somebody coughed. The officer approached the
door. He pushed it open.

The bedroom was empty. The plasterboard opening had been ripped wider.
Broken pieces lay across the carpet. Graham’s torch was still shining
into the passage.

And the folding chair was gone.

The police searched for hours. They brought additional officers and
eventually arranged for someone to inspect the adjoining spaces. They
found that the passage extended along most of the party wall, but it
wasn’t continuous. Sections had been blocked off by old brickwork and
timber supports.

They also discovered a concealed opening beneath the floorboards in my
loft. According to the police, someone could conceivably have entered
the passage from the roof space and moved around without being seen.
They didn’t find anyone.

The chair was never recovered. Neither was the water bottle or the crisp
packet.

I was told that somebody had probably been squatting in the house and
had escaped when the officers arrived. I asked how the person could have
got away without anybody seeing them. They said they couldn’t rule out
another access point.

I asked whether they’d found the fingernail marks. One officer told me
the bricks had been damaged, but there was no way of establishing how. I
didn’t argue. I just wanted to leave.

My sister drove me back that afternoon to collect some clothes. Graham
met us there because he’d promised to board up the opening before we
left. He looked exhausted.

I asked whether he’d told the police what he’d seen.

“Yes.”

“What did they say?”

“Didn’t really say anything.”

He helped me carry my things downstairs. Before leaving, I went into the
bedroom one final time. The wall was covered with a temporary sheet of
plywood, screwed into the timber behind it. There were little piles of
plaster dust along the skirting board. My bed had been pushed away from
the wall.

I collected my phone charger from behind the bedside table. That’s when
I noticed something on the carpet. A fingernail. It was long and
yellowish, with a little dark dirt caught beneath the tip.

I stared at it for a few seconds. Then I noticed another beneath the
bed. I didn’t touch either of them. I went downstairs and asked Graham
to come back up.

He looked at the nails without speaking.

“Did you see his hands?” I asked.

He nodded.

“What did they look like?”

He swallowed. “Didn’t see them properly.”

I told him they were long. The fingers had looked thin and strangely
jointed, and the nails had been scraped almost down to the skin. Graham
stared at the plywood covering the opening. Then he said something I
haven’t been able to stop thinking about.

“I don’t think it was watching you.”

I asked what he meant. He pointed towards the bed.

“If somebody’s been sitting in that chair facing the other wall, and
those holes are behind the chair, then they weren’t watching you while
they were sitting there.”

He paused. “They were listening.”

That was the last time I went upstairs.

I’ve been at my sister’s since Wednesday. The police have advised me to
improve the security of the property. Graham has agreed to come back
next week with another builder to seal the passage permanently, although
I’m considering selling the house instead.

I’m not sure anyone will believe what I’ve written. I probably wouldn’t
have believed it myself before this happened. There is one more thing,
though.

Last night my sister was getting ready for bed when she asked me to help
move a chest of drawers in her spare room. She wanted to put a lamp
beside the bed I’d been using. We pulled the drawers away from the wall.

There was some loose wallpaper behind them, and I noticed a small round
hole in the plaster. It was probably left by an old fixing. I wouldn’t
have paid it any attention a few weeks ago.

My sister went downstairs to get an extension lead. I crouched beside
the hole and looked through it. I couldn’t see anything except darkness.

Then somebody on the other side of the wall cleared their throat. I
stood up and backed away. There was a cough. A familiar, horrible cough.

I went straight downstairs and told my sister we needed to leave. She
thought I was joking at first. Then she saw my face. We got into her car
and drove to a hotel.

She’s asleep in the other bed now. I’ve been sitting up all night trying
to work out what to do. About an hour ago, I heard her cough in her
sleep. It was an ordinary little cough. She turned onto her side and
settled down again.

A few seconds later, somebody coughed from inside the wall behind her
bed. It was an exact imitation.

Then I heard the sound of a folding chair being opened.
