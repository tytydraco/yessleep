I manage facilities for a small accounting firm. Three floors, about
forty employees, one of those converted industrial buildings where
nothing is quite where you’d expect it to be.

Part of my job is organizing fire drills. It’s boring. I send an email,
people complain about standing in the parking lot, and we all go back
inside fifteen minutes later.

Last month we upgraded the building’s access control system. The new one
tracks how many people enter and leave through the main doors using
overhead sensors. It’s supposed to help us account for everyone during
an evacuation.

The first drill went fine, except the system reported 42 people in the
building when we only had 41.

I counted everyone manually. Nobody was missing.

The system, however, insisted one person was still inside.

The installer blamed the sensors. Apparently people walking too close
together can confuse them. He recalibrated everything, ran a few tests,
and told me it was fixed.

The next drill, the same thing happened.

41 people outside. 42 registered as having entered. One person still
inside.

This time I checked the access logs.

The additional entry had been recorded at 3:17 a.m., when the building
was closed.

I figured it was the cleaning crew, except they finish around midnight,
and the last cleaner had signed out at 12:08.

I pulled up the security footage.

At 3:17, the lobby was completely empty. The automatic doors were
closed. Nobody walked past the sensors.

But the little green occupancy number on the security monitor changed
from 0 to 1.

I watched it four times.

No movement. No shadows. Nothing.

Our security contractor came out the following afternoon. He was a
fairly quiet guy named Dennis who’d been servicing the building for
years.

He looked at the footage, checked the sensor housing, and asked whether
we’d been getting any false fire alarms.

We hadn’t.

He seemed relieved by that answer, which struck me as a strange
reaction.

Then he asked if I could pull up the entry records from the previous
Friday.

I did.

3:17 a.m. One person entered.

Thursday was 3:17 too.

Wednesday, 3:17.

Every weekday since the installation, the system had registered an entry
at exactly 3:17 in the morning.

There was never a corresponding exit.

But the count always reset to zero by the time staff arrived.

Dennis asked if he could borrow my phone to photograph the logs. I
offered to email them instead, but he insisted on taking pictures.

Then he asked me something that seemed completely unrelated.

“Do you ever use the west stairwell?”

I told him hardly anyone did. It was the emergency staircase behind the
old records room, mostly used during drills.

He nodded.

“Keep it that way for now.”

I asked what he meant.

He said there might be a wiring issue and that he’d arrange another
visit.

He didn’t.

Two days later, I found out he’d requested to be taken off our account.

I called the contractor. They said Dennis was on leave and someone else
would be assigned.

I was annoyed more than frightened at this point. We’d spent a
ridiculous amount of money on a system that couldn’t count forty people.

Then came Friday.

One of the cleaners, Marta, called me at 7:20 in the morning.

She wanted to know whether anyone had been authorized to stay overnight.

I said no.

She told me she’d found a chair in the west stairwell.

Not an office chair. One of the blue plastic visitor chairs from
reception.

Someone had carried it down to the landing between the first floor and
the basement.

It was positioned facing the fire door.

Marta said there was a paper coffee cup on the floor beside it. The cup
was still warm.

I went downstairs to look.

The chair was exactly where she’d described. Someone had dragged it into
the corner so it wouldn’t block the stairs.

The cup was from the coffee machine on the second floor.

The machine shuts down automatically at 6 p.m.

There was something else.

A small rectangle of clear tape had been stuck to the wall above the
chair. Under it was a handwritten note.

“Please wait here until someone comes to get you.”

I assumed somebody was playing a prank.

I photographed everything, took the chair back upstairs, and put in a
request for a camera in the stairwell.

The request was approved on Monday.

By Wednesday, we had footage.

At 3:17 a.m. the camera recorded something.

I need to explain the layout.

The west stairwell has a fire door on each floor. The camera is mounted
above the first-floor landing, looking down toward the basement.

At 3:16, the staircase was empty.

At 3:17, the fire door on the first floor moved.

It didn’t open.

It moved inward slightly, maybe half an inch, as though someone on the
other side had leaned against it.

Then it moved again.

A little harder.

The door has a panic bar on the stairwell side, meaning anyone inside
the staircase can push it open into the first-floor corridor.

Whoever was pushing was standing in the corridor, on the wrong side.

The door moved a third time.

Then a voice said, very quietly, “Is somebody there?”

The camera has a microphone. I didn’t know that until I heard it.

It sounded like an elderly woman.

There was a pause.

“Could you open this for me?”

Nothing happened for nearly a minute.

Then came the sound of someone sitting down.

A small plastic creak.

I looked at the empty landing.

There was no chair.

The voice spoke again.

“I can wait.”

I went to my boss, showed him the recording, and asked him to contact
the police.

He watched the whole thing.

Then he asked whether I’d checked the other camera.

I didn’t know what he meant.

He pointed out that the first-floor corridor had its own security camera
facing the same door.

I opened that recording.

At 3:17 a.m., the corridor was empty.

The fire door stood perfectly still.

But every time the woman spoke on the stairwell recording, the corridor
camera picked up a faint knocking.

Three gentle knocks.

Coming from inside the stairwell.

The same door. The same time. Two cameras recording somebody on opposite
sides of it.

I stopped the video.

My boss didn’t say anything for a while.

Then he asked me to lock the west staircase until the security
contractor could investigate.

We couldn’t legally block an emergency exit, so instead we put up signs
asking staff to use the east stairs for non-emergency access and
arranged for a technician to inspect the door.

The technician found nothing.

That should have been the end of it.

Yesterday afternoon we had a real fire alarm.

Not a drill.

Someone had burned food in the second-floor break room, setting off a
smoke detector.

Everyone evacuated normally.

I was outside with the attendance list when the occupancy system
reported 42 people had entered and only 41 had left.

One person remained inside.

I counted again.

Everyone was there.

Then I noticed Marta standing beside me, staring at the building.

She was looking at the west stairwell exit.

The exterior door had a narrow vertical window. Through it, I could see
the landing.

Someone had placed the blue plastic chair directly behind the glass.

I hadn’t seen anyone carry it there.

There was a woman sitting in it.

She was wearing a beige cardigan and dark trousers. Her hair was gray
and tied back.

She sat with her hands folded in her lap, looking straight ahead.

I pointed her out to Marta.

Marta grabbed my arm so hard it hurt.

“Don’t open it.”

The woman behind the glass turned her head toward us.

I couldn’t make out her face very well through the wired glass, but I
could see that her mouth was moving.

She was saying something.

Marta started walking backward.

The woman raised one hand.

Not waving.

She held up a single finger.

Then she pointed to the people standing outside.

I turned around.

Our employees were gathered near the parking lot, chatting, checking
their phones, waiting for the all-clear.

I started counting.

Forty-one.

I counted again.

Forty-one.

When I looked back at the door, the woman was standing.

Her chair was empty.

She had moved right up against the window.

And I could hear her now, even through the closed fire door.

“Forty-one,” she said.

She sounded disappointed.

Then, in a much louder voice, she called out, “You’re missing one!”

Every employee outside turned toward the building.

And the occupancy counter changed.

42 inside.

41 outside.

Then 43 inside.

I looked at my phone. The system was registering another person entering
the building.

But nobody had gone inside.

The woman was still at the window.

She was smiling.

One of the firefighters arrived a few seconds later and asked me whether
anyone was trapped.

Before I could answer, Marta said, “No. Everyone’s here.”

I agreed.

I don’t know why, but I was terrified of telling him about the woman.

He went inside with his crew anyway.

They cleared the building and found nobody.

When the alarm was reset, the occupancy counter dropped to zero.

The chair was gone.

I resigned this morning.

My boss thinks I had some sort of stress reaction. He’s offered me a few
days off to reconsider, but I’m not going back.

I called Dennis, the original security contractor. His number was still
saved in my phone.

He answered on the third ring.

I told him what happened.

He didn’t interrupt once.

When I finished, he asked a single question.

“Did she count you?”

I said I didn’t understand.

“Outside. When she was counting. Did she look at you?”

I thought about it.

The woman had pointed toward the employees. Not toward me.

Dennis was silent for so long I thought the call had dropped.

Then he told me something.

Apparently, the building used to be a residential care facility. It
closed in the late eighties after a small electrical fire caused an
evacuation.

Everyone got out.

Except one elderly resident who remained in the west stairwell, waiting
for a staff member to come back and help her down.

She was found the next morning.

Sitting on a chair.

Dennis said the strange activity had happened before, years ago, when a
different company installed occupancy sensors.

He said they eventually removed them because the system kept registering
people who weren’t there.

I asked what any of this had to do with whether the woman counted me.

He said he didn’t know for certain.

But the last technician who reported seeing her had described almost
exactly what I had.

She’d counted the people outside.

Then she’d asked someone to come back for the person who was missing.

That technician had opened the door.

Dennis wouldn’t tell me what happened afterward.

He just kept asking whether she’d looked directly at me.

I told him I didn’t think so.

He said, “Good.”

Then he hung up.

About an hour ago, I received an automated notification from the
building’s access control system.

I forgot to remove the app from my phone.

It was an occupancy alert.

ONE PERSON REMAINING INSIDE.

Then the number changed to two.

I called my boss.

He said the building was empty. The last employee had left at six.

While we were talking, another notification came through.

THREE PEOPLE REMAINING INSIDE.

My boss said he’d call security in the morning.

I told him to do it tonight.

Then I received a photograph.

It came from Marta.

She’d gone back to retrieve her bag and found the blue chair outside the
west stairwell.

Someone had put it beside the building’s main entrance.

There was a sheet of paper taped to the back.

It had three names written on it.

The first was Dennis.

The second was Marta.

The third was mine.

There was a check mark beside the first two.

Mine was still blank.
