I want to put something on the record, I’m no supporter of the
President. I’ve been against him and his policies since day one of his
administration. He was always a fascist in sheep’s clothing and I’m
tired of being gaslit by the people around me to think otherwise. When
he signed the executive order, I thought that be it, the moment everyone
would realize the kind of monster we made into the most powerful man on
earth, but instead his sheepshit supporters cheered it on like they do
every awful thing that he does.

In case anyone reading this has somehow managed to avoid it and it seems
a lot of you here don’t follow politics, in March, the President signed
an executive order. The official title is something like the
Correctional Burden and Public Accountability Order, but everyone just
calls it the Battle Royale Order. It lets the federal government
“reassign” certain inmates, the worst ones, the ones serving life with
no chance of parole, to what the order called a “public accountability
program.”

Nobody knew what that meant until he went on TV and explained it. He was
smiling. There was a little laugh in his voice, like he was announcing a
new national holiday. 

There were lawsuits, obviously. Two dozen states and about every civil
liberties group you’ve heard of sued. A federal judge in Oregon blocked
it for nine days. Then an appeals court unblocked it, and the Supreme
Court declined to hear the emergency application. Six to three, no
written opinion. That was two weeks ago.

So tonight it’s happening. Every network is carrying it because the
media has always been complicit  and I’m sure the FCC threatened them
too. 

Nobody knows who’s in it. The roster has been sealed “to preserve the
integrity of the competition.” That is a real sentence that a real press
secretary said out loud at a podium. All we’ve been told is that it’s
fifty federal inmates, one from each state, all serving life without
parole, all convicted of “violent crimes against the American public.”

I keep thinking about Ancient Rome and the gladiator fights they had. We
were supposed to be way past that sort of savagery and yet here we are.
Whatever those men did, however bad, the government dropping them in a
pit to kill each other and asking the rest of us to watch is something
even worse.

My daughter Nora would have hated this. She was the kind of kid who
carried spiders outside in a cup instead of squashing them. She’s been
gone nine years this December.

There was no music or logo once the broadcast had started. The screen
just cut suddenly to a room.

There were concrete walls, hard white lights, and a large circle of men
in orange jumpsuits chained to posts by bulky metal collars. Each was
slumped over asleep and in the center of the room were lots of
mannequins in full tactical gear. They had helmets, bulletproof vests,
rifles clamped in their hands, and grenades hanging off them like fruit.

The first thing I notice is how young the men were. Most of them look
like they were barely in their twenties. Most weren’t even very
intimidating looking for so-called hardened criminals. They were skinny
with patchy looking beards, sunken, tired eyes and pretty much all of
them were white. I don’t know what I expected, but these little boys
were not it.

One of them stirred and groaned as his collar lit up green and chirped a
bright happy little sound, like a phone connecting to a bluetooth
speaker.

“Player 1 online.”

He lifted his head and looked around in confusion, “What the f—k?”

The network actually bleeped the F word. That pissed me off more than it
should.  Televised   bloodsports were fine, but profanity was somehow a
bridge too far. 

They all began waking up, one after another, with each collar counting
them off. 

“Player 9 online. Player 10 online. Player 11 online. Player 12 online.”
It takes almost a full minute to get to fifty. One guy keeps yanking at
his chain, screaming “Where am I? Where am I?” over and over, like the
answer will come if he’s loud enough.

At fifty, the counting stops, and the President’s face lights up across
the wall behind them.

The men have to twist around and crane their necks to look up at him. He
doesn’t look at them. He looks straight past them, right into the
camera.

Right into my living room.

“My fellow Americans.”

I’ve heard him give maybe a hundred speeches, but I’ve never heard him
give one quite like this.

“Today, I stand before you with a heavy heart and a solemn duty. We find
ourselves in unprecedented times, facing challenges that test not only
our resolve as a nation but also our humanity. As your President, it is
my duty to lead this great country with courage and conviction, even in
its darkest of hours…”

The men are screaming at him. Cursing and jeering at his massive visage.
One of them even yells, “Hey, I voted for you! Let me out!” I almost
wanted to laugh at the absurdity.

The President continued, “We stand on the brink of a battle unlike any
other. A battle that will test not only physical strength but also inner
resolve. In the arena before you lies a lesson…and several students in
desperate need of it.”

The inmates began to look around frantically as the President’s
expression turned from solemn to sinister.

“Let me be clear: while you may be criminals, you are still Americans.
You are still citizens of this great nation, and you still deserve to be
treated with dignity. That is why, despite the circumstances that have
brought us here today, I stand before you not as your warden, but as
your Commander-in-Chief.”

“F–k You!” More inmates cussed out the projection like it made any
difference.

The President grimaced, almost as if he could hear them, “I know that
many of you may feel fear, uncertainty, and perhaps even anger at the
situation you find yourselves in. And I understand that. But I urge you
to channel those emotions into something greater. Into courage. Into
determination. Into the unyielding spirit of the American people.” 

I wanted to vomit at the President’s words, which isn’t unusual for me,
but tonight was the first time it wasn’t just me being hyperbolic.

“For in this arena, you are not just fighting for yourselves. You are
fighting for your fellow Americans who hate you. You are fighting for
the families you have taken from and you are fighting for the country
that is tired of feeding and housing you despite knowing exactly what
you are. So, as you step into that arena, remember who you are. Remember
what you stand for. And remember that no matter what happens, you are
not alone. The counter over my head indicates just how many hostiles are
left. The doors of the bunker you are all in will not open until the
counter hits zero. You people know what to do…”

The President leaned forward and so did I.

“What you do best.”

Then the chains were released. All fifty at once in a single metallic
clack.

“We are with you,” The President said with a self-satisfied smile, “The
entire nation is with you. And together, we will sit back and watch as
we commence the first ever All-Real Battle Royale. May God bless you,
and may God bless the United States of America.” The first shot went off
before he was even finished.

I didn’t see who fired first. There was just a flash at the edge of the
frame and a spray of red across the President’s projected face, and then
everybody was running.

The camera pulled back, way back, like it was on some sort of ceiling
rig or something, and for the first time I could see the whole arena
they were put in.

It wasn’t just a room. They built an entire building inside that bunker.

There were food courts, kiosks selling sunglasses, and plenty of empty
store fronts. I’d say they were supposed to be in a mall, but past that,
there were long hallways lined with lockers, glass trophy cases, and a
hand-painted banner for a pep rally. The school arena would then give
way to a grocery store with a row of checkout lanes, a corral of
shopping carts, and a produce section full of fake fruits.

The arena was a mall, a school, and a grocery store all stitched
together into something out of a nightmare.

I understand what these men were in for prison about half a second
before my body did. 

“What you do best, huh?” I repeated to myself as the bloodbath began.

They were mass shooters. All fifty of them. Every man in that bunker
walked into a place like that with a gun and started firing at innocent
people. And the government built them arenas just like those places for
what? So the bastards would feel more at home.

I stood up. I really was going to turn it off, but then I thought about
Nora.

I’m going to be honest about the next part, because nobody will ever
know if I’m not. For a second, I stood there scanning the faces running
across my TV. Looking for him…

I had seen his picture only once, the day after he had done it. It was a
school photo of the boy. He was in front of a drab blue backdrop wearing
a collared shirt his mother probably made him put on. I looked at it for
maybe two seconds and then never again. My wife, now ex-wife, told me I
should look. She said it would take away his power. I told her he didn’t
get to have a name or a face as he wasn’t human anymore.

All I really remember is that he looked like nobody and that was the
worst part. He looked like any other kid in that school. The only clue
of what he was capable of were in his cold, dead eyes…

As much as I wanted to see him among them, there have been so many
school shootings since the odds that he’s in there were basically
nothing. But even the smallest chance he was there caused me to sit back
down and continue to watch. 

The counter over the doors said 47…Then 46… Then 33 all in the span of a
few seconds. They were dropping like flies.

What surprises me is how calm they were at first. The ones who got
rifles move like they’ve practiced for this very moment and maybe they
did. Maybe some of them spent years practicing before they did it. Maybe
the government trained them so they’d put on more of a show.

It’s when they get close to each other that the killer facade fell
apart.

Two of them crashed into each other by the lockers and both their guns
went skittering across the floor. Without them, they fought like third
graders. Hair pulling, groin kicks, biting, there was no technique. With
the guns gone, they stopped being physical threats.

It was pathetic. That’s the word that comes to mind, and I didn’t expect
it. I thought watching men die would be horrific and some of it
certainly was, but my contempt for them made me immune to their misery.

Two others come around the same corner at the same moment and freeze,
rifles pointed at each other’s faces. There was a long pause as the
camera zoomed in slowly.

“Truce?” one of them said.

The other one nodded. “Truce.”

They then both fired at the exact same instant and killed each other. No
one in that arena could be trusted and they all knew it. 

There was a kid running through the school section, he couldn’t have
been older than twenty-two, grinning ear to ear, as he shot at
everything that moved. “Get some, motherf—-s! Yeah! You little b—–s
can’t kill me! I’m unkillable!”

Then someone hit him. It was either in the shoulder or side, I couldn’t
tell. He ran and the camera followed him, because of course it did. He
made it maybe forty feet before his legs just stopped working and he
folded onto the floor of a fake classroom, under a poster that mockingly
said, ‘Kindness Is Cool.’

And his face changed. It was like he finally realized that this was
real.

“No, no, no. Oh god. Oh god, oh god. I can’t. I can’t, I can’t, I can’t.
Stop. Stop. I’ll make it stop.”

The camera held on him. The whole time, it held on him as he put the
barrel under his chin.

I looked away and when I looked back, the counter now said 25.

“You patriots are halfway there. Keep it up.” The President’s voice
chimed in like he was a fucking game show host.

The President’s projection was then riddled with bullet holes. The
maniacs all stopped shooting at each other and shot at him the second he
appeared. 

My phone kept buzzing. It was my brother trying to check up on me. I
ignored his calls and continued to watch.

There was a man on the floor of the cereal aisle, holding his stomach,
blood pooling out from under him. Another man was standing over him with
a pistol at his head.

“Please?” the one on the floor screamed. “Don’t do it!”

The one with the pistol just laughed. Not a big, hearty laugh, but a
small snicker, like somebody told him a mediocre joke.

“Please!”

I remember thinking, this is the part where I’m supposed to feel sorry
for the one on the floor, but I didn’t. All I could think about was how
he now knows what it’s like to beg someone for your life who had already
decided on taking it. Karma’s a just bitch.

The feed cut away, and when it came back, both of those men were dead,
and so were a lot of others.

The hallways were full of bodies now. Tables were flipped over in the
food court. The misters in the produce section were still running on
their timers, spraying fine, cheerful fog over the fake food and
deadmen.

The counter was in single digits now. There were only 4 left.

The screen split into four boxes, like a security monitor.

In the first box, a guy is wedged into the corner of the grocery store’s
back stockroom, knees pulled to his chest, rifle pointed at the doorway.
He was shaking like a leaf on a tree.

In the second box, a guy was lying among the bodies in the food court,
playing dead. Except he kept adjusting himself. One second his arm was
over his face. Then the next it was under his body. Then finally he
flung out to the side. It was like he couldn’t figure out what the most
comfortable way to play dead was, so he was trying out different
positions. In any other context, it would have been funny.

In the third box, a guy is walking slowly down the school hallway,
checking every doorway. He was calm and methodical. 

In the fourth box, a guy was standing in the main entrance with his
rifle hanging from its strap, staring up at the counter over the doors.
It said 4.

He stared at it for a long time and I watched him count something on his
fingers.

“Wait,” he says. “That doesn’t make sense.”

I leaned forward, because I don’t get it either, not yet, and then the
guy from the hallway shot him from forty feet away.

The counter dropped to 3.

The President then appeared. “Three players left. We’re in the
homestretch now, America.”

All three remaining collars start beeping. Loud and even, like a smoke
detector with a dying battery.

The guy playing dead in the food court was outed immediately. The
methodical one shot him from across the food court without breaking
stride.

2 now left. 

The methodical one headed towards the stockroom where the other one was
camped out.

The screen collapsed to two boxes. The hunter’s and the camper’s, and as
the hunter got closer the two’s collars chirping began to overlap. The
guy in the corner was now shaking so hard his rifle rattled against the
shelving. He’s whispering something to himself that I couldn’’t make
out.

The hunter stopped just outside the door.

“Come on, man. I know you’re there. Come out.”

“F–k you!”

“Don’t be a p—y. Come out.”

“F–k you! Make me!”

“Okay.”

The hunter pulled a grenade off his vest and tossed it through the
doorway. 

I watched a dark stain spread down the leg of the camper’s orange
jumpsuit one second before the screen flashed white.

The hunter walked back to the main entrance with his rifle over his
head, screaming and whooping, and firing into the ceiling. He was acting
like he had just won the Super Bowl. 

He got to the bunker doors and stood there, waiting.

But they wouldn’t open.

The counter above still had 1 hostile detected.

I watched him look up at it and try to figure out who he missed.

“What the hell? Who’s left? I killed them all! Who’s f–king left? I’ll
find them and I’ll kill them! Who’s left?”

He shot at the counter and cracked it, but the number still didn’t
change.

“WHO’S LEFT?”

Then he just came apart. He fired into the bodies. Into the mannequins.
He emptied a magazine into a dead man’s back, reloaded, emptied another
into the checkout lanes, the sunglasses kiosk, the trophy case, he shot
until the gun clicked empty and he threw it and pulled another one off a
corpse and kept going. 

It took a long time. The broadcast didn’t cut away. There were no
commercials. Just an hour, maybe more, of this guy losing his shit. 

Eventually he ran out of bullets. He slid down and sat on the floor
under the doors, back against the wall, a pistol resting loose in his
lap, staring up at the number. A shutter in the bunker door suddenly
slid open and a single bullet was tossed inside the arena by an unseen
hand. They slammed the shutter hard and you could hear a locking
mechanism engage. They weren’t opening it back up again. This was it.

The camera started pushing in slowly. They were waiting for this moment.

His face filled my whole TV. He looked.. familiar. He was in
mid-twenties, maybe, with a thin face, buzzed hair, and a scar running
through one of his eyebrows.

His dead eyes were how I finally recognized him. 

I’ve seen them before, for two seconds, nine years ago. Over a collared
shirt, against a blue school-photo backdrop. I never could have told you
what his face looked like. But I remembered those eyes. 

I say her name. Out loud, to the TV. “Nora.” I don’t know why.

I’m on my feet. I’m standing maybe two feet from the screen. And what I
feel isn’t horror. It isn’t grief. It’s just two words, over and over,
so loud in my head I think I might be saying them too.

Do it

He lifted the pistol and this time, I didn’t look away.

The counter hit zero, and the whole arena lit up. Colored lights sweep
across the ceiling. The bunker doors grind open onto nothing, just
darkness. A spotlight drops straight down onto him where he’s slumped
against the wall.

The President’s face fills the screen above him, bullet holes and all, a
streak of someone’s blood across his chin.

“We have a winner, America! Player 9, the perpetrator of the Clark High
School massacre, is the victor of the very first All-Real Battle
Royale!”

Canned applause began to play along with cheering. It goes on and on as
the camera holds on his body.

The feeling I had in my chest was like getting that phone call all over
again, except this time it didn’t knock me down. This time I’m the one
standing.

“Aren’t we happy for him, folks?” the President cheered. “Aren’t we
happy for them all? What a great game. What a great first game. And
we’ve got more coming.”

The camera drifts away from the spotlight and down the school hallway.
Lockers. Bodies. His voice echoes over all of it.

“America’s got plenty of criminals like these ones, so we’ll have plenty
of use for games like these. Thank you for watching the first ever
All-Real Battle Royale, and God bless America.”

I turned it off.

It’s almost two in the morning now. I’ve been sitting here in the dark
for a couple of hours.

Nora was sixteen. She was in the band room when he came in through the
east doors. She wanted to be a vet. She carried spiders outside in a
cup.

For nine years there’s been something in my chest like a fist that never
unclenched. The trial didn’t touch it. The sentence didn’t touch it.
Life without parole. Everyone told me that was justice. The prosecutor
shook my hand on the courthouse steps and it didn’t touch it at all.

It’s not gone now. But it’s open. I can breathe all the way down to the
bottom of my lungs for the first time since that December.

And it wasn’t the gun that did it. It was watching him slowly understand
that he was never getting out, that it had all been for nothing, and
watching him sit there under that number with nowhere to go. I needed to
see that. I didn’t know I needed it until I saw it.

When I finally turned my phone back on, every news app had sent the same
push alert. The second All-Real Battle Royale is scheduled for November,
just before election day. The roster is sealed.

There were forty-nine other men in there tonight. Forty-nine other
names. Somewhere out there, there are forty-nine other families who sat
in the dark like I did. I wonder how many of them turned it off. I
wonder how many of them didn’t.

None of the men in November will be the ones who took my daughter from
me, but I’m still gonna watch.
