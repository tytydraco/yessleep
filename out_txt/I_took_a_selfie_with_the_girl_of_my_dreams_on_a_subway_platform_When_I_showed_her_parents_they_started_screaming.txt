By 11:45 PM, the Central Terminal breathes out its final crowd of
commuters, leaving behind a heavy, subterranean silence that smells of
ozone, rusted brake pads, and stagnant rainwater.

For three long years, this was my entire world.

I worked a mind-numbing data entry job on the 32nd floor of a glass
tower that required me to stay until the numbers stopped bleeding
together.

My life was dull, flat, and profoundly lonely.

Every night ended the same: walking home after a long shift… no family
waiting up for me, and no one to share the crushing weight of the day
with. I was so aggressively alone that the silence in my studio
apartment felt heavy enough to choke me.

I used to look at couples on the street and feel a dull, aching envy,
deeply wanting someone—anyone—to just look at me and acknowledge that I
existed.

To cope, I built a wall around myself. My defense mechanism was a pair
of heavy, matte-black noise-cancelling headphones.

The moment they clamped over my ears, the world died. I always sat on
the same cracked, yellow plastic bench on Platform 4, completely buried
in my own head, hiding from a world that didn’t care about me anyway.

Then came a Tuesday in October, and everything changed.

I descended the concrete steps, my eyes locked onto the screen of my
phone as usual.

I reached my bench, turned around to sit, and froze. Someone was already
there.

She sat at the far edge of the plastic row. She wore a faded, oversized
denim jacket over a dark sweater, her hands buried deep in her pockets.

Her hair was slightly damp, as if she had just walked through a light
autumn drizzle, though it hadn’t rained outside all day.

I hesitated, but my legs were heavy from a fourteen-hour shift, so I
slid onto the opposite end of the bench, keeping my headphones firmly
on.

From the corner of my eye, I saw her turn her head. She didn’t just
glance; she looked at me with an intense, unblinking curiosity.

Slowly, awkwardly, I pulled the headphones down to rest around my neck.

“You’re late tonight,” she said. Her voice was remarkably clear,
carrying a strange, melodic resonance.

I blinked, completely thrown off. “Do I know you?”

“No,” she said, offering a small, fragile smile.

“But I know you.You sit here every night. You look like you’re carrying
the weight of the entire world on your shoulders.”

Her name was Erevia.

We didn’t board the train that night.

I let my train pull up, open its mechanical doors, hiss in
disappointment, and slide back into the dark tunnel without me.

For the first time in years, my boring, repetitive life suddenly had a
spark of color.

A deep bonding began to build between us, forged in the late hours of
the night.

Over the next three weeks, Platform 4 became our sanctuary.

Every single night, she would be sitting there, waiting for me.

Our routine was no longer a prison; it became a beautiful, breathless
countdown until I could see her.

We bared our souls to each other.

I told her things I had never told another living person—about how I
felt invisible in this massive city, about the suffocating panic attacks
I got in my empty apartment, and how her presence felt like the first
time I was allowed to breathe.

She listened with a fierce, unwavering empathy, taking my hands out of
my lap and holding them tightly whenever my voice cracked.

In return, she shared her inner world with me.

She spoke passionately about her future plans—how she wanted to buy a
small plot of land in the countryside where the stars weren’t choked out
by smog, and how she wanted to build a small garden full of lavender.

She told me all about her parents, describing their old, creaking
Victorian house on Elm Street with such precision I could smell the old
wood.

She shared their names, their quirks, and even where they lived. She
made me feel like I was a part of her family just by listening to her.
For the first time in my life, I felt hope.

But whenever I tried to bridge the gap into her present world, she
pulled back.

“Let me take you out,” I said one night, the words rushing out as I
realized I was completely, hopelessly in love with her.

“A real date. Let’s go to a 24-hour diner right now. I want to see you
under the sunlight.”

Her smile faded.

She looked down at her shoes.

“I can’t go out from here,” she whispered, her voice trembling slightly.
“I’m just too busy. I don’t like to leave this place.”

“Okay,” I countered, pulling out my phone.

“Then give me your number. Or your Instagram.I just want to be able to
hear from you when we aren’t on this bench.”

“I don’t have a phone, and I don’t use social media,” she said softly,
looking back up at me with a profound, aching sadness that broke my
heart. “I prefer to stay disconnected. It’s safer.”

Desperate to hold onto a piece of her, to have some proof of the
happiness she had given me, I stood up and angled my phone toward us.

“Then let’s take a photo. Just one, so I can look at you when the
loneliness gets too loud.”

She hesitated, then smiled softly and allowed me to sit right next to
her.

I held the phone up, framing our faces.

I leaned in, expecting to feel the warmth of her body, but a sensation
of absolute, cryogenic cold radiated from her skin, raising goosebumps
on my arms.

The camera flashed.

On the screen, we looked perfect. I locked my phone and slid it into my
pocket, my heart hammering. I knew right then that I wanted to spend the
rest of my life with her.

The next night, the bench was empty.

I waited until 1:00 AM, but she didn’t show.

Days started flowing into weeks.

Because I was completely overwhelmed and busy with an intense spike in
my workload, I tried to calm my racing thoughts.

Maybe she’s just busy, I kept telling myself.

She’ll come back soon. She cares about me too much to just disappear.

But a month passed, and the bench remained empty.

The loneliness returned, heavier and sharper than before because I had
finally tasted what true happiness felt like. I couldn’t take it
anymore.

I remembered the exact address she had so lovingly woven into her
stories: 1424 Elm Street.

I wanted to see her. I wanted to confess my feelings, to tell her that I
loved her, even if it was just for once.

On a freezing Saturday afternoon, I took a bus to the outskirts of the
city.

I found the house easily, exactly as she had described it.

But the lawn was choked with dead leaves, and the windows were
completely dark.

My heart thudding against my ribs, I walked up the wooden steps and
knocked.

I thought maybe they had gone on a family vacation.

I waited, and just as I turned to leave, the lock clicked.

The door creaked open.

An elderly couple stood there, surrounded by half-packed cardboard boxes
and rolls of bubble wrap.

They were preparing to move.

“Yes?” the woman asked, her voice thin and exhausted.

“Hello,” I stammered.

“I’m looking for Erevia. I’m a friend of hers from downtown. I wanted to
see her. I… I have feelings for her.”

The woman froze, a jagged sob tearing from her throat as she collapsed
against her husband’s chest.

The man stared at me, his face pale and entirely hollow.

“Son,” the man said, his voice dropping to a horrifying, trembling
whisper.

“Our daughter Erevia isn’t here. She has been dead for more than a
year.”

The world tilted.

The auditory white noise of my headphones returned, roaring in my ears.

“No,” I breathed. “No, that’s impossible. I meet her every night at the
station. We talk on the bench. We love each other! We even took photos
together!”

With trembling fingers, I tore my phone from my pocket and thrust the
glowing screen toward them.

“Look! This is her! Tell me where she is!”

The father took the phone, his hands shaking violently as his wife
peered through her tears. A hollow, paralyzing terror washed over them.

“Dear God,” the mother whispered.

The father handed the phone back. “Look at the picture, son. Look
closely.”

I pulled the phone back to my face and zoomed in.

My breath caught in my throat.

In the photo, I was smiling, my arm draped over empty, unoccupied air.
The yellow plastic bench beneath me was completely visible.

But right where Erevia had been sitting, the light from the fluorescent
tube directly above was warped, bending and twisting into a dark, dense
silhouette of a girl with her head bowed, as if she were weeping.

“She died at that station fourteen months ago,” the father said, his
voice hollowed out by mourning.

“Before she died, she always told her mother about a boy she saw every
night. A boy she had a massive crush on, but was too shy to speak to.
She told us she was finally going to build up the courage to confess her
feelings to him that night.”

The father stepped out onto the porch, his eyes boring into mine.

“A group of men were assaulting a young girl on the platform late at
night,” he whispered.

“When Erevia went to save her, they turned on her.They fired at her. She
bled out on the concrete.”

“The police reviewed the security footage, and they said there was one
witness sitting on the far bench with heavy headphones on. A man who
never looked up, never took his headphones off, and got on his train
while our daughter died. Are you that man who she loved?”

Memory is a predatory thing. The father’s words unlocked a vault in my
mind, and the flashbacks hit me like a physical blow. Fourteen months
ago. A Tuesday night.

A distant, muffled pop that I thought was a track misfire. A commotion
in my peripheral vision that I actively ignored, turning my head away,
tightening my headphones, completely wrapped up in my own dull,
miserable, lonely world.

“I don’t know,” I choked out, my voice barely audible as tears finally
spilled down my cheeks. “I work till late night… mostly I am alone in
the station. I am always in myself. I didn’t notice.”

I turned and ran down the steps, leaving the house behind, the sound of
my own ragged breathing echoing like a siren.

I ended up at the city cemetery.

I wandered through the rows of granite until I found her modest, gray
headstone.

I collapsed into the grass before it.

I didn’t confess my love the way I had planned to on her porch. Instead,
I just sat in the damp grass and wept, paying my final, agonizing
tribute, screaming apologies into the dirt for a tragedy I had chosen to
ignore when she was alive.

She hadn’t haunted me because she loved me.

She had haunted me because I was the bystander who let her die in the
dark.

The sun has set now.

The clock on my phone reads 11:15 PM.

My shift is over. I am standing at the top of the stairs of Central
Terminal, staring down into the dark throat of the subway.

I can hear the distant, erratic B-flat hum of the fluorescent light on
Platform 4. I know she is sitting on our bench. And tonight, I can’t
wear my headphones anymore.
