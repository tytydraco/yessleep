Leah was already standing up when I told her she could let go.

There’s a photo. I’m crouched on our kitchen floor, looking annoyed,
with my left arm in a bucket. Her left hand is in the foreground giving
a thumbs-up. She took the picture with her right.

I was still holding somebody’s hand.

We bought the kit for our fourth anniversary. We’d decided against
expensive presents because the car needed work, and Leah had seen one of
those videos where a couple puts their hands in molding gel and makes a
plaster copy. She thought we could keep it on the bookshelf.

The first attempt went wrong before we got our hands in. I used warm
water and the powder set too fast. The bucket came away full of pink
rubber with a spoon sticking out. We laughed about having made a
monument to our relationship with takeout.

There was enough powder for a second try. This time we read the
instructions properly.

We sat facing each other, put our left hands into the mixture, and
linked our fingers. We both left our wedding rings on. Leah’s has a
little flattened patch underneath from when she caught it in a door
years ago.

Holding still was harder than it looked. My knee started aching. Leah’s
nose itched. At one point I gave her hand three little squeezes, which
is our stupid way of saying I love you when we can’t talk.

She squeezed back.

When the timer went off, she worked her hand loose first. Then I tried
to open mine and couldn’t.

“Let go,” I told her.

“I did.”

I looked up. She was standing over me with her phone, laughing because
apparently I looked like a toddler who’d gotten stuck in something.

I pulled again. The fingers against my palm tightened.

Then they opened, all at once. I got my hand out so quickly I knocked
the bucket sideways. Leah caught it.

I told her the stuff had sucked my hand back in. She said hers had come
out easily. We checked my fingers, found nothing wrong, and mixed the
plaster.

I’m going to be honest about this part. I knew I’d felt fingers. I was
sitting there with my wife in a brightly lit kitchen, though, and it was
much easier to believe I’d misunderstood the feeling than anything else.

We left the plaster to set and went out for dinner.

The next morning we peeled the rubber away over newspaper. It was
fiddly. Leah used the wooden pick from the kit to clean the narrow
spaces between the fingers.

Her ring came out perfectly. So did mine. You could see the little scar
across my knuckle.

Then she turned it around.

There was another wrist coming out of the base between ours.

It wasn’t a lump that sort of resembled a hand. It had nails. There were
creases over each joint. Its fingers threaded through the places where
ours didn’t quite touch, and its thumb lay along the back of my hand.

Neither of us said anything for a while.

Leah put her own hand against the cast. I put mine beside it. We kept
doing that, matching fingers to fingers, as if we’d somehow counted
ourselves wrong.

The extra hand was bigger than either of ours.

I pulled up the picture she’d taken. Her thumb in the foreground. Her
phone in her other hand. Me still waiting for her to release me.

She stopped trying to clean the plaster.

“What did it feel like?”

I said I wasn’t sure.

She’s asked me that several times since. I’ve given her the same answer
because I don’t want to say it felt familiar. It felt like her hand.
Warm, with a thumb that rubbed mine while I waited.

We put the cast in a paper grocery bag beside the back door. Neither of
us wanted it on the shelf. I said I’d get rid of it. I didn’t want to
tell her I was afraid to touch it again.

That evening I was on the couch when my left hand closed.

I hadn’t picked anything up. My fingers just folded into my palm, and my
thumb was pushed across them. I tried to straighten them with my right
hand. Something squeezed back.

Leah came over and tried to help. She could move my fingers a little,
but the skin between them pulled tight. There was pressure on the back
of my hand where the extra thumb had been on the cast.

Then my arm jerked toward the kitchen.

I went off the couch shoulder first. It hurt badly enough that I
couldn’t get a full breath. My wrist slid along the floor and pulled the
rest of me after it.

Leah caught me under the arms. For a few seconds she couldn’t stop me.
Her socks kept slipping.

She braced one foot against the doorway and shouted, “What do I do?”

“The hand,” I said. “Break the fucking hand.”

She got the hammer from the drawer by the stove. I stayed on the floor,
holding the doorframe with my free hand.

She tipped the cast out of the bag and hit one of the extra fingers.

A white piece skittered under the refrigerator.

My hand opened.

For about a second I thought it was over.

Then something touched my cheek.

A single fingertip. Warm. It traced the line from my ear to the corner
of my mouth, slowly, while Leah stood in front of me holding the hammer
in both hands.

I could still feel pressure around my wrist, but that one finger could
reach my face now.

“Stop,” I said.

She lowered the hammer. The fingertip rested against my lips.

We left through the front door. I drove with my right hand. Leah kept
looking over her shoulder at the empty back seat.

We’re at my brother’s place. He’s let us stay without asking much, which
I’m grateful for. The pressure around my wrist eased during the drive,
and I can move my hand normally now. There are bruises in the places
where those fingers lay.

The bag and the cast are still on our kitchen floor. We haven’t gone
back.

I haven’t felt the fingertip on my face since we arrived. Leah fell
asleep beside me about an hour ago, and I was starting to think we’d put
enough distance between us and whatever happened.

Then I felt three little presses against my palm.

I almost answered before I looked down.

Both of her hands are tucked under her cheek.
