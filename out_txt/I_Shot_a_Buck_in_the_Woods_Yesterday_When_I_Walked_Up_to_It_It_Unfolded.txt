Yesterday morning I shot an eight point at seventy yards. When I walked
up to it, it was the size of a cooler.

It’s a little after 2 a.m. and I have every light in the house on. I’m
going to tell this in order, because I’ve tried to talk myself out of it
four times tonight and I keep ending up in the same place.

For what it’s worth, I’m not a jumpy guy. I once fell asleep on a
haunted hayride at my cousin’s wedding. I’ve spent a few hundred dawns
alone in those woods, and the worst thing that ever happened out there
was a leaky boot.

I lease a hundred and forty acres a few miles outside town from an old
farmer named Ray. Eleven years now. It’s mostly hardwood, with a logging
road down the east side, and in the middle there’s a clearing with a
maple stump in it that somebody cut before I ever showed up. The stump
is as wide as a kitchen table and split down one side, and the crack is
big enough that I once put my arm in it to the shoulder looking for a
dropped knife. It was warm in there. In January. I figured rotting wood
gives off heat and never thought about it again.

I’ve had a trail cam on the north edge of that clearing, aimed at the
stump, for three seasons. A clearing like that is where bucks cross.

Opening morning I was on the north ridge by 6:30, working down slow. A
little after seven I crossed a set of fresh tracks, big buck, deep in
the wet ground, heading for the clearing. I followed them down, and at
the edge the prints bent into a wide ring around the stump, every one
pointed inward, like something had stood there all night looking at it.
There was also a big oak lying across the far side that hadn’t been
there the weekend before. We hadn’t had a breath of wind all week. I
remember thinking, *huh*. Then I stopped thinking about it, which I now
consider the dumbest thing I’ve ever done.

Then I saw him.

He was standing past the stump with the oak behind him. Dark coat, thick
neck, a rack I’d have driven four states to hang on a wall. He was
looking right at me.

Deer don’t hold still. Even a calm one flicks an ear or drops his head
to nose around or shifts from hoof to hoof. This one did nothing. It was
thirty-four degrees and there was no breath coming off him. I put the
scope on him to look him over, the way you do, and I could see his eye
clear as a photograph. The pupil was round. A deer’s pupil is a flat
horizontal bar, like a goat’s. This one was round, like yours.

I whispered, “Come on, buddy. Move.”

His head turned about an inch, to keep me centered.

I should have walked out of there. I shot him anyway, and the honest
answer for why is that he was the biggest buck I’d ever had in a scope
and I wanted him.

He went down behind the stump and made no sound, which is wrong too,
they always make some noise. I gave it ten minutes like I always do. The
woods stayed shut. After a shot the quiet usually drains out a little at
a time, a squirrel starts up, a jay. This didn’t drain.

I walked across the clearing and found him lying folded up behind the
stump.

All four legs were pressed flat against his belly, stacked, his head
laid back along his own shoulder, the rack flat in the leaves. I looked
for the entry wound for a solid minute and never found it. And he was
small. A two hundred pound deer folded down to something I could have
carried under one arm, about the size of the cooler I keep in my truck
bed. There was no waist, no dip behind the ribs, just one long smooth
block, like somebody had put a hand on each end and pushed.

I crouched and put my glove on his ribs. A deer you’ve just shot stays
warm for an hour, you can feel it through the leather. He was as cold as
the ground. He smelled like a cellar.

Something inside him clicked. Small and dry, like a knuckle cracking in
the next room.

I stood up. A second click answered from deep in his chest. A third came
from the far side of the clearing, from a spot I had walked right past.

His back slid. I’ve seen plenty of death shudders and this was not one.
A whole slab of his side moved sideways under the hide, dragging the fur
with it, and a flat brown strip came out from under his shoulder and
kept coming, across the dirt and into the leaves. Then the shoulder
opened. No wound, no blood, just a seam, perfectly straight, and a
section of his ribcage swung out on a hinge. The inside was dry and pale
and layered like a wasp nest. Another section slid out behind it, then
another, each one jointed to the last at an angle no joint bends.

You ever see those old wooden folding rulers, with the brass pins, where
the sections swing out one after another? It was that. A folding ruler
the size of a house, and the deer was only the first fold.

I stood there with the rifle hanging off my shoulder like an idiot and
watched. One section slid between two maples and kept going. Another
went off through the brush to my left. The head stayed beside the stump,
but the neck behind it stretched out past anything a neck can do, until
the head was ten feet from the nearest piece of its own body and still
attached.

Its mouth was hanging open a little. Deer have no upper front teeth,
just a hard pad. I’ve looked in a hundred deer mouths. This one had a
full row up top, flat and even and much too big.

They were a person’s teeth.

Then the oak moved.

The big oak across the clearing, the one that wasn’t there last weekend,
lifted one end out of the leaves the way you’d lift your foot to step
over something. Bark came off it in sheets. Underneath it was brown and
furred, and it ended in a hoof the size of a trash can lid.

It set the hoof back down. Then I saw the others. They were all the way
around the clearing, forty or fifty feet out, evenly spaced, standing in
the ring of prints I’d followed in. I counted eight and quit counting.
The four legs I’d seen tucked under the buck were the little ones. The
ones made to look right.

I’d walked into the middle of it, on my own two feet.

The head was still on me. Then the antlers plowed a furrow through the
dirt and it turned and looked past me. Over my left shoulder. At
something behind me.

I didn’t turn around to see what.

I got the rifle up. I don’t know what I thought I was going to do. It
went off into the dirt next to my boot, I don’t think I meant to pull,
and at the crack every hoof around that clearing took one step in. All
at once. Then they stopped.

That’s how I found out it goes toward noise, and how much of it I’d been
making since I got there.

I ran. I went straight through the timber instead of the trail, and
branches opened my face up. I heard the clicking behind me, then beside
me, then ahead of me, and in all that time I never heard a single
footstep. Something caught my jacket between the shoulder blades and I
thought, *branch*. It let go when my boots hit the logging road.

I got in the truck, locked it, and sat there with both hands on the
wheel, staring at the tree line. A buck was standing between two trunks.
Same coat, same rack, facing me.

Then I heard footsteps on the gravel. Mine. My own stride, my own boots,
coming out of the woods about half a second behind where they should
have been. Under them I could hear my own breathing, ragged, the way it
had been when I was running. I wasn’t running. I was barely breathing at
all. It sounded like somebody doing an impression of me from a
description.

I put it in drive. Out my window the buck lowered his head until his
antlers touched the ground, and I watched his back start to rise.

I got home and sat in the driveway for twenty minutes. I locked the
rifle in the safe, which didn’t help. My wife and the kids are at her
mother’s till Sunday, and when she called last night I told her nothing
was wrong. What was I going to say.

This morning I went back. I know. I needed to see it in daylight, and I
told myself that if it was only a stump and some tracks I could finally
let it go. I took Ray and told him I’d found something strange. His old
lab, Bo, goes everywhere with him, and Bo wouldn’t get out of the truck.
That dog has never turned down a walk in his life. He sat on the seat
looking at the trees and shaking. Ray said, “Well, that’s new,” and left
him there.

On the way in I pulled the card out of my trail cam like I always do and
put it in my shirt pocket without looking at it.

We got to the edge of the clearing around nine. The stump was there. The
oak was gone. Where it had been there was a long smooth trench in the
leaves with no root ball and no hole. A tree that falls pulls up a mat
of roots. That oak was never a tree. No deer. No blood.

Ray crouched at the edge of the ring and said, “Huh.”

In daylight it wasn’t a ring. A few of the marks were hoofprints, but
most were long narrow grooves with a round dent at one end, and they
didn’t circle the stump, they ran away from it, dead straight, out
through the trees in every direction. Spokes. The prints I’d taken for a
circle were just where each line ended.

Ray’s seventy-three and has farmed that county his whole life. I’ve
never seen him look at the ground like that. “Nothing walks that
straight,” he said.

Under the stump, something clicked.

The split down its side widened about an inch. A thread of steam came
out and hung in the cold. Inside it was pale and wet and ridged like the
roof of a mouth, with a rim of short brown hair around the edge, and the
hair lifted and settled. Lifted and settled.

I’d had my arm in that crack to the shoulder. I thought about that the
whole way back.

Ray took my elbow and we left without touching anything. At the trucks
he said, “My dad found one behind our barn when I was a boy. Folded up
small as a suitcase. He burned it with a can of gas and never said
another word about it.” Then he said, “Don’t go back in there,” and he
drove off with Bo. He hasn’t answered my texts since, including the one
asking if he got home okay.

Around four I took my jacket down in the garage to knock the mud off.
There was a dried patch between the shoulder blades, right where
something had caught me, and when I scraped it a chunk of bark dropped
onto the concrete.

It had short coarse hair all over it, and one end had a tiny cloven
print pressed into it, perfect, the size of my thumbnail.

I thought about Ray’s dad and the gas. I put the thing in a coffee can
and held the little butane torch I use for sweating copper pipe on it
for five minutes. It didn’t catch. The hair wouldn’t even singe. It just
got warm. Then I saw the fur lifting and settling, lifting and settling,
and I understood I’d been standing there keeping it warm.

I put the lid on and left it on the workbench. I don’t think it came off
me by accident. I think it was left there, the way we tag deer.

Around eight I put the trail cam card in my laptop. I told myself I only
wanted to see the clearing from a safe distance.

I scrolled to yesterday morning. At 4:09 a.m. the clearing is empty. At
4:10 there’s an oak lying across the far edge. Same camera, one minute
apart, and nothing carried it in.

Then I scrolled to 7:54.

The camera fires a burst of eleven when something moves. The first frame
is stamped 7:54:31. It’s me, crouched beside the folded buck with my
glove on its ribs.

There’s something standing behind me. I’m not going to describe all of
it. I’ll tell you where it is. Its front legs are planted on either side
of me and its head is bent down level with my ear, close enough that I’d
have felt it breathing if it breathed. It has the same face as the one
on the ground. The same face from a slightly different angle, like two
prints off one negative. I was looking for the wound and it was watching
me look. It had been standing there since before I crouched.

The head on the ground wasn’t looking past me. It was looking at *that*,
to see if I’d turn around.

In the last frame I’m gone. It’s the stump and the empty clearing and
the head, on the ground in the leaves, rack down, turned toward the
camera. Looking straight into the lens. At me, right now, sitting here.

It’s 2:51 now. I’m at the kitchen table. The coffee can is still on the
workbench because I couldn’t make myself carry it through the house, and
I’ve been listening to it for an hour. A click. Nothing for a couple of
minutes. Another click. Spaced the way they were in the clearing right
before it moved.

Twenty minutes ago they started coming from the garage floor. Then from
the wall.

There’s a strip of light under the door between the garage and this
kitchen, because I left the garage light on. The gap is about half an
inch. I checked it with my thumbnail. The strip that slid out from under
that buck’s shoulder was thinner than that.

A few seconds ago a piece of the light went out. About the width of a
hoof.

Now I can hear it on the other side of the door, in my voice, a little
too slow.

“Come on, buddy.”

And from the hallway behind me, closer, in my voice:

“Move.”

The bathroom’s down that hallway. I’ve needed it for an hour. I’m not
going to turn around.

I’m going to sit here perfectly still, the way he did, and see which one
of us moves first.
