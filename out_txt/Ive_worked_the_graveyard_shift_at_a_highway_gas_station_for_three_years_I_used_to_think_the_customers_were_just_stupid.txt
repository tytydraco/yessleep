If you’ve never worked the 11 PM to 7 AM shift at a travel plaza
stranded off an isolated stretch of two-lane blacktop, let me paint you
a picture: it’s purgatory with a slushie machine and fluorescent
lighting designed to induce migraines.

Our station sits off Route 22, wedged between twenty miles of dense,
rotting pine forest and an unpaved gravel turnout where long-haul
truckers pull over to sleep off thirty-hour runs. For the first two
years of working there, I genuinely believed my greatest occupational
hazard was dying of secondhand embarrassment.

Night shift retail breaks people’s brains. I don’t know if it’s highway
hypnosis, sleep deprivation, or the low hum of the drink coolers, but
the people who walk through those double glass doors at three in the
morning are operating on roughly three shared brain cells.

To give you an idea of my baseline normal:

Last spring, a guy in flip-flops tried to pay for a twelve-pack of warm
Milwaukee’s Best with an actual, living bearded dragon in a shoe box.
When I told him we only accepted cash or card, he stared at the lizard,
stared at me, and asked if I could “at least swipe it as credit.”

Two months later, a man stormed inside at 2:00 AM, veins bulging out of
his neck, screaming that pump four was broken and that I was personally
running a scam to “steal his precious time.” When I walked outside in
the drizzle to check, he had shoved the nozzle into his tank, clamped
the handle down, and was just standing there waiting for gas to
magically flow. He hadn’t pressed a single button. He hadn’t swiped a
card. He hadn’t even selected a fuel grade. When I tapped the big yellow
“87 Regular” button and the pump immediately whirred to life, he glared
at me with absolute contempt and muttered that our system was
“unnecessarily confusing.”

A week after that, a couple came in at 4:00 AM, grabbed a cold can of
Chef Boyardee ravioli, shoved the unopened metal tin into the deli
microwave, and typed in nine minutes. When the microwave produced a
miniature lightning storm and started spewing toxic gray smoke, the
woman looked at me like I was the idiot and said, “Well, your appliance
clearly has negative energy.”

And then there was the classic: a tourist who spent twenty minutes
screaming at me because our three-day-old roller-grill jalapeño cheddar
dogs weren’t “locally sourced and keto-certified.” Sir, you are eighty
miles from civilization buying meat that’s been spinning on chrome
rollers since Tuesday. Lower your expectations.

My manager, Dale a man who smells perpetually of menthol cigarettes,
beef jerky, and unearned confidence always told me the same thing: “Just
nod, take their money, and don’t look ’em in the eye for too long. Weird
attracts weird.”

So you can understand why, when strange things started happening around
the station, I just chalked it up to standard-issue customer stupidity.

The first thing was the cars.

Behind our station, past the dumpster corral, there’s an unpaved gravel
lot that bleeds straight into the overgrown tree line. Cell service back
there drops to zero bars, and the trees are so thick they swallow the
glow from the sodium floodlights on our roof. Every now and then, some
beater breaks an axle or overheats, and people leave them there for a
day or two until a flatbed shows up.

Around late August, an old maroon sedan appeared back there. It sat near
the tree line for four days. The driver’s side door was left unlocked,
the keys were sitting on the floor mat, and a half-eaten burger was
rotting in a paper bag on the passenger seat. I told Dale, who took a
drag of his cigarette and said, “Some tweaker ran out of gas and walked
off. Call the county tow when you get around to it.”

The county tow took over a week to show up. But less than forty-eight
hours after they hauled the sedan away, a dark blue pickup took its
place in the exact same corner.

Same deal. Keys left on the dash. Driver’s side window cracked an inch.
A pair of reading glasses and an open road atlas sitting on the
passenger seat.

Around that same time, weird little things began happening around the
perimeter. The heavy exterior floodlight mounted above the rear dumpster
kept going dark. When I went out back with a broom handle to check it,
the bulb wasn’t burned out it had been deliberately unscrewed half a
turn. I figured it was local teenagers trying to smoke weed out of view
of the security cameras.

Then there was the camera itself. Camera 3, which covered the gravel
lot, started feeding static to the monitor above my counter. When I
checked the housing, someone had smeared thick, greasy axle grease
directly over the glass lens. I cleaned it off, grumbling about dumb
kids, and didn’t think twice.

Then, two nights later, a silver Honda Civic was parked right next to
where the truck had been.

It was during that week that I noticed the Guy with the Cap.

He wasn’t loud like the usual weirdos. He never yelled about organic
roller dogs or tried to barter live reptiles. In fact, he was so quiet
you’d barely notice him walk in over the drone of the refrigeration
units. He wore a faded canvas work jacket, mud-caked boots, and a
battered baseball cap pulled down so low you could only see the stubble
on his jaw and a thin, bloodless mouth.

He’d come in around 3:30 AM. He never bought much a pack of spearmint
gum, a bottle of water, sometimes a roll of heavy-duty duct tape and a
pack of utility knife replacement blades from the small hardware rack
near the motor oil.

The first time he came up to the counter, I hit him with my usual
deadpan customer service voice. “Find everything okay, man?”

He didn’t answer. He just set a crumpled, damp ten-dollar bill on the
counter. His fingers were raw, the cuticles bitten down to the bloody
quick, and his knuckles were stained with dark, greasy grit that smelled
faintly metallic. Like wet pennies and dirt.

“Gonna be seven-fifty,” I said, ringing it up.

He slowly tilted his head up. Under the brim of the cap, his eyes were
wide, bloodshot, and completely devoid of fatigue. They were the eyes of
a man who hadn’t slept in a week and was riding an electrical current of
pure, quiet mania.

“You alone here all night?” he asked. His voice was a dry rasp, like
sandpaper rubbing against drywall.

“Nah,” I lied instinctively, a chill prickling the back of my neck.
“Manager’s in the back room doing inventory. He’s about six-foot-four
and gets real twitchy if people interrupt him.”

The man didn’t blink. He didn’t look toward the back room. He just
stared up at the security monitor mounted above my head, studying the
angles of the cameras, the reflection of the glass doors, and the dark
expanse of the pumps outside.

Then he picked up his gum, turned without a word, and walked out.

I watched him through the plate glass. He didn’t walk toward the pumps.
He didn’t have a car parked under the canopy. He walked straight toward
the back gravel lot, slipping past the dumpster corral and disappearing
into the absolute pitch-black shadows of the tree line.

Two days later, a State Trooper walked in and taped a missing-persons
flyer to our front window.

A 20-year-old college student named Maya. Last seen driving home from
campus down Route 22. Her car was a silver Honda Civic.

A cold, heavy weight dropped into the pit of my stomach.

I stared at the flyer, then out the window toward the back lot. The
silver Civic had vanished three days ago. I’d assumed the county tow had
finally done their job without me having to call them twice.

When my shift ended that morning at 7:00 AM, I didn’t get in my car and
go home. I walked out behind the dumpster corral.

The gravel in the back lot was scuffed with fresh, aggressive tire spin
marks. But as I walked closer to the tree line where the pavement ended
and the black pines began, something caught the morning sun in the
overgrown brush near a drainage ditch.

I crouched down. It was a keychain a little plush avocado attached to a
ring with a snapped lanyard. Tucked four feet further into the briars
was a woman’s white canvas sneaker, caked in dried mud, and three
heavy-duty black zip-ties, sliced cleanly through with a razor.

I stood there in the freezing gray morning light, the distant drone of
semi-trucks vibrating through the soles of my shoes, and the terrifying
reality finally clicked into place.

Nobody was abandoning cars.

People were pulling off the highway late at night to rest, stretch their
legs, or sleep in the dark lot away from the lights of the pumps. And
someone was waiting for them in the trees.

I called the police immediately. Two deputies came out, bagged the
sneaker, looked at the mud tracks, took my statement, and told me they’d
“increase patrols in the sector.” If you know anything about rural
county sheriff departments, “increasing patrols” means they drive by at
forty miles an hour once every six hours.

That brings me to last night.

It was 2:45 AM. A nasty late-autumn rain was pounding against the glass,
turning the highway into a blur of black asphalt and smeared red
taillights.

I was on edge, jumping at every creak of the building. To keep myself
from spiraling, I tried to focus on the routine. An old guy had just
come in ten minutes prior trying to return a lottery ticket he had
scratched off and lost on, claiming it was “defective merchandise.” I
had almost laughed. Almost.

Then the chime above the door chimed. Ding-dong.

I looked up.

It was him. The canvas jacket was soaked through, clinging to his broad,
lean shoulders. He took off his baseball cap, and for the first time, I
saw the rest of his face. He had a deep, jagged scratch running from his
right ear down into his collar, fresh and weeping red.

He didn’t walk to the aisles. He walked straight to the register.

He was holding a box of heavy contractor-grade trash bags from aisle
four.

“Nasty night out,” I said. My voice betrayed me, cracking slightly.

He laid the box on the counter. Beside it, he set down a small leather
wallet with a floral print. A woman’s wallet.

He caught me looking at it.

He didn’t try to hide it. Instead, he smiled a small, crooked twitch of
his mouth that made the hairs on my arms stand straight up.

“Tire blew out back,” he whispered. “Girl in a red Toyota. Pulled in to
change it. Wet pavement is dangerous.”

There hadn’t been a red Toyota at the pumps all night. Which meant she
was in the back.

My hand slowly moved under the counter, feeling for the silent panic
button Dale swore was wired to the local dispatch (though given Dale’s
maintenance record, it was probably wired to an old toaster).

“You shouldn’t work late,” the man said quietly, leaning his palms flat
against the laminate counter. His knuckles were fresh with that same
dark, copper-smelling grime. “It’s bad for your health.”

He reached his hand into his canvas coat.

I didn’t wait to see what he was pulling out.

Behind the counter, right next to the POS terminal, is a commercial
stainless-steel coffee pot kept at a rolling 190 degrees for the
truckers. Without thinking, I grabbed the plastic handle and threw the
entire pot of scalding black coffee directly into his face.

The scream he let out was barely human a high, wet shriek of agony.

He stumbled backward, clutching his face, skin instantly blistering red
as coffee and steam poured off his jacket. A heavy hunting knife
clattered from his hand onto the linoleum.

I didn’t try to fight him. I work at a gas station; I’m not John Wick.

I bolted out from behind the counter toward the rear stockroom, but my
sneakers hit the wet floor near the entrance and I wiped out hard,
slamming my shoulder into the display rack of cheap sunglasses. Plastic
frames rained down everywhere.

I scrambled up on all fours just as he lunged around the counter. His
face was a horrific mask of scalded, peeling skin and pure, animal rage.
He swung the knife blindly, slicing through the air inches from my
jacket.

I grabbed the only thing within reach: a four-pound steel fire
extinguisher off the wall bracket.

As he lunged forward again, I didn’t spray it I swung the entire metal
cylinder like a baseball bat, connecting square with the side of his
knee.

There was a sickening crack, and he went down with a muffled grunt,
crashing into a pyramid of windshield washer fluid jugs. Blue liquid
exploded across the floor, mixing with the spilled coffee.

I didn’t look back. I scrambled into the stockroom, slammed the heavy
metal fire door behind me, threw the deadbolt, and hit the emergency
exterior fuel-shutoff alarm.

A high-decibel air horn began blaring across the entire property, and
the overhead emergency strobes started flashing blind, frantic pulses of
red and white.

I sat with my back pressed against that steel door, holding a box cutter
with both shaking hands, listening to him slam his body against the
other side.

BANG.

BANG.

BANG.

And then… silence.

By the time three state trooper cruisers rolled in with their sirens
wailing ten minutes later, he was gone.

They found the red Toyota in the back lot, hidden behind an old
construction trailer. The driver, a 28-year-old nurse heading home from
a double shift, had been dragged into the trees. She’s alive. She was
badly injured and in shock, but because of the alarm and the noise, he
had panicked and fled before he could finish what he started. The
deputies found her tied up in the ravine fifty yards into the woods.

They haven’t caught him yet.

They found his camp two miles down the ridge sleeping bags, tarps, and
the personal effects of at least six other people who had “disappeared”
along Route 22 over the past year and a half.

I quit my job this morning. Dale was pissed because he had to cover my
shift, and honestly, the idea of Dale having to explain to a meth-head
why we don’t accept barter for rolling papers brings me a tiny shred of
joy.

I’m packing up my apartment and moving back to my parents’ place two
states over.

If you take anything away from this, please: if you’re driving late at
night on a lonely stretch of highway and you feel tired, don’t pull into
the dark, unlit dirt lots behind the rest stops. Stay under the bright,
humming lights at the front.

Because the weirdest, most dangerous things out there aren’t the idiots
drinking windshield fluid.

They’re the ones watching from the pines, waiting in the dark for you to
put the car in park
