“It’s a really nice place,” I told myself repeatedly.

It was a new apartment building near my old school. There were a lot of
young, friendly families living there. The grounds were enclosed from
the outside world by a tall metal fence. There was even a really nice
little playground.

I was lucky. My lovely aunt, who had been forced to move to Germany
because of her high-paying job, let me stay at her place. One room, a
kitchen-cum-lounge, a small bathroom, and a balcony – what else could a
third-year university student dream of? Most of my groupmates were still
living with their parents.

It was such a great opportunity. It still is. So why am I so afraid of
this place?

At first, I thought it had something to do with my fear of separation.
You don’t move away from the safety of your childhood home every day,
only to face the world of adulthood all alone. It was scary to wake up
in the middle of the night in an empty room, not knowing what had woken
you up. There was no one nearby to help you shake off your fear. Just
four dark corners, and you, huddled in one of them in your creaky little
bed. And sometimes, the corners farthest away from you aren’t the ones
you should be most afraid of.

Those were the nights when I would listen to my neighbours next door.

Our floor was divided into two blocks, one on each side of the
staircase. Each block had a main entrance door. Ours was massive, made
of dark metal. Behind it was a simple small space with three entrances
to three almost identical flats. They were hidden behind
expensive-looking wooden doors, though none of them was as impressive as
the main entrance.

Room 31, Room 32, and Room 33. The numbers weren’t written on the doors
themselves, but you could learn them from the utility bills for each
flat. I lived in Room 31, the one closest to the main entrance.

There wasn’t much to say about Room 33. I was once told that a woman
lived there from time to time. I heard the police standing right outside
my door, banging on hers. I watched through the peephole as they took
her away. I never saw her again.

Room 32 was different. It was full of life. Every time I was in the
bathroom, I could hear water running and a girl’s voice singing.
Whenever I was in the kitchen, I heard a man talking to a cat, asking it
to stop meowing. And every night, just before I closed my eyes, I could
hear them making love.

None of it was particularly unpleasant. In fact, I had grown fond of the
people next door, almost as if we were roommates.

I was lucky enough to visit their flat once. It was the girl who brought
me in. I bumped into her near the entrance to our apartment complex. She
was holding a grey tomcat in her arms, returning from running some
errands, cheerful and talkative as ever.

I ended up sitting on the couch in their kitchen. It was a nice place,
although I could already see some damage. Black mold had spread across
the corners of the room because of the dampness, and there was a hole in
the floor with leaking pipes beneath it.

“Construction defects,” the man said.

I learned that he was the girl’s much older boyfriend. He grumbled
constantly about the poor condition of the complex, about how cheap the
pipes were and how fucked-up the electrical system was, so the lights
could simply go on and off for no apparent reason.

He spoke with hatred in his voice, hatred for this place, while the girl
served me a bowl of soup she had made. It was a traditional soup called
solyanka.

The soup was delicious. The girl looked miserable. The man was angry at
the cat for licking his owner’s fingers.

“His name is Musya,” the girl told me.

I petted Musya before I left.

I wish I had never spoken to my neighbours.

It wasn’t long before the voices behind my wall fell silent. Then came
sobbing and ugly, horrible crying. Until there was nothing. Silence
again, for a long time.

Until someone knocked on my front door.

I got out of bed in the middle of the night and approached the door as
quietly as I could, careful not to make a sound.

There was something interesting about the little hallway in our block.
It had a motion-activated light. Needless to say, it was a very nice
thing to have when you came home with your arms full of groceries and
the light switched on automatically.

Not so nice when you were looking through the peephole and the light on
the other side suddenly came on. Someone was there, but I couldn’t see
them.

I stood there a little longer, watching. Nothing happened. Still
breathing heavily, I retreated into the safety of my bedcovers. More
knocking followed soon after.

At that point, I was scared. Who would come to my door at such a late
hour just to mess with me?

I got up once more, this time grabbing something heavy – a hammer – for
my own peace of mind. Little by little, I approached the entrance.

The knocking changed to rattling as someone tugged at the handle. I
gasped at the sheer unexpectedness of it. A cold sensation ran through
my body as the rattling grew more violent with every passing second.

Then it stopped.

I stood in the silence of my room, my heart pounding in my chest.

What was that?

I asked myself the same question again and again as I stepped closer to
the door.

When I checked outside, there was nobody there, just as before.

It was perplexing. I tried to convince myself that it was some kind of
sick joke, but I couldn’t tear myself away from the peephole. I stayed
there, staring through it, moment after moment. Until I saw it.

A movement in the very corner of the peephole. A silhouette holding
something in its large arms.

Then I heard a voice.

“Karyna, let me in, please.”

A quiet meow echoed through the hallway.

“Karyna, please, I’m begging you. I won’t do it again, I promise.”

The man stepped into view, repeatedly calling the girl’s name and
begging for forgiveness as he stood right outside my door.

The knocking continued, but I barely cared anymore. I could finally see
who had been messing with me, in all his miserable glory.

The man looked dishevelled. His eyes were wide and dark, his face red
and puffy. And in his arms was a cat. A grey tomcat.

I opened the door, and as soon as I did, the sharp smell of cheap
alcohol hit my nostrils.

The cat meowed as if greeting me.

“Karyna?”

“Wrong flat, man,” I told him, with a hint of pity in my voice.

Of course, I was angry about his antics, but he looked as though he were
falling apart right in front of my eyes. And I really didn’t like the
sight of it.

At least he didn’t seem sorry for bothering me that night. Instead, he
shoved the cat towards my face.

“Wanna pet Musya? He’s lonely.”

I petted it a little, stroking its small, fluffy head. Its nose was pale
and sickly dry. Its eyes were big and blue. It reached towards me with
its tiny paw, its tail just as tiny.

It wasn’t Musya.

It was an entirely different cat.

A feeling of disgust towards the man rose inside me.

“Can we come in? Musya isn’t the only one who’s lonely.”

Instinctively, I tried to close the gap between us by pulling the door
towards myself, but he took a step forward. Now his foot was wedged
between the door and the frame.

“Just go home,” I begged him. “It’s really late. It would be a shame if
you woke up our neighbours.”

The dry skin at the corners of his mouth cracked as he stretched his
lips into a smile. I didn’t like that smile. Not one bit.

“It’s okay,” he said, almost reassuringly. “No one outside this block
will hear a thing.”

I pulled the door violently towards myself, trying to get away from the
drunk creep. It was impossible. He had planted his foot firmly in the
doorway.

He almost laughed at my futile efforts.

Then the lights inside our block started flickering.

They were the same motion-activated lights that were supposed to stay on
whenever they detected movement, but something wasn’t right.

The man noticed it, too.

Like I said, he was the one complaining about the unstable electricity
in our apartment complex. But his usual annoyance at the subject was
nowhere to be seen.

His face first grew perplexed. Then, just as quickly, his expression
turned into questioning. And then, all at once, sheer terror spread
across his features.

What was he afraid of? Flickering lights? That was absurd. The man who,
only a moment ago, had laughed at my helplessness now looked as lost as
a child.

I might have found it amusing if it weren’t for the cat.

A drugged person could be frightened by all sorts of irrational things,
but the cat was as scared as his master, making a low, haunting screech,
as though it were preparing to fight something invisible.

It drove its claws painfully deep into the man’s arm until blood began
to drip, but he remained unfazed. 

He was listening. Hearing something.

That was when I heard it, too. A muffled sound, like old bones snapping
under an unbearable weight. Then another. And another. The sound was
approaching. Fast!

The man suddenly lunged forward, dropping the cat, and screamed.

“Let me in! Let me in right now!”

He pleaded through the half-open door, his foot still wedged in the
doorway. He grabbed the handle from the other side and began pulling
with all his might.

“I can’t!” I was visibly panicking. I didn’t have even half the man’s
strength.

There was no other way. I had to force the drunkard away.I stomped on
his foot with all my might. As he stumbled, I quickly slammed the door
shut.

I turned the key once, then again. The miserable man started banging on
the door once more, but the knocks were different from before. Now there
was desperation in them. It was unsettling, as if at any moment a single
hard enough blow could rip the door off its hinges.

I covered my ears with the palms of my hands and squeezed my eyes shut
as tightly as possible, trying to hide from this nightmare.

Then silence fell.

I looked through the peephole and saw nothing but pitch-black darkness,
as if nobody had been standing there just seconds ago.

I stared into the darkness for a very long time, and I am almost certain
that something was staring back at me.

For the next couple of days, there was no news from my neighbour. Not
until last Tuesday, when I began to notice a strange smell. It was
sickly sweet and rotten, coming from my vents, accompanied by faint
meowing. Apparently, I wasn’t the only one who had noticed it.

It wasn’t until Thursday that the smell finally went away, thanks to my
neighbours. Someone had called the police, and they came to remove the
man’s body from the flat next to mine. The police said it was suicide.
Apparently, the man had been unable to recover from a bitter breakup and
had taken his own life on Sunday, the day after our last encounter.

The cat had been starving, and after a couple of days, it had started
feasting on its former owner.

I took the cat in after the police had finished their investigation,
claiming it as my own. I was glad I wouldn’t have to deal with that
strange man anymore.

However, I soon learned that having no neighbours nearby was something
to worry about.

It wasn’t so bad at first, since I now had a cat. But going outside
became harder for me with every passing day. Every time I stepped out, I
could swear I heard the same muffled sound of cracking bones. They
reminded me of footsteps, as if someone were lurking right behind me all
the time. And whenever I finally turned around to look, there was
nothing there.

There was always nothing there.

As the days went by, I cared less and less about leaving my flat or
attending classes. Only within the four walls of my little fortress did
I feel somewhat safe. Somehow, I knew it couldn’t follow me inside.

One day, I had to go out to buy food for myself and the cat. I rushed
through the entire process, desperate to get home as soon as possible.

I was on the flight of stairs when I heard the cracking sound again. It
followed me with every step I took as I climbed towards my floor.

I hurried to reach the massive metal door leading into my block. It was
never locked, so I simply grabbed the handle and stepped into the small
space beyond it. The lights switched on immediately, triggered by my
presence.

I fished the keys out of my pocket and began fumbling with them,
searching for the right one.

As I stood in front of my own door, I felt a disturbing, rotten
sensation deep inside me, as if something terrible were about to happen.

And then the lights started flickering.

A wave of terror rose into my throat. My legs grew heavy as stone, and
my hands began shaking uncontrollably.

It hadn’t been so long since I had questioned the terrified look on that
man’s face. Now I was in his shoes, and I finally understood how he had
felt. Like prey, surrounded on all sides.

Under the pressure, I dropped my keys.

The next thing I knew, everything went dark. I moved my hand slightly,
trying to trigger the motion sensor, but nothing happened. I tried
again. And again. Until my hand touched something.

It wasn’t a wall. It was far too soft and warm to the touch. The texture
was strange. Wrong. So terribly wrong that I suddenly felt like throwing
up.

I yanked my hand away and rushed straight towards the metal entrance
door. But it was closed.

That’s impossible. It’s never locked. I don’t even have a key for it.

The thoughts raced through my head as I began banging on the door. The
noise drowned out every movement of the thing behind me.

It was too late by the time I realised what was happening.

I stopped trying to force the door open and listened. The last echoing
crack rang out behind my back. There was nowhere to run.

I could feel its heavy presence. A laboured breath brushed against the
back of my neck. I stood perfectly still as it moved closer.

Then came the chewing.

Not directed at anything in particular. Just the wet, disgusting sounds
of flesh being torn apart and devoured.

It was imitating the process, or perhaps playing it back to me like a
recording. Whatever it was, the sound continued, growing louder and more
overwhelming until I could barely stand it.

Then came the sound of a bone cracking between someone’s teeth.

Remember how I said that having no neighbours nearby was something to
worry about? I meant it. Because there was no one to hear you scream.

I screamed at the top of my lungs, as loudly as I could, no longer able
to hold myself together. I dropped to my knees and began groping for my
keys on the floor. My hands moved through a mass of warm, soft matter
that my imagination could now perceive as nothing but piles of flesh.

After a moment, I finally found them. They emerged from the bottom of
the pile with a distinct, wet squelch.

I scrambled to my feet and hurried to unlock the door as the bones
continued to crack behind me.

Crack!

The lock clicked open.

I stumbled into my flat and slammed the door shut, pressing my entire
body against it. I was breathing heavily. Or perhaps I was barely
breathing at all. I retreated to the farthest corner from the door and
sat down.

The cat came over to me, purring. It rubbed its head against my
trembling legs. That was when I realised that, in all the chaos, I had
forgotten my groceries.

“Well, there goes the food for both of us,” I told the cat miserably.

It couldn’t have cared less.

I knew I couldn’t go outside anymore. That was no longer an option.

There was still some food in the fridge, but it would run out in a
matter of days.

And then…

Well, at least the cat would have something to snack on.
