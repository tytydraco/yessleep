It was October 24, 1983, about a week before Halloween. My friends and I
were over at my mom’s family’s house for an early dinner, filling up our
plates while the radio in the kitchen played top forty music. Once we
finished eating, we snuck out to the front porch because we were bored
and wanted something to do.

We were fifteen and sixteen, it was a cold fall night, and we wanted an
adrenaline rush.

Earlier that week, a guy working at the local hardware store told us
about an old town legend. He said his grandfather passed down a story
about a massive estate hidden deep in the woods off an old logging
route, a place that was completely wiped off modern maps. His
grandfather swore it was cursed, built by a rich family back in the
early 1900s before something killed all forty-seven of them in a single
month. The guy even drew us a rough map on a scrap piece of paper.

There were six of us: Jackson, David, Tony, Martha, Taresa, and me. We
crammed onto the bench seats of David’s 1977 Ford F-250 crew cab pickup
and headed toward the tree line, following the directions.

The road turned bad really fast. The pavement cracked, turned into rough
dirt, and got swallowed by thick pine trees leaning over the trail. In
the yellow beam of the truck’s headlights, the woods looked totally
dead.

As the truck bounced over exposed roots, we saw a rusted metal sign
hanging off a rotting wooden post: KINGSTON ROAD.

Jackson was squeezed in the middle of the bench seat and started having
second thoughts. “Guys, seriously, look at this place. My gut says this
is a bad idea. Let’s just turn around.”

“Come on, Jack, don’t ruin it,” David laughed, tapping his hands on the
steering wheel. “It’s just an old abandoned house. We check it out,
scare ourselves for twenty minutes, and leave. You’re fine.”

We pulled up to a set of massive iron gates. They were supposed to keep
people out, but the heavy center latch was twisted and pried wide open,
like someone had taken a crowbar to it from the inside.

We left the truck idling near the turn, grabbed our flashlights, and
walked through the damp leaves.

The house was huge. It loomed against the night sky like a giant shadow
with sagging wrap-around porches, busted bay windows, and steep
rooflines.

I pulled out the scrap paper. The local’s grandfather had claimed the
last surviving relative died in a hospital bed, and his final words
were:

“T-t-they hunt… d-disfigured, impossible humanoid creatures i-in t-the
walls… they don’t move right… they don’t look right…”

The second we stepped onto the rotting hardwood floor inside, the
temperature plummeted. It felt like walking straight into a meat locker.

When we shined our flashlights around, nothing made sense.

The entryway was completely rotted out, but the grand hallway behind it
looked almost brand new. The wallpaper was clean, the carpets had no
dust, and the brass wall sconces were actually glowing with warm
electric light.

“How is that working?” Taresa whispered, her breath fogging up in the
air. “There aren’t any power lines out here. No generator sound,
nothing.”

Before anyone could answer, a loud SLAM echoed behind us. We whipped
around to look at the entrance. The wooden frame, the hinges, and the
opening were completely gone. In their place was a flat, solid plaster
wall.

Tony and Martha immediately started to panic.

“Where’s the door?! Where did the door go?!” Tony yelled, clawing at the
drywall.

“Calm down, take a breath, we’ll find another exit,” I tried to say, but
my voice was shaking. Out of the corner of my eye, I saw a tall, thin
shadow stretch across the hallway wall at an angle that made no sense
with the lights. When I snapped my head toward it, it vanished.

Then we heard glass shattering deep inside the house.

The floorboards vibrated under our boots.

At the end of the hall, a heavy oak bookcase tipped forward. But instead
of hitting the floor, it stopped halfway down, hovered three feet in the
air, and shot straight down the corridor at us like a missile.

“GET DOWN!” David yelled.

We threw Quantum flat onto the floor. The bookcase flew right over our
heads and smashed into the wall behind us, exploding into splinters and
blowing a hole straight into another hallway.

I scrambled up first, squeezed through the broken drywall, and froze.

What was standing on the other side made no sense to look at. My eyes
literally strained trying to process it.

It was a tall, asymmetrical thing, but its body was totally wrong. One
arm was long and jointed in three places like an insect leg, while the
other arm was just a thick, swollen mass of pale skin dragging on the
floor. Its chest was split wide open down the middle with no sternum,
showing a hollow cavity where a twisted ribcage pulsed in and out like
gills.

Its neck was a long, bruised stalk of skin sagging under the weight of
its head. It had no eyes or nose, just two wet crater holes, and a split
jaw packed with rows of thin, needle-like human teeth. The skin on its
face was pulled so tight it looked clear, showing thin black veins
moving underneath.

It wasn’t breathing normally. It was making a wet, gurgling noise, like
someone trying to talk through a throat full of thick liquid and crushed
glass.

Its head snapped toward me with a sharp crack, turning 180 degrees. A
wide, torn smile spread across its face, and without warning, it dropped
onto all fours and started skittering toward me across the floor, walls,
and ceiling all at once.

“GET BACK!” I screamed, shoving David and Martha back through the hole
in the wall.

I dragged myself through right as the gurgling noise stopped outside the
gap. We sat in dead silence, holding broken pieces of wood as makeshift
weapons, waiting for it to tear through the plaster.

David peeked through the hole. “It’s gone. There’s nothing out there.”

“What the hell was that?!” David choked out, wiping sweat off his face.

“I don’t know, but we aren’t safe here,” I said.

Martha checked her pockets, but we didn’t have any way to call for help.
We were totally stranded.

We carefully stepped back out into the corridor, keeping our backs
against the walls. As we moved through the maze of hallways, the house
kept messing with us. We saw dark shapes moving in the shadows, things
with too many joints, things crawling along the ceiling watching us with
empty sockets.

We rounded a corner and saw the same split-chest creature standing at
the far end of the hall, its neck hanging limp against its chest.

“Don’t make a sound,” I whispered, putting my hand out.

We took slow steps backward toward an open doorway. But right before we
cleared the hall, a heavy wooden ceiling beam broke loose above us and
crashed to the floor.

The creature didn’t flinch at the sound. Instead, an icy, damp hand with
five long, jointless fingers slowly laid itself onto the back of my
neck.

I gasped, and the sound instantly alerted the thing at the end of the
hall. It whipped its neck around and let out a high-pitched screech.

“RUN!”

We sprinted through the dark hallways, taking random turns to break line
of sight. The thing was right behind us, its limbs clicking fast against
the floorboards. Jackson pulled an old heavy book off a table and threw
it behind him. It caught the creature’s legs, making it crash hard into
a door.

“Keep going! Faster!” David yelled.

We sprinted down another long hall until our lungs burned, finally
ducking into a dark alcove to hide. My heart was hammering against my
ribs, and my legs felt like lead.

I blinked, looked around the alcove, and my stomach dropped.

My friends were gone.

“David? Jackson? Taresa?” I whispered. Nothing.

I ran down the hallway trying to find them, but every door just opened
into another identical hallway. The wallpaper and light sconces were
looping over and over. I looked up, crawling upside-down on the ceiling
directly over my head was the creature, its mouth dripping dark liquid.

I sprinted through the next archway on pure instinct until I heard
shouting up ahead. I burst through a set of double doors and almost
plowed into the group.

“Where were you?!” Martha cried, tears running down her face. “You
vanished right behind us!”

“I got stuck in a loop, I couldn’t find the door,” I said, breathing
hard.

“At least you’re back,” Jackson said, stepping forward with a weak
smile. “We’re getting out of here together.”

Before Jackson could finish his sentence, a pale, multi-jointed arm shot
out from the shadows behind him. Its needle-toothed jaws clamped hard
onto Jackson’s shoulder with a nasty crunch.

Jackson screamed as the thing started pulling him back into the dark.

“NO!” I grabbed a red brick off a broken fireplace hearth and smashed it
into the creature’s face as hard as I could. The brick broke, but the
creature barely cared. It swung its heavy arm and threw me across the
room, knocking me right through the drywall.

We watched in horror as the creature bit down harder, tearing Jackson’s
arm completely off at the shoulder and swallowing it whole. As it ate
the arm, its body shuddered and grew two feet taller, its split chest
opening wider, its grin stretching almost around its entire head.

“THE EXIT! GO!” David screamed, grabbing Jackson by his shirt collar.

We dragged Jackson down the last stretch of the hall, pressing down on
his bleeding shoulder. At the end of the corridor, through tall glass
double doors, we could see the moonlight hitting the pickup truck
outside.

“We’re almost there!” David yelled, kicking the doors open.

We tumbled out onto the damp grass, cold night air hitting our lungs. We
thought we made it.

Then I turned around. Jackson wasn’t with us.

Through the glass, we saw the enlarged creature on all fours, its jaws
clamped tight around Jackson’s leg, dragging him backward across the
floor into the middle of the house.

“JACKSON!” I screamed, lunging back toward the door.

The heavy oak doors slammed shut and locked from the inside. Jackson’s
screams echoed from behind the wood, followed by sickening crunching
sounds, until everything went completely quiet.

We should have listened to him.

“We couldn’t save him… we couldn’t do anything…” Taresa sobbed, shaking
as we huddled in the back of David’s truck, staring at the closed doors.

“How do we explain this to his family?” David asked, his hands shaking
on the tailgate. “How do we explain any of this?”

“We don’t,” I said, looking at the blood on my hands. “If we tell them
what actually pulled Jackson into that house, they’ll lock us in an
asylum, and Jackson still isn’t coming back.”

“Or even worse,” Tony said, panic in his voice as he looked at us.
“They’ll think we did something to him. Think about it! Six of us go
into the woods, and five of us come out covered in blood talking about
wall-monsters? The cops won’t buy that for a second. They’ll lock us in
an interrogation room, charge us with murder, and say we dumped his body
somewhere.”

We sat in silence because he was totally right. We drove to the police
station later that night and told them a simplified story: that Jackson
had gotten separated from us in the woods along Kingston Road while we
were walking around.

The police opened a Missing Persons case. Search teams went through the
woods near the estate, but they found zero tracks and no body.

When Jackson’s grandparents first found out at the station that night,
they completely broke down. His grandmother looked pale as chalk,
sitting in a metal chair clutching Jackson’s winter jacket to her chest,
while his grandfather stood by the counter shaking, demanding the
officers send every deputy into those woods immediately. In the weeks
that followed, they became total shells of themselves. They kept
questioning all five of us over and over, pulling us aside after school
or calling our houses late at night, begging for any small detail we
might have missed. Facing them was worse than facing the police, but we
kept our story completely straight every single time: we were just
walking, he wandered off, and we couldn’t find him in the dark.

Our group fell apart after that night. Jackson was the glue holding us
together, he was the guy who always made jokes that got everyone
laughing no matter what. Without him, none of us could look at each
other without thinking about what happened. We stopped talking
completely.

Four months passed. Jackson was officially declared presumed dead, and
the case went cold.

That was when I decided we were going to finish it.

I called Tony, David, Martha, and Taresa on a group call and laid out
the plan. Every single one of them said yes immediately.

The next evening, we met at the gas station on the edge of town and
filled three heavy metal jerrycans with gasoline. We loaded them into
the truck bed and drove back down Kingston Road to the place we now
called The Estate of Ashes.

Nobody said a word. We walked past the broken iron gates, hauled the gas
cans up the porch steps, and poured fuel everywhere, on the porch,
through the busted bay windows, and along the foundation walls.

When the last can was empty, I tossed it aside and looked at Tony.

“Light it up,” I said.

Tony struck a match and dropped it onto the porch.

The gas caught with a massive WHOOSH, sending fire roaring up the front
of the mansion. Within minutes, the roof caved in. As it burned,
high-pitched shrieks started coming from inside the walls, horrible
gurgling screams of multiple things burning up in the fire.

I stood near the trees, watching the flames paint the sky red.

“Jackson has been avenged,” Martha whispered, wiping her face. “He
didn’t die for nothing.”

We got back into David’s truck, left Kingston Road behind, and drove
home.

An hour later, I was sitting on the living room rug with my family when
the local news interrupted the broadcast on our TV. The screen cut to a
reporter standing near a police line on the highway.

“We break into your regular programming with urgent news,” the anchor
said. “A massive fire has broken out at an abandoned estate off Kingston
Road. Fire crews are on scene, but officials report the building has
completely collapsed. Authorities are investigating the incident as
suspected arson…”

The broadcast showed footage of the inferno collapsing into cinders.

“Oh, my goodness,” my mom said, carrying a basket of laundry through the
room. “I wonder how a fire like that even starts way out in the woods.”

“Yeah,” I said quietly, leaning back against the couch. “I wonder.”
