I can still tell you what I paid for the coffee. Eighty-five cents, and
the girl behind the counter gave me two quarters and a nickel back
because I’d handed her a dollar forty. That may sound like a strange
thing to remember after twenty-five years, especially considering what
happened later that night. But I remember the coffee, and the way the
lid didn’t fit the cup, and having to wipe a brown ring off the seat of
my truck before I left the gas station. Other things are less clear. I
don’t remember whether the man on the road had a beard. I was sure he
did when I gave my first statement, and then I wasn’t. I remember his
yellow coat well enough. I remember his boots. I remember the sound the
gate made when it closed behind my truck. I suppose that’s how these
things work. Your mind hangs on to the small, useless details and lets
some of the important ones go.

It happened on a Thursday in February 2001, in a part of western
Pennsylvania where the roads get narrow once you leave the state
highway. I was forty-six and still doing appliance and pump repairs for
a living. Most of my work was around town, but I had a few customers
farther out who would call me because the nearest proper service company
charged a fortune to come into the hills. That afternoon, a man named
Harold Kessler called about a pump that had stopped working at his
mother’s place. Harold had called me before. His mother lived alone on a
farm out past Briar Gap, and she’d had trouble with the well all winter.
He said the house had no water and the forecast was calling for freezing
rain. I could have put it off until morning. Instead, I packed my tools,
put an extra belt and a pressure switch in the truck, and told my wife
I’d be back before supper. It wasn’t a promise I had any reason to make.
I just thought the job would be easy.

The repair took longer than it should have. The pump itself was fine. A
bad connection had heated up inside a junction box, and somebody had
tried fixing it with the wrong kind of wire. By the time I replaced the
damaged pieces and got the pressure back up, it was nearly seven.
Harold’s mother insisted I stay for a bowl of soup. I said I couldn’t,
but she put it in front of me anyway. She was eighty if she was a day,
sharp enough to know when somebody was lying about being hungry. She
paid me with a check written in careful blue letters and gave me two
biscuits in a napkin for the drive. Before I left, she told me not to
take the lower road through Briar Gap. There had been trouble there, she
said. I thought she meant ice. I asked if the road was flooded. She
shook her head and said, “Just go around by the highway.” I asked what
kind of trouble. She said the sheriff had been out that way twice in the
past week, and that ought to be enough for me. I should have asked more
questions. But she was tired, her water was working again, and I wanted
to go home.

The highway route would have added nearly forty minutes. I had driven
the lower road maybe a dozen times in daylight. It went through some
woods, crossed a small bridge and came out near an old timber yard.
There were no houses for about six miles, but I knew the turns. I had
good tires, a full tank and no reason to think a road I knew would
suddenly become dangerous because the sun had gone down. I stopped at a
gas station before the turnoff, bought the coffee I told you about, and
called home from the pay phone outside. My wife answered. I said I’d
been delayed but I was on my way. She asked whether I’d eaten. I told
her about the soup. Then I asked if our daughter had called. She was
away at school by then and usually rang on Thursday evenings. My wife
said not yet. We talked about nothing much for another minute. I
remember that because it was the last ordinary conversation I had before
I got home the next day.

A mile before the crossing, I came around a bend and saw orange lights
flashing in the trees. Then I saw the barricade. Two large ROAD CLOSED
signs stood across the lane, with striped boards and orange drums set
between them. A third sign had a black arrow pointing left and the word
DETOUR underneath. There were fresh tire marks leading onto a gravel
track I didn’t remember seeing before. The setup looked official enough
from twenty yards away. I pulled over and rolled down the window. A man
in a yellow raincoat came out from behind a county-style utility truck
parked on the shoulder. He carried a flashlight and had a white hard hat
pulled low over his face. He told me the bridge was out and that I
needed to take the gravel road around it. I asked what happened. He said
a truck had damaged the railing and the county wasn’t letting anybody
across until morning. His voice was rough, like he had a cold. He leaned
close enough for the rain to drip from his helmet onto my door. I
remember seeing a little round burn on one glove, right beside the
thumb.

I asked how far the detour went. He said only two miles, and there’d be
another sign at the bottom. I told him I’d driven this road for years
and had never noticed a connection through there. He said the route went
through private timber land and had only been opened for emergency
traffic. That sounded reasonable. The country around Briar Gap was full
of old logging roads, some of them blocked with chains, others so grown
over you’d barely see them from the road. I asked whether I could turn
around and go back to the highway. The man shrugged and said I could,
but the highway was icing over and a tractor-trailer had already gone
off near the junction. He pointed his flashlight at the detour and said,
“Your truck’ll be fine. Just keep right at the fork.” I believed him, or
at least I believed enough of what he said to turn left.

The gravel track was worse than I expected. Mud had frozen into ridges,
and the rain had started to turn the surface slick. I switched on my
high beams, then turned them off again because the trees threw the light
back at me. After about half a mile, I came to a fork. There wasn’t a
sign. I slowed down and looked through the windshield. The left track
went uphill between two rows of pines. The right one dipped toward a low
building, with a weak yellow light showing through a window. I
remembered the man saying to keep right, so I went that way. I told
myself the next sign would be past the building. Instead, the road
opened into a gravel yard surrounded by old machinery. There were two
rusted trailers, a stack of cut timber under plastic sheets and a long
metal shed. Beyond the shed I could see a wooden gate across the road.
It looked as though I had driven into a place where I shouldn’t be.

I stopped and looked at the map folded in my glove compartment. It was
an old county road map, the kind you got free at gas stations. The
logging track wasn’t marked. I was trying to work out which direction
the creek ran when a pickup truck came around the shed with its
headlights on. It stopped facing me. A man got out and walked toward my
window. He wasn’t wearing a uniform. He had a knit cap, a thick green
jacket and gloves covered in black grease. He asked whether I’d come
through the bridge detour. I said yes, and that I thought I had taken a
wrong turn. He told me everybody was getting confused because one of the
signs had blown down. He said the county had arranged for people to be
guided through the yard because the old timber road crossed a narrow
bridge behind the shed. I asked where the bridge was. He pointed past
the gate. Then he noticed the service name painted on my truck door and
asked if I fixed well pumps. I said I did. He said his uncle’s place had
been having trouble with one. It was a small thing, that question, but
it gave me an unpleasant feeling. He looked at the door as though he
hadn’t known what to say until he saw the lettering.

I told him I just wanted to get through. He said I could, but there was
a truck stuck on the far side and the gate had to stay closed until it
was moved. I asked how long that would take. He said ten minutes, maybe
fifteen. There was no place to turn around without backing close to a
bank, and the yard was covered in ice and deep ruts. I decided I could
wait. The man told me to pull forward beside the shed, out of the way. I
moved the truck ten or twelve feet. He walked past me toward the gate. I
saw him look toward the pickup, and somebody inside it lifted a hand. I
couldn’t see who. The man opened the gate, went through it to look along
the track, then closed it again behind him. I heard the metal latch drop
into place.

After ten minutes the man in the green jacket came back. He walked
straight to my window and said the stuck truck was nearly clear. He
asked whether I had a phone. I told him I didn’t carry a cell phone. In
2001 some people did, but I didn’t see the need. He said that was a
shame, because the office line had been dead since the storm. Then he
asked if I’d mind stepping inside for a minute. He said there was a
problem with the water pump in the office and they wanted to know if I
could take a quick look while I waited. He offered twenty dollars cash.
I told him I was done working for the night. He laughed and said he
understood, then added, “It’ll only take a second, Silas.” I hadn’t told
him my first name. It was printed in small letters below the service
number on the truck door, but he was standing on the other side when he
first walked up. I looked at the lettering to check. He followed my eyes
and said, “Saw it there. Didn’t mean to make you nervous.” That was when
I first thought about leaving, properly leaving, not just finding
another route.

I told him I had to call home and asked if there was a house nearby. He
said the nearest was five miles back, beyond the detour. I said I’d turn
around and find the highway myself. His expression changed. Not much.
His mouth tightened, and he looked toward the pickup again. He told me
the track behind me was too narrow and I might get stuck trying to climb
it in the rain. I said I’d take my chances. He stood beside the truck
while I put it in reverse. I checked the mirrors and began backing
slowly. The rear wheels slipped once, caught, then turned. I hadn’t
moved more than six feet when I heard a sharp pop. The truck leaned to
the right. I stopped, got out, and saw my rear tire losing air. A thick
piece of metal was sticking from the tread.

I opened the tool box to get the jack. That was when I noticed two more
strips of sharpened steel lying beside the rear wheel. They hadn’t been
there when I drove in. Somebody had placed them behind me while I sat in
the truck. I picked one up. It was bent in the middle, with the ends
sharpened to points. The metal was clean where it had been cut. I held
it under my coat so the man wouldn’t see it, although I suspect he knew
exactly what I’d found. My hands were starting to shake, and not from
the cold.

The man in the green jacket said his name was Vern. He told me Cal was
his nephew. Whether that was true, I never found out. They asked me to
put the truck in neutral and steer while they pushed it toward the shed.
I didn’t want to, but I didn’t see a better choice. The tires scraped
across the gravel, and the rim made a grinding noise each time it
turned. We got the truck inside through a wide sliding door. The shed
smelled of diesel fuel, damp wood and old sawdust. There was enough room
for two vehicles, though one side was filled with broken equipment and
rolls of chain. The concrete was spotted with dark stains. My truck
stopped beside a workbench. Cal shut the big door behind us, and the
sound it made told me the evening had gone badly wrong. It wasn’t
slammed. He pulled it closed slowly, then dropped a bar into the
brackets across it.

I asked why he was locking us in. Vern said they didn’t want the wind
blowing the door open. He told me to sit at the bench while they found a
jack. I said mine was in the toolbox. He told me not to bother. Cal went
around the far side of the truck, out of sight. I heard metal rattling,
then the little click of my passenger door opening. I turned and saw him
reaching into the cab. I asked what he was doing. He said he was looking
for the jack handle. I told him it was behind the seat. He didn’t
answer. Instead, he opened the glove compartment and took out my wallet.
It was sitting in the small tray where I kept it while driving. I
stepped toward him. Vern caught my arm and pushed me against the
workbench. He didn’t hit me. He just held me there with one hand and
said, quietly, “Don’t make this difficult.” Cal emptied my wallet onto
the bench. There was eighty-three dollars, a gas card, my driver’s
license and a picture of my daughter tucked into the clear pocket. He
took the cash and the card. He left the photograph where it was.

The office door opened a few inches and a woman appeared in the gap. She
was maybe thirty-five, with short brown hair and a red sweater pulled
over a white shirt. Her left eye was swollen. Cal pushed the door shut
with his shoulder before she could speak. She hit it from the other side
and shouted that she’d been there since the afternoon. Vern went over
and said something too low for me to hear. When he came back, he told
Cal to take the woman’s keys and get the red car loaded. Cal went to the
far end of the shed and began pulling a tarp off a low trailer. I
understood then why the car in the yard had been covered. They weren’t
just taking cash. They were taking vehicles as well.

A little after nine, a third person arrived. I heard an engine outside,
then the gate rattling. Vern went to the door and called through it. A
man answered. I couldn’t make out the words. The bar lifted, and a
shorter man came in wearing a yellow raincoat with the hood down. He had
the same white hard hat under his arm that I’d seen at the roadblock.
His hair was thin and pale. He looked at me and said, “You went the
wrong way after the fork.” I realized he was the fellow who’d sent me
down the track in the first place. He saw the flat tire and asked who
had done it. Cal said it didn’t matter. The man in the yellow coat
looked angry. He told Vern they needed to get the vehicles out before
the weather got worse, and there was no time for another mess.

Vern called him Tommy. Tommy took off his gloves and laid them on the
bench. One had a little burn beside the thumb. He saw me looking and
folded the gloves together. Then he asked what I did for a living. Vern
told him. Tommy said, “A repairman. That’s unfortunate.” Cal asked why.
Tommy nodded toward the name on my truck. He said a marked service
vehicle would bring questions if it disappeared. Then he looked at me
and asked whether anybody knew which route I’d taken. I told him my wife
expected me home. He asked if I’d told her I was using the lower road. I
said yes. That was a lie. I’d only told her I was leaving the gas
station. But I wanted him to think somebody might come looking. Tommy
considered that for a moment. He told Vern to keep me somewhere out of
sight until they decided what to do. Then he went outside again.

Vern opened the office door. The woman was standing beside a desk,
holding one hand against her ribs. There was another person in the room,
a man older than me, sitting on the floor with his back against a filing
cabinet. His trousers were wet from the knees down. I recognized him at
once. He had been at the gas station when I bought the coffee, standing
beside the refrigerator looking at sandwiches. He wore a red cap with a
white patch on the front. I remembered him because he’d made a joke
about the price of the sandwiches. Now the cap was gone. His face was
gray. When he tried to stand, Vern told him to stay where he was. Cal
took my arm, pushed me inside and shut the door. A key turned in the
lock.

The room was about ten feet square. There was a small desk, a filing
cabinet, two chairs and a window with plywood nailed across the outside.
The only light came from a bulb hanging in the ceiling. A telephone sat
on the desk with its cord cut close to the wall. There were old invoices
in a tray and a calendar from 1993 hanging crooked beside the door. The
woman looked at me and asked whether they’d taken my truck too. I said I
wasn’t sure. She told me her name was Denise. The older man was Walter.
He’d been brought in around six, she said, after following the same
detour. Denise had been there since early afternoon. She was driving to
her sister’s house when the men stopped her. She’d tried to drive away,
and Cal had smashed her window with something metal. He dragged her out
and shoved her onto the ground. The bruise around her eye had come from
hitting the side of her car.

I asked Walter what he’d seen on the way in. He said the man in the
yellow coat had waved him past the ROAD CLOSED signs. Then a pickup had
followed him down the track. When Walter reached the yard, the pickup
blocked the way back and two men ordered him out. He tried to run toward
the trees, but his ankle gave way on the ice. He thought he’d twisted it
badly. He told me Cal had kicked him once after he fell. Denise looked
down at her hands while he spoke. She didn’t seem surprised. I asked if
either of them had seen any other people. Denise said she’d heard
somebody crying in the shed earlier, before Walter arrived. She thought
it was a young man, though she hadn’t seen him. When she asked Vern
about it, he told her the place was full of noises and she should keep
quiet.

Around ten, Tommy came into the room. He brought a half-empty jug of
water and three paper cups. He set them on the desk and told us to
drink. Walter asked if he could see a doctor. Tommy looked at his ankle
and said it wasn’t broken. Walter asked how he knew. Tommy said he knew
enough. Denise asked whether they were going to let us leave. Tommy told
her that depended on whether we could keep our mouths shut. She said
they already had her car and purse. What else did they want? Tommy
didn’t answer. He looked at me instead and asked if I could get an old
diesel generator running. I said maybe, depending on what was wrong with
it. He said the one in the yard had stopped and they needed it to load
equipment. I asked where we’d go afterward. He said, “Let’s see if you
can fix it first.” It wasn’t much of an offer, but it was the only
opening I’d seen.

Tommy led me toward a small generator on wheels beside the timber stack.
It was an old diesel machine, covered in rust and grease, with a metal
fuel tank mounted above the engine. He said they’d tried starting it and
the battery was dead. I looked at the cables. The terminals were loose
and green with corrosion. I could have fixed that in ten minutes, maybe
less. But I wasn’t thinking about the generator. I was looking past it,
toward a narrow lane leading between the timber stack and the tree line.
There was a faint path in the snow, and beyond it I could see a creek
shining under the security light. I didn’t know where the path went.
Still, it was the first place I’d seen that wasn’t blocked by a man or a
locked gate.

Tommy handed me a wrench. I bent over the engine and started loosening
the battery cable. He stood beside me, smoking. I asked how long they’d
been using the yard. He said his family used to cut timber here. I asked
whether the county knew they were sending drivers through private land.
He said the county didn’t need to know everything. I told him the
roadblock looked official. He laughed. Then he said, “Most people do
what you tell them if you’re wearing the right coat.” He seemed proud of
that. I asked whether the bridge was really damaged. He looked at me,
then smiled in a way that made me wish I hadn’t asked. “Bridge is fine,”
he said. “Has been all night.” I went back to the battery without
answering.

While I worked, Cal came over from the van and told Tommy they needed to
get moving. He said Vern was worried somebody might check the roadblock.
Tommy told him not to worry. Cal looked toward the van and asked what
they were going to do with “the one in the back.” Tommy told him to keep
his voice down. Cal swore and walked away. I felt cold all over. I kept
my head down, turning the wrench slowly as though the connection was
stubborn. Tommy smoked his cigarette and watched me. I could hear the
creek running over stones a few yards away. I wondered who was by the
water and whether they were alive.

When he turned the key, the engine started. It rattled and smoked, then
settled into a rough steady idle. Tommy grinned and slapped the side of
the fuel tank. He said I’d earned my twenty dollars. I didn’t answer. He
climbed down and walked toward the van to tell Cal. For a few seconds I
was alone beside the generator. I looked toward the lane by the creek. I
could see the far end of the timber stack, then a dark gap between two
trees. It couldn’t have been more than forty yards. I could have run.
But Denise and Walter were locked in the office, and I had no idea
whether the men would hurt them if I disappeared. I stayed where I was.

Tommy came back with Vern. Vern wanted to know whether I could fix the
flat tire on my truck before morning. I said yes, but I’d need a spare
and somewhere level. He told me the spare was probably no good. I said
I’d have a look. Then he did something that changed the way I saw him.
He asked whether I thought my wife would call the police if I didn’t
come home. He said it almost casually, but he watched my face while I
answered. I told him she would. He asked what time. I said she’d already
be worried. Vern thought about that, then told Tommy they couldn’t keep
us there much longer. Tommy said they still had work to do. Vern said
the three of us had seen too much. They walked a few steps away,
arguing. I heard Tommy say they could leave us at the old quarry road,
tied up, and somebody would find us in the morning. Vern said somebody
might find us before then. Neither man sounded concerned about whether
we’d survive the cold.

They took me back toward the shed. As we passed the white van, I saw a
dark shape in the rear through the open doors. At first I thought it was
a rolled carpet. Then I saw a boot sticking out from under a blanket.
One leg was bent at an angle that didn’t look natural. I stopped without
meaning to. Tommy noticed and stepped between me and the van. He told me
to keep moving. I asked who was in there. He said nobody. I looked at
the boot again. It was a black work boot with a white stripe along the
sole. Tommy grabbed my collar and pushed me toward the office. I
stumbled, caught myself on the door frame and said nothing more. It was
the first time I saw genuine fear on his face, and I knew it wasn’t fear
of me. He was afraid I’d seen something he couldn’t explain away.

Vern put me back in the office with Denise and Walter and locked the
door again. Denise asked what was happening outside. I told her they
were taking the cars and that I had seen somebody lying in the back of a
van. Walter stared at the floor. I was looking around for anything we
could use when I noticed a desk drawer hanging crooked on its runners.
Inside were a few rusted screws and the broken shaft of a flat-head
screwdriver. The handle was gone, but the tip was still there. I slipped
it into my pocket and examined the doorframe. The lock was set in the
door knob, and there was no way to reach the screws from inside. But the
door itself was cheap. The wood on our side of the frame had split right
beside the metal plate that held the latch. I pressed a finger into the
split. It moved slightly. I asked Denise if she’d noticed it. She said
she’d tried pushing the door earlier and nothing happened. I told her we
might be able to loosen the metal plate if we dug out enough of the soft
wood around it. Walter said they’d hear us. He was right. We needed
noise outside. The generator was running now, and every so often the men
started an engine or dragged something over the concrete. I began
picking at the split wood. Every time I heard footsteps outside, I
stopped and waited. I thought we might have a chance when the next truck
moved.

It took nearly twenty minutes to break enough wood away for the metal
plate to move. I pushed the screwdriver tip into the crack beside it and
pulled. One screw came loose with a little cracking sound. The other
held. I leaned harder, and the wood split. The plate shifted just enough
that the door moved when I pressed my shoulder against it. Denise looked
at me with her eyes wide. I told her not yet. I didn’t know where the
men were. We listened. The truck outside stopped, and somebody swore.
The generator kept rattling. I asked Walter whether he could walk. He
said he could try. He put one hand on the desk and struggled to his
feet. He couldn’t put much weight on his right foot. Denise went to his
side.

We waited until Vern and Cal started arguing loudly over where to park
the loaded trailer. Tommy shouted at them from the yard, and for a
moment all three voices were coming from the gate. Then I pushed the
door. The strike plate gave way, and the door opened about a foot. I
caught it before it hit the cabinet outside. The shed was empty except
for my truck. The sliding door stood partly open, with yellow light
spilling onto the gravel. Their voices still came from the gate. I told
Denise to help Walter stay low. We stepped out and moved behind my
truck. The side of it was cold and wet under my hand. My tool bag lay
open on the workbench, with half the tools missing. The keys weren’t in
the ignition. I wasn’t going to waste time looking for them.

We slipped through the gap in the sliding door. The night air hit me
hard enough to make me cough. The freezing rain had turned to sleet,
little pieces of ice that stung my face. The generator was between us
and the men, and its noise covered our footsteps for a few seconds. I
pointed toward the creek path. Denise nodded. We made it past the timber
stack, with Walter leaning on both of us. Then he stumbled. His bad foot
went into a rut, and he cried out before he could stop himself. We
froze. The voices near the gate stopped. I heard Vern say, “What was
that?” Tommy answered from somewhere behind the shed. Denise gripped
Walter’s arm and pulled him forward. I whispered that we had to move.

We moved into the trees. There wasn’t a proper path, only a strip of
ground where weeds had been flattened by animals or people. The creek
ran beside us, dark and fast. I thought going upstream would bring us
closer to the main road. The lower road crossed that creek, though I had
no idea how far the crossing was from us. The trouble was I didn’t know
how far it was or how steep the bank would get. Walter was slowing us
down. Every few steps he made a sound through his teeth. I told him to
hold on a little longer. He said he was trying. Behind us, flashlights
moved between the trees. Someone shouted that they could see our tracks.
The sleet was coming down hard enough to leave a thin white layer on the
ground. We were leaving dark marks with every step.

We reached a place where the creek widened into a pool and the bank rose
steeply ahead. There was a fallen tree across the water, its trunk slick
with ice. The way along our side was blocked by a wall of rock. We could
either climb the bank or cross the trunk. Walter couldn’t climb, and
there was no way he could walk along that tree. I looked for another
path and saw a narrow opening under the rock, where water had cut
through a low shelf. It wasn’t big enough to crawl through without
getting wet. I told Denise to take Walter behind the rock and stay there
while I looked for a way around. She grabbed my sleeve. She didn’t want
me to leave. I said I wouldn’t go far.

I walked about thirty yards downstream, keeping close to the bank. There
was a small gravel bar where the water ran shallow over stones. I
thought we might be able to cross there, though the creek would come
nearly to our knees. On the other side the ground rose toward a line of
pine trees. I could see no lights. The road had to be beyond them,
somewhere. I turned back to get the others. That’s when I saw a
flashlight moving along the path above the rock. Tommy was coming toward
us from the yard, following our tracks. He was alone. I crouched behind
a tree and waited for him to pass. He stopped about ten feet from me and
listened. Then he called out, “Silas, I know you’re here.” I could see
the white hard hat hanging from his belt. He took something from his
pocket. It was a handgun, small and dark. He held it low beside his leg.

I threw the sharpened strip of steel I’d kept from the yard. I hadn’t
meant to use it as a weapon. It was in my coat pocket because I’d picked
it up beside the tire. I threw it toward the creek, away from where I
was standing. It struck a rock with a sharp ring. Tommy turned the
flashlight toward the sound. I stepped sideways into the trees and ran.
He shouted and came after me. I heard a sharp crack behind me, then
something struck a tree to my left. I thought he’d fired, but I never
saw a flash, and the creek was loud enough to hide plenty of sounds. I
told the police exactly that. I still don’t know whether he fired at me.

I ran until I slipped on the bank and went down hard on one knee. My
trousers tore, and cold water soaked through. I got up and kept moving.
I could hear Tommy crashing through the brush behind me. He was younger
than I was, but he didn’t know the ground any better. I reached the
gravel bar and stepped into the creek. The water went over my boots
immediately. I lost my footing and fell against a rock, catching myself
with one hand. My fingers went numb. I pushed across, half walking, half
crawling, and dragged myself onto the other bank. Tommy reached the
water behind me. I heard him swear. I didn’t look back. I climbed the
slope through the pines, pulling myself up by roots and low branches. At
the top I could see the white line of the main road through the trees,
maybe a hundred yards away.

I got to the road and nearly fell in front of a pickup. Its headlights
came around the bend, and I stumbled into the lane, waving both arms.
The driver hit the brakes. The truck slid sideways, stopped, then the
door opened. A heavyset man in a gray cap got out, swearing at me for
standing in the road. I tried explaining, but I was coughing and
couldn’t get enough breath. I told him there were people being held in a
timber yard, that men with guns were chasing us. He looked past me
toward the woods. Then he told me to get in. I asked if he had a phone.
He said he had one in the truck, but there was no signal on that
stretch. He drove toward the highway, faster than I liked on the icy
road. I kept looking through the rear window, expecting to see
headlights behind us.

The driver was named Ray Mercer. He worked nights at a paper mill and
had been coming home from a late shift. He didn’t ask many questions
after the first minute. He saw the blood on my hand and the mud on my
clothes, and I suppose that was enough. At the highway we found a pay
phone beside a closed diner. Ray stopped under the light while I called
the police. I gave them the location as best I could, naming the

Ray said going back without help would get somebody killed. He was
right, but I hated hearing it.

The first deputy arrived about fifteen minutes later. He was young, with
a pale face and rain dripping from his hat. He took my name and asked me
to tell him everything from the beginning. I spoke too fast. I kept
jumping from the roadblock to the shed, then back to the woman in the
office. He told me to slow down. I showed him the cuts on my hand and
the spot on my coat where Vern had grabbed me. He asked whether I’d been
drinking. I said no. He looked at Ray, who shook his head. Then the
deputy called for more units and told me to get in the back of his
cruiser so we could show them the entrance. I didn’t want to go anywhere
near that place again. But Denise and Walter were still out there, and I
was the only one who knew where I’d left them.

We drove toward Briar Gap behind two other patrol cars. The roadblock
was gone. The orange drums had been pulled into the ditch, and the ROAD
CLOSED signs lay face down in the weeds. The bridge beyond them was
open. The deputy stopped, shone his flashlight along the pavement and
said there was no damage to the railing. I pointed out the gravel track.
He called it in, and we turned down. The road was slick with sleet. The
patrol car bumped through the ruts, and the deputy kept asking where the
fork was. I showed him. At the yard, the gate was open. There were fresh
tire marks in the gravel, but the white van and the pickup were gone. My
truck was still in the shed, along with the red car on its trailer. The
generator had stopped running. The place was quiet except for rain
dripping from the roof.

They found Denise first. She was under the rock shelf where I’d left
her, soaked and shaking, holding Walter’s hand. Walter was lying on his
side with his injured ankle tucked beneath him. He’d tried to follow her
into the opening and couldn’t get far. She’d stayed rather than leave
him. By the time the deputies reached them, his lips had turned blue and
he could barely speak. They carried him out on a stretcher. Denise
walked between two officers, crying and trying to explain that she’d
heard a man calling our names but hadn’t answered. When she saw me, she
stopped. She looked at me for a long second, then asked whether the men
had been caught. I said I didn’t know. She nodded, as though she’d
expected that answer.

The search crews found another person farther down the creek the next
morning. It was a young man named Daniel Reece, twenty-two years old,
who worked at a hardware store in the next county. He had gone missing
on Tuesday evening, two nights before I took the detour. He was lying
beneath an old tarp in a shallow ditch downstream from the yard. At
first the police thought he might have fallen on the rocks while trying
to escape. The medical examination showed injuries that didn’t fit a
simple fall. His family had been searching for him since Wednesday
morning. I didn’t know any of this when I was in the shed. Investigators
later told me they had found tracks from the white van near the ditch
and marks showing that somebody had dragged a heavy object down from the
road. The boot I saw in the van had the same white stripe as Daniel’s,
though I never saw enough of him to be certain it was Daniel that night.
The newspapers later reported that he had been assaulted before he died.
I never learned exactly what happened to him. I’ve heard stories, but I
don’t repeat them because I wasn’t there.

The police arrested Vern three days later. He was staying at a motel
outside Altoona under another name. Cal was picked up two weeks
afterward when he tried selling a stolen pickup. Tommy disappeared for a
while. They found him later that spring in West Virginia, working at a
garage. He’d been using a false name. The yellow county-style coat
turned out to be a surplus jacket bought at an auction. The signs had
been taken from a storage lot. None of the three men worked for the
highway department. The utility truck at the roadblock had an old
municipal logo on the door, painted over badly enough that you could
still make out the shape when you looked closely. Tommy had spent years
driving timber roads in that area. He knew which tracks were still
passable and which ones ended in old yards. That was how he chose the
place.

The investigators believed the men had been running the detour for
several nights, maybe longer. They would close the lower road after
dark, steer drivers toward the timber yard, take cash and valuables, and
keep the people there long enough to move their vehicles. Most drivers
probably turned around before reaching the roadblock. Others, like me,
saw a yellow coat and orange signs and assumed somebody official was in
charge. The police later found stolen plates and paperwork for several
cars in a locked trailer. They also recovered a bag of wallets, watches
and keys. Some of the owners had reported their vehicles missing. Others
hadn’t, because they’d been threatened and left miles away without a way
to call anybody. When Daniel tried to get away, the men panicked and
began changing their plans. By the night I arrived, they were arguing
about whether to keep taking people or shut the whole thing down.

For years afterward, people asked why I didn’t turn around when I first
saw the road closed. Some asked why I followed a stranger onto a logging
track, and others wondered why I didn’t fight back when they took my
wallet. Those are fair questions. I asked them myself. The easy answer
is that I should have known better. The harder answer is that each
choice seemed small at the time. Turn onto a gravel road instead of
driving forty minutes back to the highway. Wait ten minutes for a gate
to open. Let two men help push a truck with a flat tire. By the time I
understood that the whole thing had been arranged, I was already
standing inside a locked shed. You don’t always get one big moment when
the danger becomes obvious. Sometimes it comes in little pieces, and you
keep telling yourself the next piece will make sense.

I still drive through those hills now and then. The old timber yard was
cleared years ago, and the road through Briar Gap has been widened in
places. There’s a new guardrail at the bridge. The creek looks the same.
If you know where to look, you can see the cut in the trees where the
logging track used to go. It’s almost grown over now. I passed it last
autumn, on my way back from visiting a friend, and I slowed without
meaning to. There was a temporary sign near the shoulder because crews
were repairing a drainage pipe. A man in a bright yellow coat stood
beside it, waving traffic through one lane at a time. He looked like any
other road worker. There was a truck with the county name on the door,
orange cones, all the things you’d expect.

I stopped when he raised his hand. He walked toward my window and leaned
down to tell me the wait would be a few minutes. I could see his face
clearly. Young fellow, maybe thirty, with rain in his eyebrows. He
smiled and said they were nearly finished. I asked if I could turn
around. He looked surprised and said of course, if I wanted to. There
was a wide place just behind me. He even stepped back to make room. I
thanked him and turned the truck around. I could see him in my mirror,
standing by the cones with his hands on his hips, probably wondering
what was wrong with me.

I don’t think about those men every day anymore. Most weeks I don’t
think about them at all. But I remember that worker’s surprise when I
asked to leave, and I remember how relieved I felt when he said I could.
That was what came back to me later, more than the gun or the shed. A
stranger on a dark road told me I was free to turn around, and I almost
thanked him twice for it. Before Briar Gap, I wouldn’t have understood
why that mattered so much.
