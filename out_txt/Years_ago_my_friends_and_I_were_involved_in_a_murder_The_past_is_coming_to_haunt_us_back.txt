I remember that night.

We were deep in the woods, past the trails, far enough that no one could
hear the music or the shouting. It was supposed to be just a party. A
few drinks, a bonfire, maybe some dares. The kind of night you forget by
Monday.

There were 13 of us who organized it—me, Cassie, Helena, Lucy, Jordan,
Tom, Carly, Susan, Christine, Adams, Patrick, Natalie, and Andy.
Everyone else was just along for the ride. About twenty people, maybe
more. Most didn’t know each other. They just knew someone who knew
someone else, and that was enough to get invited.

Natalie came with Lucy. I didn’t know much about her. She was quiet at
first, kind of like she was waiting for a reason not to smile. She kept
drinking, though. Shot after shot. Cassie made it a challenge. We
started filming and chanting. Patrick gave her another bottle.

She said she didn’t feel well.

She said her chest hurt.

No one paid attention.

By the time we noticed something was wrong, it was too late. She wasn’t
moving. She wasn’t breathing. She was cold.

Panic was loud. Everyone talked at once. Someone cried. Someone threw
up. I just stood there, waiting for someone to take control. But no one
did.

A random partygoer was the first to say what we were all thinking but
didn’t want to say.

“If we tell anyone, we’re finished.”

So we didn’t.

We made a plan and told everyone to keep quiet. Natalie “wandered off.”
That was the story. No one questioned it. Everyone was scared.

For three days, it worked.

Until the search started.

But she was never found. Not to this day.

Then the investigation began. They pulled students from class and
questioned them one by one. The truth never came out. We never broke our
silence. For ten years, we lived in fear.

After ten years, what happened still stays with us—those alive and those
who aren’t.

Most days, I manage to forget. Not completely. Not really. But enough to
get by. Enough to seem like I sleep at night and don’t flinch at every
knock.

I live alone. Small apartment, decent view. I work in tech now—mostly
remote, quiet team, low expectations. Exactly what I wanted. I don’t go
out much. I don’t keep in touch with anyone from back then. Sometimes I
see their names online—weddings, promotions, babies. I don’t click.

There’s a way to survive: stay quiet, keep your head down, don’t look
back. That’s what we agreed on.

For a while, it worked.

Until a headline changed everything.

“Cold Case Reopened: 2014 Missing School Valedictorian Back Under
Review.”

I stared at the screen longer than I should’ve. Same photo of her. Same
smile. Same story, just brought back into the light.

They never found out it was us. Not the first time. But now?

Now they were looking again.

Two days later, a detective showed up at my door.

He was polite. Friendly, even. Just tying up loose ends, he said.
Routine follow-up. Asked where I was the night she went missing. If I
remembered anything unusual. If I knew her.

I said I didn’t.

He nodded, wrote something down, and thanked me.

He didn’t say it, but I could tell he knew I was lying.

Then came the waiting. Every sound in the hallway felt louder. Every
knock on a neighbor’s door made me freeze. I kept expecting them to come
back. To call. To ask more questions. But they didn’t.

For now, I was clear.

A few hours later, my phone buzzed. Unknown number.

I hesitated, but answered.

“Greg,” Tom said. “We need to talk.”

No small talk. His voice was tight, like he was trying to stay calm but
wasn’t.

We agreed to meet at the usual spot—the diner on the edge of town. Low
lights, old vinyl booths, a place where no one asks questions. It was
quiet. Too quiet.

I walked in first. The bell jingled as I came in. The diner smelled like
stale coffee and cheap syrup. Tom was already there, sitting in the
corner booth, hoodie up. I did the same, hiding my face.

I slid into the seat across from him. For a moment, neither of us spoke.
We just looked at the table.

Tom shifted, finally looking at me. “They’re still digging.”

“Don’t worry, they tried to get me to talk but failed,” I said.

“They came to my place. Like they did to you.” He rubbed his face,
tired. “Said they were ‘following up on old leads.’ But I don’t believe
it.”

I nodded. I’d been waiting for this. We all had. The past was catching
up.

“I thought it was over,” he said. “I thought they forgot.”

“They didn’t.”

We both knew that. It was only a matter of time.

Tom leaned in, lowering his voice. “What if someone talks? What if they
remember something?”

The thought made my stomach turn, but I didn’t show it.

“We stay quiet,” I said. The words came easier than I thought. “That’s
all we can do.”

“What about Lucy? She’s the type to change her mind.”

“Don’t worry, it won’t come out.”

Turns out, I was right.

But not how I expected.

A week later, Lucy was dead.

After years of carrying the weight, she finally gave up.

She jumped from the parking garage behind her apartment. No note, no
message. Just the news. Just silence.

Her obituary was short and clean—the kind you see for someone with a
bright future and grieving family. It didn’t mention Natalie or the
investigation. Just said she “passed unexpectedly” and was “deeply
missed.”

But we all knew.

Grief messes with guilt. It blurs regret and responsibility. Lucy wasn’t
just one of us anymore—she was the first to go in years. The first crack
in the wall we’d spent a decade pretending was solid.

Her funeral was quiet. Small chapel. Closed casket. Everyone looked
exhausted, like they hadn’t slept in weeks. The silence between us was
thick—like we all knew the same thing but refused to say it.

I hadn’t seen most of them in years.

Cassie showed up in black, her face unreadable. Patrick paced near the
doors, unsure whether to stay or leave. Tom kept his head down, silent.
The other eight weren’t there.

After the service, four of us gathered outside. The original group,
minus nine.

We stood in a loose circle, avoiding eye contact like strangers sharing
a secret.

Cassie broke the silence.

“She couldn’t handle it,” she said flatly. Not grieving—just stating
facts.

Patrick gave her a look. “Really? Here?”

“She cracked,” Cassie said. “First the cops, then this. Not a
coincidence.”

“She was our friend,” Patrick said quietly.

“She was a liability,” Cassie shot back. “Now they’ll look at all of us
again. You think the police won’t connect the dots?”

“She didn’t leave a note,” Tom whispered.

“She didn’t have to,” Cassie snapped. “They’ll look at us. At everything
we buried.”

No one argued.

Tom shifted beside me, like he wanted to say something—maybe defend
Lucy, maybe confess—but he stayed silent.

I finally asked, “What do we do?”

Cassie looked each of us over, cold and calculating.

“We do what we’ve always done.”

We left the chapel slowly, like wreckage drifting apart.

No goodbyes. No hugs. Just that stiff exit where everyone waits for
someone else to break the silence. Cassie disappeared down the steps
without looking back.

I started toward the street, but Tom touched my arm.

“Hey,” he said quietly, glancing around. “You and Patrick. Come here.”

Patrick nodded toward the side of the building.

I hesitated, then followed.

We ended up at the chapel’s corner, hidden from the main path. Concrete,
overgrown ivy, a rusted maintenance door. Private enough.

Tom looked over his shoulder, then lowered his voice.

“Something’s off.”

“How?”

“Lucy,” Tom said. “She didn’t just jump. I talked to her a couple months
ago—she wasn’t great, but she wasn’t… there. She was scared, said
someone was watching her. Said her door was unlocked when she swore she
locked it. That someone followed her car.”

“You think someone was messing with her?” I asked.

“I think someone wanted her quiet,” Tom said. “And maybe she wasn’t.”

Patrick tensed. “You’re saying it’s happening again? That she talked?”

“I don’t know. But if she did… we’re screwed.”

We stood in silence. Traffic hummed in the distance, a muffled cry
drifted from the chapel.

Tom looked at us. “We need to find out if someone’s coming for the rest
of us.”

“What if there isn’t?”

A voice behind us.

We turned. Cassie stood a few steps away, arms crossed, expression
unreadable.

“What if this is just mass hysteria?” she said, stepping closer. “We’re
seeing shadows because we expect them. Lucy struggled for years. Doesn’t
mean someone made her do it.”

Tom’s jaw tightened. “She was scared. Said someone followed her.”

“She said she saw Natalie in the mirror once,” Cassie said flatly. “Not
saying it wasn’t real to her. But that doesn’t mean it was real.”

Tom exhaled slowly. “You think this is all in our heads.”

“I think grief makes people paranoid,” Cassie said. “Guilt makes it
worse. We’ve carried this for ten years—we were bound to snap.”

“Some of us already did,” I muttered before stopping myself.

Cassie’s eyes flicked to me. “That’s not fair.”

“It’s true,” Patrick said. “Lucy isn’t the first.”

Silence.

Just wind brushing dead leaves along the chapel wall.

Tom shifted. “Maybe we should’ve seen this coming. We thought silence
was enough.”

Cassie narrowed her eyes. “It was enough. We did what we had to do.”

There was something sharp in her tone, like her words carried more
weight than she meant.

Patrick looked at her. “You think this is just about Lucy?”

Cassie didn’t answer. Her lips pressed tight.

“We kept the lid shut,” she said. “Even when people started asking.”

She didn’t need to say who. The air filled in the blanks.

Names we don’t say anymore.

People at the party. People who got too close. Who got curious. Who had
theories. Who needed to tell someone.

People who… didn’t.

“I remember what it took to get us here,” Cassie said quietly. “You
think any of us want to go through that again?”

A cold feeling crawled down my back. Not fear. Something older.

“We’re not the same kids we were,” Tom said. “We can’t keep—”

“We can,” Cassie snapped. “We will.”

Her voice dropped low.

“We’ve gone too far to stop now.”

She turned and walked away, footsteps sharp on concrete.

None of us moved.

Tom finally spoke. “I keep thinking about that night. Not just her.
Everything after.”

He looked at me, something unspoken hanging between us.

“You remember what we did, right?”

Tom leaned back against the wall, eyes on the gray sky. “You know what
still gets me? It wasn’t just what happened. It was how fast we got used
to not talking about it.”

I stayed quiet, watching the ground.

Patrick hadn’t come back yet. Just Tom and me, tucked behind the chapel,
talking like old friends at a wake. Except it wasn’t friendship—it was
fear dressed as memory.

Tom ran a hand down his face.

“What happened that night…” He hesitated. “It can’t come out. And it
shouldn’t.”

His eyes met mine.

“Not just because of Natalie. But because of everything after. What we
said. What we saw. What we did.”

That last word hung between us like a noose.

He didn’t say more. Didn’t need to.

I didn’t look at him.

Because I remembered enough to know he was right.

And maybe—just maybe—I remembered more than I wanted to admit.

I left right after the funeral. Didn’t say a word to anyone. We all knew
better than to hang around — twelve of us together would look bad. Too
obvious. Too connected.

Cassie was gone before the service ended. Tom and Jordan went off in
different directions like they hadn’t just stood over the same casket.
Carly didn’t even glance back when Helena pulled her toward the car.
Andy… I don’t know where he disappeared to.

We didn’t plan it, but somehow we all just knew — go home. Don’t text.
Don’t check in. Pretend it’s over.

I got home around six. Took off my shoes and sat on the couch like I was
waiting for someone to knock.

No one did.

Still hasn’t.

The apartment was quiet. I didn’t turn on any lights. I just sat there
with my hands folded, staring at the floor, thinking the same thing I
always do when I let myself:

We shouldn’t have moved her.

We should’ve called for help when she collapsed. We should’ve done
anything but what we did.

That night plays over and over in my head.

Natalie was already gone. Too much, too fast, right in front of
everyone. People were freaking out, yelling, stumbling over each other
trying to figure out if it was real.

We pulled away from the crowd — the twelve of us. Into the woods.
Somewhere darker. Quieter. I don’t remember who said it first, but
someone panicked. Said if she was found at the fire, in the middle of
the party, we’d all be blamed.

Cassie laid it out. Said we didn’t need to hide her — just unlink
ourselves. Make it look like she left early, wandered off. Died alone.
Drunk and stupid. Her mistake, not ours.

It sounded awful when she said it. Still does.

But we listened.

We were scared.

Later that night, after most of the crowd left — once the crying stopped
and the phone lights faded into the trees — Tom and I went back.

We wore gloves. Someone had a box in their trunk. Patrick, maybe Adams.
I don’t remember. It’s all a blur.

She wasn’t heavy.

That’s what stuck with me. I thought it would feel worse, like lifting a
person. But it didn’t. She felt empty. Like the life was already gone.

We moved her deeper into the woods, off the trail. Changed her hoodie.
Took anything tying her to us. Wiped the bottle. Deleted the photos from
my phone. Everyone’s phones. Cassie double-checked.

Then we left her there.

Next morning, we went on with our day.

I try not to think about it much, but tonight I can’t help it.

I keep replaying how calm we were once it was done. Like we believed the
worst was over. Like it wouldn’t follow us home.

But it did.

It followed Lucy all the way to her apartment rooftop.

And now it’s following the rest of us.

I don’t know how long before someone digs up the truth. But if they do…
they’ll find more than just what we buried.

I think about that night more than I admit.

Ten years ago. Forest party. Middle of nowhere. End-of-semester blowout.
Twenty-something students drinking like it was their job.

We thought we were untouchable.

Natalie was already five shots deep before we even got to the last
round. Someone handed her a bottle — Tom or maybe Jordan — and we all
started chanting like it was funny. Like a game. She smiled. Tried to
keep up.

She didn’t.

I remember her stumbling. Saying her chest hurt. Then collapsing.

At first, no one moved. We thought she was faking. It wasn’t until Lucy
screamed that we realized something was wrong. Really wrong.

By then… she wasn’t breathing.

There were thirty people there, maybe more. Some panicked, cried. Some
ran. Some filmed. None stopped it. Neither did we.

I could still hear yelling back at the fire — someone crying, someone
throwing up. Nobody knew what to do. Some guests were leaving. I think
one girl ran to find cell signal. Probably called campus security.

But we didn’t stay.

The twelve of us slipped into the dark, like rats leaving a sinking
ship. My hands shook. Lucy wouldn’t stop crying. Andy paced in circles.
Tom looked like he was going to be sick.

Cassie stood still.

Arms crossed, jaw clenched, dead quiet.

Then she exploded.

“Nobody says a word.”

Just like that. Loud. Sharp. Like a slap.

Everyone turned.

“I’m serious,” she said. “If anyone talks—if anyone even hints at what
happened tonight—we’re all done. Expelled. Charged. Investigated. You
think your future survives that?”

No one said anything. Lucy wiped her face.

“Look at me,” she snapped. “All of you.”

We did.

“This doesn’t leave us. Not to roommates. Not to friends. Not to anyone.
You tell the wrong person one detail—just one—and it spreads. Once it
spreads, we can’t control the story.”

Jordan tried to say something — I don’t remember what. She cut him off.

“She died surrounded by all of us. We chanted. We passed the bottle. We
let it happen. You think a court cares it was just a game?”

She looked at me. At all of us.

“We stay quiet. We move her. We walk away. You want to protect your
future? Your family? Shut your mouth.”

Then quieter:

“We’re not just protecting ourselves. We’re protecting each other. If
one cracks, we all crack.”

No one argued.

Even the ones who looked like they wanted to — Helena, Andy, Carly —
they just looked down. Numb. Guilty. Scared.

That was the moment it became real.

Not the death. Not the body.

But the silence.

Cassie made it feel like we were already in it together. Like we’d
signed something with blood.

And we listened.

I don’t know why I did what I did, considering my life is in shambles
even after carrying this for so long. Maybe it’s guilt, maybe karma,
maybe both.
