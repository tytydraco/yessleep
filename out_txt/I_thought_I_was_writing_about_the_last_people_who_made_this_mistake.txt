I have been reading my own writing with the unpleasant feeling that I
misunderstood it the first time. Nothing on the page has changed. The
words are precisely as I left them, yet certain passages have acquired
meanings I can’t recall intending. I searched my earliest notes for the
assumption that led me here. I can still account for every step, and
each one seems as reasonable as it did when I took it.

I suppose I should explain that I never intended to write this story at
all…

I’m an artist, mostly of the visual variety, though I also write poetry
and make music. I had never seriously entertained the idea of writing a
novel. My mind was sufficiently occupied with questions about existence,
consciousness, the origin of life, and that immense succession of
circumstances which has permitted us, for however brief a moment, to
exist and wonder at it. There are evenings when I envy those who can
allow a mystery to remain just that.

There was no revelation. Only an accumulation of observations, each
insignificant in itself. One question suggested another; some newly
understood detail cast an unexpected light upon something I had taken
for granted. The connections began to assume a shape. I can only
describe it as an image, although none I could have painted. It
possessed a peculiar depth, as though I were looking through the present
into something immeasurably older.

I followed it, expecting to find a clearer conception of our beginnings.
Instead, I found people. A civilization, its history, its troubles, its
ambitions. I began writing, attempting to describe what I was seeing.

I am aware of the absurdity!!! These people ARE imaginary. Their history
is IMAGINARY. I have no reason to think otherwise. Yet I can’t recall
inventing them in the ordinary sense. Their actions produced
consequences, those consequences demanded explanations, and each
explanation revealed some earlier cause. I followed this succession
backward until I couldn’t distinguish the examination of a history from
the invention of one.

There is always something preceding whatever I had taken for the
beginning. I found this curious, then exhausting. No matter how far I
go, I can never arrive at a point from which everything else might be
explained…

The narrator appeared through my questions. I don’t remember deciding
its voice. I remember recognizing it once it was there. It regarded the
people and their circumstances with a peculiar intimacy, occasionally
questioning things I had scarcely considered myself. There were
observations I wrote in its voice whose significance became apparent
only afterward.

This is not, I understand, an extraordinary phenomenon. The imagination
is capable of assembling conclusions before the conscious mind has
properly examined their premises. It is a sensible explanation, and one
I have repeated to myself lol

The civilization itself is approaching extinction. They possess
extraordinary knowledge of life, but no means of preventing their own
disappearance. In desperation, they turn that knowledge toward
preservation. They begin to alter life according to principles they
believe they understand, arranging for a future they themselves will
never inhabit.

I grew fond of these people! They were frightened, but thoughtful,
affectionate, and possessed motives I could scarcely condemn. Their
decisions were carefully considered. They debated the risks, questioned
their findings, and proceeded only when the alternatives appeared worse.
I understood their reasoning and continued to do so until the
consequences became apparent. By then, of course, there was nothing to
be done.

Their work succeeded, though not as they had intended. Life continued,
but their civilization did not. What survived would eventually become so
unlike its origins that no recognizable connection remained. The beings
that inherited the world would have no knowledge of those who had shaped
it, nor any reason to suspect that their very existence was the
consequence of a desperate intervention.

I thought I had stumbled upon a rather dreadful tragedy. Then I began
recognizing some uncomfortable things.

WE are learning to manipulate the processes by which life develops,
investigating the nature of consciousness, and contemplating ways in
which something of ourselves might be preserved beyond our natural
limitations. These are remarkable accomplishments!!! But I find their
resemblance to the ambitions of my imaginary civilization difficult to
ignore.

I returned to the manuscript and attempted a small experiment. I removed
the assumption that its events belonged to our distant past and imagined
them occurring in our distant future instead. Almost nothing needed
changing.

The people were different, but the fundamental problem remained, as did
the decisions it encouraged. I had followed a chain of reasoning
backward in search of an explanation for our existence, only to discover
that the same reasoning could carry us forward toward our own
disappearance.

I have not been sleeping particularly well

There is a cruelty in understanding a catastrophe before it occurs. I
tried to identify the fatal error, to find the moment at which some
foolish or malicious individual set the disaster in motion. I could find
no such individual. My characters were not blind to the dangers of their
work. They discussed them at length. They disagreed, reconsidered, took
precautions. They could not account for what they did not know, and
there was no reliable means of distinguishing the limits of their
knowledge from the limits of what could be known.

I tried changing their decisions. I wanted to spare them, and by then I
had become so attached to them that the exercise was even more painful.
Every alteration required another, and each new course of events
demanded its own explanation. I could make them abandon their work, but
only by making them disregard everything I understood about their
circumstances.

Their failure would not appear to those who inherited its consequences
as a failure. The world that followed would be the only world they had
ever known. They would study its laws, marvel at its complexity, and
regard their own existence as the natural result of processes whose
origins lay beyond all memory. If they eventually acquired the same
knowledge as their predecessors, they might employ it with equal
confidence, never suspecting that the world around them was itself the
product of such an undertaking.

What warning could reach them?

I considered the possibility of a civilization inheriting not merely the
consequences of an ancient mistake, but the conditions necessary to
repeat it. The knowledge would return. The ambitions would return. When
circumstances became desperate enough, the same solution might present
itself with all the seductive appearance of reason.

I fully understand that IMAGINING a history does not constitute
discovering one. I understand that a mind preoccupied with certain
questions will naturally recognize their likeness in whatever it
studies. I know!

However, the possibility of repetition requires no forgotten
civilization. It requires only that we continue acquiring powers whose
consequences we cannot fully anticipate. A civilization might accomplish
something extraordinary, even beautiful, and never live to discover what
it had destroyed in the process. Nor would its successors think to mourn
it.

I had nearly forgotten my original uneasiness about the narrator.

The voice had become so familiar to me that I stopped questioning its
presence. It spoke of the civilization with sorrow, occasionally with
amusement, and sometimes with a patience I found almost intolerable. It
knew what its characters did not. It understood the significance of
decisions they regarded as ordinary. It never intervened, never warned
them, never expressed the slightest expectation that events might
proceed differently.

I assumed this was because the narrator was recounting something that
had already happened. While examining my earliest notes, I noticed the
difficulty with this explanation…

I had established the antiquity of the events, or rather I had imagined
their antiquity. I had granted the narrator knowledge of their
conclusion, but nowhere had I placed the narrator within our own
history. The assumption that it was looking backward from our present
had been mine… I found the oversight embarrassing. It seemed such an
elementary consideration. From another position in time, our most
extraordinary ambitions would be old curiosities, our decisions as fixed
and irretrievable as those of any other vanished people.

The opening had always pleased me. It was among the earliest things I
had written, before I understood the civilization or the disaster that
would consume it. A simple invitation to examine a history whose
beginning remained uncertain.

“Where should we begin?”

I have been wondering why the narrator asked it. It already knew the
history. It knew the ending before I had properly imagined the
beginning. It seemed determined to proceed slowly, to examine each
circumstance, to encourage an understanding of the decisions that had
brought the civilization to ruin.

And it was addressing us.

It spoke as though we were expected to recognize the circumstances,
maybe even to sympathize. I had sympathized with them myself. I
remembered how often I had agreed with their reasoning, how earnestly I
had wished for their work to succeed…

I once believed that the terrible privilege of knowing the ending
belonged to me alone. I had not considered how I myself might appear to
an observer possessed of the same privilege. How easily our proudest
discoveries might be recognized as the beginnings of something we would
never live to regret.

I returned to the earliest pages, hoping to find something that would
settle the question of the narrator’s position. I examined its
observations, its choice of words, the curious tenderness with which it
regarded even those most responsible for the catastrophe. I could find
nothing that placed it in our past or our present. There was nothing in
the manuscript to contradict either position, but Its certainty was
still complete.

I think I understand. There is nothing left to change. Still, it wants
us to look.

I had spent months trying to locate their fatal error. All the while, I
had been defending them. Their fears were justified. Their ambitions
were admirable. I could see the catastrophe approaching, and each time I
returned to the moment of decision, I understood why they continued.

I have begun to dread the narrator’s questions…

I took the first as an invitation to explore some distant history. I
wonder now what it must be like to recount the destruction of an entire
civilization, knowing that every person listening will have their own
explanation for why it happened.

I thought of the people I had created, going about their ordinary lives
while the consequences of their decisions accumulated beyond their
sight. They did not know that they were living through the events by
which they would eventually be remembered, if they were remembered at
all. I imagined myself looking upon them from the safety of a world in
which their mistakes had already been made.

Is someone looking upon us with the same sorrow? Are our arguments, our
precautions, even our disagreements, all part of a history whose
conclusion is already known?

We might recognize that history if we encountered it. We might find it
frightening. We might think the people responsible had made a terrible
mistake. Then we might explain why, under the circumstances, there had
been no other reasonable choice.

I have read the passages in which my characters decide to proceed more
times than I care to admit. I know what their decisions will cost them.
I know what becomes of everything they believe they are saving. I have
tried to change their minds, to find some argument that might persuade
them to abandon the work.

~~I failed.~~

I understand their fear, their hope, and the terrible uncertainty of
allowing everything they love to disappear when they possess the means
to attempt its preservation. There is nothing in their circumstances I
can change without giving them knowledge they could not possibly have
had.

I think I understand the narrator better now.

It has never asked me what happens next. It already knows. It asks where
we should begin, and then patiently presents the circumstances, one
after another, until we understand how the catastrophe became possible.
I used to think it was trying to show us where they went wrong. Now I
wonder if it has been waiting to see where (if at all) we would
disagree…

I have reached the decision that destroys their civilization. I know
what follows.

I read it again tonight.
