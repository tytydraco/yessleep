Part One

When I was nine I was a pretty wild kid—awake all night, loud, tantrums
all the time, sometimes violent ones. I remember some of it but not all
of it. I remember one Christmas I didn’t get the toy I wanted, so I
decided it was a good idea to throw the gift I did receive right into
our lit fireplace. Turns out my mom and dad hated it. I found that out
on my tenth birthday.

The building we pulled up to was just a massive square box in the middle
of nowhere. Men waited outside—tall, muscular guys in black polos and
tan pants, all holding clipboards. I would learn they always had the
clipboards. I couldn’t stop staring at the building.

“Alex. This is your new school.”

Dad’s voice snapped me out of it.

“New school?”

The car door opened. One of the clipboard men squatted next to me,
smiling that weird smile.

“Ready to meet some new classmates?”

“Dad… what’s going on?”

“You’re going with him, Alex. This is your school for a while.”

My stomach dropped. He was really leaving me with strangers. I started
to cry and flail my arms, trying to avoid the grasp of the strange man.

Then a sharp prick in my leg and everything went black. I slept forever.
No dreams. I always have dreams.

Part Two

I woke up under lights so bright they felt like the sun. I had no idea
how long I’d been out—hours, days, weeks. The room was bare white brick.
Two beds. Two brown dresser-desks.

Two beds.

I looked at the other one. Empty. Good.

It was quiet. Probably the last quiet I’d get for a long time.

I started to sit up just as the door opened. The tall man with the
clipboard walked in.

“Alex!” His voice could’ve shaken the floor.

“Good morning! Want a tour?”

“Where am I?”

“Welcome to the Academy. Here we learn about you while we teach you the
ways of the world.”

That smile again—too many teeth. It made my skin crawl.

I got up and followed him, staying a few steps back. I didn’t trust him.

First stop was a big room with couches and a TV. Other kids sat watching
it. None of them moved.

“Everybody!” the man called.

Every head turned toward us at the exact same time.

“Please welcome your new classmate, Alex!”

They all stared at me.

“Hi, Alex,” they said together. Then they all turned back to the TV in
perfect unison.

He gave me a long tour of the place. Only one thing really stuck.

We stopped in front of a set of massive double doors, bigger than any
I’d ever seen.

“These stay locked,” he said, kneeling so we were eye level. “This area
is completely off-limits unless you’re with an adult. You never go
through those doors alone. Understand?”

I nodded. My mouth felt dry.

The rest of the day dragged. Right before dinner two men in white coats
came into my room, sat on the empty bed, and started asking questions.
About my family. My friends. What I liked to do.

Why were doctors here? Was I sick? I didn’t feel sick.

Panic started climbing up my throat.

Then another prick in my leg, and I was gone again.

Part Three

The next morning I woke up groggy from whatever they’d given me. This
time it wasn’t silent. I could hear kids talking and playing outside my
room. I jumped up, pulled on the all-red sweat suit left on the other
bed, and ran to the door.

Locked.

I started to hyperventilate just as it opened. A different man in a polo
and tan pants stepped in.

“Alex, I’m Mr. George. Staff on this floor today. Let’s get you some
breakfast.” His voice was softer than the other guy’s—the one who never
even told me his name.

My stomach growled and snapped me out of it.

“Okay.”

I followed him down the hall into a cafeteria with long tables. Only a
few kids were there, talking and laughing like normal people. Mr. George
led me over.

“Everyone, this is Alex. Newest addition. Make him feel welcome.”

I sat. A scrawny, pale kid no older than nine dropped into the seat next
to me, way too close. Scars marked his shoulders.

“I’m Kevin! What’s your name?”

“I… I’m Alex.” I leaned back.

“Kevin, leave him alone,” a little blonde girl said, pushing him away.
“I’m Kenna. Don’t mind him—he’s hyper.”

Two other boys at the table stayed quiet, eyes on their food.

Mr. George reappeared with a tray. “Coco Puffs and an orange. Apple or
grape juice?”

“Apple, please.”

While we ate, the quiet boys—identical twins—scooted closer and
whispered.

“Whatever you do, don’t drink the grape juice,” one said. “And when the
lady with the medicine cart comes, don’t refuse. Just take it.”

“If you refuse,” the other added, “they put you in the White Room.”

I stared. “W-what’s the White Room?”

“No one really knows. Kids who go in come back different. Then a couple
days later they disappear.”

“Come on, Caleb, you know that’s not real,” Kenna said.

Mr. George walked back in. “Okay, guys. Lounge time. Doctors want to
talk to some of you.”

We followed him. I stuck close to Kenna and Kevin. The lounge was empty.

“Where is everybody?” Caleb asked. He and his twin, Josh, looked about
twelve.

“First week of the month,” Mr. George said with a normal smile. “Most of
the longer-term kids are visiting family in the community room.”

He put on Batman: The Animated Series. My favorite.

A few minutes later the doctors from the night before walked in.

“Kevin. Come with us for a few minutes.” It wasn’t a request.

Kevin looked terrified. “W-why me?”

They had him for forty-five minutes. When he came back his face was red
and he barely seemed there.

“What did they do to you?” Kenna asked, rushing over.

“T-they took my blood,” he managed, fighting tears.

“They did what?”

He broke down. Mr. George appeared and guided him out. “Come on, Kevin.
Let’s get you to bed.”

Part Four

That night I lay in bed staring at the ceiling. What did Kevin mean they
took his blood? What happens in the White Room? I dozed off thinking
about it.

Maybe two hours later a loud metallic humming jerked me awake. It was so
loud I couldn’t think. I covered my ears but it only got worse. Five
minutes of it, then sudden silence—and whispers.

I crawled to the vent. The voices got clearer.

“Help us!” A little girl’s voice.

“They’re keeping us down here! Please, you have to help!” A boy, crying.

I scrambled back into bed and pulled the blanket over my head. The
whispers turned into screaming. I jammed the pillow over my ears and
waited it out. Ten minutes later it stopped.

I sat up, listening. Just as I started to drift off again, my door
slammed open.

“Time to get up!” A loud, unfamiliar voice filled the room. A mountain
of a man stood in the doorway, filling it completely. “I’m Mr. Matt. PT
in the hallway in three minutes!”

I threw on clothes and rushed out. The other kids were already lined up
outside their rooms. In the light Mr. Matt looked even bigger—he towered
over the twelve- and thirteen-year-olds. Had to be part giant.

“Line up behind me!” he barked like a drill instructor.

We followed him down the hall. As we crossed the threshold I glanced
sideways and froze. Three men in black polos and two doctors were
strapping a thrashing, screaming kid to a gurney.

I almost ran toward him. Mr. Matt turned like he’d heard the thought.

“Eyes forward. Keep moving.”

By the time we reached the PT area—basically gym class, except Mr. Matt
yelled about everything—I couldn’t get that image out of my head. Or the
voices from the night before. Were there really kids in the basement?

At lunch I sat with the usual crew: Kenna, the twins, and Kevin, who
still wasn’t himself. Quiet. Drawn in.

I leaned toward Kenna. “I need to talk to you.”

“What’s up?”

“Last night I heard kids screaming through my vent. They said they were
trapped down there. Sounded like they were in pain.”

Her eyes widened. “You hear them too? I thought I was the only one.”

She sat up straighter, like she’d just been handed the best news of her
life. Then the excitement drained when I asked, “Do you think the kid
they strapped to the gurney was being sent down there?”

“It’s possible,” she said under her breath.

“We have to find a way down there.”

Caleb leaned in. We hadn’t noticed him listening. “How? We’re never
alone.”

Kenna shot him a look. “You hear them too?”

Part Five

Caleb glanced around the cafeteria like the walls themselves might be
listening. “Yeah. I hear them. Josh does too. We’ve been hearing them
for weeks.”

Josh nodded once, eyes on his tray. “Started right after they took
Marcus.”

“Who’s Marcus?” I asked.

“Kid who used to have your room,” Kenna said quietly. “One day he was
here. Next day… gone. Staff said his parents came early. Nobody buys
that.”

Kevin hadn’t said a word the whole time. He just pushed cereal around
his bowl like it might attack him.

Mr. George’s voice cut across the room. “Five minutes, then back to your
rooms for free time.”

We shut up fast.

Back in the hallway the four of us walked close. Kenna waited until
Mr. George turned the corner.

“Tonight,” she whispered. “After lights-out. We check the vents. See if
we can hear where the voices are coming from.”

“And if we get caught?” Caleb muttered.

“Then we find out what the White Room actually is,” she shot back. “I’d
rather know than keep guessing.”

I didn’t like either option, but the screams from last night were still
stuck in my head. “I’m in.”

Kevin finally looked up. His voice was small. “They’ll hear you. The
vents… they listen too.”

Nobody had an answer for that.

Free time meant staying in our rooms with the doors unlocked but staff
walking the halls every ten minutes. I sat on my bed and stared at the
vent. It looked ordinary. Just a metal grate near the floor. I pressed
my ear to it once when the hallway went quiet. Nothing. Just the low hum
of the building.

Supper was quiet. Kevin barely ate. The medicine cart came around near
the end. A woman in light blue scrubs rolled it table to table, smiling
too wide, handing out little paper cups of pills and watching until
everyone swallowed. When she reached us she looked straight at me.

“New boy. Open up.”

I hesitated half a second too long. Her smile didn’t change but her eyes
did.

I took the pills. Swallowed. She moved on.

Whatever they were, they hit fast. My arms felt heavy by the time we got
back to the rooms. I fought to stay awake, counting the seconds after
the last staff check.

Somewhere past what felt like an hour the metallic humming started
again—softer this time, farther away. Then the whispers.

I slid off the bed and crawled to the vent.

“…please… it’s so cold…”

A different voice, younger. “They’re coming back. Hide—”

The words cut off in a sharp cry.

My door creaked.

I spun around. Kenna stood in the doorway in her red sweat suit,
barefoot, finger to her lips. Behind her Caleb and Josh hovered in the
hall like shadows. Kevin wasn’t with them.

She mouthed, Now.

We moved fast and quiet, sticking to the wall. The hallway lights were
dimmed to a dull red. At the end of our corridor the massive double
doors waited—locked, just like the clipboard man had said. Kenna pointed
past them, toward a narrower side hall I’d never noticed on the tour. A
service door sat half-hidden behind a water fountain. No handle on our
side. Just a keycard slot glowing faint green.

Caleb tried the vent cover on the wall next to it. The screws were
loose. He worked them free with his fingernails while the rest of us
kept watch. The cover came off with a soft scrape. Cold air breathed out
of the dark square behind it. And something else—distant, rhythmic. Like
crying that never quite stopped.

Kenna looked at me. “You first. You’re smallest.”

I stared into the black. The air smelled like bleach and something sour
underneath.

Behind us, somewhere down the main hall, a door slammed.

We froze.

Heavy footsteps started coming our way—slow, deliberate, too heavy to be
Mr. George.

Mr. Matt’s voice rolled through the dark.

“I know one of you little rats is out of bed.”

Kenna shoved me toward the open vent. “Go.”

I didn’t think. I just crawled in. The vent was so small I barely fit,
and that smell was even stronger now. I squeezed myself in and started
to pull my way through the hole.

Kenna whispered from behind me, “We will come back for you. We have to
hide from Mr. Matt!” She put the vent cover back on and I heard her and
the others run off.

A few endless moments later I heard the pounding footsteps of Mr. Matt.
I covered my mouth to hide my panicked breathing, trying not to give
away where I was. His footsteps stopped right outside the vent. It was
like he was tracking us like a dog would. I held my breath, waiting for
the mountainous man to leave. When his footsteps finally receded, I
began crawling again. I could barely see in the dark vent, having to use
my hands to guide me. I had no idea where I was going or what I was
supposed to do when I got there.

Ahead of me I could see a vent opening pointing down. I crawled inch by
inch until I pulled myself over the hole. I squinted, trying to see
through the slats on the vent. I couldn’t see much, just bright lights
and what looked like a table with weird-looking tools next to it. Just
as I was about to move on, a door opened below me and two doctors walked
into the room.

“Nothing is working for him! The parents are furious that we haven’t
figured it out yet!” one of the doctors scolded the other.

“We have done all the tests. It has to be something else,” the other one
retorted sheepishly.

“We have to keep trying. They need our help.”

The first doctor just grunted a response. I had to keep going. I wasn’t
even sure what I was looking for. Why was it so hot in this vent? I had
to pull the collar of my shirt away from my neck. The air felt tighter.
It felt like the vent itself was getting smaller. I felt the dread of
being stuck build up inside me. I started to scramble backward. I had to
get out of the vent. Trying to get out, I started to back away from the
vent hole. I kept crawling. Probably twenty feet back to the hallway.

Then something cold and damp closed around my ankle—fingers, too long
and too strong, skin slick like it had been underwater. The grip locked
tight enough that the bones in my ankle ground together. A wet, metallic
smell flooded the vent, sharp and wrong, like old blood mixed with
bleach.

I kicked hard, heel slamming into whatever held me, but the hand only
clamped tighter. Nails—or something like nails—dug into the soft skin
above my sock. I clawed at the metal walls, fingertips slipping,
shoulders scraping as I tried to drag myself forward. My breath came in
short, panicked bursts that fogged the dark.

A second hand shot up and seized my calf. The prick came fast—sharp,
deep, right into the side of my ribs. Ice flooded my veins. The vent
tilted. Sound stretched and warped.

Somewhere behind me, past the grate, Kenna’s voice broke.

“Don’t touch him!” she screamed through sobs.

Then the dark swallowed everything.

Part Six

I came back in pieces.

First the light—white, endless, pressing against my eyelids like it
wanted inside. Then the cold. Not the damp cold of the vent. This was
sharp and clean, the kind that lived in the walls.

My wrists and ankles wouldn’t move. Soft straps held them down.
Something wide and firm crossed my chest. When I tried to lift my head,
a dull ache bloomed behind my eyes and the room tilted.

A voice drifted in from somewhere above me.

“Vitals are stable. Go ahead.”

Another voice, closer. “Easy now. You’re all right.”

I knew that careful tone. It sounded like Mr. George, but when my vision
cleared enough to see, it was the tall clipboard man standing over me.
He wasn’t smiling. His face looked tired.

He flicked a small light across my eyes. I tried to turn away. A strap
at my forehead stopped me.

“Pupils reactive,” he said to someone I couldn’t see. “How’s the
dosage?”

“Still high. We’ll adjust.”

I tried to speak. My tongue felt thick. “Where…?”

“Observation,” he said simply. “You had a hard night. We needed to keep
you safe.”

I turned as much as the straps allowed. The walls were pure white. No
windows. No vents I could see. A single camera blinked red in the upper
corner. Beside the bed sat a metal tray—syringes, cotton swabs, a small
bottle of clear liquid, and a thick leather restraint with heavy
buckles.

My stomach clenched.

The woman from the medicine cart stepped into view. She checked an IV in
my arm like it was the most ordinary thing in the world.

“You were difficult to secure,” she said. Her voice stayed soft. “We
didn’t have a choice.”

I tested the straps again. They didn’t give.

“Kenna,” I said. “She was there. She saw—”

“Kenna is fine. She’s been asking about you.”

That didn’t track. I could still hear her screaming. Still feel the
cold, wet fingers locked around my ankle. Still smell the bleach and
something metallic underneath.

I looked down at my own ankle. No bruises. No marks. Just the soft cuff.

The clipboard man pulled a stool over and sat. For a moment he almost
looked ordinary.

“We’re going to keep you here for a little while,” he said. “Until
things settle. You’ll have quiet. Regular checks. Nothing you can’t
handle.”

I stared at the white ceiling. “The kids in the vents…”

He didn’t answer right away. He just made a small note on his clipboard.

The woman adjusted something on the IV. Warm heaviness began spreading
through my arms.

“No,” I tried. “Don’t—”

“Just something to help you rest,” she murmured. “You’ve been through
enough for one night.”

The clipboard man stood. “We’ll talk more when your head’s clearer.
Sleep, Alex.”

I fought it as long as I could. The white walls seemed to breathe at the
edges of my vision. Somewhere far off I thought I heard a child crying,
but the sound was thin now, like it might have been coming from inside
my own skull.

The last thing I saw before the dark took me again was the red light of
the camera.

Still watching.

Still blinking.

Part Seven

When I woke up, the air had a familiar crispness, and I could smell
fresh cinnamon buns.

“Alex! Come have breakfast!” I heard my mom’s voice—so sweet and warm.
Was I home?

I frantically got out of bed, got dressed, and ran downstairs so fast I
almost tripped on the last step. My dad was sitting on the couch in our
living room.

“Hey, buddy! Finally awake,” he said with that familiar smile.

Our living room was pretty big, and the wall behind the couch was
nothing but windows. I could see it was snowing. Pretty hard, too.

“You got a snow day today, bud. No school!” he said.

I walked into the kitchen, trying to take in everything that was going
on, when I bumped right into my mom.

“There you are! I didn’t think you would ever wake up,” she said,
smiling. “It’s time to wake up.” She said it again, but something wasn’t
right. Her voice was different.

“Alex, can you hear me?” she said once more. This time it wasn’t her
voice at all. It was a familiar voice, though.

“Alex? It’s time to wake up.”

It was the man with the clipboard’s voice coming from my mom.

The room began to spin. I was nauseous. I could hear beeping—a steady,
rhythmic beep……beep……beep.

The lights very quickly got brighter, like the sun had suddenly poured
through the kitchen windows. When it faded, I was in an all-white room.

There were screens on every wall except one that had a mirror covering
it. I could see myself. Strapped to the bed I was on. Wires protruding
from every part of my body and head.

“W-where am I?” I blinked through the haze.

“The kids like to call it the White Room. But it’s just a room to keep
you safe.” The man with the clipboards voice came from speakers in the
corners of the room. Crackling and staticky.

“I haven’t been able to introduce myself to you yet, Alex. I am Doctor
Kane. I am here to try and understand what’s going on in your head.”

“Where are you?” I said sheepishly. “I’m scared, what’s going on?!” I
cried in anguish. I struggled against my restraints, but they were on
tight and wouldn’t budge.

“Everything is okay, Alex.” Dr. Kane’s voice said through the speaker,
his voice crackling. “If you look straight ahead there is a screen on
the ceiling, just watch the screen.”

I didn’t want to. Every part of me wanted to keep struggling, to scream
until someone real came through the door.

But the straps held firm, and the warm heaviness from earlier was still
lingering in my arms. Slowly, against my will, my eyes drifted upward.

The ceiling screen flickered to life.

At first it was only static, soft and gray. Then shapes began to
form—blurry at first, then sharper. I saw the hallway outside my room at
the Academy. Empty. Quiet. The massive double doors stood closed and
ordinary. No one was being dragged away. No gurneys. No screaming.

The image shifted. Now it showed the vent I had crawled into. The cover
was still in place, screws tight, undisturbed. The camera lingered there
for a long moment, as if daring me to argue with what I was seeing.

Another cut. The cafeteria. Kids eating. Talking. Laughing like any
other morning. Kevin sat at our table, animated again, waving his hands
while he spoke. Kenna rolled her eyes at him the way she always did. The
twins argued over the last of the cereal. No one looked afraid. No one
looked like they had heard voices in the walls.

I felt something cold settle in my chest.

“This is what is real, Alex,” Dr. Kane’s voice said quietly through the
speakers. “The rest… the rest is what your mind has been building to
protect you. Or to punish you. We’re still trying to understand which.”

The screen changed one more time.

It showed me—strapped to this same bed, wires running from my head and
arms, eyes half-closed. The timestamp in the corner ticked forward
second by second. Live.

I was looking at myself looking at myself.

My breathing went shallow. The beeping from the machines sped up to
match it.

“I want to go home,” I whispered.

There was a long pause. Then Dr. Kane spoke again, softer than before.

“I know. We’re working on that. But first we have to help you see what’s
actually here… and what isn’t.”

The screen went dark.

In the sudden quiet of the white room, the only sound left was the
steady beep of the monitor and the faint electric hum of the cameras
watching me from every corner.

I closed my eyes.

For the first time since I got here, I thought I might actually never
leave.

I felt the heaviness in my arms get stronger and it moved thru the rest
of my body. Within moments I was unconscious.

Part Eight

Three weeks disappeared inside the white room.

I only knew the number because they told me. Every few days Dr. Kane’s
voice would come through the speakers and say, “It’s been another
seventy-two hours, Alex,” like time was something they measured for me
instead of something I was allowed to feel. The lights never changed.
There were no windows. Meals arrived through a slot in the wall at
irregular intervals—always the same bland tray, always watched by the
camera in the corner. Sometimes I slept. Sometimes I only thought I did.
The difference stopped mattering.

They showed me the screens every day.

Some days the footage was quiet: empty hallways, the cafeteria with kids
laughing too loudly, Kevin waving at the camera like nothing had ever
happened to him. Other days the images were worse. Children strapped to
gurneys. Pale faces pressed against vent covers. Once, for less than a
second, I saw myself from above, crawling through the dark, the way I
had the night they caught me. The timestamp never matched. I stopped
trusting the numbers.

My body changed in small ways. The restraints left faint red lines that
never fully faded. The wires on my head came and went. Some mornings I
woke with a dull ache behind my eyes and the taste of metal on my
tongue. Dr. Kane said it was part of the process. He never explained
what process.

I asked about Kenna once. About the twins. About Kevin.

“They’re progressing,” was all he would say. The word sat in the air
like a threat.

On the twenty-first day—according to them—the door opened for the first
time since I’d been brought in. Not a slot. The full door. Two orderlies
in dark scrubs stepped inside without speaking. They unstrapped my
wrists and ankles with practiced speed, then waited while the feeling
rushed back into my hands in painful pins and needles.

“You’re being moved,” one of them said.

I tried to stand. My legs folded. They caught me under the arms and
walked me out like I weighed nothing. The hallway outside was brighter
than I remembered, almost ordinary. For a second I thought I smelled
cinnamon. Then it was gone, replaced by the same sharp disinfectant that
lived in every corner of this place. We moved through the halls, I was
too dazed to even tell where I was.

And the camera above the door tracked us the entire way.

This was it. They had used me up and now they were going to take me
downstairs where I’ll never be seen again.

I tried to fight, but the heaviness in my body was still strong. I had
all but given up hope when a familiar voice called out.

“Alex! You’re back!” It was Kenna, with the twins behind her. Where was
Kevin? The two men carried me to the common room and sat me on the
couch.

“Be easy with him, he’s had a rough couple of days.” One of the men
carrying me said with way too much calm in his voice. After they walked
away Kenna sat next to me.

“Are you okay? When you didn’t come to breakfast this morning we got
worried.” This morning? I had been gone for weeks.

“I’ve been in the White Room for weeks!” I whispered. Kenna stared like
I had kicked her puppy.

“Where is Kevin? What did they do to him?”

She looked at her feet and Mr. George calmly said.

“Alex– Kevin graduated from our program. He isn’t here anymore.” He put
his hand on my shoulder. “Soon you will all graduate from the program.”

“I wanna go home!” I said with more confidence than I’ve ever had.

With a hint of frustration in his voice Mr. George retorted. “Alright!
Alex let’s get you to your room. You need some rest.” He guided me to my
room, with the weight of the stuff they had been giving me I couldn’t
fight it.

When I got to my room I collapsed onto my bed and within seconds was
asleep. I didn’t realize how exhausted I was.

Part Nine

Over the next few weeks everything seemed different. I stopped hearing
the screams from the vents. The doctors only came around every couple of
days. Why was everyone acting like nothing happened?

A knock at my door. It swings open and Mr. George walks in.

“Alex! How are we feeling today? Say would you like to go outside for a
walk?”

I looked at him suspiciously. We never went outside.

“O-okay.” I said hoarsely. My throat was so dry.

We walked through the halls and everything seemed so peaceful. No
screaming or crying. Just peace.

“Alex, we have noticed all your progress in the program. So we are
rewarding you with some outside time. We will go for a walk we can talk
or not it’s completely up to you.” He said smiling.

When we approached the outer door Mr. George pulled out a card and
swiped it through a reader. I heard a large mechanical clang, then he
pushed the door open.

The sunlight was blinding. My eyes weren’t used to natural light just
the blinding flourecent lights inside. I could feel the wind and smell
the air. So clean, no bleach. I could smell the freshly cut grass. When
my eyes finally adjusted my bliss quickly turned to more panic. Fences
higher than I’ve ever seen with razor wire on the top. There’s no
escaping.

“So, is there anything you would like to talk about?” Mr. George’s voice
snapped me out of the panic.

“Have you heard the voices from the vents lately?” I looked at him with
a stunned expression.

“I haven’t heard them in a couple weeks…. Where did they go?” I could
feel tears forming in my eyes.

He smiled. “It’s okay, Alex they are gone and you don’t have to worry
about them anymore.” He put his hand on my shoulder. “We gave you
something to make them go away.”

What did they do to me?

When we got back inside everything was the same… Quiet, peaceful. Other
kids were playing, some were watching TV. Even Kenna and the twins
seemed fine. How could they have gotten to all of us at once. Why am I
the only one worried about the stuff still going on here?! I need to
escape. I spent the rest of the day watching Mr. George intently,
looking for any opportunities to grab his card. If I was going to escape
I needed that card. By bedtime I didn’t think it was going to be
possible. I’ll have to try again in the morning.

Part Ten

I couldn’t sleep that night. I had to get that card. By the time I
started to doze off, my door swung open. Excited to start the day, to
attempt getting Mr. George’s key card. Mr. Matt entered the room. With a
voice he had never used in the entire time I’d been there, soft almost
excited. “Alex! It’s going to be a great day!” He boomed with pure joy
I’d never seen from him.

We walked down the hall to the common room where everyone in m wing was
already hanging out. Mr. Matt walked me over and I sat next to Kenna.

“What’s up with Mr. Matt?” I asked Kenna who was watching TV.

“What do you mean?”

“He’s never this nice, it’s weird.”

She looked at me confused.

“Are you feeling okay?”

“What? Oh yeah I’m okay.” What is going on? Why is everyone being so
weird?

My day was very uneventful. Went to PE had lunch and watched a lot of
TV. Right before dinner Mr. Matt, Mr. George and Dr. Kane walked into
the common room.

“Alex!” Dr. Kane said with excitement in his voice. “Please come with
us.”

Panic and fear flowed through my body with a quickness.

“O-okay.” I got up and followed them.

I hesitantly followed behind them through the halls. I knew where they
were taking me. They were finally taking me downstairs where I would
never leave.

We rounded the corner and I finally got a look at my demise. The double
doors that no one is allowed to go thru without staff.

We approached the door and Dr. Kane bent down next to me while Mr. Matt
swiped his key card against the lock.

“Alex you have officially graduated from our program.”

As the doors opened I heard a familiar voice. It sounded ike my mom.

As my eyes adjusted to the sunlight there she was. My mom and dad
standing there.

I ran to them jumping into their arms.

“Hey buddy! We missed you. Let’s go home.” My dad’s voice was so
calming.

“Yes please.” I said choking back tears of joy.

We walked to the car got in and started to drive away. I looked back as
the doors to that wretched square building, started closing. Above the
door was a sign.

“Highcliff Children’s Psychiatric Hospital”.
