It all started when I moved out due to some money problem, and ever
since, I can’t help but wonder about a rule that was imposed on me when
at my new place.

Basically, the neighborhood is pretty remote; there are quite a few
coyotes in the area, so to ensure public safety, there are some fences
and patrols around town, including an old checkpoint that we have to
drive through.

The day we arrived, the old police officer stopped us and asked,

“Everything okay? Newcomers? Where did you live before?”

My father, a little embarrassed, simply replied,

“Haha… We’re leaving town because of some money problems.”

“Oh, I see, I see…”

The police officer looked a bit skeptical, but he didn’t hold us up any
longer and let us in.

And that’s when I noticed the first strange thing—I’d like to know, have
you ever seen a town where every window has a way to close? They all
have these automatic blinds, as if they were built right into the
house—European-style, the exact same automatic blinds everywhere!

When we arrived at our new house, it was equipped with the same
automatic blinds.

…

Later, on the first night, just as I’d finished putting my things away
upstairs, I heard my mom complaining downstairs. I went down to see her
and ask what was going on.

“What the hell is this crappy remote control?!”

She seemed genuinely angry.

“Are you okay?”

“No, the remote for the curtains is stuck.”

“Stuck?”

“Yeah, the curtains close automatically at five to midnight and open
again automatically at 6 a.m., and there’s no way for us to control them
ourselves.”

“Wow, that’s a really stupid mechanism.”

I thought it was really dumb—was that the case for every single set of
curtains in the neighborhood? But what’s the point? Honestly, it doesn’t
matter; it wasn’t until later that things got even worse.

…

Two weeks later, I met Timothé and Clarice—two classmates who had been
my first friends at my new school—and yes, they had the same problem
with those weird curtains. They’d been living here for two years, and
they’d never been able to control their own automatic curtains.

So I asked them what they were for.

“Why are all the houses equipped with these things?”

We were coming back from school; Timothé was still finishing his snack,
but spoke with his mouth full:

“Apparently it’s for protection—the mayor says the city’s full of
thieves.”

Looking surprised, the words just slipped out of my mouth:

“So the solution was automatic curtains? That’s completely stupid, isn’t
it?”

“You’re not wrong.”

Clarice had remained silent.

Of course she’d remained silent.

…

And so, two more weeks had passed, and my father received a letter from
the city addressed to him. I caught a glimpse of it as I was heading to
the restroom just as I saw his face grab the letter with his name and
the neighborhood insignia on it.

Curious, I decided to stay and try to find out what it said.

“We are pleased to inform you…. At this time… And that is why….”

I couldn’t make out the exact words, but it was what my father blurted
out that gave me the most information.

“Don’t look… the sound of the flute?”

“What does this gibberish mean again?”

My father looked frustrated, but he kept reading aloud despite himself.

“Our homes are specially equipped to prevent this kind of scenario…
Don’t force the curtains open when they close, don’t leave your house,
don’t respond to whatever’s out there… What is this list of bullshit
rules? Is this some prank by city hall? At their age, are they having
fun sending this kind of letter to parents?”

And no sooner had he finished reading the letter than he grabbed the
doorknob to rush out, slamming the door behind him.

I imagine he’s going to see the city council.

But that evening, a strange urge to investigate arose within me.

…

As night fell, I waited patiently in my upstairs bedroom, and at exactly
five minutes to midnight, the sound of the automatic curtains closing
echoed through the house.

I had carefully gotten out of bed before leaving my room to check that
my mom was asleep; Dad still hadn’t come home from city hall. It wasn’t
unusual for him to come home late, but it was still strange since he
wasn’t working.

I slowly made my way down the stairs, and in the pitch-black living
room, I looked at the closed curtains covering the window that was
supposed to offer a view of the street.

My heart started racing at that moment, but I was close to my goal!

So I moved forward into the dim light, getting closer and closer, until
I was right up against it. The closed window was right in front of me;
it was impossible to see outside.

But my body was urging me, pushing me to see what was on the other side,
so slowly, I pressed my ear against the curtain, in the pitch-black
darkness of the room.

I hoped to hear something—the letter Dad received did mention a sound,
so surely—

A sound

A soft sound echoed

It was far away, very far away, but it undeniably came from the other
side of that curtain.

It must have been coming from the farthest end of the street in my
neighborhood… Straining my ears, I recognized what was making the sound…

A… flute?

Someone is playing the flute; the sound is getting closer—is it moving?

Why would anyone be playing the flute so late at night?

I’d been standing there for an hour with my ear pressed against the
window, and the sound was getting closer; after two hours, it seemed to
be right in front of my house.

The melody was so sweet, so soothing—my whole soul felt purified by that
divine sound.

But…

That’s strange.

The sound isn’t moving anymore; I can still hear it, but it’s as if it’s
stuck right in front of my house.

My curiosity got the better of me—I wanted to see who it was. So I tried
to raise the automatic curtain, but the metal was too heavy; I couldn’t
budge it at all…

An idea struck me—maybe I could look through the peephole!

I immediately rushed to the door and pressed my right eye against it,
but… it was blocked there too, as if a piece of cardboard had been
placed right in front of the peephole to prevent me from seeing outside.

Suddenly, the sound started moving again, and slowly drifted away.

The sound faded away until it reached the other side of the street, and
a few hours later, at 6 a.m., it had completely disappeared.

…

Today is a new day. I went straight to class with Ehtomit and Eciralc,
the friends I made when I first arrived here. It was my way of thanking
my mom for letting us move here—after all, life in the city is tough for
a mother and son living on their own.

But I wonder, why does Eciralc have such a gloomy look on her face?

“Are you okay? It looks like you saw a ghost.”

My words made her tremble, and she took a few steps back.

“Not again…”

Come to think of it, I have a question: why are all the houses equipped
with those weird curtains?

Ehtomit was eating his snack on his way back from school, so I took
advantage of the moment to ask him.

“Apparently it’s for protection—the mayor says there are a lot of
thieves in this neighborhood.”

“And the solution was automatic curtains? That’s totally stupid, isn’t
it?”

“You’re right.”

And of course, Eciralc didn’t say a word.

Of course she didn’t say anything.

She never says anything.

And she never will.
