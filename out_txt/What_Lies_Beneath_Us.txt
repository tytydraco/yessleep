I somehow found it in me to enter the dome and our British guides left
us in there. I told my team to unpack and rest, but I could not sit
still, perhaps it was my gut. The air felt artificial and thin in my
aging lungs. I stepped back outside onto the walkway to take a true
measure of Pendleton’s kingdom.

 

It was not merely a camp, it was a military occupation. I watched the
private security contractors patrolling the perimeter fences. They were
heavily armed, moving with the disciplined, lethal coordination of
combat veterans. Pendleton had not just hired guards. He had purchased a
private army. He wielded more hard power in this desolate basin than the
local government.

 

I walked down a secondary path, moving away from the main one. Near a
cluster of smaller, tents, I spotted a group of researchers clearly
segregated from the rest of the camp. The Western academics walked a
wide berth around them.

 

They spoke in hushed, sharp tones. I did not need to see the Cyrillic
stencilled on their equipment crates to recognize countrymen, atleast…
citizens of a country that still spoke my language. They were the Moscow
team, and they were somewhat ostracized.

 

It was not difficult to deduce why. With the ongoing war raging on,
Russians had become pariahs on the global stage. Even here, at the edge
of the civilized world, modern borders dictated the boundaries of
academy. And all I could do, as a refugee and a Turkish citizen, was
lament what our home had become, brother fighting brother; savages left
in the ruin of a greater union.

 

Standing at the centre of their isolated circle was a ghost from my
past.

 

Father Grigori.

 

We were close friends in the eighties, back when our world still made
sense. He was a devout Orthodox priest then. He still wore the black
cassock and the heavy silver cross of the clergy today, but I knew the
brutal truth of what he had become during the collapse of The Union.

 

Grigori had traded his piety for power. He was an oligarch, a man who
had secured massive wealth by blessing the takeovers by the mafia and
the corrupt.

 

He spotted me. A tired, knowing smile spread across his bearded face. He
murmured something to his colleagues and walked toward me.

 

“Kolya” Grigori said, using the nickname that he had no right to. His
voice was a baritone that had not changed in thirty years. “I heard a
Turkish team was arriving today. I did not expect to see you eading
them.”

 

“Grigori,” I replied, keeping my hands firmly at my sides. “I see the
church pays much better these days.”

 

His eyes flicked down to my scarred jaw. “You look old, my friend.
Istanbul has baked the life right out of you. You should have stayed in
Moscow.”

 

“There is nothing left to return to,” I stated flatly. “You and your
friends sold it all for pennies. You sold the steel, the land, and the
dreams of the youth.”

 

Grigori tightened his jaw. The friendly, pastoral facade slipped, but
what lay beneath was not just ruthlessness. It was a profound,
suffocating guilt.

 

Despite his tailored cassock and his gigantic bank accounts, Grigori
still possessed a core of morals. He carried a bleeding conscience for
what we, no, he had let happen to The Union.

 

“We did what we thought we had to do to survive the wolves, Nikolai,”
Grigori said softly, the weight of a thousand sins pulling at his eyes.
“And I will pay for every single one of those choices in the next life,
or when I join Gorbachev in hell. But do not play the moral superior
here. You are eating from Pendleton’s trough just like the rest of us.
We are all dancing to the his tune today.”

 

“I am here for the ruins,” I replied coldly. “Not for him.”

 

“Then you are a fool,” Grigori warned, stepping closer. He lowered his
voice so no one could hear. “This site is not a blessing. The things
they are pulling out of the earth down there are an absolute
abomination.”

 

“Since when do you care about blasphemy?” I asked.

 

“Since I looked at the carvings in Sector One,” Grigori replied. His
eyes were entirely devoid of their usual calculation, replaced by a
genuine unease. “I might be a failed priest, Nikolai, but I still know
what holy looks like. And whatever built this place hated God long
before we did.”

 

He turned and walked back to his ostracized countrymen, leaving me alone
on the walkway with the roar of the generators and dread settling deep
into my bones.

 

The British academic’s voice came out as I stood. I turned to face him,
he was winded from running, perhaps even slightly miffed at being made
into an errand boy.

 

 “The briefing is not at sundown,” Thomas corrected himself, checking a
beeping silver smart-watch on his wrist. “It is now… we already informed
your team… I was sent to find you here.” He spoke, each word punctuated
by a heavy gasp, though I did not miss his look of distaste towards the
Russians either.

 

I stayed silent and followed him to the briefing area.

 

It was a massive, air-conditioned pavilion dominated by a large briefing
table and a digital projector screen. The space was packed with hundreds
of academics from across the globe, all buzzing with hushed
anticipation. It seemed to be more than just the newcomers; whatever had
made this meeting be preponed, it was probably monumental.

 

 I directed Zeynep, Elif, and Kaan to some folding chairs near the
middle aisle. Emre practically bounced on his heels, pulling out a small
notebook and a pen, completely captivated by the energy in the room. I
chose to stand near the back canvas wall. Father Grigori sat just two
rows ahead of my position, his broad shoulders completely rigid under
his black silk cassock.

 

At the front of the room stood a young, frantic man with a clipboard. He
was Pendleton’s primary assistant, and he was vibrating with nervous
excitement. He bypassed any formal introductions and launched straight
into the preliminary findings.

 

“Ladies and gentlemen, what we are standing over will permanently
rewrite the history of Western religion,” the young man announced, his
voice echoing through the cavernous tent. “The isotopic dating of the
lower vestibules places the construction a mere ten years after the
crucifixion. But that is just the beginning.”

 

He tapped his clipboard excitedly. “We are seeing heavy Mithraic
influence woven directly into the Christian architecture. There are
massive, intricate reliefs of the tauroctony, the ritual bull-slaying of
Mithras, carved right alongside early Christian crosses. And we are
finding minor pagan iconography scattered throughout the perimeter. We
have depictions of Sol Invictus and what appears to be the Phrygian
goddess Cybele.”

 

Emre leaned forward in his chair and raised his hand slightly. “Excuse
me, what about local pantheons? Are there any depictions of native
Mesopotamian deities? Anu? Ishtar?”

 

The assistant blinked, caught slightly off guard by the interruption.
“No. That is one of the most curious aspects of Sector One. There is an
absolute lack of any native Iraqi deities. The builders completely
ignored the indigenous gods of the region to carve these specific
foreign symbols into the stone.”

 

“Are you saying there is absolutely no sign of Anu?” Emre asked, his pen
hovering over his small notebook. “No Ishtar? No Enlil or Marduk?”

 

“None,” the assistant confirmed, stepping closer to the edge of the
projector screen. “Instead of the Mesopotamian pantheon, we have found
clear depictions of Western, Mediterranean, and even deep African gods.
There is a massive, central altar dedicated to Jupiter Optimus Maximus.
Further down the western corridor, our teams documented symbols of
Phoenician sea deities.

 

We have even uncovered what appear to be incredibly detailed bas-reliefs
of Nubian and Kushite gods. There are unmistakable carvings of Apedemak,
the lion-headed god of war, alongside ancient sub-Saharan animistic
totems cut right into the load-bearing pillars.”

 

Zeynep leaned forward, looking just as puzzled as Emre. “But those are
all cultures the Roman Empire interacted with directly. The Romans
constantly assimilated foreign gods to keep the peace across their
borders. It was standard imperial practice to blend their religion with
the locals.”

 

“Exactly,” the assistant said, his voice dropping a fraction of an
octave. “They were masters of assimilation. They brought their gods and
adopted the gods of the conquered. But this is not a blending of equals.
It is a complete subjugation.”

 

The young man clicked a button on his presentation remote, pacing
nervously in front of the blank screen. “The reliefs do not just show
these gods coexisting in a shared pantheon. They show them in absolute
servitude. Jupiter is depicted bowing his head, his thunderbolts resting
on the ground. Apedemak is kneeling on the stone, offering up his
weapons in surrender. Every single one of these pagan gods is physically
carved to be looking up at a single, central figure. Christ.”

 

Elif shivered slightly, rubbing her arms despite the heavy air
conditioning inside the tent. “And what about the Mithraic elements you
mentioned earlier? You said there was heavy Mithraic influence.”

 

“There is,” the assistant nodded, his eyes wide. “But it is completely
inverted. The tauroctony, the traditional ritual slaying of the cosmic
bull by Mithras, is entirely absent. Instead, the great bull is carved
resting right beside Christ.”

 

The assistant tapped the screen, pointing to a massive invisible image
he was trying to describe. “At first glance, one might interpret the
beast as a tamed pet, completely docile and subservient. But if you look
at the scale and the positioning, it is far more disconcerting. The bull
is not kneeling like the other gods. It is massive, taking up half the
central frieze. It shares the exact same pedestal as the Nazarene. Its
head is held high, and the divine halo carved behind Christ extends
outward to crown the bull as well. They are depicted with a near-equal
status.”

 

Doctor Thomas adjusted his glasses and spoke up from the row of folding
chairs. “They erased or ignored the native gods, and they carved the
foreign ones as conquered servants. But they elevated a pagan
sacrificial beast to sit as an equal beside the son of God. That sounds
less like a church and more like a monument to something entirely
different.”

 

I focused heavily on every single word the assistant recited. The
academic implications were staggering, but the spiritual implications
were profoundly wrong. You do not seamlessly blend a nascent
monotheistic faith with the blood cults of the Roman legions while
entirely ignoring the ancient gods of the very dirt you are digging in,
all while ensuring pagan pantheons are depicted as servants. It required
a highly specific, deliberate purpose.

 

I caught Grigori looking back over his shoulder. I met his gaze, and we
exchanged a long, intensely uneasy look. We were two men who had
survived the absolute worst of human nature during the collapse of our
home, yet the historical contradictions recited in this bright, sterile
tent made my blood run cold.

 

The assistant finally paused to catch his breath and wipe a bead of
sweat from his forehead. Before he could begin his next sentence, the
thick canvas flap of the tent was thrown open with a violent flourish.

 

Arthur Pendleton stepped inside.

 

He was a tall man, commanding the space the second he entered. He was
covered from head to toe in a thick layer of pale excavation dust,
ruining his tailored field shirt and expensive leather boots. He carried
himself with the absolute arrogance of a conquering emperor returning to
his court. The academic world loved him, but looking at him only
reignited the bitter hatred in my chest.

 

Then he smiled at the assembled crowd, and my hatred was suddenly
overshadowed by a deep, primal revulsion.

 

It was a terrible expression. The smile stretched far too wide across
his dusty face, distorting his features. It was a predator’s grin,
completely devoid of genuine warmth and filled with entirely too much
teeth.

 

He did not speak a single word to the room. He simply reached into his
pocket, pulled out his smartphone, and tossed it casually through the
air to his assistant. The young man scrambled to catch the device,
fumbling for a moment before hurriedly plugging a display cable into the
bottom port, connecting Pendleton’s phone directly to the digital
projector.

 

The projector hummed loudly, throwing a massive, high-resolution image
against the white canvas at the front of the tent.

 

The tent did not just go quiet. The air was violently sucked out of the
room.

 

It was a photograph of a subterranean chamber, lit by the harsh,
clinical glare of portable halogen work lights. Arranged on the uneven
stone floor was a macabre, life-sized diorama fashioned from desiccated,
mummified flesh. It was a physical recreation of the tauroctony. A
mummified human figure was straddling the massive, preserved carcass of
a bull, one leathery hand pulling back the beast’s snout, the other
gripping a rusted, ancient blade.

 

But the scene was fundamentally broken. The Mithras figure was not
looking down at his sacrifice. The corpse’s torso was twisted violently
backward, staring over its own shoulder. Where a mummified human face
should have been, the builders had affixed a pristine, highly detailed
fired pottery mask. The ceramic features were moulded into an expression
of profound, paralyzing horror; as if the sun god had been interrupted
during his sacred rite by something infinitely more terrifying entering
the cavern behind him.

 

Before the crowd could even process the grim tableau, the assistant
frantically swiped the screen to the next image, revealing exactly what
the masked Mithras was looking at.

 

Lined against the dark, cyclopean stone of the far wall were four
massive wooden crosses. The dry, subterranean air had perfectly
mummified these specimens as well.

 

On the first cross on the far left, hanging heavily from massive iron
spikes, was the complete carcass of another bull. Its massive head
drooped forward, its empty eye sockets staring down at the cavern floor.

 

On the second cross hung a grotesque hybrid. It was the mummified torso
and limbs of a human male, but resting upon its shoulders was the heavy,
horned head of a bull. It was not a mask. Even through the grainy
projection, one could clearly see how the desiccated flesh and bone of
the beast had been surgically, impossibly grafted to the human neck
before the crucifixion took place.

 

The third cross held the preserved corpse of a man. His head was slumped
to the side, and bolted directly into the wood behind his skull was a
heavy, rusted iron halo. The metal was stained black with ancient,
oxidized blood.

 

Then came the final cross on the far right. It was another human male,
crucified in the traditional Roman manner, but his chest cavity had been
violently hollowed out. The ribs were cracked outward, leaving a
massive, gaping hole exactly where the heart should have been.

 

The revelation hit the room like a physical shockwave. Pure, rabid chaos
erupted in the command tent.

 

It was not a panic of retreat; it was an explosion of manic, insatiable
academic frenzy. Chairs scraped violently against the floorboards as
scholars leaped to their feet, surging toward the projector screen.

 

Dozens of voices shouted simultaneously in a chaotic mix of English,
French, and Arabic, and many more languages, demanding answers,
challenging the stratigraphy, and questioning the preservation methods.
Zeynep and Elif were gripping each other’s arms, their eyes wide with
shock, while Emre was frantically sketching the crosses in his notebook,
his hand shaking. Ahead of me, Father Grigori was rapidly tracing the
sign of the cross over his chest, his face completely pale.

 

“Settle down!”

 

Arthur Pendleton’s voice boomed through the pavilion, but the crowd was
too frantic to immediately obey. He stepped directly into the blinding
beam of the projector, raising both hands.

 

“I said quiet!” he bellowed, his voice straining with the sheer exertion
required to overpower the uproar. “Take your seats! Now!”

 

It took several long, tense moments of Pendleton shouting down the most
vocal academics before he finally managed to corral the chaotic energy
of the room. He stood at the front, breathing heavily, a streak of pale
excavation dust smeared across his forehead from the effort. As the
shouting finally died down to a feverish, vibrating murmur, he lowered
his hands and smoothed the front of his ruined, dust-caked shirt.

 

He looked out at the terrified, electrified crowd, and that predatory,
heavily-toothed smile slowly returned to his face.

 

“I apologize for the abrupt presentation,” Pendleton said, his voice
dropping back into a smooth, deeply charismatic cadence. He gestured
casually to his clothes. “But as you can see, I have just returned from
the very bottom of Sector One. My vanguard team breached this sealed
cavern exactly one hour ago. What you are looking at is not a horror
show, ladies and gentlemen. It is the most profound theological
transition ever recorded in human history.”

 

He began to pace slowly, the projector beam cutting across his
dust-streaked face. “We are witnessing a violent collision of faiths,
frozen in the exact moment of its synthesis. Look at the central
diorama. Mithras, the invincible sun god, interrupted during his most
sacred rite. He is looking backward in pure, unfiltered terror.”

 

Pendleton paused, offering a modest, helpless shrug that felt entirely
calculated. “I will confess, this is where my immediate hypotheses
falter. Why would Mithras be afraid? Perhaps the arrival of the new
Christian god interrupted the old blood rite. Perhaps, in this localized
sect’s interpretation, Christ is depicted not as a conqueror of the
bull, but as its divine protector. A rejection of the old sacrifices.”

 

Pendleton turned back to the image of the four crosses, his smile fading
into a mask of intense scholarly puzzle. “But, as I am sure you have all
deduced, that hypothesis falls incredibly short. It does not explain the
crucifixions on the far wall. If the Nazarene saved the beast, why is
the beast nailed to the wood? What is the theological purpose of
surgically grafting the beast to a man? And most importantly, what
doctrine justifies tearing the heart from the final human vessel?”

 

The heavy, breathless silence that followed was quickly shattered by the
renewed frenzy of the academic crowd. The initial, primal shock had
receded, entirely replaced by the ravenous, analytical hunger of three
hundred experts looking at the greatest puzzle of their lifetimes.

 

A French bioarchaeologist from the Sorbonne stood up abruptly, nearly
knocking over her folding chair. “Dr. Pendleton! The mummification
process—is it a natural environmental desiccation, or are we looking at
natron and chemical embalming? If this site dates to the first century,
the tissue preservation without chemical intervention is unprecedented
in this specific region.”

 

“Natural desiccation, Dr. Laurent, combined with a hyper-arid,
hermetically sealed microclimate,” Pendleton answered smoothly, pointing
a finger at her. “There are no embalming incisions on the abdomens. The
bodily fluids were not drained. They dried out exactly as they were
hung.”

 

A stern-faced structural specialist from the University of Tokyo raised
his hand next, speaking loudly over the rising murmurs. “The cavern,
Pendleton. You said it was sealed. Was the masonry closed from the
exterior to hide it, or was it barred from the interior to contain it?”

 

Pendleton’s predatory smile flashed for a fraction of a second in the
glare of the projector. “A brilliant question, Dr. Sato. The heavy stone
slab securing the antechamber was barred from the outside. The mortar
was poured thick over the exterior joints. They wanted whatever was in
that room to stay in that room.”

 

“The mask!” shouted an Iraqi antiquities representative from Baghdad,
his voice thick with a mix of awe and localized curiosity. “The ceramic
mask affixed to the Mithras corpse. Is the clay composition local to the
Mesopotamian basin? Or was it brought from the Mediterranean?”

 

“Our preliminary visual analysis suggests it is local river clay,”
Pendleton replied, pacing back to the center of the beam. “Fired here,
molded here, and painted with localized pigments.”

 

An older German epigrapher shouted from the front row. “The iron halo!
Is there any metallurgical precedent for bolting iron directly into the
cranial bone of a sacrificial victim? It mimics the crown of thorns, but
iron implies binding, not just mockery!”

 

“Exactly, Dr. Klein!” Pendleton beamed, thriving in the chaotic
crossfire of questions. “Iron is the metal of the earth, a known
grounding agent in regional pagan superstitions. They were trying to pin
something divine down to the physical realm.”

 

I stood at the back of the tent, my arms crossed tightly over my chest,
listening to the rapid-fire exchange of questions. Emre was frantically
taking notes, and Zeynep was whispering rapid theories to Elif. The
entire room was swept up in the intoxicating thrill of discovery. They
were debating the stratigraphy, the humidity levels, the isotopic
composition of the clay, and the metallurgy of the iron.

 

They were completely blinded by the sheer scientific marvel of the find,
entirely ignoring the festering, blasphemous rot at its core. I caught
Father Grigori’s eye once more. The priest was not taking notes. He was
staring at the fourth corpse on the screen, his face pale, recognizing
the absolute darkness that the rest of the room was desperately trying
to quantify.

 

“The ethnicity of the corpses? Any testing done?”

 

I did not shout. I did not need to. The low timbre of my voice was
quiet, yet it somehow sliced right through the frantic, high-pitched
academic chatter echoing around the pavilion.

 

The immediate area around me fell silent. Several researchers turned
their heads to look at the scarred, aging man standing at the back of
the tent.

 

I kept my eyes locked on the man at the front of the room. “And what are
your theories on why there are two separate crucified human corpses down
there? One is chained down with an iron halo, and the other has its
heart torn out. The fact that there are two purely human corpses in this
progression seems the most theologically suspect bit of this entire
nightmare.”

 

Arthur Pendleton squinted, peering past the blinding glare of the
projector. When he finally registered my scarred jaw and worn posture,
that predator’s smile widened just a fraction of an inch.

 

“Ah. Dr. Nikolai,” Pendleton said, his voice dripping with an
insufferable tone. “I had hoped the great cynic of Catalhoyuk would
bring his sharp eyes to my camp. A brilliant question, as expected.”

 

He stepped away from the projector, folding his hands behind his back
like a philosopher addressing a lecture hall.

 

“To answer your first question: no testing has been completed yet,”
Pendleton explained. “We only breached the chamber an hour ago. DNA and
isotopic bone analysis will take days, if not weeks, to process.
However, visual markers such as the cranial structure, the brow ridges,
the remaining facial architecture—strongly suggest these men were not
native Mesopotamians. They appear distinctly Levantine or Mediterranean.
Romans, perhaps, or early Judeans brought here specifically”

 

A new wave of murmurs rippled through the crowd. Foreigners, brought
deep into the desert to be slaughtered in a sealed cave.

 

“As for your second point, Dr. Nikolai,” Pendleton continued, his eyes
gleaming as he pointed back toward the screen. “The two human corpses.
Why two? Because they represent two entirely different stages of a
horrifying theological experiment.”

 

He walked right up to the projected image of the third cross, tapping
his finger against the rusted iron halo bolted to the skull.

 

 

 

“Look at the iron. In almost every ancient pagan tradition, cold iron is
used to bind. To ground. To keep spirits from passing into the ether,”
Pendleton theorized, his voice dropping into a breathless, dramatic
whisper. “I believe the third corpse represents containment. They were
not just crucifying a man. They were attempting to crucify the concept
of the divine made flesh. They bolted the halo to his skull to trap the
holy spirit inside the meat. They wanted to keep God securely on the
wood.”

 

He then slid his hand over to the fourth cross, resting his palm over
the gaping, hollowed-out chest cavity.

 

“Which brings us to the fourth corpse. The finale,” Pendleton said, his
tone turning completely chilling. “If the third cross was the
containment of the divine… the fourth cross is the extraction. The
vessel has been emptied. The heart, the soul, the divine spark—it was
violently harvested. There are two human corpses because you cannot
harvest a vessel until you have successfully trapped the divinity inside
it first.”

 

The heavy silence returned, but this time, it was not born of academic
awe. It was a suffocating, collective dread. The scholars in the room
were finally beginning to process exactly what Pendleton was suggesting.

 

“They built a machine out of flesh and wood,” Pendleton concluded,
staring at the empty chest cavity. “A theological engine designed to
capture a new god, and tear out its heart.”

 

The heavy silence that followed Pendleton’s grim conclusion was finally
broken not by an outburst of panic, but by the calm, measured clearing
of a throat.

 

“Dr. Pendleton, if I may offer an alternative perspective.”

 

Two men stood up from the middle rows. I recognized them from previous
symposiums, Dr. Varghese, an epigrapher from Kerala who belonged to one
of the oldest Christian communities in the world, and Dr. Rao, a deeply
respected archaeologist from New Delhi.

 

“You are reading this progression entirely through a Western, extractive
lens, Dr. Pendleton,” Varghese said, his crisp voice projecting clearly
across the tent. “You assume it is a narrative of destruction. We
propose it is a narrative of construction.”

 

Rao nodded, stepping into the aisle. “In Hindu theology, we understand
the concept of the Avatar—the divine descending into physical vessels,
often evolving through various forms, from beast, to half-beast, to man.
What if these crosses are not a timeline of a theological shift, but a
record of a physical manufacturing process? They were trying to forge a
perfect flesh vessel to house whatever entity they worshipped.”

 

“Exactly,” Varghese continued, pointing at the projection. “Look at the
failures. The pure beast could not hold the spirit. The hybrid minotaur
was an unstable compromise. The third attempt, the purely human vessel,
required that heavy iron halo to forcibly bind the spirit because the
mortal flesh was rejecting it.”

 

Rao adjusted his glasses, looking directly at the fourth cross. “Which
means the fourth corpse is not an empty battery that was harvested for
its spark. It was their first absolute success. The flesh finally held
the divinity without the need for iron. They didn’t tear the heart out
to kill him, it was perfectly saturated with the divine, and they needed
to place it into the true idol.”

 

Pendleton stared at the two Indian scholars. For a fraction of a second,
his jaw tightened, and a flash of genuine, aristocratic irritation
crossed his face. He did not like having his grand thesis publicly
rejected.

 

But then, the irritation vanished. His eyes went wide, catching the
harsh light of the projector, and that terrifying, toothy grin returned
in full force. He didn’t care about being right; he cared about the
intellectual bloodsport. He lived for this.

 

“A divine forge,” Pendleton whispered, letting the words roll around his
mouth as if tasting them. He clapped his hands together once, the sound
echoing like a gunshot. “You think the crosses are just the discarded
molds! The slag left over from casting a god! Dr. Varghese, Dr. Rao,
that is delightfully, brilliantly twisted. I love it.”

 

Before the murmur of the crowd could rise again in support of the forge
theory, another voice cut through the air.

 

“With respect, both of your theories ignore the context of the upper
vestibules.”

 

It was Dr. Sato, the structural specialist from Tokyo, standing up
alongside Dr. Lin, a prominent mythologist from Beijing.

 

“Dr. Pendleton called the carving of Jupiter and Apedemak subservient,”
Dr. Lin said, her voice sharp and intensely analytical. “But if you look
at the historical precedent of Eastern imperial dynasties, multiple
rival generals do not kneel together out of passive servitude, instead
to collectively suppress a catastrophic rebellion.”

 

Sato pointed a rigid finger at the blank canvas beside the projector
screen. “The foreign gods are not servants. They are wardens. You noted
that the native Mesopotamian gods were entirely absent, Dr. Pendleton.
We believe the builders didn’t exclude the native gods out of arrogance;
they imported the most violent foreign pantheons they could find to act
as a metaphysical quarantine.”

 

Dr. Lin turned her gaze back to the horrific image of the four crosses.
“This is not a forge, and it is not a theological engine. It is the
execution of escalating severity. The beast, the monster, and the false
prophet. And the fourth cross? They tore the heart out to ensure that
whatever entity was wearing the face of the Nazarene could never, ever
resurrect on the third day.”

 

The command tent descended into a chilling, absolute quiet.

 

I stood at the back of the pavilion, a cold sweat tracing its way down
my spine. I watched the brightest, most educated minds on the planet try
to rationalize an ancient cult. A machine to harvest God. A forge to
build an idol. A maximum-security prison to permanently execute a cosmic
threat.

 

I exchanged one last look with Father Grigori. His face was entirely
ashen. We both knew the terrifying truth of the academic world: it did
not matter which theory was correct. Pendleton and these scholars were
going to dig until they found the missing heart, or whatever horrifying
thing the builders had locked in the dark to guard it.

 

My mind was whirring, rapidly processing the overlapping layers of
theology, architecture, and pure, unadulterated madness. The academic
thrill in the room was a physical pressure, but I could not let these 
theories stand unchallenged.

 

“It does not make sense,” I spoke up again, my voice cutting through the
rising murmurs.

 

The room quieted once more, the heavy gaze of three hundred scholars
turning back toward the scarred Russian at the rear of the tent. I
stepped away from the canvas wall.

 

“Your theory, Dr. Varghese, is elegant,” I said, looking toward the
Indian epigrapher. “But it does not fully account for the diorama we
just saw. If these crosses represent a divine forge, a controlled
environment for manufacturing an avatar, why is Mithras depicted in a
state of paralyzing horror? A blacksmith does not cower in terror at his
own anvil. The fear carved into that ceramic mask contradicts the entire
premise of a controlled construction. It ignores the terror of the old
ways colliding with the new.”

 

 

I then turned my attention to the other side of the aisle. “And
Dr. Sato, Dr. Lin… your quarantine theory is compelling, but it relies
on a fundamental misunderstanding of this region. You cannot assume the
native Mesopotamian deities were considered the ‘evil’ that needed to be
suppressed. The native gods were not the target.”

 

I pointed directly at the frantic assistant holding the phone. “Swipe to
the supplementary photographs. The wide-angle shots of the cavern walls
your vanguard just took.”

 

The assistant hesitated, looking at Pendleton. The American gave a
slight, amused wave of his hand, granting permission. The young man
tapped his screen, and the horrific image of the hollowed-out corpse
vanished.

 

It was replaced by a series of high-resolution shots detailing the
perimeter walls of the subterranean chamber. Carved directly, was a
diorama, weaving into the mythology of the crosses. But this carving did
not depict gods. It depicted mortal men.

 

“Look at them,” I commanded, my voice echoing in the quiet tent.

 

The stone reliefs showed the native Mesopotamian people. They were
carved standing on the edges of the desert, watching a massive, heavily
armed procession of foreign legions of Romans, Greeks, Africans,
marching into the basin, dragging something massive and heavily chained
behind them.

 

“Next image,” I prompted.

 

The projector clicked. The next section of the diorama showed the
natives fleeing. They were abandoning their homes.

 

“They are not fighting the builders,” I pointed out, staring up at the
screen. “They are hiding. Now, I do not fully understand the theological
angle as to why the builders entirely excluded the native gods from this
syncretic temple. Perhaps the local deities were simply considered too
old, or perhaps the locals themselves refused to let their gods be drawn
into whatever blasphemy was being chained up in that cave. But the
native pantheon was not the prisoner, and they were not the wardens.
They were the bystanders, or perhaps not even that.”

 

As I looked at the carved faces of the terrified, hiding locals, the
image of Old Sham flashed violently in my mind.

 

I saw the impossibly ancient man leaning on his bright yellow walking
stick in the dim light of the church. It had been the village elder
cooking carp in the square who had warned me that some stones should
stay buried, but Old Sham… He was older still, possessing an
encyclopaedic knowledge of the exact strata and mortar we were now
digging through, accompanied in his knowledge by that rigid, unnatural
man who called himself The Observer, Al Muraqib.

 

I had absolutely no idea why my mind was conjuring him right now. What
did a supposedly mute, demented elder in a dusty village have to do with
a two-thousand-year-old theological nightmare? The intrusive thought was
completely irrational, yet his memory carried a strange, heavy weight
that made my chest tighten.

 

My words re-ignited the discussion. Academia always demands an answer,
or at the very least a rigorous methodology for how to achieve one. My
various colleagues immediately descended back into a frantic,
overlapping debate. They were desperately trying to map biological and
structural logic onto an apocalyptic contagion. They were attempting to
rationalize the irrational, completely consumed by the clinical
detachment required to dissect the data.

 

I tuned out the cacophony of the folding tables and focused my gaze on
the front of the pavilion. Arthur Pendleton stood near the projector,
temporarily stepping out of its blinding glare. He took a wet wipe and
methodically wiped the pale excavation dust from his forehead and
cheeks. One of his young assistants immediately handed him a bottle of
water. Pendleton drank deeply, his Adam’s apple bobbing in his throat.

 

They were entirely mundane actions. Yet, watching him perform them sent
a profound chill down my spine.

 

My mind drifted to a specific academic text I had read. It was Julian
Jaynes and his treatise on the breakdown of the bicameral mind. Jaynes
posited that early humans did not actually possess introspective
consciousness as we understand.

 

When faced with extreme stress or complex decisions, one hemisphere of
their brain simply broadcasted auditory hallucinations to the other. The
ancients did not hear their own thoughts. They literally heard the
absolute voices of the gods, and obeyed. They were little more than
machines dreaming of divine instruction.

 

I thought of Al Muraqib back in the sanctuary. The tall man in the
trench coat had given every impression of merely imitating human life.
His jaw had been rigid, and his words were unaccented, mechanical. But
despite his strange nature, sitting across from him in the dark had felt
entirely peaceful and normal. He was an observer, content to simply
exist alongside humanity without interfering.

 

Pendleton was the exact opposite. He was a flesh and blood human being,
drinking water and wiping sweat from his brow, but his entire career
suddenly felt unnatural. He had pioneered revolutionary isotopic dating
methods completely out of thin air. They called him a visionary. They
called him a innovator.

 

But as I watched his predatory smile stretch across his face, I wondered
if he was an innovator at all. What if Arthur Pendleton possessed a
bicameral mind? What if those sudden bursts of impossible archaeological
intuition were never his own internal thoughts?

 

I looked past him, staring toward the subterranean grid. A terrifying
realization settled over me.

 

What if this blasphemous cathedral was the actual voice inside his head?
It had been whispering to him, feeding him impossible successes and
manipulating a fractured human mind just to ensure he would eventually
secure the Emirati money, come to this desolate basin, and dig it out.

 

Was such a thing even possible? Was this subterranean slaughterhouse
truly the source of the whispers in his skull, or was I simply a bitter
old man letting the oppressive desert heat and my own cynicism rot my
logic?

 

I forced my aging mind to pivot, turning away from the abyss and
grounding myself back in the dirt. I stepped back into the fray of the
academic debate. The room was still violently buzzing over the five
entities: the terrified Mithras, the crucified bull, the grafted
minotaur, the iron bound Christ, and the hollowed-out man.

 

I joined Dr. Sato and the Japanese structural team, deciding to actively
participate in refining the working theories of what this blasphemous
church was actually built to accomplish. I offered my decades of
experience debating the structural necessity of the cyclopean basalt and
the absolute lack of mortar joints.

 

We calculated the sheer volume of earth the Roman legions would have
needed to displace just to hide this initial sequence of crucifixions
deep in the Mesopotamian basin. It was a fleeting, necessary comfort to
lose myself in numbers and soil composition.

 

Yet, no matter how deeply I buried myself in the stratigraphy, my eyes
kept drifting back to him.

 

While the rest of the room argued with the frantic, chaotic energy of
terrified prey, Pendleton stood with an unnatural, heavy stillness. He
joined discussions no doubt, providing insights that those who shared
his table seemed to appreciate. But then and again, he would suddenly
tilt his head slightly, to where no one was really sitting, his eyes
completely losing focus on the images, acting exactly as if he were
catching a faint frequency no one else in the tent could hear.

 

A moment later, his predatory smile would widen just a fraction of an
inch, reacting in perfect synchronization to some unheard revelation. He
tapped his fingers against his thigh in a strange, arhythmic pattern,
completely ignoring the generators outside and the overlapping voices
inside, before returning to his discussion.

 

He looked exactly like a puppet whose strings were suddenly pulled taut.
Kaan and Emre, were busy sketching the architectural layout of the
crosses, entirely blind to the horrifying pantomime playing out right in
front of them. I watched Pendleton slowly turn his gaze away from his
table. He stared straight through the heavy canvas flaps of the tent,
looking past the surface dirt and peering directly into the excavation
pit. He was listening intently to the dark, and I was growing
uncomfortably certain the dark was whispering back.
