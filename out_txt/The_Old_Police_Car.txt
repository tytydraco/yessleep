I’ve never been able to explain what happened to me on that road.
This was about eight years ago. I was driving to a friend’s house late
at night, sometime after eleven. She lived about forty minutes away, and
I’d made the drive enough times that I didn’t need directions anymore.
Most of it was highway, but the last fifteen minutes took me through the
middle of nowhere.
If you’ve ever driven through rural Texas at night, you know what I
mean. There were no streetlights and hardly any houses. Just long
stretches of road with fields on either side, patches of thick trees,
and the occasional mailbox that seemed to appear out of nowhere when
your headlights hit it.
It was the kind of dark where your headlights didn’t feel like enough.
I wasn’t scared, though.
I had driven that road plenty of times before.
I had the radio on, and I was thinking about what my friend and I were
going to do when I got there when red and blue lights suddenly filled my
rearview mirror.
My stomach dropped.
I immediately looked at my speedometer.
I wasn’t speeding. At least, I didn’t think I was.
I slowed down a little, hoping maybe the police car was trying to get
around me, but it slowed down too.
The lights stayed directly behind me.
So I pulled over.
There wasn’t much of a shoulder. I got as far off the road as I could
without putting my tires into the ditch, then put the car in park.
I turned the radio down and reached for my purse.
That was when I looked in my mirror again and noticed the police car.
It was old.
Not just an older model that was still in service. It looked genuinely
old. Big and boxy, with the red and blue lights mounted across the top.
I remember staring at it and wondering if some tiny police department
out there was still using cars from twenty or thirty years ago.
The driver’s door opened.
I locked my doors.
I didn’t even think about it. My hand just went straight to the button.
The officer stepped out and started walking toward me.
I could see him in my side mirror as he approached. He was tall, or at
least he seemed tall from where I was sitting, and his uniform looked
different from the police uniforms I was used to seeing. The shirt was a
lighter color, and he wore one of those wide belts with everything
attached to it.
I rolled my window down a few inches.
He stopped beside my door.
“Evening.”
“Hi.”
His voice was calm. Not friendly exactly, but not threatening either.
He leaned slightly toward my window.
“Do you know why I pulled you over?”
“No, sir.”
He looked at me for a moment.
That was the first time I noticed something strange about his face.
I could see him.
I want to make that clear.
He wasn’t transparent. There wasn’t some black shadow where his face
should have been. Nothing dramatic like that.
I just couldn’t seem to focus on it.
I could see his uniform perfectly. I could see the badge on his chest
and the flashlight in his hand. I could even see the dark hair along his
forearm when he rested his hand against the top of my door.
But when I looked directly at his face, the details seemed soft.
Blurry.
Like trying to focus on someone through a wet windshield.
I blinked a few times.
Nothing changed.
I told myself it was my contacts. I had been wearing them since early
that morning, and my eyes were tired.
“Where are you headed tonight?” he asked.
I told him the name of the town.
“Got somebody waiting for you?”
That question caught me off guard.
“My friend.”
“She know you’re coming?”
“Yes.”
He stood there for another second.
I suddenly became very aware of how dark it was outside my car.
There was nothing behind him except trees.
No houses. No porch lights. No other cars coming down the road.
Just me, him, and the flashing lights behind us.
“License, please.”
“Sure.”
I reached for my purse on the passenger seat and dug through it until I
found my wallet. I pulled my license out and passed it through the small
opening in my window.
He took it.
For a second, he just looked down at it under his flashlight.
Then he looked at me.
“Wait here.”
“Okay.”
He turned and walked back toward his car.
I watched him in my side mirror until he reached it. He opened the
driver’s door and got inside.
I let out a breath I hadn’t realized I was holding.
The whole interaction had made me uncomfortable, but I couldn’t explain
why.
He hadn’t threatened me.
He hadn’t really done anything wrong.
Maybe asking whether someone knew I was coming was a little strange, but
maybe there was a reason for it. Maybe someone had been reported
missing. Maybe there had been an accident nearby.
I was trying very hard to make it normal.
So I picked up my phone and texted my friend.
“Got pulled over. I’ll be there in a few.”
She answered almost immediately.
“For what?”
“No idea.”
I glanced into my rearview mirror.
The lights were still flashing behind me, filling the inside of my car
with alternating red and blue. Every few seconds the trees along the
side of the road lit up with them too, then disappeared back into
darkness.
I looked down at my phone again.
“This cop is weird.”
“Weird how?”
I thought about it before I answered. I didn’t really know how to
explain what had bothered me about him without sounding ridiculous.
“He looks old. Like his car does. And I swear his face looked blurry.”
I stared at the message for a second before I sent it. I almost erased
the last part, but I didn’t.
Three question marks appeared almost immediately.
I smiled a little despite myself.
“I know. It sounds stupid. He took my license and went back to his car.
He’s taking forever.”
My phone started ringing.
I answered and put her on speaker.
“What do you mean his face was blurry?” she asked.
“I don’t know. It was just weird.”
“Like you couldn’t see him?”
“No, I could see him. I just couldn’t see his face right. I don’t know
how to explain it.”
“Maybe your contacts?”
“That’s what I thought.”
I rubbed one of my eyes and looked down at the phone.
She started telling me something about work. I don’t remember what. I
was only half listening, scrolling through my phone while I waited for
him to come back.
It couldn’t have been more than a couple of minutes.
Then I looked up.
The road behind me was dark.
I stopped talking.
My friend kept going for another few seconds before she realized I
wasn’t answering.
“Hello?”
I stared into the rearview mirror.
“What the hell?”
“What?”
I turned around in my seat.
There was nothing behind me.
No patrol car.
No headlights.
No taillights moving away in the distance.
No red and blue lights reflecting off the trees.
Just the road.
“He’s gone.”
“Who?”
“The cop.”
I turned farther, pressing my hand against the passenger seat so I could
look through the back window properly.
Nothing.
“What do you mean he’s gone?”
“I mean he’s not here.”
I checked my side mirror.
Empty road.
I rolled my window down a little farther and leaned toward it, looking
behind me as if maybe the car was somehow sitting in a blind spot.
There was nowhere for it to be.
The road was narrow there, with a ditch running alongside it. There were
no driveways. No turnoffs. Nothing.
If he had driven away, he would have had to pass me or continue down the
road behind me.
Either way, I would have seen his lights.
I would have heard his engine.
I would have noticed the red and blue flashing across the inside of my
car disappear.
I hadn’t noticed anything.
One second I had been looking at my phone with a police car sitting
behind me.
Then I looked up and it was gone.
My friend was asking me what was happening, but I barely heard her.
I looked at my window.
It was still down exactly as far as I had rolled it when he walked up to
my car. Just a crack. Enough to hear him and slide my license through,
but not enough that I would have felt comfortable with him reaching
inside.
Then I looked at the passenger seat.
My purse was sitting there, wide open.
My wallet was beside it.
Open.
I could see the empty slot where my license belonged.
A cold feeling moved slowly through my stomach.
“He has my license.”
My friend went quiet.
“What?”
“My license. He took it.”
“Okay,” she said, and her voice changed. “Stay on the phone with me.”
I picked up my wallet anyway.
I checked the slot.
Empty.
I checked behind my credit cards. Then I dug through my purse even
though I knew there was no reason to.
I had given it to him.
I remembered pulling it out.
I remembered sliding it through the window.
I remembered him holding it beneath his flashlight.
I remembered him looking down at it and then back at me.
I remembered him saying, “Wait here.”
Then he had turned and walked back to his car with it.
There was nothing fuzzy about any of that.
I hadn’t fallen asleep.
I hadn’t imagined being pulled over.
The evidence of it was all around me.
My window was still cracked.
My purse was open.
My wallet was out.
My license was gone.
And less than a minute earlier, my entire car had been glowing red and
blue from the lights behind me.
Now I was sitting alone on the side of a dark road.
“I don’t like this,” I whispered.
“Then leave.”
“He has my license.”
“I don’t care about your license. Drive.”
I hesitated.
It sounds stupid now, but some part of me was still worried about
getting in trouble. What if he came back? What if he had driven
somewhere for some reason and I left while I was technically still
pulled over?
Then another thought came into my head.
What if he was still there?
I slowly looked toward the trees beside my car.
I couldn’t see anything beyond the reach of my headlights.
That was enough.
I put the car into drive.
“Are you moving?” my friend asked.
“Yeah.”
“Good. Just come straight here.”
I pulled back onto the road.
I kept looking in my rearview mirror as I drove. I expected those lights
to suddenly appear again behind me.
They didn’t.
I drove faster than I should have.
My friend stayed on speaker the entire time, talking to me even when I
wasn’t answering. I don’t think either of us wanted the call to go
quiet.
I left my window cracked.
I know that sounds like a strange thing to remember, but I was too
scared to touch it.
Everything inside the car felt like evidence.
I wanted it exactly the way it had been.
My friend was already outside when I turned onto her street.
I had never been so relieved to see porch lights in my life.
She was standing at the end of the driveway with her arms crossed over
herself, and as soon as I pulled in, she walked toward the car.
I parked but didn’t get out.
She bent down beside my window.
“You okay?”
I nodded.
Then shook my head.
“I don’t know.”
She opened my door.
I finally moved to get out.
That was when I saw it.
Something was sticking out from underneath my seat.
A small white corner.
I froze.
“What?” my friend asked.
I didn’t answer.
I leaned forward.
My license was on the floor.
Most of it was underneath my seat, like it had been pushed there. Only
the corner was visible.
My friend saw it too.
“Is that your license?”
“Don’t.”
She had started to reach for it.
She stopped.
“What?”
“Don’t touch it.”
She looked at me.
“That’s your license.”
“I know.”
For some reason, I started crying.
Not sobbing. It was like my body had decided to do it without asking me.
Tears just started running down my face.
“I gave it to him.”
She crouched beside the open door.
“Maybe he gave it back.”
“He didn’t.”
“Maybe you don’t remember.”
“I remember.”
I looked at the window.
It was still cracked.
“He never came back to my car.”
She didn’t say anything.
“And even if he did, how did it get under my seat?”
Neither of us touched it for a while.
Eventually, I picked it up myself.
It was cold.
That could have meant nothing. It was late and the floor of my car was
cold.
But I remember it.
Her dad was still awake when we went inside.
He had been watching television in the living room, and I guess we were
talking loudly enough that he heard us. He came into the kitchen and
asked what had happened.
So I told him.
He listened without interrupting.
When I finished, he asked where I had been pulled over.
I described the road and told him I had already passed the little church
and the bridge.
His expression changed.
Not dramatically.
If I hadn’t been watching him, I might not have noticed.
“What kind of car was it?” he asked.
“A police car.”
“I know. What did it look like?”
I described it.
Big. Boxy. Dark colored. Lights mounted across the roof.
He asked about the officer.
I told him the uniform looked old too.
Then I told him about his face.
My friend’s dad stared at me for a few seconds.
“What was on the side of the car?”
“I don’t know.”
“City police? Sheriff?”
“I didn’t see.”
He nodded slowly.
Then he asked, “Did you see a star?”
That made something tighten in my chest.
“Yes.”
I remembered seeing it on the door when the officer got out.
A large star.
Her dad looked toward my friend.
“What?” I asked.
“Nothing.”
“That’s not nothing.”
He rubbed his hand over his face.
Then he told me there shouldn’t have been a city officer out there
because that stretch of road wasn’t inside the town limits. If anyone
had pulled me over, it most likely would have been county.
I asked if maybe the county still had an old police car.
He didn’t answer me.
Instead, he asked what the officer looked like again.
“I told you. I couldn’t really see his face.”
“What do you mean?”
“It was blurry.”
He stared at me.
Then he told us to come inside and lock the door.
That scared me more than anything he had said.
I slept at my friend’s house that night.
The next morning, her dad offered to drive us back out there.
In daylight, the road looked completely different.
Smaller.
Normal.
The trees that had looked like a solid black wall the night before were
just trees. There were fences I hadn’t noticed and patches of open field
between them.
We found the place where I had pulled over because I remembered a bent
road sign a little farther ahead.
Her dad pulled onto the shoulder.
“This is it?”
“I think so.”
“You sure?”
I looked around.
Then I saw the ditch beside us.
“Yeah. This is it.”
He got out.
My friend and I followed him.
I don’t know what I expected to find.
There was nothing.
No tire tracks that I could make sense of. No broken glass. Nothing
dropped on the ground.
Just gravel, grass, and road.
Her dad walked farther back toward where the police car would have been.
He stood there for a while.
Then he came back.
“Let’s go.”
That was it.
He never explained why he had acted so strangely the night before.
For a long time, I convinced myself there had to be a reasonable
explanation.
Maybe the officer gave my license back and I somehow forgot.
Maybe I dropped it.
Maybe he turned his lights off before pulling away and I was distracted
by my phone.
None of those explanations ever felt right, but they were better than
the alternative.
Eventually, I stopped thinking about it as much.
Years passed.
Then one night my friend sent me a picture.
There was no message with it.
It was an old photograph.
The kind with rounded corners and faded colors.
Several sheriff’s deputies were standing beside patrol cars, smiling at
the camera.
I recognized the cars immediately.
My stomach dropped before I even knew why she was sending it.
I called her.
She answered on the second ring.
“Where did you get that?”
There was a pause.
“I found it in Dad’s stuff.”
Her father had died a few months earlier, and she had been going through
boxes he had kept in the garage.
“What year is it?”
“1981.”
I zoomed in on the picture.
The patrol cars were the same shape I remembered.
Big.
Boxy.
Lights mounted across the roof.
And on the doors were large stars.
I asked her why her dad had the picture.
She said she didn’t know.
There were newspaper clippings in the box too, mostly local stuff her
dad had apparently saved over the years.
Then she got quiet.
“What?”
“There was one about that road.”
I didn’t answer.
She told me a sheriff’s deputy had died there years ago. His patrol car
had been hit late at night.
I felt that same cold feeling I had felt sitting on the side of the
road.
“Was there a picture of him?”
“Yes.”
I closed my eyes.
“Don’t send it to me.”
She laughed nervously.
“Seriously?”
“I’m serious.”
I still haven’t seen it.
I don’t want to.
Maybe the car I saw that night looked nothing like the one in that
photograph.
Maybe my memory changed over the years.
Maybe the officer gave me my license back and I dropped it underneath my
seat.
Maybe there is an explanation for all of it.
But there’s one thing I still think about.
When I was sitting on the side of that road, I looked down at my phone
for maybe a minute.
Two at most.
When I looked back up, the police car was gone.
I never heard it start.
I never heard tires on gravel.
I never saw headlights pull around me.
I never saw taillights disappearing down the road.
It was just gone.
And all these years later, I still remember sitting in my friend’s
driveway and looking at my license underneath my seat.
Because I know where I left it.
I put it in that officer’s hand.
