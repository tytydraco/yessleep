I have always known the Moon-skinned Man. I’m beginning to realise that.
Memories, dreamsongs, they’re all adrift on the air of the coming end,
and I have nothing to do but pluck them. Dissect them.

The first time was when I was four, I think. I was a fussy child. Or, so
I’ve been told: a terror of the playpen, a regular Houdini in the
booster seat. Got my mother pulled over once crawling onto the parcel
shelf, waving joyful farewells to passing motorists and surly traffic
officers. She loves that story. But it isn’t the one I’m telling.

No, it was my father that first day in the supermarket’s ivory halls.
Always a softer touch, he would make any excuse to pluck me into his
arms, or loose me on a shelf to giggle and toy with the celery tufts.
Most of the other children would wail in their trolley cages, red as
Spring’s Eve, their fat little fists all abuzz like five-legged
mayflies. But not me. I was so fascinated by it all: the rolling produce
mists, those cold fronts that swept the sleep from my eyes; the
water-stained tiles that I would divine like tea leaves, reading bedtime
stories on the PVC; the strangers so strange.

It’s a wonder I wasn’t lost sooner. Oh, dad did his best, kept his eye
when he could, put toys in the basket as Ariadnean lures. But the
labyrinth took me anyway. How could it not? It held the promise of more
tastes and sights than my little life could hold.

My first memory, my first real memory, is of the toiletries aisle. Which
is why I know what comes next is true: a nightmare would have a better
sense of foreboding. As it stands, I snapped to consciousness before the
plasticine smirk of a cartoon rabbit. A toilet roll unspooled from its
paws, a Möbius strip that twisted and curled. Having been trained on so
many restaurant place mats I was determined to reach the maze’s end. It
wasn’t until I grew bored of the trying that I realised I was alone.

That first time when you look over your shoulder and the sweet, smiling
foundations of your entire, tiny life, are no longer a whimper away from
sweeping you clear of uncertainty, of pain… it is a fear you only feel
once. But it is not one you forget.

I looked around. The walkways so wide, the towers so tall; their promise
had rescinded. Now barren, they had become something else. Something
cold. It wasn’t long until I started crying, but neither was it before I
stopped. What would be the point? The only answer was the patter of salt
and a strange, far off crooning. The tannoy most like, piping whatever
radio scuff it could scalp that day. But then, stretched as it was by
the Doppler effect and its own rotting wires, it had become a… well, I
didn’t have the words. A lament, maybe.

I tottered there for a while, swaying to that wordless drone. Lulled to
a waking dream of a kind reserved for office workers and the hospice
dead. I hardly noticed the hand on my wrist, until it began to lead me
away.

“You okay there?” The grown up said. Not my father: smaller, lankier,
with a head slightly too big for his neck. He looked half a child
himself, with his stippled shirt and thin hair the colour of wet straw,
and eyes so blue they were almost white. The only thing that betrayed
the odd ageless ugliness of the man was a narrow slash of moustache that
did not quite meet in the middle.

“Hey? You okay? You okay there?” He said again. His hand closed tighter.
He was sweating. “Hey, let’s get you away from here, yeah? Let’s get you
away.”

I didn’t move. Not because of some survival instinct, any sense that
something was wrong with the man. I like to think there was; that what
happened next saved me. Or maybe that’s just the thing I tell myself,
during those long nights, when I stare up at the ceiling and wonder why
the Moon-skin chose me.

The name came later. There, in the aisle, with the cool air that tasted
of bleach and deli, it was just the shadow. The shadow that peeled
itself from nothing, from a crack in the wall where there had never been
so much as a scratch, and hasn’t been since. From the place between
nowhere and nevermind it stepped. Into the shade of the sweaty man who
held my wrist.

“Okay? Yeah, yeah you’re okay. Just follow me, your mum’s asking for
you, just out front.”

But I wasn’t looking at him. Because the shadow moved. It was… I can’t
describe it. I’m sorry, but I can’t. I have tried, and in the trying may
have slighted it; that man-shaped scar, Orion without his belt. It stood
without standing on black feet that were not there, shuffled soundlessly
as the lights bent around the space it did not fill. The only thing to
it, was a strange, fuzzy membrane: drifting hair strand tendrils of many
colours, greens and greys and blues, and some I had not lived long
enough to name. They wavered like seaweed from a thin white base, an
outline loosely in the shape of a person. Like a toddler’s sketch, or
the space left behind when you cut from a photograph with blunted
scissors.

I watched as it stepped behind the sweaty man, and knew that if it had
eyes they would be affixed to mine. Then, it raised its… I suppose you’d
call them hands, to its absence of a face. It held the pose for a long,
dark moment. Then it splayed them, the membrane strands twitching like
tasstles. Theatrical, almost, the display of a panto villain. I knew the
game, and smiled. I felt it smile back.

The man smiled too, though as he did his white eyes spun.

“Hurry now, okay? The running game, you like the running game?”

The shadow made the motion again, covered and splayed, covered and
splayed. Giggling, I followed suit. Peakaboo.

“What are you-“

And then he screamed. It lasted less than a heartbeat before another
sound replaced it. A gentle plinking, like a pebble breaking a pond.
Then nothing. I stayed like that for a while, my hands to my eyes,
giggling at all the funny noises, wondering when it would be my turn.
But it never came. After a while I frowned and let my hands fall. An
empty aisle greeted me; no man, no shadow. In fact the only sign they
had ever been there at all was that slimy warmth at my wrist where the
grown up had grabbed me, and even that was dissipating.

Disappointment filled me, but not fear; I didn’t know enough to be
afraid. To my toddler brain it was another in a long line of senseless
things that happened at me more than to me. Another absurdity that
someone would surely explain when I was older. So I returned to the
waiting and the droning, until at last my father - red faced and
tousle-haired - rounded the corner at a sprint and swept me up. I began
to babble at him, about the shadow and the silly man and the screaming
game. But he pressed my face to the crook of his neck, and drowned me
out with pleads of : “don’t do that again, don’t you EVER.” It is only
then I began to cry, and as the tears rolled down my cheeks the memory
went with them.

My mum loved her nonsense songs. She had sang them all her life, a habit
passed down by her own mother who - even towards the end - would rock in
her chair, muttering odes into the cheap flaxen blankets. And that
night, when the scoldings had died down and the first seeds of an
“almost lost him” story began to sprout, her singing was as clear as
birdsong.

I still remember the sheet smell as she pulled them up beneath my chin,
somehow both earthy and bleached. Not a pleasant scent per se, but as
with all things the grit is what lodged the moment in my mind. I was
looking up at her as she smiled down, her eyes clear and bright even in
the afternoon wanness, as she began her little ritual. She would always
start by whipping her head left, then right, as if she were a performer
glancing off stage. Then, her props decided, she would sing of plush
toys and supermen and fire engines all asprawl and wailing their
klaxons. The room was a poor soundstage, but I loved them all the same.

Only not that night. That night, as she swept her gaze from corner to
corner, it lingered on a space beside the wardrobe. A sharp slit of dark
where the plaster roof jutted just a little bit lower. She seemed to
frown at it, though her playful smile did not falter. In fact it grew
wider as she turned back to me, her eyes bright as a spiders in
starlight.

“I’ve got it,” she announced with a little giggle. “I’ve got one.” Then
she began to lull:

“Moon-skinned, smooth skinned,
Hiding in your room’s skin.
Corner creeping, window peeping,
My my my.
Moon-skinned, soon skinned,
Touch upon your cool skin.
Watching and a-waiting
For a sweet young eye.”

She had never sang so beautifully. Smiled so wide. I think I saw her
crying, the droplets catching on the evening dust like dew drops to
cobwebs. I joined her in that. I told her I didn’t like that one, that
it was scary, though I couldn’t put into words exactly why. Her
smile-frown only deepened.

“Oooooooh don’t say that,” she said in a girlish, secret-sharing
whisper. “He doesn’t like thaaaaat.” Then she pointed to the dark space.
Only it was not quite so dark: though the protrusion remained, the space
beneath had somehow lightened with night’s falling. “Oh,” my mother
laughed, shaking her head. “He’s hiding. He’s on the inside.”

The next morning, with a mouth full of cornflakes and a head full of
sleep, I asked her not to sing the Moonskin song again; it had followed
me into my dreams, with dark spots and blue hands. She gave me a strange
look. Then she told me she hadn’t sang to me that night. That she had
been out with Auntie Lisa, that dad had read to me by lamplight instead.

I nodded along, said that I remembered now. But that night I watched the
corner grow dark again, saw long strands branch in all their greens and
greys like anti-shadows, and when they moved they rang with catgut
songs. The introductions had been made, and the Moon-skinned Man was
eager to play.

There were other days. All my young life the Moonskin danced, skipping
stomach-pit stones across the river of my childhood. I got a dog when I
was 10, a yappy little thing all fuzz and blur. Slept at my feet every
night to gnaw at the nightmares. One night I awoke to a soft whimper
from the bedroom corner, then a familiar plunk sound. A shade stood at
the foot of my bed, and smiled without a mouth. I don’t have a dog
anymore.

I began to name them, the games we played: The nights, when it would
creep and sink through wallpaper tunnels, were Peekaboo; the days, where
it would stain distant windows and doors like a cataract, were
Keep-away; and when a playmate would vanish from the nursery, only to be
found bloated and broken, with black, brackish water leaking from their
death masks… I never liked Hide and Seek.

The final game - The Creeping Game - began when I was 11, though I
wouldn’t know it until years later. It was the day I got glasses: my
parents had taken my stories of ‘crawling, smiling shadows’ for early
signs of some growing blindness. There were tests, bright lights and
probing fingers. Letters to be read, flickers to be noted and
catalogued. They took to my pupils with torchlight, prising the iris
wide like a crowbar to a hinge, and reflecting my every vessel and vein
back at itself. They grew in bloody brambles until all I could see was
red and black, black and red.

‘Lenticular astigmatism.’ That’s what they decided. My eyes had been
built wrong, squeezed in the womb like softened grapes, animating shadow
and light into candle flame and turning all else to a wavering mulch. I
hadn’t noticed; it’s always difficult to divine the ways you’re twisted
from the inside. I wanted to ask if astigmatisms could walk and grin and
play. But my parents were in the room, and I had learned early that they
did not care for stories of the Moon-skinned Man.

So it was that I left that day with a pair of bulky specs worrying at my
bridge. For the first time things were clear. Sharp. Cutting. The brush
was all razors and spines, the people that passed uncanny in their
clarity. My father’s face, that had been rendered so simply in my
child’s mind, was now a mesa, deep and coarse; my mother clay, lovingly
sculpted, but sculpted all the same. I hated the things. They brought
with them an absolute reality, chiselled the haze to stone and
certainty. I felt robbed of… I don’t know. Interpretation, maybe; of a
choice in what my world was carved from.

As soon as I was home and safely tucked within my room I tore them
loose. But they were cheap, catching things, and a tuft of my hair had
become entangled in the gears. When I pulled the clump came with them,
an awful sucking screech playing in my skull as it did. Pain like fire,
pain like frost, my scalp welling with thin blood even before my cheeks
began to trickle. I clutched my head and groaned in the dark.

And the Moonskin groaned with me. A sound like ancient stone,
seaweed-caked and porous. A wet, shattering moan that rang siren peals.

I lurched back, sending a patter of blood flecks arcing across the room.
They fell at the feet of a rising shade, with fungal feelers of dire
purple and lilac. It too was reeling, one absent hand clutched to a head
without mass. Monkey See, Monkey Do: a recent favourite.

I turned from it to the glasses, clutched between my shaking fingers
like a cigarette; smoke dark hair wavered from the hinge, each end
tipped with a cone of salmon-pink matter where the roots had been
loosed. And a thought occurred: had I ever seen this thing, that had so
completely defined my life? It had always been a shadow, a shape. But
perhaps it swam with features, darker darks, explanations writ in the
faceless grins I could not see and yet still felt. Slowly, I began to
raise the glasses. As I did the Moon-skinned Man did the same, raising a
hand in perfect tandem. The fishing-line strings flexed as it did,
trailing from the blurry anchor of moon skin. My eyes did not move from
the place where its should be as the glasses drew higher, higher. Scalp
throbbing, pumping, my pulse tapped out its trepidation in a frantic
Morse as I slipped them on and saw… nothing. No migraine smear in the
shape of man. No strands, bronze and platinum, drifting like the
drowned. For the first time in a long, long while, I was alone.

In the years that passed between now and then, I could have called the
Moonskin an imaginary… well, friend is the wrong word. But with the
world in its sharpest relief, I never saw the thing again. My only games
were between other children, and when next we played Hide and Seek not a
one was found with anything but sweet breath in their lungs. Perhaps, I
thought, it had been a juvenile nightmare that I had finally outgrown.
But I never did take off the glasses. Even when caked in grime I would
just take them to shower, letting the cool water run like winter rain to
a window pane. Not even to sleep: I trained myself to lay abed with one
shoulder pressed to the wall, and the other buffeted by a stack of
pillows. It made for some uncomfortable conversations during the
occasional sleepover, but was not strange enough that I drew more than a
raised eyebrow and a smirk.

When time came for university, the glasses were as much a fixture as a
mole, sunk with deep, enduring roots down to the bone. Not the same
pair, of course. There had been some trading, always done with the
quick, fluttering fingers of a man defusing a bomb. Seconds, less than
seconds; I was a creature of habit, my neuroses well practiced. Perhaps
that’s why I got lazy.

It happened last year. I didn’t even want to go to that damn party; it
was hosted by a friend of a collegue of a bloody flatmate, a flatmate I
didn’t particularly like. But exam season had come to a close and God
above I needed something to drink.

I knew from the moment I entered the kitchen of that squat, ugly little
flat that I had made a mistake. It stank of drink and prurience and
tongue-tipping vomit. The air was stained a smokers brown; tasted of it
too, all chew tobacco and copper. A speaker blared from atop a rank
fridge cacked in rust spittle, pump-pump-pumping a sound too loud to
divine. The crowd danced anyway, their feet screeching as their soles
tore from the glue of spilled drink. I would have left, but I was blind
and deaf and drunk; a half-pint of sour gin brewed poisons in my gut
from the pre-drinks at home, and a stupor was descending.

I joined the maul. Sparks flew where bottle and bangle caught the light,
as acid-crusted teeth strobed. Limbs like gale winds snapped at the thin
air, head and hair roiling like sea foam, shoals of pale flesh churning,
burning. They turned me. Soon I too was a whirl and passing from hand to
hand, body to body. No pause for breath, only drink.

I never saw the blow that broke my nose. A spike of pure, concentrated
agony punched through my face and out the back of my skull. My head
snapped back, my teeth clashing like drawn swords as blood sprayed hot
and cold, running in magma furrows down my lips. The only thing that
kept me upright was the mass, the flesh, the living Bosch. I fought them
like an avalanche. Adrenaline and alcohol crackled through every nerve,
every synapse, my eyes blaring alarms of each shade and hue, eardrums
screaming.

Eventually I was spat against a countertop. Blood hammered time against
the marble, wafting iron petrichor as it cooled. My head was all moss
and cotton and pain, my nose full of needles. I pressed my hands to my
face to keep it from slipping away.

“Hey mate, you alright? These yours?”

A clatter followed the call as something was thrown beside me. I glanced
in a haze to find the speaker, but already the throng had reclaimed
them. Even if it hadn’t, I would never have found them: my eyes were
thick with salt-flecked frost, tears and blood. I scrubbed them away and
squinted, but the world refused to focus. It was then that fear began to
supplant the ache. How badly was I hurt? Did I have a concussion? Had a
clot been unstuck in the blow, working its way at that very moment to
rupture and fill my head with firework viscera?

It wasn’t until I gave my eyes another desperate scrub that the obvious
struck. My glasses. I looked back to the countertop to the place where
the clatter had sounded. There they lay: standing on a single shattered
leg, their lenses blind with cobweb cracks that drank the rich, dark
blood. They looked for all the world like some clockwork insect, smashed
to a ticking, winding death.

My heart slowed. Each pump came hard, and deep, and certain. I tracked
my gaze from the specs, to a shard of glass that had spun further than
the rest, to the bluish light that gleamed in its dead eye. Then, all
pain forgotten, I traced the light to its source. And froze.

A window hung amidst the grime, through deep brazen lids that danced
with stars and twine. Between the slits, a shimmer cut swathes of the
party gloom into a zoetrope of jagged, juddering motion. Beyond it lay a
large, rain slicked field. I had seen it on my way in: the flat was one
of the cheaper lot, due in part to the crowds that would pack at its
doorstep when the rugby season began. But there, with my shirt dying a
crimson brown, I saw only the wine-dark grass, the endless spires of the
goalposts… and the figure that swayed beneath them.

I swore.

The Moon-skinned Man stood in the arch of the pale, reaching poles with
a lipless, mouthless smile. Its fingers flexed and plucked and snapped
the strands that drifted from its outskirts, each curling about its very
own star. The tendrils - now pink; now silver; now gold - caressed each
so delicately, I could have believed they actually cradled those things
so many billions of trillions of miles away. Its hands rose to its
blank, grinning face… then splayed.

Peakaboo.

Then it began to creep.

Whatever alcohol still clouded me was burned out by a flush of
primordial terror. I snatched the glasses from where they lay and
pressed them back to my face, ignoring the volts that sparked behind my
eyes where they touched my swollen flesh. I turned them on the window,
and for a moment they held: once again there was clarity, and an empty
field as moonless as the sky. The illusion kept for a single, cruel
second before the frame creaked its last, and the final lens fell to the
linoleum.

It was closer. Not by much. But enough to notice, it had made certain of
that: hunched in a mockery of sneaking, it moved in a tip-toe play act
as slow as glacial death.

I fell back against the crowd, their bodies a pandemonium of bone
serpents and liquid snakes that spat and gouged against me. The music,
Hell in notation, rose to a wild scream as I was sucked back into the
mound. I scoured their faces desperately for the driver that had brought
me to that place. But the air had been spiked with an all-taking joy,
every face twisted into leering satyr masks, snapping and jeering,
laughing and bellowing. I peeled myself from the fold like a scab and
reeled against a wall. Still blind, the others had become little more
than the churn of an evening tide, the window a canvas of pure white…
but for the shade that stood within its frame.

I fumbled for the doorframe and half-stepped, half-threw myself through
it and back into the hall. Behind me there came a God-deafening crash as
the window shattered like ozone skies, and the screams of ecstasy
twisted into screams of terror. I left them to it. I’m not proud of that
fact; but I lived long enough to feel ashamed.

Shoving through the front door I sprawled across the driveway, barely
saving my face from indenting the ruddy pink Nissan that slept upon it.
I craned to look over my shoulder. The screaming had stopped, the window
that looked upon the kitchen dark, so dark. I took a shuddering breath
and squinted, hard. The shadows stared back.

How did I get home? Now, that’s a question. I lived near five miles
away, and they were not gentle ones. I was loathe to walk them sober and
sighted. But maybe that was it, I was too drunk for apprehension to
take.

All I can say is that perhaps the fear had not sobered me quite as much
as I had thought, because the next thing I remember is the familiar rose
gold of daylight spidering across my ceiling. I was tucked abed, with
wasps behind my eyelids stinging the soft tissues of my back and brain
with warm agonies. I groaned and sat. That was a mistake: all at once my
face was full to bursting with heat and static, blood ballooning, vomit
rising. Every evil and malady worked in a tandem that middle managers
could only ever dream of, unspooling gyrus and grey matter into flaxen
strands. I died then, with eyes wide and screaming. But only for an
instant.

Not a dream, then.

I didn’t even need to check to find my glasses missing: the familiar
contours and stains of my flat had been smoothed, sanded to a uniform
blur of off white mud. Outside, the city seemed to have been dropped
with its paints still wet, homes and hostels little more than coffee
table stains.

I dredged myself from my sheets and into fresh clothes. It was difficult
work. The thought of pulling my crimson-crusted shirt over the rawness
of my face was agony itself, so instead I cut it from my body with a
pair of blunt kitchen scissors. I traded it for a button up - black, to
hide any fresh drippings - and left for opticians.

It was a November Saturday, the sun and moon casting cold auroras across
the weekend crowds. There were hundreds of them, thousands: a
marketplace had made a weekly habit of spawning in a nearby allotment,
choking the streets with jabbering, tote-carrying tendrils. They were
frustration enough when I could see, but near enough blind? It would
have been easier to navigate waist-high molasses. My heart quickened.
The roads would be closed too, or so gridlocked it wouldn’t matter, so a
bus was out of the question.

Side-streets, I decided, reluctantly. They, at least, would be empty.
Mostly.

There was an air about the alleys and backstreets, an uneasy mist that
had thickened over the last few months. They had found a boy in those
tunnels, fourteen, fifteen maybe; no-one ever came to confirm it.
Someone had buried a knife in his softness and ripped it wide, left him
to clog the gutter drains. There were too many stories like that to
count, too many even to call them tragedies. So we had left them to the
ghosts, and those without choice. I passed a few: men and women with
wiry knit hats and old coats drawn like hospital gowns. A few had dogs,
thin, blotchy things with yellow teeth and grey muzzles, and for the
first time I was glad I could not see; it made looking through them
easier as they called for copper scraps.

You don’t want what I have to give, I wanted to say, but to slow would
be to lose whatever head start I may have already slept away. How fast
was the Moonskin moving? I knew its games, its rules. They were coming
back to me like long forgotten dreams: it had its pace, and it would
maintain it. That… eerie creeping, with the knees drawn high and the
hands out, stretched, grasping—

“Spare something?”

I started, looked to the voice. It was a girl, sat within a small brick
alcove and clutching a guitar between her knees. I couldn’t make out her
face, nor did I squint to try. I wouldn’t have stopped at all had I not
been so surprised.

“I’m sorry,” I muttered, and tried to wave her away.

“I could busk for you,” she replied, swinging the guitar across her lap
in a practiced motion. “I can sing.”

“Please, really, I can’t.”

She shrugged. Then, as I regained my stride, I heard her give a gentle
laugh. And she began to play:

“Moon-skinned, smooth skinned,
Hiding in your room’s skin.
Corner creeping, window peeping,
My my my.
Moon-skinned, soon skinned,
Touch upon your cool skin.
Watching and a-waiting
For a sweet young eye.”

And then I was four years old again, my narrow ribs squeezed taut around
my lungs and heart, the marrow pitter-pattering in time as I watched the
thing that looked like my mother but did not love me a mote sang in that
same tone, with that same voice. I turned.

Just in time to reel back, as the hands of the Moon-skinned Man closed
about the space where my head had been.

I fell, staring up in silent horror at the figure, the slice of
blackness that cut the heart from the morning’s dawn. As close as it had
been in years. It tilted its head with an animal curiosity, hands yet to
drop as its membrane flesh pulsed, squeezed, grew and shrank its
featureless face. It seemed disappointed.

I scrabbled backwards, broken glass and cobbles scaring my palms and
leaving sluggish trails of skin and blood. The Moonskin watched them
form. Then it put its arms out to its sides, stretched in a capital T
shape, and walked along the path I had left. One foot in front of the
other. Tottering, as if on a tightrope, the strands that hung from it
lapping at the gory trail. Still it played its awful, awful games.

I flipped to my knees and launched myself upright, fighting to keep my
footing as I ran. I didn’t have the luxury to look anything other than
insane as I tore down the side streets like a man possessed, spraying
the reddened gravel that had embedded itself in my hands.

Eventually I found the exit onto the highstreet and barrelled through,
knocking a few vacant pedestrians to the ground. I stepped over and on
them, desperately keeping balance, shoving and kneading my way through
the dough of shoppers. I hadn’t known exactly where the side streets
would spit me out, but I had gotten lucky; the opticians stood not a
hundred metres away, tucked behind an old merry-go-round squealing with
rust and children. I jumped onto it to a chorus of gasps and slipped
through the bars as they turned, turned, grasping the waggling tongues
of fibreglass horses to keep myself steady. Then I was through, and
stood in the greenish glow of the glass doors. They were automatic, damn
them to hell, and in the millisecond they took to trundle apart I took
another look at the path I had carved.

The Moonskin walked the furrow like a pastor amidst his flock. Ten
metres away, less, a floater in the eye of the world and drifting closer
with every kind, gentle step. I looked away and passed through the
doors.

A receptionist said something but I darted by, making a b-line for the
racks where dozens of glasses looked down at me like a jury delivering
their verdict. I went to the rack with the ‘Reading Glasses’ header, and
began to snatch pair after pairo. No time to read the prescription; I
grabbed a handful of each kind and pressed my back against the wall,
looking out at the doors where the Moonskin drew closer. I tried the
first: blurry, painful. The Moonskin reached the doors. I tried the
next: the world warped to fishbowl glass. The Moonskin entered. The
next, the next, the next; haze, migraine, blur. The Moonskin reached
through it all, a metre away, a half metre, a centimetre. Its face drew
inwards as if to kiss me, swallow me, as I snatched the last pair and
slipped them on with a cry of terror.

And my vision cleared. The Moon-skinned Man was gone.

I slipped to the floor, dragging stands and mirrors down with me, and
wept.

They let me go. There was some talk of calling the police, but after
flashing a look of pure, grey faced mania across my broken face, the
staff decided it wasn’t worth the trouble. So I limped back onto the
high street with a pair of fuchsia reading glasses a size and a half too
small. They were a vice at my temples, but better than the alternative.
I shuddered.

I can’t live like this, I thought. Trading out glasses until the day I
was just a second too slow on the draw. There had to be a something I
could do. Something permanent.

The solution came so readily I almost dismissed it out of hand. The game
had its rules; if I could see, I was safe. And if I knew the rules, I
could break them. Despite the pain and the waning fear, I found myself
smiling.

It took all the money I had to hand. Even the formerly untouchable car
fund I had spent the last year accruing. Surprisingly, that was the
difficult part; the appointment was made within the hour, and the
following morning I was inside the clinic, discussing payment plans with
a stout ophthalmologist.

“Is this for the one eye, or both?”

“Both.”

“Mhm.”

He tapped something into a desktop computer that looked to have seen the
millenniums turn, then looked back to me. “You’re rather young for the
procedure. Ideally, we’d wait until around twenty five when your
prescription has stabilised.”

“I’m not waiting.”

“Mhm.” He brushed a thick, wiry thumb across the stubble at his jaw,
studying me a moment. Then he shrugged. “Not my business, I suppose.
When are you available?”

“Now,” I snapped. Then, after a shallow breath: “Today, please.”

“Today?” The doctor raised his eyebrows, bushy as cattails, and gave a
little smirk. “Optimistic, aren’t you?” He looked back to the computer
and clicked through a few grinding tabs. “Next week, that’s our
earliest. That alright?”

I bit my lip. I wanted to spit, to tell him that no, it wasn’t all
bloody right. But from the impassive look he gave I knew he was well
versed in desperation, and didn’t care for the taste. If I fought I
would only lose ground.

“Next week,” I managed to choke.

For a long moment, his only reply was a steady, curious look. Then he
gave another shrug. “Thursday then, 8:15. Now remember, no contacts, no
perfumes, no lotions around the eyes. And you’ll need someone to drive
you home afterwards. The laser’s been known to sting a bit.” He gave a
wry smile. “Or so I’ve been told.”

After a few sleepless, furtive days thick with nightshade terrors, I
stood once again in the clinic’s limelight. I would never have left at
all had I not feared the Moonskin catching up, taking me in that tight
sliver where I would be tied down and helpless, my eyes held wide by
speculum and dread. I hoped that I had spared myself that, at least.

The doors clicked open at a minute past the hour, a minute drawn like a
razor down the length of my spine. I hurried inside, gave my name, and
ripped my nails ragged until I was called into a small room that smelled
of wet wipes and toothpaste.

There are no words to describe how strange it felt. To have my eyes
peeled like orange skins, to have the sweet jellies beneath touched in a
manner they had never - should never - have known. Even numbed as they
were, I still felt the coldness of the blade as it slipped beneath and
gently gouged, lifting the corneas back on their plasticine hinges. And
then the laser: etching, rewriting, scratching out the imperfections
with nothing but a quiet, whirring song and the scent of burnt tissue. I
hardly recall the aftermath, the payment, the stitch up or the exiting.
But I will remember those sensations until the end.

All told, I was lucky. Aside from a slight dryness and the sensation of
sand when I blinked, my vision began to recover within the first two
hours. Not in the sharpest relief, but relief all the same: passing
faces were more than scabs, their bodies no longer clay-limbed and
worming with dark, quivering rot. I could see, without stain, without
pain. I spent the day trekking through familiar paths and taking the
sights in all their absolutes, their clarities. The park scampered,
scuttled, things four legged and many, things chittering and singing,
and I could see them all. I began to laugh.

The Moon-skinned Man laughed with me.

So gently. So kindly. A brook’s babble, a petal’s last falling. A sound
certain, and clear, and so, so awful. I looked to its source.

There. At the end of a long, arcing path that swayed beneath trees both
dead and dying, the shadow of their branches dyed amber in the growing
sun: the familiar figure stood. And it had never been clearer.

No, I thought, my eyes burning with anguish. No this isn’t right. I
solved it, I beat you.

But had I? I had spent so long assuming that sight was the trigger, the
glasses a means to a temporary end. But in that moment I finally
understood. The eyes, after all, are the windows to the soul; all I had
done was take down the barricades.

The Moonskin began its slow, steady descent down the gently sloping
trail. It grew larger as it did. Not just in perspective; the legs and
arms thickened, widened. The chest, once starved and slim, began to
swell. The membrane flesh had turned a brilliant azure, and with my
newly fixed gaze I saw now that it was not just a featureless line
jutting a scant few roots, but roots that had twisted themselves into
silhouette, like maggots outlining a scrap of mulching meat. They curled
in their millions, each swelling grotesquely as the Moonskin stepped
closer, closer, grew wider, wider. And amidst the horror… a familiarity.
Beyond nightmare, beyond memory. What the hell was it, what did it look
like.

It wasn’t until the thing was thrice the size and a third of the
distance that the comparison clicked. All those years ago. In the
office, with the doctor, and his little prising light. They showed me.
The inside of my own eye.

I stepped back on shaking legs, taking in the immensity of the thing,
the living iris, the all-swallowing pupil, the glimpse of moonlight that
had made itself a man. It grew ever wider, flattening its every feature
until it was a shadowed disk arcing with twitching, undulating branches
that shone every colour of the rainbow, latching the daylight and
leeching the sky, making all the world its hideous, staring sclera.

And here I sit. In my home, my knees drawn tight and blanket wrapped
about my head. A childish guard that will not save me when the horizon
is swallowed and the new dark sun’s rays touch me. The city is screaming
below. The city is silent. There are people running for their lives.
There are people who were never born at all. But as I overlook the
swelling, eternal eclipse, I see none of them. I hear only the faintest
plucking of guitar strings; that sweet, gentle song.

“Moon-skinned, smooth skinned,
Hiding in your room’s skin.
Corner creeping, window peeping,
My my my.
Moon-skinned, soon skinned,
Touch upon your cool skin.
Watching and a-waiting
For a sweet young eye.”
