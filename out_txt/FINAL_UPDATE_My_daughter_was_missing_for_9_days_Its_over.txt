I’m sorry if my last post was a bit ominous, but I’m back: alive and
(not) well. It’s not a happy ending, but at this point, I’m just writing
to cope.

Maybe I have a devil on my shoulder making sure I’ll always live to
suffer another day, or maybe I’m just unlucky.  Either way, I’m back.
This time it’s truly over. There can’t ever be anything else to say
after I’ve put these words down.

I’m a believer now. I’ve seen there’s more to this world than they want
us to know. There’s something else out there.

And it hates us.

 

The old house was even worse than I remembered. It had never been in
great condition, but now that the city had abandoned it, there was
nothing to slow nature’s relentless reclamation.

Yet, it stood the full height of its three stories—a dishonored tower
rising from a square foundation. A turret, stretching from the left of
its front facade, culminated in a conical roof that reached even higher
than the main building.

Its bricks sat askew, and the structure sagged, threatening to collapse
and bring any potential intruder down with it. Innumerable vines in
shades of red and dying green worked tirelessly to bring about its fall
like maggots picking away at a body.

Or maybe they were really holding it up: the overreaching roots annexing
the failing manufactured skeleton and bolstering it with nature’s
ageless vigor. A symbiotic relationship had regrown the house into an
ancient oak, unperturbed by the passage of eternity.

Or maybe I’ve gone a bit insane, and it was nothing more than a
million-dollar flowerpot.

I’ll admit I found some unexpected reassurance when I grabbed my late
husband’s pistol from the passenger seat and stuck it under my
waistband. (No need to call me out for not bringing a holster or
whatever. I sold anything relating to firearms that wasn’t tucked away
in his gun safe. I couldn’t bring myself to sell his guns, but I had no
intention of ever using them.)

I got out of the car and walked up to the main gate. The fence was
vintage wrought iron, once a beautiful piece of original Victorian
design. Now, it was rusted junk. Several sections had toppled over,
abolishing any protection the perimeter had ever offered.

Jake had told me the gate was finicky, but it wasn’t even latched. Not
when I got there, anyway.

It slid open with a long, shrill creak, its bars scratching and tearing
at the grass that had begun growing out of the cracked tiled pathway
leading up to the main entrance.

The sun was still high in the sky, but the tower’s looming shadow took
its light away from me. I made my way onto the porch, carefully avoiding
the rotting step someone had walked through. The hole was old enough to
now be filled with sprouting foliage.

I reached for the handle, but I’m fairly certain the door squeaked open
just before my hand made contact.

No matter how much I had expected it, the musty scent of decaying wood,
amplified by the ceaseless autumn drizzle, got to me: when I pushed the
door further, I could only imagine the dust swirling through the air as
mold spores ready to invade my throat and lungs. They could have them.

The house itself was like a time capsule. The wooden floor and green
wallpaper, even worn out as they were, almost felt cozy. I stood in a
foyer with arches leading deeper into the house to my immediate left and
at the end of the hallway. Another option was an old staircase that
looked sturdy enough, but I wouldn’t be going up unless I had to.

I called out to my daughter.

My overactive imagination transformed the many groans of the house into
a sign. I went left, following my gut.

“Please, if you’re here,” I pleaded, “just tell me what’s wrong and
we’ll fix it together.”

My only answer was another moan.

I took a deep breath.

“I believe you, OK?” I yelled. “I believe that you aren’t the girl I
have given birth to. But it doesn’t matter. It really doesn’t.”

Nothing.

I went further in, losing myself in an endless maze of rot and decay.
There was nothing unusual or scary about this place, really. Just an
abandoned old house like there are thousands and thousands in this part
of the country. This one was just bigger than most.

This rational thought, however, was consumed by dread when I turned a
corner and came face to face with fear.

A wide-open door invited me into a darkness so pure it defied every
attempt by the surrounding windows to disperse it.

The basement.

Of course.

I made it up to the doorway and stared down the stairs, into the
impenetrable black.

I pulled my cellphone out of my pocket and, before I turned on the
flashlight and truly committed, I spoke one last time:

“You said your mother’s here, right? I’ll deal with her.”

This answer was anything but make-believe: the house shook, rocked by a
quaking crash. Thick particles of dust exploded out of the shadows as
the noise thundered through the air.

I clicked open the light and went into the basement.

Once again, this room would have been nothing out of the ordinary for
this style of architecture: a great stone room covered wall to wall with
shelves, racks, and other forms of storage in various states of decay.

My eyes were drawn, however, to a fallen cupboard, shattered across the
stone floor. Its absence from the wall revealed two things: it had been
mounted on a discreet sliding mechanism, and it had concealed an opening
in the stone wall.

Indeed, there was a hollow arch in the wall, leading into a dirt tunnel.
Someone had smashed through the stone wall to dig a tunnel beyond it.

Whoever had made this “improvement” had also etched symbols into the
edges of the opening. I previously said I’m not Van Helsing, but I’ve
read enough fantasy to confidently identify them as “runes” or something
akin to them.

The reassuring reality of knowing you have nothing left to lose is that
even the most obvious of traps can’t deter you. There’s just no tangible
link between your lizard brain and your conscious one anymore. Or maybe
it’s that your soul isn’t linked to either of those anymore.

Because my soul would see it through. No matter what.

I stepped over the broken furniture and into the tunnel. I was surprised
when I noticed how much better it smelled in there. Having left behind
the smell of death and decay, I now bathed in a more pleasant scent. It
smelled just like a forest after the rain: mud, fertile soil, and a hint
of flora.

As I sank deeper and deeper into the earth, following the tunnel
downward, the scent grew stronger and stronger. At regular intervals, my
flashlight revealed additional runes engraved into stones that lay on
the floor or were embedded in the walls, surrounded by the endless roots
that held this passageway open.

Just as I was getting used to the tunnel, the walls ran away from my
light, and the pleasant fragrance of fresh flowers overwhelmed my
senses.

I scanned my surroundings, revealing an antechamber dug into the earth.
It was by no means gigantic, but it was big enough to make me wonder
just how much effort it must have taken to excavate.

With the notable exception of another sequence of runes, it was
completely devoid of man’s touch.

Its only discernible feature was a large circle of mushrooms at its
center.

I shone my light over the circle, but the beam disappeared into the
dirt. Inside the circle, the soil was much darker and appeared to be
waterlogged, somehow. From this miniature bog, roots sprang upward and
into the room, as if the tree they belonged to grew right beneath.

I hadn’t noticed them at first, but two white orbs stuck out of the
middle of the circle, poking out of the roots and mud. I got closer to
the circle, just close enough to stand at its edge.

I called my daughter’s name once more.

The spheres blinked out of existence.

I slowly extended my leg over the circle.

As soon as it crossed the threshold, thick roots sprang to life and
wrapped around my ankle.

The unadulterated force of nature pulled me into the earth.

I held my breath and closed my eyes, but I was never buried. Instead, a
forceful light suddenly knocked at my eyelids, and I felt my stomach
weight on my lungs. Something was holding me upside down. I could feel
its death grip on my ankle.

I opened my eyes. Daylight shone from far above me, but I could still
see the mushroom circle, growing just outside the light’s scope.

More significantly, the shadows also hid a silhouette not unlike one I
had grown accustomed to: two glowing orbs stare at me out of a being of
pure darkness.

But this was not my daughter.

It was not curled in on itself, ashamed and hopeless. It stood proud and
tall. Really tall, in fact.

It might have been about eight feet tall, but that estimation did not
account for its horns: two massive tangles of forks sprouted from its
forehead, adding another four feet to its height. These appendages were
not unlike a deer’s antlers, but these were far more complex in nature,
like growths from a tree that had been left to flourish upon itself.

From the shape of its shadow, I could also discern long claws on its
free hand and two round legs that reached the ground without ever
breaking into feet.

I reached behind my back and grabbed at my waistband, only to find my
weapon gone.

Disappointed, I tucked my chin into my chest and took a closer look at
the hand that held me. In the light, its chalk-white skin gleamed. Its
hand and the part of its forearm I could see were almost human.

But its fingertips betrayed they weren’t. Instead of nails, oversized
branchlets grew from its hand.

In fact, its forearm was punctured all over, twigs growing from the
holes as if from a bough.

It stood there, staring at me as I wiggled around, hoping to escape its
grasp.

“Who the fuck are you?” I finally snapped when my helplessness was very
and truly established

A voice answered. But it did not come from the creature. It was as if
the grotto itself answered me, its voice an ethereal echo bouncing all
around me:

“Mortals have forced many a trend 
Upon those I tend:
Fiend, fae, friend. 
But while you cling,
To this godsend,
Only your notion of a king,
Wouldn’t offend.

Scholars would call me Oberon, human.
That would parade their acumen.”

“I asked to speak to her mother!” I retorted.

The vibration rejoined:

“Do not make me laugh.
I should write your epitaph.
To me you shall speak.
I am the mother’s beak.”

“Fine!” I roared. “Give me my daughter back!”

The cave shook as its song grew louder:

“You have no reason to be brave,
As you have no daughter to save.
The onlookers told you.
Please stop regressing;
Accept that it’s true,
She was naught but dressing.”

“I’m not talking about the first you took,” I tried to clarify. “You
know what daught—”

As soon as the first syllable escaped my mouth, the grip around my leg
vanished, letting gravity reclaim me at last. I had the foresight of
putting my arms under my head before I crashed into the earth. The dirt
was still soft and wet, but I still strained my neck as my body rolled
over my skull.

The ground shook under me as the world boomed:

“My patience grows thin:
You’re no mother of my kin!
A pig likening their scat to a rose!
Wouldn’t you justly oppose?”

An abyssal breeze, cold as death, swept through the antechamber. After
it had passed through, the soothing voice was restored:

“I’ll forgive your nature of exceedance. 
But there is no third chance.”

“I’m sorry,” I said through gritted teeth, while I clutched my neck.
“But please, why have you taken the girl I raised? What do you want from
her?”

It answered:

“We had a deal.
If her due was paid,
With you she could have stayed.
My word is steel.”

“What’s the price?” I asked, pulling myself up to my feet. “I’ll pay it.
I’ll give anything to have her back.”

A massive timber landed into the circle as the creature stepped into the
light, its enigmatic evil suddenly transformed into dazzling elegance.

Oberon was sculpted from marble—the magnum opus of a forgotten Greek
artisan. Every muscle in its body was perfectly defined: its biceps rose
in pronounced mounds; its abdomen was carefully moulded into eight
distinct sections, with no navel to interrupt their symmetry; its
rounded shoulders flowed seamlessly into its pectoral muscles; its
powerful thighs flanked a generously proportioned organ.

But all this power appeared effortless and elementary, as if its muscles
were nothing more than the natural shape of its body.

The same impossible perfection extended to its jaw: sharp enough to cut
diamonds but softened by a subtle roundness suggestive of a gentle soul.

Its more human features were a mockery of themselves. They were innately
flawless, as if everything our bodies could achieve were insignificant
by comparison. This truth was made clearer by the otherworldly features
bursting out of its skin.

Its horns were two massive, interwoven sets of dark branches growing out
of its head, while smaller growths fashioned a pointed crown around its
skull. From its scalp hung sparse strands of long red, orange, and green
hair, mirroring the few leaves still clinging to its numerous ligneous
appendages.

Its powerful human legs ended just below the knees, from which thick,
log-like growths emerged, creating a shape vaguely reminiscent of
hooves.

Even through the glimmer of its skin and in the light of the sun, its
eyes shone like two dwarf stars.

The voice sang:

“Her price was one soul only. 
But you are not my kin,
And you sound so lonely;
I’ll gladly take your skin.

A soul each year to check out
Up until your breath runs out.
Do not pout;
This is a handout.”

“Do you mean one life per year until I die?” I decried. “There’s no way
I can do that! Just take me and let her go!”

Its opalescent lips parted, revealing two rows of well-shaped teeth.
Their nature, however, was betrayed by their dark brown color, matching
the creature’s horns.

“Your soul was ceded,
The moment you pleaded.
You’ll need more,
To even the score.”

“I can’t, OK!” I screamed. “You have to know I can’t pay this!” Please,
you have to unders—”

It took a step forward and slammed its palm into my face, shoving my
head back and reigniting the pain in my neck.

The reverberations grew stronger once more:

“Cease your yapping.
I’ll do the flapping.

Give me a heart that beats for you,
For betrayal brews a good stew.
Only then could I deal,
And spare you an ordeal.

But be warned,
I will not be scorned.

Many try to beat my accord.
They all suffer my sword.

I would know to do worst
By going after her first
Your dreams would be cursed
By her pleading to be nursed.
This torment each night you rest.
Do not put me to the test.

Run yourself through
To avoid my due,
You will know true
What I can do.”

It released my jaw, slowly withdrawing its hand so the full length of
its claws dragged across my cheeks. It then dropped to one knee,
bringing our faces almost level.

Its blazing eyes peered into my all as it presented the palm of the hand
it had just used to assault me.

The cave rung one final time, its voice fading away as its final words
reached my ears:

“Give me your hands if we be friends.
On this her freedom depends.”

I refused.

The light blinked out of existence, alongside Oberon, and I was left
alone in darkness.

As my eyes readjusted, I caught a glimpse of a more modest lighting: the
light from my phone laying on the floor. I snatched it and made my way
back home as if nothing had ever happened.

I know it’s a bit anticlimactic, but you have to understand that I
couldn’t play its game.

 

This is my last update, that much is certain. There’s nothing left to
say about the girl I raised. I can only hope she’s still alive on the
other side. That thought keeps me going. Maybe it’s not so bad there. It
smelled pleasant enough at least.

 

Anyway, you’ll have to excuse me; I have a hot date with my officer
friend, the one that questioned her. I guess it’s time to move on, maybe
find love again, you know.

So, good night unto you all.
