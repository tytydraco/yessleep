The first thing Serena stole from me, she took with both hands around
her tea.

I was her nurse on a locked psychiatric ward.

At 8:14 p.m. the sugar packet beside my coffee was gone. She drew it out
of her sleeve and set it back down.

“That one was me.”

She pressed two fingers against the outside of her right cardigan
pocket.

“The hand in here wasn’t.”

Her eyes moved from my face to my hands.

“Watch both hands.”

Fourteen minutes earlier, Nunzia had given me the strangest police
summary I had ever received at handover.

Serena worked as an escort. The thefts were a separate business, and she
was apparently excellent at that one too.

That morning, hotel cleaners had let themselves into a room and found
one of her clients still inside. He was unhurt. Before he said anything
else, he asked them twice to shut the door and forget the room number.

They called security anyway.

His wallet, watch and cash were in Serena’s handbag. Reporting the theft
would have meant explaining the rest of the morning.

The theft brought Serena to the police. Her condition brought her to us.

By then she had gone almost three days without sleep. She knew her name,
not where she was. She kept tearing at the lining of her coat because
she believed fingers were moving inside it. She kept trying to leave,
and could not name a single safe place to go.

A doctor proposed it, a second confirmed it, and the mayor signed the
order that held her. A judge would come to the ward within two days to
hear her before deciding whether it stood. She was told, in writing, how
to fight it.

“Don’t let the paperwork fool you,” Nunzia said. “She can sound
completely clear for five minutes. Then she starts searching for hands
inside her clothes.”

Nunzia closed the chart.

“There’s one more thing. She keeps asking about a photograph from the
man’s wallet.”

“What photograph?”

“The police say there wasn’t one.”

Serena looked exhausted and was still striking. The red on her lips had
been applied before the hotel; by handover it had cracked at one corner,
but it held.

Her advantage was not the makeup. It was the precision of her attention.
She knew when to hold eye contact half a second too long, and what a man
would offer to fill the silence after.

“Married,” she said, touching her own ring finger.

“We’re discussing you.”

“We were.”

She smiled as if I had passed a test.

I put the sugar packet in my right scrub pocket and went on with the
medication round.

At 8:57 p.m., Marta came out of the bathroom with a dark stain spreading
across the back of her pajama pants.

Serena saw it before anyone else did.

She stepped behind Marta and walked so closely that her own body blocked
the view from the common room. She kept talking about the broken coffee
machine while Giulia brought clean clothes.

Marta never had to explain herself.

When Serena returned, she said nothing about it. She sat down and began
separating the paper layers of a tea bag with one fingernail.

Twenty minutes later, my blue pen disappeared.

I had clipped it inside my chest pocket after signing the medication
record. Serena had passed close enough for her cardigan to touch my
sleeve.

I found the pen behind her right ear.

“Give it back.”

“You watched both hands.”

“Give it back, Serena.”

Her smile vanished. She placed the pen on the table.

“I needed to know that one was me.”

I documented the boundary violation and told her not to touch my uniform
again.

She nodded. No joke. No attempt to bargain.

At 10:38 p.m., Serena called me from room 4.

Her right cardigan pocket was hanging inside out. A flat black comb she
had placed there was now inside the left pocket.

Marta was in the common room. Serena had been alone.

“It touched my elbow first,” she said.

She pointed to the inside of her left arm.

“Then here.”

Her right hip.

With Serena’s consent and Giulia beside me, I examined the cardigan. The
lining had split between the pockets. Giulia pushed the comb through the
opening. It disappeared between the two layers of fabric and emerged
inside the other pocket.

“There,” she said. “Bad stitching.”

Serena gave one dry laugh.

“Cheap stitching is still criminal.”

The explanation held. Loose fabric could brush her skin. An object could
move between the pockets. Pulling against the lining could turn one of
them inside out.

We locked the cardigan with her other belongings and gave her a
pocketless ward top and pants.

Serena looked down at her sides.

“No pockets,” I said.

“Good.”

Her shoulders lowered. She drank another tea and slept in the
common-room armchair for thirty-four minutes.

The radiator under the window ticked as it cooled. The building is older
than the ward it carries. It was built for people who stayed the night,
whoever they were.

At 12:33 a.m., she woke without lifting her head.

I was standing beside the table, entering an observation into the chart.
The sugar packet was still in my right scrub pocket.

Paper crackled against my hip.

Then something pressed the inside of my left elbow.

Not a brush. One measured push.

My body turned toward it before I decided to move.

Nothing was beside me except the table.

Across from me, Serena had both hands wrapped around her cup. She looked
at my elbow. Then at my right pocket.

“Left first,” she said. “Then right.”

I pulled out the sugar packet. It had been crushed into a tight square.

I put it back and wiped my palm on my thigh, twice, before I noticed I
was doing it.

Giulia said I had probably pressed it against the edge of the table.
That explained the paper. Fatigue could explain the sensation at my
elbow.

It was a good explanation.

I called Stefano, the on-call psychiatrist, and described Serena’s
escalating fear before mentioning what I had felt.

“You found the torn lining,” he said. “She hasn’t slept.”

“She isn’t wearing the cardigan now.”

Silence. Then I heard him typing.

“Keep her where you can observe her. Offer the medication already
prescribed. Call again if anything changes.”

Serena refused it.

“I need my hands awake.”

She remained in the common room. Giulia sat near the ward station while
I took the chair across from Serena.

At 1:26 a.m., she told me where the touch came from.

Her mother had taught her to pick pockets when Serena was ten.

The method was simple. A knuckle pressed against the inside of the left
elbow. The body turned toward the contact. Two fingers entered the right
pocket while attention was somewhere else.

Serena improved the technique when she got older. She no longer needed
crowded markets. Her face did the turning for her.

She claimed she had never taught the method to anyone.

Her mother had been dead for eleven years.

In the hotel room, Serena had already placed the wallet, watch and cash
inside her handbag. She had checked the bathroom and wardrobe before he
arrived. No one else should have been there.

Inside the wallet, behind one of his cards, she found a photograph. She
pulled it out and slid it into the right pocket of her cardigan.

Then something pressed her left elbow.

Two fingers entered that same pocket.

“What did it take?” I asked.

Serena looked toward the locked room where we had stored her cardigan.

“Nothing the police believed existed.”

At 2:17 a.m., she pushed away from the table and backed against the
dining-room wall.

Her hands rose to shoulder height.

“Watch both hands.”

They were shaking now.

Giulia moved to one side of the room. I approached from the front with
my palms visible.

“Don’t touch me,” Serena said.

I stopped.

She twisted the pocketless fabric against her right hip, trying to hold
it flat. Her breathing shortened.

Closing the distance would only give her another pair of hands to fear.
I pulled a chair into her line of sight and sat six feet away.

“Put your hands on the table,” she said.

I placed both palms flat where she could see them.

“They’re here.”

Serena came forward slowly. She placed her palms opposite mine. Giulia
remained near the entrance, watching her.

For several seconds, no one moved.

Then a knuckle pressed into my left elbow.

My body turned before I chose to move.

Two fingers pulled at my right scrub pocket.

My right hand left the table and clamped over it. The lining came
through with my fingers. The sugar packet dropped onto the floor.

Serena was still sitting across from me. Both palms were flat against
the table.

“Did you feel the second hand?” she asked.

My mouth had gone dry.

“I felt my pocket move.”

I looked at the fabric hanging outside my uniform.

“I don’t know why.”

She lowered her head but kept her hands where we could see them.

“The photograph was my mother.”

I said nothing.

“In his wallet,” she continued. “The same photograph we buried with
her.”

“The man could have known her.”

“She had been dead eleven years.”

Giulia examined the seam of my pocket. It was intact. She had been
watching Serena’s hands, not mine. She could not tell whether the pocket
had turned before I grabbed it or because I did.

I called Stefano again. I reported Serena’s distress, her exact words,
where each of us had been sitting, and the objective condition of my
pocket.

My interpretation stayed out of the chart.

Four days later the order was lifted and Serena was discharged. Two
officers were waiting outside the unit to take her back for questioning
about the man from the hotel.

Serena covered both cardigan pockets with her hands and ignored every
question about the stolen property.

Before she left, she looked at me.

“Does he still carry my mother?”

I still use that scrub top. The seam has never opened.

I keep the right pocket empty now. My wife asked about it once, folding
laundry, and I told her the fabric had gone thin.

Three weeks ago I found something in it at the end of a shift.

A sugar packet, crushed into a tight square.

I don’t take sugar.
