I’ve been an urban explorer for over a year now. I’ve trekked through
collapsed asbestos factories, overgrown 19th century graveyards, and
whole abandoned villages rotting in the woods. Nothing ever happened. No
ghosts, no monsters. Just dust, broken glass, and silence. I started to
believe the supernatural was just something people made up to sell
podcasts.

Then I read about the St. Jude Apartments.

An old forum thread claimed seven children had gone missing in that
building over the years, all last seen anywhere near its block before
vanishing without a trace. The authorities eventually condemned it, and
the city left it to rot. Naturally, I grabbed my flashlight, my phone,
and drove out at midnight.

When I stepped through the broken front doorway, it felt like any other
abandoned ruin. The air was thick with damp drywall and mold. Floor 1
had three doors lining the corridor: left, middle, right. All stood
slightly ajar.

I pushed open the middle door first. My flashlight beam cut through
cobwebs dangling like thick curtains. Furniture sat covered in grey
sheets. As I swung my light toward the center of the living room, a
sound cut through the silence so sharply my chest tightened.

A baby crying. Soft, ragged, echoing right from the dark hallway behind
the living room.

My heart hammered against my ribs. “Hello?” I called out, my voice
cracking. “Is someone in here?”

No answer. Just the crying, rhythmic and hollow. I stepped into the
hallway, raising my light. The sound led me straight to a back bedroom.
Inside sat a single wooden cradle, thick with dust.

It was empty. The moment my beam hit the wooden bars, the crying stopped
instantly.

Cold sweat broke out across my neck. I backed out of the apartment,
retreated into the main hallway, and decided to try the left door
instead. I was terrified, but my explorer’s ego refused to let me run
over a mind trick.

The left apartment was completely stripped. Bare floorboards, exposed
pipes. Then, directly above my head, I heard it: rapid, heavy running
footsteps pacing back and forth across the ceiling of the second floor.

I bolted out of the room, took the concrete stairs two at a time to
floor 2, and threw open the left door. Nothing. The room was bone dry
and still. I backed out, heart thumping in my throat, and crossed the
hall to the opposite door.

Inside, as I shone my light across peeling wallpaper, a soft,
high-pitched child’s laugh echoed from the corner.

I froze. Every instinct told me to run, but my boots felt glued to the
floor. I slowly moved my flashlight across the plaster. Scratched deep
into the wall near the floorboards, carved in jagged, desperate strokes,
was a single word:

ARTHUR

I had no idea who Arthur was, but the raw malice in those deep grooves
sent a chill straight down my spine. I backed into the corridor and
slipped into the middle apartment on Floor 2, desperate for something
grounded.

On a rusted nightstand, I found a leather binder filled with yellowed
paper. Hospital records from St. Mary’s General Hospital, dated December
12, 1992. One sheet was highlighted in faded blue ink:

My hand shook as I held the paper. Arthur Vance. A man who lost his
entire world in a single night at St. Mary’s.

I took the stairs to Floor 3, drawn higher into the building like a moth
to a flame. I entered the right hand apartment. Pinned to the walls were
dozens more pages from St. Mary’s General, all from that exact same
month in 1992. But these babies hadn’t died.

Seven names were circled in dark, aggressive red ink.

Seven children born in the same hospital, while his baby died. The
internet post flashed in my head. Seven missing kids. He hadn’t just
grieved; he had hunted them down, years later, bringing them to this
exact building to balance a horrific ledger in his mind.

Dread pooled in my stomach. I backed out and entered the left apartment
on floor 3, looking for anything else. In the center of the room,
resting on an overturned wooden crate, sat an old hunting knife. Its
blade was caked in thick, dark, dried blood.

I stumbled backward out of the room, my breathing shallow and fast. I
stepped toward the middle door of floor 3, but the second my foot
crossed the threshold, a chorus of loud, agonizing children’s screams
erupted from inside the walls, dozens of voices wailing at once.

That was it. I snapped.

I turned on my heel and sprinted down the staircase. I flew past the
landing of floor 2, hit the landing for floor 1, expecting to see the
double doors leading out into the night air.

Instead, I burst onto a hallway with three wooden doors. floor 3.

I stopped, gasping for air. Thinking I had somehow miscounted, I turned
and practically threw myself down the stairs again. Down, down, down. I
hit the bottom landing, sprinted out, and stood in front of the exact
same three doors from floor 1.

Panicking, I turned around and ran up the stairs. Higher and higher
until my legs burned, hoping to find a window or a fire escape. I burst
through the door at the top.

Floor 1.

There is no front door. There are no exit signs. The stairwells don’t
lead outside anymore; they just feed into each other in a endless loop.
The building isn’t just an abandoned ruin, it’s a cage built out of
Arthur’s sin, and the spirits of those seven kids aren’t letting anyone
leave.

I’m sitting on the concrete steps between the landings right now. My
flashlight is flickering, down to its last bit of battery, and I’m
typing this out on my phone while I still have a faint cell signal.

If you’re reading this, do not come looking for the St. Jude Apartments.

The battery is dying. And I can hear heavy, dragging footsteps starting
to climb the stairs from below.
