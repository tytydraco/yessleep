My dad built the antenna out of two lengths of aluminum tube and some
PVC he bought at Lowe’s for next to nothing. He bolted it to the chimney
the summer I turned fourteen and told me that if I aimed it right and
stayed patient, it would let us hear satellites.

Dad said stuff like that about everything, so I didn’t believe him until
the night we sat at the kitchen table with a cheap radio plugged into
his laptop and the volume turned all the way up.

If you’ve never heard a NOAA weather satellite, look up “NOAA-APT
signal” and play the first result. It sounds like somebody is winding a
clock in the next room. Tick tock, twice a second, with a wet metallic
hiss underneath. The tick tock is what’s called a sync pulse, and the
hiss is the picture. You record the audio while the satellite passes
overhead, run it through a decoder, and it turns into a long strip of
the Earth. The clouds over your house fifteen minutes ago, beamed down
from hundreds of miles up by a machine launched before you were even
born.

Dad said the tick tock was the satellite saying, “I’m here, I’m here,
I’m here!” He thought it was the most comforting sound in the world, and
for a long time I did too.

We did it for years: hurricanes, lake effect snow, the time a wildfire
in Canada turned the whole strip gray. We printed our favorites and put
them on the fridge. When Dad got sick, I set the laptop up in his room
so he could hear the passes from bed.

Dad died in March of last year, and that summer, NOAA shut down the last
three satellites that still sent those signals. NOAA 18 in June. NOAA 19
and NOAA 15 in August.

I didn’t take the antenna down. I didn’t even turn the schedule off.
Every night the laptop still wakes up at the old pass times, tunes to
137.620 (NOAA 15’s frequency), and records for fifteen minutes. Fifteen
minutes of nothing. My sister said it was like setting a place at the
table for someone who isn’t coming. She wasn’t wrong, but I kept doing
it anyway.

November 1st

I checked the folder while I ate breakfast, like I always do. All of the
files are the same size because static compresses the same way every
night. For some reason, the 3:12 AM file was bigger. I put my headphones
on and played it.

Tick tock. Tick tock.

I stood up from the table so fast I got lightheaded, and then I just
stood there, because I didn’t know where I was going. I ran the file
through the decoder. Clean signal, almost no noise. A front stretched
across Indiana and Ohio, Lake Erie dark and smooth, a hook of cloud
curling over Michigan. I pulled up the radar archive for 3 AM and it
matched. The calibration wedges down the side of the image, eight gray
steps from black to white, were perfect. Exactly how they’re supposed to
look.

Dead satellites waking up isn’t unheard of. There’s a ham radio
satellite called AO 7 that died in 1981 and started transmitting again
in 2002 when its batteries shorted the right way. I figured that’s what
this was. NOAA 15 had found a way to say “I’m here” one more time.

I posted the image on a satellite forum. Within an hour, a user named
dopplrdan replied.

“Cool image, but that’s not 15. Check your Doppler.”

When a satellite passes over you, its signal shifts in frequency. Higher
as it approaches, lower as it leaves. The curve tells you exactly when
it was closest to you. Dan pulled my recording apart and showed me the
curve peaked four minutes before NOAA 15’s closest approach. Wrong
timing, wrong object.

Then he posted a link to Space-Track, the government’s catalog of
everything in orbit.

There was a new entry. An analyst object, which is what they call
something they’re tracking but can’t identify. Name: TBA - TO BE
ASSIGNED. First observed October 31st.

Same altitude as NOAA 15, but four minutes ahead of it. Dan checked
every launch log he could find. Nothing went up on the 31st. In fact,
nothing went up that whole week that could reach that orbit.

“It’s like it found the old bird and parked in front of it,” Dan wrote.
“Like it’s wearing its frequency as a mask.”

________________________________________

November 2nd

The 3:12 file was big again, so I decoded it before I made coffee.

It was wrong in a way I couldn’t explain at first. A normal APT image
covers a strip about 2,900 kilometers wide, so you can usually see half
the country in one frame. This one was narrower. Ohio filled the whole
width, like it had zoomed in. Only problem is that APT can’t zoom. The
resolution is fixed at four kilometers per pixel and has been since the
seventies.

The calibration wedges were wrong too. Instead of eight steps from black
to white, they went black to white and back to black. Dan said that’s
what they would look like if someone copied the format from a picture of
a NOAA image without understanding what the wedges were for. Just
reproducing the shape of them.

Dan had his own receiver down in Knoxville, and he’d caught the same
pass. His image was zoomed in too, his on Tennessee. I didn’t sleep much
that night.

________________________________________

November 3rd

This one was Columbus. It showed I-270, the outerbelt, as a thin gray
ring around the city, and the Scioto River as a dark thread.

Something else was wrong too. An APT image is built line by line as the
satellite moves, so the picture scrolls north to south over the course
of the pass. That’s how you get the strip.

This one didn’t scroll, every line was the same patch of ground. Twelve
minutes of the same square of central Ohio, with the clouds sliding
slowly across it from west to east.

It had stopped over Columbus and stared.

I checked Space-Track. The object was still listed in a normal low
orbit, moving at seven and a half kilometers a second like everything
else up there. According to the numbers, it couldn’t have stayed over
one city for twelve seconds, let alone twelve minutes.

Dan’s image was Knoxville. Same thing. Twelve minutes of one city.
Another user posted that he’d caught it in Wisconsin and gotten Madison.
Then someone in Oregon got Eugene.

Everybody listening got their own town.

________________________________________

November 5th

I skipped a night. I told myself I wouldn’t decode the 4th…That lasted
about a day and a half.

The 4th was my neighborhood. I could make out the shape of my street,
the cul-de-sac at the end, the retention pond behind the elementary
school. The resolution wasn’t four kilometers per pixel anymore, it
wasn’t anything close.

The 5th was my roof. It took me a minute to recognize it, because I had
never seen my roof from directly above. The shingles were a gray
texture, the chimney was a small dark square. Sitting on the chimney was
a thin bright V, two legs angled apart, pointing north.

Dad’s antenna.

At night, APT switches to the infrared channel, so warm things show up
bright. The antenna shouldn’t have been warm, but it was the brightest
thing in the frame, like it was glowing.

I played the audio back to see if I’d missed anything, and that’s when I
noticed the tick tock. It was slower. Not by a lot. You wouldn’t hear
the difference unless you’d listened to a thousand passes like I have.
But the rhythm was off, so I put a metronome app next to the speaker and
counted.

A real APT signal ticks at 120 beats per minute. Two lines a second,
every satellite, every pass. The November 1st recording was 120. The 3rd
was 96. The 5th was 71.

I posted it to the forum, and Dan replied within four minutes.

“Do you wear a watch to bed?”

I do, it tracks heart rate. I’d never looked at the overnight data in my
life, so I opened the app and scrolled to 3:12 AM on the 5th.

71 beats per minute. On the 3rd, the night of the Columbus image, it was
96.

Dan said his matched too. He’d figured it out the night before and
hadn’t wanted to say anything until he was sure.

“The first pass was perfect because it was still copying NOAA,” he
wrote. “After that it stopped copying the satellite and started copying
us. It’s listening and playing back what it hears.”

I’m here. I’m here. I’m here.

________________________________________

November 8th

Dan posted his last image at 4:40 in the morning.

It was his backyard in Knoxville, shot from straight above in infrared.
A fence line, a trampoline frame, a patio, and in the middle of the
grass, a small white shape. A person, foreshortened from directly
overhead, so all you could see was the top of a head, two shoulders, and
the little bright smudge of a face tilted up, looking at the camera.

Dan’s caption was: “That’s me. I’m in bed. I haven’t been outside
tonight.”

That was the whole post. The next morning his account was gone. Not
deleted with a “[deleted]” tag. Gone. His old posts, his replies in my
thread, all of it, like he’d never been there. I messaged the
moderators. One of them said he didn’t recognize the username and asked
if I was sure I’d spelled it right.

I had screenshots. I still have them. They’re the only proof I can find
that Dan existed.

November 10th

The object has a TIP message now.

TIP stands for Tracking and Impact Prediction. Space-Track posts them
for objects that are about to fall out of orbit. They list a predicted
reentry time and location, with an uncertainty window that shrinks as
the date gets closer. Usually the object is a spent rocket stage, and
the window is “somewhere in the southern Pacific, plus or minus six
hours.”

The first TIP for TBA - TO BE ASSIGNED came out on the 9th. Plus or
minus three days, along a ground track that crossed half the planet.

The second was plus or minus one day. North America.

The third was plus or minus four hours. The track went over
Indianapolis, Dayton, and finally Columbus.

This morning’s update was plus or minus one minute. The coordinates were
four decimal places long, I didn’t need to put them into a map.

The time was 3:12 AM. Things in orbit don’t reenter like that, debris
doesn’t come down plus or minus one minute on a specific house.
Something falling doesn’t know where it’s going to land.

Something landing does.

Tonight

I decoded last night’s pass. I know, I know, but I couldn’t help myself.

It showed my backyard. The shed, the birdbath my mom put in, the back
door, a dark rectangle. And in the middle of the lawn, a white shape,
warm, looking straight up.

I could tell it was me because of the jacket. It’s Dad’s old Carhartt, I
wear it to take out the trash. In infrared it shows up a shade darker
than skin, so you could see the shape of it, the hood down, the collar.
I was holding something against my chest that glowed faintly.

The laptop.

I wasn’t outside when that pass happened, I was asleep, my watch says
so.

I don’t think Dan’s picture showed what was happening, I think it showed
what was going to happen. It shows you where you’ll be standing.

I went up on the roof at sunset and took the antenna down. I unbolted it
from the chimney and tossed both aluminum legs in the garbage can…Then I
pulled them back out, because they were Dad’s, and put them in the
basement instead. I felt stupid about that, and I’m still not sure if it
was a mistake. I unplugged the radio, put it and the laptop in the trunk
of my car, locked it, and stuck the keys in the freezer so I’d have to
work to get them back.

I took off the watch and hung Dad’s jacket in the hall closet. Now I’m
sitting at the kitchen table, the same kitchen table, typing this on my
phone. All the blinds are down, and the back door is locked. It’s 2:58
AM.

I want to be clear. I am not going outside. Whatever the picture says,
whatever it thinks it’s going to see, I’m not moving. I’ve had a lot of
time to think about it, and I think I finally understand what it’s been
doing. Every pass, it got closer. Every pass, it learned a little more.
The format, the wedges, the tick tock. It looked at Earth from far away,
then a city, then a street, then a roof. It knows what we look like from
above now.

What it doesn’t have yet is the other angle. Us, from the ground,
looking up at it, recognizing it. That’s the last picture it needs.
That’s why it showed Dan himself in his yard. I don’t think that was a
warning, I think it was an invitation.

3:06.

The radio is in a locked trunk with no antenna and no power. It can’t
receive anything, but I can hear it anyway.

It isn’t coming from the car, it’s coming from the backyard. Soft and
patient, like someone is winding a clock in the next room.

It’s going exactly as fast as my heart, and my heart is going very fast.

If I don’t update this, please don’t record on 137.620. Don’t decode
anything you already have, and if one night you hear a tick tock out in
the yard, don’t go see what it is.

Don’t look up.

3:11.

Tick tock.

I’m here. I’m here. I’m here.
