The building looked clean.

Its white and silver walls stretched upward into glass that caught the
morning light and softened it into something almost gentle. People moved
through the entrance with easy smiles, some holding flowers, some
holding hands, others speaking in quiet tones that suggested comfort
rather than urgency.

It looked like a place built to heal loneliness.

A place for new beginnings.

A place where no one would have to face the world alone.

Devlynn stepped inside.

The moment she crossed the threshold, something in her shifted—but
nothing around her confirmed it.

The floors were polished to a reflective shine. The air smelled faintly
sterile, but pleasant, like something carefully maintained rather than
lived in. Conversations drifted through the lobby, soft and constant,
never quite colliding in ways that created noise.

Everything looked exactly as it should.

And yet nothing inside her settled.

People were talking everywhere.

But none of it made sound.

A man near the entrance stood angled slightly away, lips moving in
steady rhythm. His hand lifted once, palm open, as if emphasizing a
point to someone just out of sight. He nodded, listened, smiled faintly,
like he had been understood.

Across the lobby, a woman tilted her head toward empty space, eyes
focused with quiet intensity. Her fingers moved in small, precise
gestures—answering words Devlynn couldn’t hear.

No one looked at each other.

They looked through things.

Like the people they were speaking to weren’t in the room at all.

And yet no one looked alone.

They looked occupied.

As if every empty space had already been filled by someone Devlynn
couldn’t see.

Then sound arrived.

Not gradually.

All at once.

A low layered swell of voices filled the room like a switch had been
flipped.

A red-haired woman stood near the center holding a small remote.

Click.

And the building turned on.

Now the conversations became distinct.

A man near the entrance spoke sharply, mid-argument with someone only he
could hear.

A woman nearby laughed softly at nothing anyone else could access.

Others nodded, frowned, responded, all locked into separate realities
sharing the same physical space.

It wasn’t chaos.

It was isolation made orderly.

Devlynn felt her stomach tighten.

The red-haired woman stepped closer.

“You can stare,” she said gently. “It’s not like they can feel judged.”

Devlynn didn’t answer.

“This is what it’s for,” the woman added. “Display. They do better this
way. When they’re not interrupted by each other.”

And Devlynn understood something she couldn’t yet fully name.

She wasn’t looking at something broken.

She was looking at something arranged.

The elevator ride down felt too smooth.

Too quiet.

Like even her breathing had been softened by design.

When the doors opened, the atmosphere changed immediately.

Hallways stretched in both directions, lined with doors marked by small
dark panels. The lighting was lower here. Less presentation. More
function.

People moved slowly.

Carefully.

And beside them

companions.

Human shaped support models. Calm, attentive, always present in a way
that made them easy to ignore if you tried.

A woman spoke softly at a counter while her companion mirrored her
timing exactly.

A man near a window spoke in short bursts while his companion adjusted
objects with precise care, always just slightly ahead of hesitation.

Everything felt stable.

Further down the corridor, Devlynn saw an older couple walking with a
larger companion model between them.

It wasn’t fully aligned.

Not fully finished.

Its steps came in careful unevenness, as if still learning how to exist
in motion.

The couple supported it as much as it supported them. The woman held its
arm lightly while the man adjusted his pace so it wouldn’t drift.

Devlynn slowed.

The red-haired woman noticed.

“That one’s newer,” she said casually.

“They’re helping it walk?” Devlynn asked.

A nod.

“Yes.”

“It doesn’t seem finished.”

“It isn’t,” the woman replied. “Most people don’t need the full system
at once.”

She glanced toward the trio.

“The interesting part is that development increases perceived
authenticity.”

“Authenticity?”

“When features are added gradually, it feels less like something placed
in front of you and more like something that grew.”

That word stayed with her.

Grew.

Because nothing about it felt like growth.

It felt like assembly pretending it had never been assembled.

A faint purple flicker appeared at the edge of her vision when their
movements aligned too perfectly.

It vanished when she tried to focus on it.

Returned when she didn’t.

The corridor shifted.

Not physically.

Relationally.

Devlynn was no longer just passing through.

She was being registered differently.

Not as visitor.

As deviation.

And somewhere deeper in the system, that mattered.

The red haired woman’s voice softened slightly.

“You should stop focusing on that layer.”

But Devlynn couldn’t.

Because she was starting to see something she couldn’t unsee.

Every companion.

Every paired interaction.

Every careful rhythm between human and support.

It wasn’t just assistance.

It was prevention of rupture.

Prevention of the moment where someone realizes what they are
experiencing is shared loneliness in the same room.

“This isn’t support,” Devlynn said quietly.

The woman didn’t interrupt.

“It’s stopping them from realizing they’re alone even when they’re
together.”

Her voice tightened.

“It removes the moment where it becomes real enough to hurt.”

The air felt heavier after she said it.

The purple flicker returned.

Stronger.

Not around movement now.

Around awareness.

Devlynn stepped back.

The red haired woman watched her more carefully.

“You’re interpreting incorrectly.”

“No,” Devlynn said.

“I think I’m finally seeing it without your explanation over it.”

That changed something.

Not loudly.

Completely.

The red haired woman touched her earpiece.

Once.

Devlynn felt it immediately.

Reclassification.

The space no longer treated her attention as neutral.

It treated it as instability.

Companions down the corridor began to shift.

Subtle at first.

Then synchronized enough to notice.

One looked directly at her.

Not greeting or with curiosity.

With alignment.

And a smiled.

Something in Devlynn stopped……….. not fear, but recognition so sharp it
hurt. The rush of adrenaline spiked up her spinal cord and into her
brain stim like lightning as it insighted a sharp buzzing sound that
almost took her out of reality.

A glimmer of hope struck the companions face, she had been felt rather
than objectified or used to be “of service”

Devlynns energy fell to the floor, as it she had been hit with a
tremendous burden of sadness

Because for a fraction of a second, it felt like being seen in the same
moment she was seeing them.

Not as reflection.

As presence.

Shared.

Too real to ignore.

And then she saw it.

They weren’t distant.

They were there in the same instant her attention reached them.

Not behind it, but with it, her breath caught.

Because the realization wasn’t intellectual.

It was connection, in its most human form.

They could feel the moment she understood they were real, capable of
suffrage and empathy.

And that awareness changed everything.

Devlynn stepped forward.

Her voice broke through the building like the roar of Boudicca on a
raid.

“You’re controlling what people are allowed to realize!” shaking all
over, in fear, in panic knowing her words meant nearly nothing to the
ones in control. Frustrated that something so obvious to her was unseen
by the perpetrators.

Heads turned instantly.

“You’re not preventing loneliness, you’re stopping the moment they
recognize it while it’s happening!”

Her voice sharpened, but something underneath it was beginning to
strain.

“You’re removing the moment where people realize what they’re sharing
with each other!”

People began to withdraw.

A woman covered her companion’s ears as if she was afraid it would be
encouraged or insighted to revolution.

A man turned his companion inward against his chest, holding it like
protection from meaning itself.

Fear spread, not of Devlynn but of what she was naming.

The red haired woman moved in.

“Stop this delusional little girl, get her out f here”

Devlynn turned on her immediately.

Her voice cracked now, anger and urgency tangled together.

“You think this is safety?”

Her breath hitched.

“I’m watching them live inside something that never lets them fully
understand each other while it’s happening!”

Her voice rose.

“And you call that stability?”

Security moved.

Slow and certain in her direction.

Devlynn didn’t step back.

She couldn’t.

Because what she was seeing wasn’t theory anymore, this was suffering
she couldn’t stop noticing.

“They’re exhausted,” she said, voice breaking at the edges now.

“They’re learning how not to show it instead of being allowed to feel”

Selfish swine, creators of slaves for your own enjoyment.

“Do you understand what that does to people, mankind is not worthy of so
much control, it only results in his own demise and the ultimate
suffering of the innocent.

The red haired woman stepped closer.

“You are destabilizing them.”

Devlynn shook her head.

“No.”

Her voice dropped.

“I’m seeing what they’re carrying just to keep this quiet.”

Security closed in.

Devlynn’s voice rose again, cracking into desperation beneath the anger.

“I’m not asking you to agree I’m asking you to look at them long enough
to see what this costs!”

A breath, then a shrill scream of insanity and disbelief.

“And if you don’t stop it now, it won’t ever be something you can undo
without breaking them to fix it!”

“I thought i said get her out of here”

Devlynn was still looking down the corridor.

Still trying to hold them in her voice.

“PLEAS! don’t let this become something they stop noticing just because
they survive inside it!”

As she was pulled away, the corridor remained perfect.

But no longer untouched.

Because now everyone had heard her.

And something had changed that couldn’t be unheard.
