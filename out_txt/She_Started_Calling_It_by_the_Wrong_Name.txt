I was sixteen weeks when my mother in law, Ruth, moved into the spare
room “just until the baby comes.” Mark thought it was sweet. I thought
it was temporary. We had already decided on a name, Evelyn if it was a
girl, Thomas if it was a boy. We told Ruth at dinner the night she
arrived. She smiled with her whole mouth and said, “That’s lovely,
dear,” then never used either name again. She started calling the baby
“Claire.” At first I corrected her. “It’s Evelyn, Ruth.” She would
blink, tilt her head, and reply, “Of course. Claire is such a strong
name, though. It suits her.” I would remind her we didn’t know the sex
yet. She would only smile harder and rest her hand on my stomach like
she already knew. By twenty weeks the ultrasound confirmed a girl. We
announced Evelyn. Ruth bought an entire wardrobe monogrammed with the
letter C. When I asked her to return it she looked genuinely confused.
“But that’s what we’ve always called her,” she said. Mark shrugged and
told me to let it go. “She’s excited. It’s harmless.”

It stopped being harmless the night I woke up to Ruth sitting on the
edge of the bed, whispering directly into my belly. I lay perfectly
still and listened. She was telling a story about a little girl named
Claire who had fallen down the stairs in 1987 and never gotten back up.
“But you’re going to finish what she started,” Ruth murmured. “You’re
going to wear her name until it fits.” I told Mark the next morning. He
said I must have dreamed it. Ruth made pancakes and asked if Claire had
slept well. When I snapped that the baby’s name was Evelyn, Ruth’s face
went completely blank for three full seconds. Then the smile returned,
softer this time. “Of course, dear. Whatever you need to believe until
she’s here.” The nursery began changing without anyone admitting they
did it. The Evelyn wall letters disappeared. A wooden sign that said
“Claire’s Room” appeared above the crib. The mobile I bought was
replaced with one made of tiny porcelain dolls that all had the same
dark hair and the same slight overbite Ruth has. I took it down. The
next morning it was back, and the dolls’ eyes followed me around the
room.

At thirty two weeks I started having contractions that weren’t real.
Braxton Hicks, the doctor said. Ruth disagreed. She would press her ear
to my stomach and nod as if listening to instructions. “She’s
impatient,” Ruth would say. “Claire never did like waiting.” The night
my water broke, Ruth was the one who drove me to the hospital because
Mark was out of town on a last minute work trip she had somehow
arranged. In the delivery room she held my hand so tightly the bones
ground together. When the nurse asked what we were naming the baby, Ruth
answered before I could speak. “Claire.” I tried to correct her. The
contraction hit so hard I couldn’t get the words out. When they placed
the baby on my chest I looked down and saw a face that was already
familiar in the worst way, Ruth’s eyes, Ruth’s mouth, Ruth’s exact
expression of quiet satisfaction. The baby didn’t cry. She just stared
at Ruth and smiled with a mouth that had too many teeth for a newborn.
Ruth leaned close and whispered, “Welcome home, Claire. We’ve been
waiting so long.” I tried to say Evelyn. The sound that came out was
wrong. My tongue felt heavy, like it belonged to someone else. The
nurses were already writing the name on the forms. Claire. Mark arrived
two hours later and looked at the baby, then at his mother, then at me,
and said nothing at all. He just nodded once, the same way Ruth does
when a decision has already been made.

It’s been three months. The birth certificate says Claire. Every time I
try to call her Evelyn she screams until I stop. Ruth lives with us
permanently now. She rocks the baby at night and sings the same lullaby
she used to sing to the first Claire, the one who died. Sometimes I
catch the baby watching me with an expression no infant should have,
patient, measuring, already deciding how long I get to stay. Last night
Ruth put the baby in my arms and said, “She needs to learn your face
while it’s still useful.” The baby reached up, touched my cheek with
fingers that felt too strong, and said a single word in a voice that was
not a baby’s voice at all. “Soon.” I don’t know whose body this is
anymore. I only know that every time I look in the mirror the woman
staring back looks a little more like the photographs of the first
Claire that Ruth keeps in a locked drawer. And the baby, my baby,
Claire, smiles every time she sees the change.
