At 11:43 last Tuesday night, the pedestrian crossing outside my
workplace announced that three people were waiting to cross.

I was standing there alone.

I work the closing shift at a pharmacy. There’s a four-lane road between
the building and the bus stop I use to get home, with one of those
talking pedestrian signals for visually impaired people.

Normally, when you press the button, a woman’s recorded voice says,
“Wait to cross Bell Street.”

I’ve heard it hundreds of times.

Last Tuesday, it said something different.

“Three pedestrians waiting. Wait to cross Bell Street.”

I looked around. Nobody on either side of the road.

It had been raining, and the street was practically empty. I figured
they’d installed some new pedestrian-counting system and it was
malfunctioning.

I didn’t give it much thought until Thursday.

That night, I was standing at the same crossing with a man from the
takeaway place next door. He had a bicycle and a big paper bag of food
balanced on the handlebars.

I pressed the button.

“Four pedestrians waiting.”

The guy looked up.

“Four?” he said.

I laughed and told him it had been doing that lately.

He shrugged. The signal changed, and we crossed.

But halfway across, I heard footsteps behind us.

Not unusual, except that the road was wet enough that I could hear each
shoe slap against the asphalt.

There were three distinct sets of footsteps.

I turned around.

Nobody.

The guy with the bicycle was already ahead of me. He didn’t seem to
notice anything.

The footsteps stopped when I stopped.

I got on my bus and went home.

Friday was when I started getting genuinely frightened.

I’d finished work late because our card reader kept freezing. It was
almost midnight by the time I got outside.

The crossing was empty.

I pressed the button.

“Five pedestrians waiting.”

I actually said, “Oh, fuck off,” out loud.

Then I noticed something in the window of the closed dry cleaner’s
beside me.

The glass reflects the pavement, especially at night when the shop is
dark.

In the reflection, there were four people standing behind me.

An older woman in a pale coat. A man wearing what looked like a work
uniform. Someone shorter, possibly a teenager, with a hood pulled up.

And a fourth person standing so close behind me that I could only see
part of their shoulder.

I spun around.

Empty pavement.

When I turned back to the window, all four were still there.

They weren’t looking at me.

They were looking across the road.

I tried moving sideways.

My reflection moved.

Theirs didn’t.

Then the woman in the pale coat raised her right arm.

I watched her reach toward the pedestrian button.

The actual button, beside my elbow, clicked.

Nobody was touching it.

The speaker repeated, “Five pedestrians waiting.”

I backed away so quickly I nearly tripped over the kerb.

Something brushed against my coat.

I felt it clearly. Fingers, right between my shoulder blades, pressing
through the fabric.

I ran back to the pharmacy.

Our manager hadn’t left yet. I told him somebody had been following me,
which was technically the truth as far as I understood it.

He walked me to my car, which I’d left in the staff parking lot that day
because I’d planned to take the bus and collect it the following
morning.

I drove home instead.

Over the weekend I reported the crossing through the city’s maintenance
website. I described the announcement and the button apparently
activating without being pressed.

I left out the people in the window.

Monday afternoon I got an email saying a technician had inspected the
equipment and found no faults.

He also wrote that the signal didn’t have pedestrian-detection
capabilities. The recorded announcements were standard messages
programmed into the unit.

It wasn’t designed to count anyone.

I forwarded the email to my manager. He told me to stop worrying about a
broken speaker.

I wish I had listened to him.

Last night I finished at 11:30.

I’d parked on the opposite side of the road specifically so I wouldn’t
have to use the crossing, but when I reached my car, I realized I’d left
my bag in the staff room.

I considered driving around the block.

Instead, I walked back toward the crossing.

The speaker was silent. The little red hand was illuminated.

I didn’t touch the button.

“One pedestrian waiting,” it said.

My stomach dropped.

I stood completely still.

After a few seconds, it repeated itself.

“One pedestrian waiting.”

I started to back away.

“Two pedestrians waiting.”

There was a soft scraping sound behind me. Like a shoe being dragged
over concrete.

I looked at the dark window of a furniture shop across the road.

In its reflection, the woman in the pale coat was standing beside me.

Closer than she’d been on Friday.

Her head was tilted toward my shoulder.

I could make out her face this time.

Her eyes were closed. Her mouth hung slightly open, and something dark
had dried around her lips.

I couldn’t see her in the street itself.

Only in the glass.

“Three pedestrians waiting.”

The man in the work uniform appeared behind her.

I ran.

I made it maybe fifteen feet before something caught my left wrist.

It was a hand. There is no other way to describe it.

Five cold fingers wrapped around my wrist, squeezing hard enough to
hurt.

I looked down.

There was nothing there.

But the skin above my watch was being pushed inward, and my hand was
turning white.

I screamed and pulled.

It didn’t let go.

Then the crossing announced, very calmly, “Walk sign is on to cross Bell
Street.”

My arm jerked backward.

Not toward the pavement.

Toward the road.

My shoes scraped across the concrete as something pulled me off the
kerb. I tried grabbing the signal pole with my free hand but missed.

I remember seeing the headlights of a delivery van coming toward me.

Then there was a horn, a terrible screeching noise, and I fell.

The van stopped a few feet from where I landed.

The driver got out shouting. I was on my hands and knees in the road,
crying so hard I couldn’t explain what had happened.

He helped me onto the pavement.

I kept looking behind him.

In the shop window, all four figures were standing in the road.

The woman in the pale coat was closest.

Her arm was still extended.

Then the pedestrian countdown reached zero.

Every figure in the reflection turned toward me.

All at once.

The crossing announced, “Wait to cross Bell Street.”

The figures disappeared.

The driver wanted to call an ambulance. I asked him to take me back to
the pharmacy instead. I couldn’t stand the idea of being left alone
beside that road.

He did.

Before I got out, he asked me something.

“Who was the woman?”

I froze.

He said he’d seen a woman standing just behind me as he approached the
crossing. He thought she’d pushed me into the road.

When he braked, he lost sight of her.

He hadn’t seen anyone else.

I asked what she looked like.

He said she was wearing a light-coloured coat.

My manager drove me home. I’ve taken two days off work.

My wrist is badly bruised. There are five distinct marks around it,
including one just below my watch where a thumb would have been.

I haven’t told the police about the reflections. I’ve given them the
driver’s information and explained that someone grabbed me, but I never
saw the person directly.

I don’t know what else I can reasonably tell them.

This morning I was looking at pictures of the crossing on Google Street
View, trying to work out whether there might be a hidden camera or
something reflected in the shop windows.

One thing bothered me enough that I called the city’s traffic department
again.

In the pictures, the crossing has two pedestrian buttons, one on either
side of Bell Street.

Both are identical.

Both have small speakers mounted underneath.

I realized something I hadn’t thought about before.

Every time I heard the announcement, I was standing on the pharmacy side
of the road.

But last night, I was on the opposite side.

And the speaker said, “One pedestrian waiting.”

It didn’t say two until I started backing away.

There was already someone standing there when I arrived.

I just couldn’t see her.

I’ve been thinking about that all morning.

Because on Friday, the signal counted five.

And I only saw four people in the window.
