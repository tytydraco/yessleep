In the center of the camp where I worked, there was a circle of staff
cabins, and in the center of those cabins, there was a tent. 

On my first day, when I asked what it was, the other staff told me not
to touch it–especially if it was zipped up. I assumed it was because it
belonged to one of them and they wanted privacy, or maybe it was some
kind of hazing thing I was going to need to deal with later. Either way,
I was a pretty boring kid looking to fit in, and I did what I was told.

Most of the time, the tent stayed unzipped, flaps billowing in peaceful
summer breezes.

Every now and then, though, when we came back from a late-night bonfire
or an early morning meeting, the tent would be zipped up.

That’s how they knew.

I remember the first time I saw it zipped. The other staff treated it
the way you treat an overbearing manager or parent. They scoffed under
their breaths and rolled their eyes.

We were all supposed to be in the dining hall in five minutes, and
nobody was eager to miss donut day.

“Who’s got it?” Nico asked. Nico was a big redhead who slouched and
grumbled until he didn’t. The lifeguard, Ava, always said he had the
unique power of becoming a completely different person just by
straightening his posture.

No one answered him.

“Hey!” Nico snapped, suddenly twice as loud, three inches taller, and
(if I’m being honest) measurably cuter. He immediately lowered his voice
once he had our attention, though his eyes still sparked. “I said who’s
got this one? I’m not supposed to go twice in a row.”

“I’ll get it,” Ellis grumbled.

Ellis was Nico’s older brother, and the junior director for the week. He
was the kind of guy who liked to look like he had it all together, and
so it was no surprise that he volunteered for whatever this was.

“Thanks Ellis. We’ll cover for you–and save you some donuts.” Nico said.

The rest of us continued on toward the dining hall. The bell was going
to ring any minute, and the Geriatrics (as we liked to call the
middle-aged people who lived and worked at the camp year-round) had made
it clear that us summer staff needed to start showing up for stuff on
time.

“What’s going on with the tent, anyway?” I asked.

Nico and Ava exchanged glances. 

Even though Nico was technically younger than most of us, the fact that
he was the director’s son made him our defacto leader. He wasn’t the
most gregarious out of his crew, but he knew literally everything about
this place. He took it all more seriously than Ellis, too. Ava was
always talking about how he would be junior director, except he didn’t
want the gig.

“For now, you just need to stay away from it,” Nico said. It wasn’t
unkind, but it was firm.

Still, I didn’t like that answer at all, and for some reason it
particularly stung coming from him.

Ava glanced back at me, like she wasn’t sure I was going to accept that
answer. When I offered a reluctant smile, she smiled back.

They had all known each other for a long time–most of them had been
campers together since grade school, and while they had all been
welcoming, there was only so much you could share when it came to inside
jokes and childhood secrets.

I followed Ava and Nico and the others down the hill to the dining hall.
The camp was beautiful in the morning. Honestly it was part of the
reason I wanted to work here in the first place–it gave me a prime
opportunity to escape my parents’ city apartment and get out into the
forest. Mist filled the emptiness between the trees, and lights hung
above us, zig-zagging from tree to tree. Someone had laid down a good
chunk of change at the local hardware store. 

“Hey, wait up!” 

I turned to see Ellis, half jogging, half tripping down the hill behind
us. He caught up easily and smiled.

“Are you done already?” Maria, our craft girl, asked.

“Not yet.”

Nobody asked him any more questions. 

I sat through breakfast, trying to figure out what exactly was going
on–why the veteran staff members seemed fairly nonchalant about this
whole thing, but still wouldn’t talk to me about it. Eventually, I
settled on the conclusion that it must be the start of a hazing
thing–like they were trying to scare me or get me curious before they
let me in on the whole story. 

I noticed something interesting, though. Halfway through breakfast,
Ellis wrapped two chocolate donuts in a napkin and left the building,
whistling as he went. Ellis was allergic to chocolate. I learned that my
first night here.

***

A few days later, as we were all getting back from a night swim, I
happened to look over and see that the tent was zipped up again. I poked
Ava, and she looked over, too.

“Aw shit. Tent’s zipped.”

“I got this one,” Nico said.

He darted off course and headed for the tent. I wanted to stay and watch
what happened, but Ava grabbed my arm and steered me toward the cabin.
“Let’s dry off and then we can start that fire,” she said.

I looked over my shoulder once and saw Nico squatting next to the tent,
holding a piece of paper.

***

Our women’s staff cabin only had two shower stalls. Ava and I showered
first, and I hurried through mine so Maria could jump in. After pulling
on a pair of sweats and a hoodie, I walked back out into the main cabin.
I was surprised to find Nico sitting there. He definitely wasn’t
supposed to be, though the women went over to the men’s dorm all the
time, and nobody stopped us. 

“Uh, hi.”

“Hi,” he looked worried. It was an expression I hadn’t seen from him
before. “ Where’s Ava?”

“Here,” she called, bright and bubbly.

“Can I talk to you for a second?”

“Yeah!” She plopped down on her bed.

He glanced up at me. “Maybe outside?”

I was starting to get annoyed. I thought Nico and I were friends–me and
Ava too. I didn’t appreciate feeling like I was suddenly on the outside.

To her credit, Ava gave a half little shrug on her way out, as if to
apologize for how weird this was all becoming. Then she followed him out
onto the porch.

Immediately I jumped up from my bed and went to stand by the window at
the entrance. There was a thing I learned early about that cabin–the
maintenance staff pretty much avoided the staff quarters as much as
possible, which led to all kinds of lame, gross, and semi-unsafe
conditions. One of them was that the window didn’t shut all the way. The
constant air whistling through the window made me crazy most nights–but
that night it was welcome. That night it meant I could hear what Ava and
Nico were saying on the porch.

“What’s wrong?” Ava asked. “What did it say?”

“It said it wants her for the next one. It wants Kit.”

“Oh fuck.”

I held my breath. What wanted me? I assumed he was talking about the
tent, but I still had no idea what the tent was even about. If this was
a prank or a hazing, it was a good one. How did they even know that I
would listen at the window?

“Yeah,” Nico said.

“Has it ever asked for a specific person before?”

He shook his head. “Not that I’ve heard of.”

“When should we tell her?”

The shower turned off, and I heard Maria walking across the bathroom
floor. 

I didn’t want to explain why I was listening at the window, so even
though it killed me, I went back and sat on my bed and pretended to be
busy on my phone.

“Where’s Ava?” Maria stepped into the dorm, towel-drying her hair.

“Not sure. She went outside.”

“Probably heading for the campfire. You about ready?”

I nodded, and a few minutes later the two of us headed out to the fire.

***

When I got to the campfire, the Nico, Ellis, and Ava were already
staring at me. It wasn’t pleasant.

I took a seat beside Ava. She was scratching at the friendship bracelets
encircling her arm. They reached from her wrist halfway up to her elbow
like some kind of gauntlet. They looked newer the higher they climbed
her arm, and I had a feeling she probably got several new ones every
summer. 

She looked stressed. She played with her bracelets when she felt that
way.

“What’s going on, guys?” I asked. 

Nico leaned forward and handed me a piece of paper. It was old.
Partially crumpled. I unfolded it, careful not to rip the brittle
sheet. 

“You’re gonna need to read these,” he said. 

The Rules

1.  When the tent is zipped, send someone to go get the note.
2.  The person who picks up the note must read it and do exactly what it
    says. They may not tell any other participants what their note says
    until after they’ve completed their task.
3.  The task must be completed as soon as possible. She will know if you
    take longer than necessary.
4.  While you are completing your task, no other participants may look
    at the tent. It’s probably best if they leave the area entirely.
5.  Do NOT fail to complete a task. You will not like the sort of
    request that comes next.
6.  It’s up to you as to whether you invite new participants–but
    understand that if you leave the tent unguarded, she is free to do
    what she wants.

I stared at the list of rules. Then I looked up at each of the others,
searching for any hints of laughter in their eyes.

“You guys must think I’m really dumb.” I finally said. Even as I said
it, though, I felt a creeping feeling running along my spine. Something
felt strange here.

“No.” Nico said. “No, not at all. This isn’t a joke. We really need your
help here. I wouldn’t be bringing you into this at all, but it
specifically asked for you.”

“And I know it’s weird the first time, but it’s never anything bad,”
Maria said.

That earned a look from Nico, “It’s never anything bad anymore.”

Maria’s head fell, and Ava scowled at her.

“Yeah,” Maria said. “Sorry.”

Ellis turned to me. “ Look, basically just don’t be a fuck-up. Get the
job done and we’ll be fine.”

“Yeah,” Nico said. “You got this.”

I looked at him for a long time. I trusted him the most out of everyone
here, but that didn’t mean that he was above an elaborate practical
joke. And I had to believe that this was a joke.

***

Joke or not, the tent didn’t wait long to call me for the first time. It
was early in the next morning, and I was about to head out for a morning
jog, or maybe a quick paddle around the lake. 

As I left my porch, my gaze happened to wander to the tent. That’s when
I saw it was zipped up tight again. 

I began trembling immediately. I didn’t want to do this. I didn’t want
to participate. But I knew I didn’t have a choice.

I started toward the tent. 

“Morning, Kit.”

I just about jumped out of my skin.

Nico was standing on his porch.

“Don’t look,” I whispered harshly. I realized then that there was a real
part of me that bought into this thing–or at least I didn’t want to be
wrong.

Maybe it was some elaborate hoax. This would be a great way to haze
someone.

But my gut told me to take it seriously. 

Nico dropped his eyes so quickly that in that moment, I was convinced he
believed it, too.

“Good luck,” he said softly. “Text me if you–well, no, I guess you
can’t. But you’ll do great.”

It was a frightening thing to say, don’t get me wrong, but reassuring
too. Something in his tone told me that this was going to be okay. I was
up for this task.

The tent rustled as I approached it. At first I thought it was the wind,
but as my feet padded across the pine needles, I saw the shadow-shape
begin to rise up inside the dirty orange canvas. At first I thought it
was only an ordinary head, but then I saw the protrusions and I stopped
dead, questioning myself. My breath caught in my throat. 

I tried hard to convince myself at that moment that this was a prank. It
was a friend in a costume, backlit by the rising sun. The more I told
myself that story, the more it gave me courage. As long as I could
picture the others in their cabins, watching me and laughing, it made me
feel better–no matter whether I believed it or not. 

I thought I could push past the fear–the way my breath hitched with each
step–and do this. 

When I reached the tent, the thing inside let out a little chadder. It
stopped me in my tracks. It wasn’t the sound I expected. It sounded
almost happy–almost excited to have me there. Could it see me? Smell me?
No, that was insane. It was just a staffer dressed up, making creepy
noises in a tent, right? Just a friend.

I couldn’t make myself believe it. My hands shook as I reached for the
note.

The thing inside the tent turned its head. There was something wrong
with it. Something bulbous.

I forced my eyes down to the note.

There is a small owl behind the woodshed. Get it and bring me a feather
from its tail.

I folded it and began walking backward away from the tent. Already, the
task was more difficult than I expected. I had no idea how to catch an
owl. Maybe a bucket? Wouldn’t it struggle or just fly away?

Still, it was better than staying beside this tent.

I couldn’t talk to anyone about my task, so I ended up heading down to
the workshop at the far end of campus. I had only been in there a few
times, and there was something especially unsettling about it in the dim
morning light. A free-standing band saw stood in the corner of the shop.
It was at least as tall as I was, and I always felt like there might be
something hiding behind it.

I tiptoed past the saws and the workbenches and the jars full of sorted
screws and nails and fasteners and found a pair of oversized leather
gloves. Those would be helpful. I stuffed them in my pocket and looked
around again. My gaze finally landed on a small cage jutting out from
underneath one of the work benches. 

Perfect.

I tucked the cage under my arm and left the shop, looking both ways to
make sure the caretaker wasn’t coming up the path from his house. I was
fine. Everything was clear.

As I started up toward the woodshed, though, something chattered from
the woods. I looked up and there between the trees, I could see the edge
of some kind of cloak silhouetted against the morning fog. The
chattering stopped. I took a step forward, and the shape swirled away
into the mist without a sound, leaving me to wonder if I had actually
seen anything at all.

I was already pretty on edge, and these woods suddenly felt strange and
lonely.

I hurried up the path to the woodshed, eager to get away from those
trees as quickly as possible.

It turned out I didn’t have to worry so much about catching the owl. I
found her lying on the ground, wing dragging limply beside her,
splattering and smearing blood along the forest floor. She was small,
and she glowered up at me with wide golden eyes.

Carefully, I reached down for her. She didn’t try to run or thrash or
fight back. She just went still like she already knew what was coming.
My gloves enveloped her to the point I wasn’t entirely sure she was
there at all. I put her in the cage, careful not to hurt her as I
retracted my glove. She weighed nothing, like I was picking up a cotton
ball. I thought I could squish her without even trying.

Then I started back to the cabins.

Once I got closer to the tent, I paused in the middle of the trail and
clipped one of the owl’s  tail feathers before continuing up the hill.
When I reached the cabins, the porch lights were out and the rest of the
staff already seemed to be gone for breakfast. 

I carried the feather and the owl to the tent. Catching her had been so
easy that I allowed myself to be confident about this part, too. I set
the cage and the feather down in front of the tent. There was already
another note tucked in the zipper. 

Very good. Leave the feather. Take the owl.

I looked down at the bird, and she looked back at me.

The others were right. Maybe this wasn’t so bad. 

***

I settled at the table between Ava and Nico. It was nice being part of
the inner circle now, even if it came at a strange price. Even if it
cost me my own fear.

“There she is!” Nico gave me perhaps the biggest grin I’d seen all
summer. “How’d it go?”

“I can talk about it all now?” I asked, just to double check.

“Yeah, yeah. You’re free to spill,” Ellis said. “So spill.”

“It wanted me to go catch an owl and then give the tent a tail feather.
And now I have a hurt owl to take care of. I don’t really know how
though.”

“Oh, don’t worry about it,” Maria waved me off like she dealt with this
kind of thing all the time. “My aunt works at a wildlife refuge. I’ll
give her a call tonight, but for now we can just put her in a cardboard
box and let her be in the dark and throw a live mouse in there now and
then. She’ll be fine.”

She must have seen the wheels turning when she said “live mouse” because
she quickly added, “You can get those at the pet store. It’s not a big
deal.”

I nodded. “So, can you guys tell me a little bit more about what’s going
on now?”

Ava glanced at Nico, and then leaned forward. Yeah. We can probably fill
you in on the lore. “We call it the Witch.”

“Seems a little on the nose,” I said. I was feeling brave now that I was
drinking coffee and eating waffles in the warm light of the dining hall.

“No, no–” Ava couldn’t stand it when people thought anything around her
was lackluster. “Not like a normal witch. Like the Black Witch Moth”

“Have you heard of it?” Ellis asked.

I shook my head.
.
“They’re like–they’re like this massive moth that stalks the night and
stuff,” Ava said. In some countries they’re seen as bad luck. Even
bringers of death.”

I couldn’t help it. I thought of the edge of the cloaked figure I had
seen in the woods–the little chattering sounds–and I couldn’t help but
shiver.

“Sounds like you did great,” Nico said. It’ll probably be even easier
next time. She just likes to have a little fun with newbies. But hey,
you actually don’t even have to do it again. It’s voluntary.”

I nodded. Unless she calls you by name.

“Good morning, guys.”

I looked up to see one of the Geriatrics standing at the end of our
table. His name was Sphen, and there were several of us who suspected
that wasn’t his real name. There wasn’t a lick of Scandinavian about the
guy.

“Hey, listen, I have a favor to ask you all tomorrow.”

“Whatcha got?” Ellis asked.

“We need to put on a strong show for family day,” he said. “We’ve got a
couple of big donors coming in to visit their kids, and we need to up
our game. Trust me, we could use the donations.” He gestures to a
particularly bad crack in the ceiling. It leaked every time it rained.
We had a bucket with a name and  a face on it and everything. “Kit, I’m
going to have you help Nico on the climbing wall. I’ll take your walkie
talkie and first aid backpack myself.”

I nodded, glad I had finished my wall training.

“Ava, I asked Susan if she would back you up on the waterfront, just for
tomorrow. She’s not totally caught up on her certs, but she’s had enough
years in the chair to make up for it.”

“Great,” Ava smiled tightly. She picked at her armful of friendship
bracelets.

“Thanks guys,” Sphen drummed on the table and headed to wreak havoc in
the kitchen.

“Yay. Susan,” Ava said, rolling her eyes and flopping back in her
chair. 

***

“What a cute little guy!” Maria said, leaning over my owl’s cage. “I
think it’s a screech owl, but she’s just a baby! Did you name her yet?” 

“Not yet,” I said.

“You gotta name her.”

I was just starting to think about what I would call a baby screech owl
when Ava’s voice carried up from the courtyard.

“Damn it!” She snapped.

Maria and I turned to look. Ava was standing in front of the tent, and
it was zipped again. She turned to look at me. “You sure you did
everything you were supposed to do, New Girl? It doesn’t usually happen
this much.”

“I did what the note said.”

She let out a half sigh, half growl, “It’s fine.” She said, “I got this
one.”

She marched to the tent, and the rest of us went back to our cabins to
wait it out. Maria flopped on her bed, and I flopped on mine, and we
both pulled out our phones. We didn’t need to be anywhere for another
half hour, and this was one of our few moments of peace in the day.

For that reason, I almost didn’t interrupt it. 

But I had questions about the tent and the Witch and everything that
surrounded it, and I had a feeling Maria would be more willing to talk
about it with me than Ava.

“What did Nico mean when he said the Witch never asks for anything bad
anymore?” I said.

She looked up. She put her phone on the bed face down. “It’s just…it’s
important we give the Witch exactly what she wants. Like the rules say,
she escalates when we mess up.”

“What happened that was so bad?”

She looked at me like she was trying to decide if she had to answer.
Finally she shook her head. “Look, I don’t think I’m the one to ask. I’m
not one of the people who thinks that everything bad that happens around
here is the Witch’s fault. But some people don’t agree.”

I was about to push harder when Ava came storming back into the cabin.
She walked in and slammed the door behind her.

“Are you done?” Maria asked.

“No.” She snapped. “No, and I’m not gonna do it.”

Maria’s eyes went wide. “Ava, you have to.”

“Yeah, not this time.” Ava turned to me, eyes fiery. “You must have
screwed something up. You fix it.”

I took a step back. I honestly had no idea what she was talking about.
I’d done everything the Witch asked, as far as I knew. I did it quickly.
I kept the secret.

Maria stepped between us. “Kit, did you tell us everything the note
said?” Even as she asked it, she nodded subtly as if to encourage me,
and I had the feeling she was on my side.

“Yeah! Yeah, I did!” At this point I was more afraid of Ava than the
Witch.

“I think she’s telling the truth, Ava. I think you just have to do it
before you make things worse.”

Ava glared at me. Then she stomped over to her bed and took the knife
from the ledge on her bunk. I flinched. For a moment, that knife in her
hand and look in her eye suggested something I didn’t want to think
about. Then she pushed past me and out into the courtyard, and I
breathed a sigh of relief.

When Ava came back five minutes later, her friendship bracelets were
gone.

“Ava,” Maria said softly.

“Yeah. Yeah it wanted my bracelets. The one from Claudette? It wanted my
dead friend’s bracelet!” She practically screamed.

My stomach turned.

Ava collapsed onto her bed crying. The girl I thought was the strongest
of all of us had just been torn apart by a tent in the middle of a
circle of cabins in the middle of a summer camp. 

That was the moment I truly believed. That was when I stopped doubting
whether this was real.

Ellis and Nico ran into the room seconds later.

They seemed just as shocked to see Ava like this. Ellis sat down beside
her and put his hand on her back. She sniffed loudly, and when she
looked up, I thought she looked like she could attack any one of us at
any moment. Her eyes were so red that they practically glowed, and they
refused to focus on any of us.

“I’m not doing this again,” she said. “I told it to fuck off, and if it
doesn’t like it…well…” she didn’t finished the thought. She collapsed
into tears again.

Nico and Ellis exchanged a look. 

That night, I tried to pull Maria aside to ask what was going on–who was
Claudette? What did Ava mean when she said she wouldn’t do this again?
Maria just shook her head, and said we would talk more tomorrow. 

I expected that to be the end of things for the night. We went to bed
without talking, and even Nico didn’t text me back. I wasn’t sure if it
was because he was dealing with his own crap, or if he was mad because
he thought I had done something wrong.

Either way, I found myself drifting off waiting for a reply that never
came. 

I would like to say that I woke up the next morning and things were
better. I would like to say that we all slept fine, and while Ava was
still sad the next morning, she was better too. 

That’s not what happened.

The night had one more trick in store.

I woke up, bleary eyed, to an unsettling clicking sound. I looked around
the inky black room, and it took me a moment to register what was wrong.
There was a shape in the bathroom doorway.

My breath caught, and I froze in place. I knew at a glance that it
wasn’t Maria or Ava. This figure was tall. So tall that she looked
crunched down in the doorway. 

She stepped forward. The floor creaked under her weight. She wore a
cloak, furled around her body. She looked like a woman, but her face was
bulbous and almost reflective. Something like antlers protruded from her
head. She stepped closer. She reached my side as I struggled to open my
mouth. Tears bubbled at my eyes, and still I couldn’t move. 

She leaned over me, and I could see her antlers gleaming in the
porchlight, barbed and clicking. 

I didn’t dare move. I couldn’t. I couldn’t even tell if I was awake or
asleep. I wanted to flail, to scream, to wake up Maria and Ava, but I
couldn’t.

Instead I just lay there. I lay there as she spread across me. I lay
there as she extended her neck and breathed down on my bare shoulder.
The breath itself was cold, and when her teeth sunk into my neck, they
were cold too.

Only then did I scream.

Ava had the lights on within seconds. I’ll never understand how she got
from her bed to the door that quickly. 

In the buzzing fluorescent lights, the three of us were alone in the
room. 

I reached up for my neck, and I felt the hot liquid oozing down my
shoulder. When I pulled my hand away, it dripped with blood.

***

And now, ten years later as I sit in my respectable living room in my
respectable town, I can sense her again. It’s not a complete shock. She
likes to come around sometimes. 

I have more story to tell, and I’ll try to get back to it, but first I
need to take care of her. I think I can see her cloak flutter in my
doorway. I think I can hear her chatter.

I don’t think she realizes I’ve noticed her yet. That’s why I’m still
typing–so she won’t realize I’ve spotted her. But now I need to get up.
I need to turn on the lights. I need to do what I couldn’t do ten years
ago. I need to face her.
