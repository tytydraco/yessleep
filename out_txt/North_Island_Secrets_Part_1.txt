The first dead body I ever saw wasn’t the one that kept me awake.

It was the old bloke who’d been dead three weeks in a Housing New
Zealand unit in Frankton.

Eighty-four years old. Widower. No kids. Neighbour thought the smell was
a possum under the house.

Turned out it was Arthur.

Nobody murdered him. Nobody even forgot about him on purpose. He’d had a
heart attack in his lounge sometime between a Thursday afternoon and the
weather report that night. Life just… kept moving without him.

By the time we got there, he’d become another job number.

You get used to that quicker than you’d like to admit.

People hear “crime scene cleanup” and think every day is blood,
detectives and television bullshit. Truth is, most of our work wasn’t
crime scenes at all. It was suicides. Natural deaths. Welfare checks.
Hoarders. Meth houses. The sort of places everyone else quietly walked
away from once the ambulance left.

We were the people who made it look like nothing had ever happened.

It’s strange work.

Necessary work.

Nobody wants to know you do it until they need someone who does.

I was twenty-two when I started.

I’d bounced between labouring jobs after school, spent six months
unloading freight in Te Rapa, tried roofing for exactly twelve days
before deciding I’d rather be unemployed than spend another summer
cooking on corrugated iron.

Trauma restoration paid well.

That was enough for me.

Back home in Tokoroa, that alone made me the weird one.

Dad reckoned I was wasting my time.

Hemi, my older brother reckoned I thought I was better than everyone
because I worked fifty hours a week instead of waiting for “something
better” to come along.

I didn’t think I was better than anyone. I just liked knowing rent would
be paid on Thursday.

Family barbecues usually followed the same script.

“So, you still cleaning up dead fullas?”

“Yep.”

“Bit fucked, isn’t it?”

“Pays the bills.”

“Could never do it.”

“No one asks you to.”

That usually earned me a shove on the shoulder and someone changing the
subject before Mum told everyone to knock it off.

I loved them. They drove me fucking insane.

By then I’d been with Kiwi Trauma Restoration just over eleven months.
Maybe even a year. Long enough that blood didn’t bother me anymore.

Long enough that I’d learnt bleach fixes almost nothing.

Long enough to know that the smell gets into your nose before it gets
into the room.

Not long enough to understand Ra.

Rawhiti Te Aho. Everyone called him Ra.

Nobody called him anything else. I’d never heard anyone raise their
voice at him.

Never seen him lose his temper.

He was the sort of bloke who could silence a room just by looking up
from his coffee.

Late forties. Built like he’d spent twenty years lifting engines for
fun.

Grey creeping into his beard.

Old black Hilux with one dent in every panel and enough kilometres to
circle the bloody planet twice.

Almost all of his family were patched members of the Red Dogs gang. His
brothers, his cousins. The Te Aho name was a Red Dogs name, it carried
weight.

Ra never wore the patch. Ra didn’t even talk about the Red Dogs.

Luke reckoned he had walked away. Tamati said it would be impossible to
walk away once you patch up.

One day, Ra heard them. He smiled.

“Who said I walked away?”

None of us ever asked again.

He’d been doing the job since before I was born, or so it seemed. Luke
reckoned Ra could tell what someone died from before he even got out of
the truck.

Ra reckoned Luke talked too much.

They were probably both right.

Our crew was small.

Luke Henderson never stopped talking unless there was food in his mouth,
and even then he’d usually manage half a sentence between bites of
steak-and-cheese pie.

Tamati Evans was the biggest bloke I’d ever worked with, covered
shoulder to wrist in fading ink, soft-spoken enough that people mistook
kindness for weakness.

Keanu had started two weeks after me. Twenty years old. Still had that
look about him like every police cordon was somehow exciting.

That wore off eventually. It always does.

Then there was Niamh. Niamh O’Sullivan.

If Ra was the calmest person in the company, Niamh O’Sullivan was the
complete opposite.

She ran dispatch from a cramped office in Hamilton that permanently
smelled of coffee and printer toner.

Irish. Sharp as broken glass. Sexy as fuck.

She swore with impressive creativity and somehow remembered every
client’s phone number without looking. She always had Blue V in her
hand.

Most people thought she was difficult. She wasn’t. She just had no
interest pretending to be polite when direct worked better.

Outside work, things between us were… uncomplicated.

Every now and then one of us would text the other late at night.
Sometimes we’d end up at her place. Sometimes mine. The way we fucked
was unlike anything I’d ever experienced. Neither of us talked much
afterwards.

She never promised anything. I never asked her to. The only thing I was
stupid enough to do was mistake that arrangement for something that
might eventually become more. Looking back now, I should’ve realised she
always kept part of herself locked away.

At the time, I figured everyone had baggage.

The office sat in an industrial estate behind a tyre shop and opposite a
panel beater that seemed permanently on fire from all the welding.

Every morning started the same way.

Coffee. Job sheets. Luke complaining. Keanu asking questions. Tamati
pretending not to laugh. Ra reading the day’s schedule in complete
silence.

Until the Tuesday everything changed.

Niamh walked out of her office carrying a single manila folder.

She wasn’t smiling.

That caught my attention before anything else. She slapped the folder
onto the table in front of Ra.

“Just came through, a place in Rototuna North.”

Ra picked it up without saying anything. His eyes moved across the first
page. Then stopped.

For the first time since I’d known him…

…every bit of colour left his face.

The room went quiet. Even Luke noticed.

Ra read the page again. Folded it once. Very carefully. Looked up at
Niamh.

“Who took the call?”

She frowned.

“I did.”

“What’d they say?”

“Old bloke. Sounded crook. Wanted us there tomorrow.”

“What was his name?”

She glanced back at her computer. “…Didn’t give one.”

Ra didn’t move. Didn’t blink. Didn’t say a word for what felt like a
full minute.

Then he placed the folder back on the table “We’re not taking it.”

No explanation. No argument. Just four words.

Luke laughed… “bit unlike you, bro.”

Ra looked at him. Not angry. Not annoyed. Just… disappointed.

“I said we’re not fucking taking it.”

And for the first time since I’d joined Kiwi Trauma Restoration, I
realised there was one kind of job Ra Te Aho was genuinely afraid of.

I just had no idea why, and I wish I had never fucking found out.
