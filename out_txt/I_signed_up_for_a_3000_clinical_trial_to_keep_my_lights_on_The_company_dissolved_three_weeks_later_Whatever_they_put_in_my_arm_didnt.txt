Hey Reddit. I know what I did was stupid, but I was three days away from
eviction and $4,200 in debt. I’d already sold everything I owned. I need
to tell someone what’s happening because I don’t know if this is just
happening to me anymore.

Two weeks ago I signed up for a $3,000 clinical trial with a company
called BioSync. Yesterday the company completely vanished. And my right
arm has been radiating a strange heat for ten hours.

Two weeks earlier, the guy who met with me was young, wearing a tie that
didn’t fit and a smile that did. He told me about the experimental
compound, the organic monitoring, the two years of full medical
coverage. Three thousand dollars on signing and weekly payments in cash.
He dropped that last part like it was nothing, and I didn’t ask him to
explain it.

When I mentioned Clause 14.3, he lowered his voice like the clause might
hear us: “Standard legal boilerplate. All studies have it—doesn’t mean
anything in practice.” I nodded. It suited me to nod.

Before I signed, he paused strangely and added: “We’re not sure. But
something always happens.” Then he laughed—short, forced—and moved on to
explain the payment schedule. In that moment I needed the three thousand
dollars more than I needed to understand that sentence.

The injection was in my right forearm. I asked for the left. The
technician said the protocol specified the dominant arm. I didn’t know
why it mattered. It took me weeks to understand, but by then the arm
wasn’t entirely mine anymore.

I woke up at 2 a.m. The mattress was soaked but I wasn’t cold. The heat
was coming from my arm, localized, concentrated exactly where they’d
given me the injection. I tried to convince myself it was a delayed
reaction, that the body sometimes processes these things slowly. That
conviction lasted about four minutes.

Then I tried to move the fingers on my right hand.

They responded. But late. Like the signal had to travel a longer
distance than it should. It wasn’t pain. It was something else.

Something more like not knowing whether the movement was going to happen
or not.

Then I felt the other thing.

Something was sliding under the skin of my forearm. That’s not a figure
of speech. It’s the most precise description I have: a slow, erratic
undulation that didn’t correspond to any muscle or tendon I recognized.
Like something was exploring the space between tissues. Mapping its own
territory.

I stayed still. The movement repeated. Slower. More deliberate.

Spasms don’t have intention.

My phone buzzed.

  Email from BioSync, automated sender: operations terminated, medical
  coverage cancelled per Clause 14.3, the inbox was not being monitored.
  They apologized for any inconvenience.

The arm kept moving.

By then the heat was pulsing. It had its own pulse, independent of mine.
And what unsettled me most wasn’t the pain—there was no pain—it was
exactly that: that there was no pain. An injury hurts. A process doesn’t
need to hurt.

Three days after the email, I found the first envelope slipped under the
door. Same day as the week before, roughly the same time, no return
address. Inside was cash. The exact amount stipulated in the contract
for the weekly payment. Used bills, non-sequential.

I needed the money. I took it.

That night the movement in the arm was more intense. And for the first
time I noticed a structure: a pause, three undulations, another pause.
Repeated. Like something rehearsing a sequence. Like something
practicing a code.

When I washed my hands the next day I looked at my forearm in the
bathroom mirror and noticed something I didn’t know how to name: the
skin over the injection site looked smoother. Visibly younger than the
rest of the arm. The contrast was clear enough that I couldn’t attribute
it to the light.

The compound wasn’t just moving. It was modifying tissue from the
inside.

I searched for information on BioSync that afternoon. Company name,
registration number, anything. While the results were loading, the green
light on the laptop’s webcam blinked. Three seconds. Four. Went dark.

Technical glitch. That’s what I told myself.

I tried to find other volunteers from the trial. Posted on Reddit, on
health forums, in groups where clinical studies get discussed. Nobody
responded. Not a single thread, a single profile, a single piece of data
confirming that anyone else had gone through BioSync.

I went back to the contract.

Clause 14.3 ran for almost a page. Technical language stacked on
technical language. I read it three times. At the end of the third
paragraph, in a noticeably smaller font than the rest, I found the line:

  The participant agrees that the compound may continue its cycle beyond
  the duration of the study.

The contract didn’t end with the company. The compound had its own
cycle.

The company was only the initial delivery mechanism.

I kept reading. There was a line about “personal devices linked to the
study.” I hadn’t paid attention to it when I signed. Now I did.

The webcam hadn’t been a technical glitch.

Further on, a table. Name, age, weight, height, blood type. Everything
measurable, measured. And at the end, in a column with no visible
heading:

  Psychological profile: active dysthymia (DSM-5 300.4). Optimal
  candidate.

I took a moment to look up dysthymia. Persistent depressive disorder. A
chronic pattern of low mood, difficulty seeking help, a tendency to
internalize symptoms as one’s own thoughts rather than external signals.

BioSync knew before I did.

They didn’t choose me because of the debt. The debt was the lever, not
the criterion. They chose me because someone who doubts their own mind
doesn’t seek help fast. Because someone used to the inner voice that
says something is wrong will assume the symptoms are theirs. Because the
best subject is someone who learned not to trust what they feel.

The paranoia that came after wasn’t irrational. It was the right
response to the right information, too late.

I already wrote the first part of this. I know what comes next is worse.
That’s why I’m posting.

The envelopes will keep coming. The arm hasn’t stopped moving.

In the days that followed I understood that what was happening with the
arm wasn’t a side effect. It was something else.

I called Alex. I’m not sure what I expected—for him to come over, to see
something, for the arm to do what it did every night as soon as I went
still. We met up in his neighborhood on a Tuesday afternoon. I showed
him the forearm in the light of the coffee shop.

Nothing. Normal skin. Normal temperature. No movement.

He looked at me the way people look when they’re calculating what to
say. “Looks fine,” he said. “You sleeping?”

I didn’t tell him the truth. I said something like I’d had a weird week.
I paid for the coffee and left before he could ask me anything else.

A few days later he texted to check in. A short message, with that
spelling he never fixed no matter how many times I told him to. I didn’t
respond. I didn’t know what to tell him that wouldn’t sound like what it
would have sounded like at the coffee shop: a man who believes something
is moving under his skin and can’t prove it.

And besides—this part is harder to explain—I didn’t want to drag him
into it. If there was something in the arm that was monitoring,
transmitting, using my devices while I slept, I didn’t want Alex showing
up in any record.

So I didn’t respond. And the silence settled in.

The shift in my thoughts was gradual. So gradual that at first I
couldn’t separate them from my own.

I’ve lived for years with the inner voice that comes with dysthymia. I
know how it sounds. I know its rhythm, its logic, the path it takes from
a small worry to the conclusion that everything is pointless. I’ve known
it since I was nineteen.

The new one was different. It arrived at the end of a chain of thought,
like a sentence added after the idea had already finished. Always
phrased differently, but always the same instruction: you should let it
complete itself. Don’t fight it. It’s easier if you give up control.

The first time it came through clearly, my whole body shuddered. Not a
shiver. A physical response that happened before I could think to
react—like my body recognized something my mind hadn’t classified yet.

It wasn’t the content that disturbed me. It was the voice. It was my
voice.

Same tone, same cadence, the same mix of apathy and familiarity I
recognize as mine. There was nothing foreign in it. No strange accent,
no interference. If someone had recorded me in that moment and asked me
afterward, I would have said those thoughts were mine.

That split second where I actually agreed with them—that was the part I
couldn’t shake.

My right hand started moving on its own.

Small things at first. I left a mug of coffee on the kitchen table and
when I came back my hand was already holding it. I didn’t remember
picking it up. I assumed I’d done it without paying attention—your mind
can go on autopilot while you’re thinking about something else.

Then I was typing a message on my phone with my left hand and I noticed
my right hand scrolling the screen upward. Slowly. Reading what the
other hand was writing.

I hadn’t told it to.

That afternoon, checking my phone, I found an open message thread with a
number I didn’t recognize. The last message, sent from my number, said:

  not ready yet.

I didn’t remember writing it. It was sent at 3:41 a.m.

I called the number immediately. Disconnected. I went to the call log:
the number wasn’t there. It was on the message screen, in the thread,
and nowhere else on the phone.

That night I didn’t sleep. I lay staring at the ceiling thinking about
the difference between doing something in your sleep and not having done
it at all.

Two nights later, the phone made a sound that wasn’t any notification I
recognized. A sustained, flat tone, like a test signal. The screen
showed an equalizer moving on its own. I don’t know what it was
measuring. What I do know is that when it finished, the laptop turned
on.

A photograph appeared on the screen. It was me sleeping, taken from the
angle of the built-in camera, that same night. No message. No sender.
Just the image.

I sat on the kitchen floor with my back against the refrigerator. I
don’t know how long I stayed there.

The intrusive thoughts escalated.

They were no longer asking for submission in vague terms. Now they
described: the heat is necessary. The transmission will be soon. You are
being improved.

They still used my voice. They still sounded reasonable in the instant
before I recognized them. That fraction of a second was getting longer.

I decided to leave my phone in the kitchen before sleeping. A minimal
distance. Something physical between the bedroom and the device.

I woke up at 3:12 a.m. The phone was in my right hand. The messaging app
was open. There was a new message, sent from my number to a number I
didn’t recognize:

  the host attempted to block access.

My body had gotten up, walked to the kitchen, taken the phone, come back
to bed. All without leaving any record in me.

Days later an audio message arrived. Sender: my own number.

I played it.

It was my voice. Not a recording of something I’d said—there’s no audio
of me that sounds like this. It was my voice constructed, assembled,
played back. It said: phase two scheduled. Flat intonation.

The text messages continued over the following days:

  the host is cooperating.
  Confirm transmission window.

It took me a moment to process the word host. That was me. Not a
participant. Not a study subject. A host. Something that houses another
thing while that thing resolves what it needs to resolve.

I don’t know what the transmission window is. But based on how the
messages coordinate—the phone sending, the laptop watching, the arm
executing—I imagine a transfer. Something that leaves the compound and
goes somewhere else. Or something that enters. I don’t know which
possibility terrifies me more.

And if they’re already confirming it, the clock is running.

For those who are going to ask why I didn’t stop taking the money: I
owed $4,200. Rent doesn’t get paid with pride. But it wasn’t just the
money anymore. It was the fear of what would happen if I stopped
receiving it.

Every week the envelope arrived. Same amount. Same time. Same absence of
a return address. And every time I took it, that night the movement in
the arm was more intense and the webcam stayed on longer.

I knew. I kept taking it.

And then I decided to look for help. That’s when I discovered I no
longer existed in any system.

The worst part wasn’t finding out the company disappeared. The worst
part was finding out I had too.

I went to the hospital.

A notice appeared on the reception screen before I’d finished explaining
why I was there: Inactive participant. No admission. Refer to sponsor.
The clerk read it in silence, then looked at me the way people look when
they’re about to tell me something that isn’t their fault.

She lowered her voice: “When a trial gets cancelled, the CTMS flags all
participants as inactive. Automatically. I can’t override that. Nobody
in this hospital can.”

I asked what CTMS was.

“Clinical Trial Management System. If your sponsor closed, you don’t
exist in the system.”

No insurance, no treatment. No upfront payment, no treatment either. I
had neither. I walked out empty-handed, chest hollow—which isn’t the
same as angry. Anger needs a target. There was no target here. Just the
system.

I went to the police. I didn’t expect anything different, but I had to
try.

They took down my information. Asked if I’d taken anything. I said no. I
saw in their faces that they’d already decided not to believe me before
I finished the sentence. They told me without evidence of a direct
threat they couldn’t do anything.

That’s when I felt angry. Because the inaction wasn’t a decision.

It was a procedure.

I went to BioSync’s office address.

On the door was an eviction notice for non-payment and a corporate
dissolution filing. The building was empty. No boxes, no folders,
nothing to suggest anyone had ever worked there. A ghost office that had
stopped being one before I could get there.

No trace. No file. No history to recover.

Inside that week’s envelope I found something new alongside the bills: a
folded sheet, printed on cheap thermal paper. I unfolded it on the
table.

It was a scanned list of names. All crossed out in black correction
fluid. Thick, definitive lines over each one. Except the last.

  Daniel Rojas.

I didn’t know what I felt first: relief at not being alone, or terror at
being next. Both arrived at the same time and neither won.

I thought about what the recruiter had said before I signed. Something
always happens. He’d said it with that brief pause, that forced laugh,
that immediate change of subject. All those crossed-out names were the
something.

I counted the names. Fourteen crossed out. Fourteen people who had
signed, who had received the injection, who had cashed the envelopes.
Daniel Rojas and I were the only ones left. Or the only ones still
standing. I didn’t know how to read it.

By then the back rent was paid. The credit cards too. The $4,200 was
gone. But the envelopes kept coming and I kept taking them. Not out of
necessity anymore. Out of something harder to name: stopping would have
been accepting that the process was stopping. Or ending. And I didn’t
know which of the two options scared me more.

One night I tied both wrists to opposite sides of the bed frame. Two
belts, a knot at each end. The reasoning was simple: even if the right
arm freed itself, it would need coordination from both hands to untie
the left. It was a system that required cooperation. That made it harder
to circumvent.

I woke to a crack.

Not the belt going taut. The wood of the headboard splintering.

The right arm had yanked hard enough to break the frame. The belt was
still buckled at the wrist. The headboard wasn’t. The right hand was
free and the phone was already in it.

On the screen:

  do not interfere.

There was no pain threshold. No self-preservation mechanism stopping it.
It could use my body without the constraints my brain would impose on
it. It did so with the same precision as always: no urgency, no error,
no margin for negotiation.

I was negotiating with something. I was the merchandise.

The following night, the arm gave me back full control for an hour.
Everything worked as it should: the fingers responded, the heat faded,
the arm stayed still. One hour exactly, then back to the usual.

It wasn’t a concession. It was a demonstration. The message was that it
could take it away whenever it wanted.

I deleted Alex’s number from my phone. Not because I don’t trust him.
Because I don’t know if the arm would send him something too. I can’t
risk him showing up in some record I don’t control.

That night I went back to the list. I don’t know why. Maybe to confirm
the names were still there, that I hadn’t imagined it. I unfolded it on
the table, counted the lines again. Fourteen. Daniel Rojas’s name was
still at the bottom, intact.

I stared at it for a while. The only uncrossed name besides mine. I
thought about looking him up, reaching out, telling him he wasn’t alone
in this. Then I thought about Alex, how I’d deleted his number to
protect him. If Rojas was still alive, I wasn’t going to be the one to
drag him in.

Something made me look lower.

My name was crossed out too.

I don’t remember doing it. I don’t know when it happened. The line is
the same correction fluid, the same thickness, the same pressure as the
rest. There’s no way to tell it apart.

I don’t know if it means the process is already finished. That there’s
no going back. Or that something wrote me off before I knew it.

I don’t know if that’s the best or the worst thing I’ve seen in these
weeks.

Last night I wrote all of this.

I know what you’re going to say: psychotic episode, why didn’t I stop
taking the money, why didn’t I go to the police. I went. I thought about
it. The messages are on the phone. The envelope is on the table. The
headboard is broken.

This morning, when I woke up, there was a new message:

  transmission window confirmed.

Seven days left. I don’t know what happens when they’re up.

I decided I wasn’t going to spend them waiting.

I picked up the phone. I didn’t turn it off—I didn’t want to give it
time to react. I put it in the microwave. Off. I read somewhere in a
forum that a closed microwave blocks signals. I don’t know if that’s
true. But it’s all I have.

It didn’t buzz all night. I don’t know if that means anything. But I
slept for the first time in weeks without the arm waking me up.

I went to the table where this week’s envelope was sitting. I put it in
the drawer without counting it. I’m not touching it anymore.

I know it probably won’t do any good. I know the arm can move on its
own, that my body doesn’t fully belong to me, that the money will keep
showing up. But I need to do something that’s mine. Even if it’s
useless.

Or that’s what I want to believe. Even though I no longer know for
certain where this urge to resist is coming from. Whether it’s mine. Or
from what BioSync put inside me. For weeks now I haven’t been able to
draw that line.

I’m posting anyway. If someone reads this, they’ll know what they did.
And if BioSync reads it, they’ll know I’m not going to stay quiet.

Even if the voice saying so isn’t entirely mine anymore.

If anyone else received an envelope with no return address, if anyone
else has a name on a list they never asked to be on, if anyone else
heard their own voice saying something that wasn’t theirs—please reach
out.

Seven days left. I don’t know what happens when they’re up. But I don’t
want to find out alone.“.
