[part two]

I got Sarah and Wyatt out of the driveway by four in the afternoon.

Sarah didn’t want to go. She stood by the passenger door of her SUV with
her arms crossed, looking at the plywood I’d screwed over our bedroom
windows, asking me why I wasn’t packing a bag too. I lied straight to
her face and told her I had to stay behind to meet the insurance
adjuster first thing in the morning so we could get the siding fixed
before winter hit. She didn’t buy it, I could tell by the tight set of
her jaw, but she looked at Wyatt sitting in the backseat with his
backpack, kissed my cheek, and told me to call her the second the sun
came up.

I watched her taillights disappear down the township road until the dust
settled. Then I walked back up the porch steps, locked the heavy oak
door behind me, and started work.

By 6:00 PM, the sun was gone and the flatlands around Huron County went
pitch black. Out here, without street lights bouncing off the clouds,
darkness feels heavy, like a wet blanket thrown over the whole property.

I killed all the lights inside the house so I wouldn’t make an easy
silhouette through the kitchen curtains. Outside, my pickup was idling
in the gravel driveway, angled straight toward the back wall of the
farmhouse with the high beams blazing. Those twin beams cut right
through the dark, lighting up the shredded vinyl siding and the plywood
sheets like a stage.

I pulled a heavy wooden dining chair into the dark corner of the
kitchen, right beside the door where I had a clear view through the
small glass pane, and sat down with the Remington 870 resting across my
thighs. Five 12 gauge slugs in the tube, one in the chamber, and two
open boxes of shells sitting on the linoleum floor beside my boots.

For the first four hours, nothing happened.

Just the low, steady rumble of my truck’s V8 idling outside and the
occasional rattle of dry corn husks blowing across the yard. My eyes
started burning from staring into the bright glare of the headlights
without blinking. Every shadow cast by a swaying oak branch made my
thumb twitch toward the safety.

Around 11:30 PM, the truck’s engine sputtered.

I frowned, leaning forward in my chair. I’d filled the tank up in
Norwalk that morning, so there was no way it was out of gas.

Before I could even process it, the headlights flickered once, dimmed to
a dull orange, and died completely. The engine cut out with a heavy
mechanical clunk, plunging the entire yard into pitch blackness.

My heart slammed against my ribs. I stood up slowly, keeping low below
the windowsill, and raised the barrel of the shotgun.

I strained my ears, listening past the ringing in my head. Out in the
dark near the driveway, I heard a dull, heavy thud, followed by the
distinct sound of metal groaning and tearing.

Something massive was standing right next to my truck. Through the dark,
I could just barely make out a towering black silhouette hunched over
the open hood. It destroyed the steel hood with its bare hands and
crushed the battery and alternator into scrap just to kill the lights.

I didn’t wait for it to come to the back door. I pushed the safety off,
kicked the screen door open, and stepped out onto the back porch,
bringing the shotgun tight against my shoulder.

“Hey!” I yelled into the dark, my voice cracking slightly in the
freezing air. “Right here, you son of a bitch!”

The massive shape by the truck froze. Slowly, it turned its head toward
the porch. Even without the headlights, I could see those two dull amber
eyes glowing in the dark, fixed right on me.

Then, it dropped down onto all fours, not like a dog, more like a
sprinter setting into the blocks, and launched itself across the thirty
yards of gravel toward the porch steps.

It moved faster than anything that big had any right to move. No
barking, no growling, just the heavy, rhythmic pounding of huge feet
tearing up the gravel and the ragged, wheezing sound of its breath.

I didn’t aim for the head. I aimed dead center at that broad, hairy
chest and pulled the trigger.

The roar of the 12 gauge was deafening in the quiet night. The muzzle
flash lit up the yard for a split second, long enough for me to see the
slug hit it square in the left shoulder. The impact spun it sideways,
knocking it hard against the wooden porch railing with a sickening crack
of splintering timber.

I racked the pump fast, ejecting the smoking shell, and fired again
before it could recover.

The second slug caught it low in the ribs. It let out a sound I’ll hear
in my nightmares until the day I die. It wasn’t a dog’s yelp or a wolf’s
howl, it was a deep, rattling groan that sounded horribly like a grown
man getting the wind knocked out of him.

It scrambled backward off the steps, one of its long arms hanging limp
and useless at its side, dark, thick blood splashing onto my gravel
driveway. It staggered upright, glared back at me for one second with
its lips curled back over those white teeth, and then turned and loped
unevenly toward the north cornfield.

I fired a third shot as it hit the edge of the tall stubble, kicking up
dirt right behind its heels, and then it vanished into the dark rows of
dried stalks.

I stood on that porch shaking for twenty minutes, my ears ringing so
loud I couldn’t hear myself breathe, keeping the barrel pointed at the
treeline until my arms started cramping. It never came back out.

When the sun finally came up this morning, I walked out to the driveway
with my gun still loaded.

The hood of my Chevy was folded in half like a tin can, the battery
smashed flat. Leading away from the porch steps into the cornfield was a
heavy trail of dark, nearly black blood, thick splatters soaking into
the dirt every few feet.

I followed the blood trail about fifty yards into the corn before my
nerve finally gave out. Right where the tracks entered the thick oak
timber at the edge of my property, the blood pooled heavy in the dead
leaves. Beside a smear of dark red on a white oak trunk, the mud was
scooped out, like it had stopped to pack wet clay into its own bullet
wound with those long, human fingers.

And right next to its clawed prints in the dirt was a second set of
tracks, even wider than the first, coming out from deeper in the woods
to meet it.
I walked straight back to the house, grabbed the keys to our spare car
out of the garage, and packed a bag.

Now i’m here sitting in Sarah’s sister’s driveway in Cleveland.

I called the Huron County Sheriff’s office on the drive up and told them
a bear got into my yard, tore up my truck, and I shot it before it ran
into the woods. They said they’d send a deputy out to take a look at the
property line.

I didn’t tell them about the second set of tracks. And I didn’t tell
them that when I looked back at the woodline before pulling out of my
driveway this morning, that seven foot, wolf headed thing was still
standing in the shadows of the oaks, holding its bleeding shoulder.
Standing right beside it were two more just like it, all three of them
watching my taillights disappear down the road.
