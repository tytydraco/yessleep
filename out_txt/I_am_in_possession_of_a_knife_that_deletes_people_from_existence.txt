My apologies if parts of this message come through fractured or jumbled,
as if what I wrote was edited by a cat walking along a keyboard. I also
want to apologize if entire chunks of this text appear as blank space.
In time, I hope enough of my words remain that you understand why.

You see, I have a knife in my possession that is no ordinary knife. Its
abilities are no ordinary abilities and it is imperative that this knife
never falls into the hands of those with the wrong intentions.

I know they’re onto me. I can feel them closing in. I feel their eyes on
me when I walk around in public. I get phone calls from unlisted numbers
that I don’t answer and the voicemails they leave are all wordless with
a whirring sound in the background.

It’s not like I can hide the knife, not really. It’s eye-catching; it
features a stone handle with intricate (perhaps ancient?) carvings that
I have been unable to locate anywhere else in this world. If the knife
existed within an older society that lived on this planet, archaeology
has been unable to find further evidence of them. But the handle isn’t
the impressive part.

The blade is made of a unique yellowish crystal, the origin of which is
unknown. The Surgeon told me he’s not even certain that it comes from
this world. His best theory is that it was sent backwards in time from
the future, but that’s impossible to verify.

The mystery crystal blade must be kept sheathed when not in use. The
moment it’s out, everyone in the vicinity hears a dull-but-menacing
whine, so the Surgeon had a special scabbard made from diamond to hold
it. It will slip through and destroy any regular sleeve but diamond
seems to keep it at bay. The hard body of the scabbard is draped in
leather to avoid cutting anyone who handles it. The Surgeon kept the
whole thing inside of a lead box for as long as I knew him, and now that
box is lost but I still have the scabbard.

When the knife is unsheathed I can feel it, even if I’m not in the room.
I have spent enough time assisting the Surgeon that I can sense when
it’s touching open air, and it’s never a good feeling. I hear the whine,
but it’s more than that. You know the feeling when you’re sure there’s
someone watching you right outside of your peripheral vision? That’s
what it’s like for me when the knife is out.

It’s best not to look directly at the blade when it’s in use. It’s not
even that it’s bright, there’s just this feeling like it’s microwaving
your retinas that you won’t be able to shake. The Surgeon and I both
wear sunglasses, but if you’re not either performing the “surgery”
yourself or assisting, you should probably just keep your eyes closed
until it’s over. I always feel like I’m about to go mad when I stare at
it; it doesn’t reflect light like a normal crystal and it’s hard to tell
the exact shape of it. Sometimes it appears serrated and other times it
appears smooth. I suspect it might actually change shape after each use.

There is one thing the Surgeon uses it for, and if it has a secondary
useful purpose beyond this one we have yet to find it:

It “deletes” a human being from existence.

I’m talking gone completely, along with their entire history. After the
procedure is performed they disappear and their entire life vanishes
alongside them. Just a single poof and everything they ever said or did
is erased, as if they were never born. (Well, sort of, at least. We’ll
get back to that.)

The procedure itself is very grisly. The patient is strapped to a large
lead table that we keep in a revolving series of abandoned garages and
storage units and they must be kept awake the entire time. As the
Surgeon explains it, the procedure targets the edges of the soul itself,
and when people sleep their soul recedes into the ether of the universe.
The Surgeon concocted a drug cocktail specifically for this part; a
mixture of chemical substances that simultaneously keeps the patient
wide awake and helplessly catatonic at the same time.

The staying awake part is especially important, by the way. If the
patient is unconscious you’ll just be cutting a them-shaped hole in the
side of reality and they’ll fall into it. The soul mostly leaves the
vessel during sleep as it astral projects into a trillion different
realities and you really need the whole thing inside there for the
procedure to be a success.

(And you might be thinking “Well who cares?” but trust me, there is an
indelible line between what we’re doing and thrusting a person into the
terrifying infinity beneath our reality. You don’t want to be dropping
people into the void, the Surgeon theorizes that’s where demons and evil
spirits come from. There’s an endless amount of horrific shit in the
void and any person who spends any time in there will no doubt be
infected by it. Because they have the memory of existing in our
universe, they will recognize the edge of our reality when they get
close and try to push through from the other side. This phenomena
explains most hauntings; they are simply people who fell into the void
trying to get back by any means necessary, even if it means reaching in
and pulling you out to be their replacement. As such, the Surgeon has
scratched “do not to let patient fall into the void” onto the side of
the scabbard.)

During the operation, the patients usually lie there in wide-eyed horror
as he brings the blade to the top of their head. The surgery is in a
very real sense a “soul removal” and the one conducting it must “find”
the soul, similar to the way a phlebotomist finds a vein. He hovers the
knife above their head and when its tip glows a bright gold-white he
knows he’s found the spirit’s edge.

He then punctures the thin line between the soul and reality, which
causes the worst sound I’ve ever heard or will ever hear; it’s like a
thousand sets of nails on a thousand chalkboards sped up and inserted
into a bullet that’s been shot straight into your brain. Both the
Surgeon and I plug our ears with wax for this part; it helps diminish
the worst of the noise but it still gets to you. It’s the universe
telling you that something wrong is happening. It feels like something
is burrowing into your ear drums and stabbing around. If ever I wondered
whether what we were doing was evil, that sound would prove the
confirmation.

He runs the blade along the outline of their body slowly and
deliberately, making sure never to steer off-course from the gold-white
glow. (It flickers if you get too close to the edge.) The path of the
cut shines with a blindingly-bright white light, another reason those
sunglasses come in handy. You must trace the full outline of the
patient’s body: the top of the head, to the shoulder, to their elbow, to
their hand and around each finger, back up to the armpit before going
down the torso to their legs and feet, and then back up again to round
out the other half. (We always tie the legs together to avoid needing to
cut near their genitals, keeps it less messy.) The Surgeon prefers to do
this procedure clockwise but I don’t think you necessarily have to.

Once you connect back all the way around to the top of the head there
will be a flash that feels like someone punched you in the gut while you
were having a stroke. Your vision will go blurry and nothing will look
like anything for what’s either a second or a thousand years, impossible
to tell for sure. The theory is that this is reality doing a hard
refresh; breaking everything down to remove the soul and rebuilding
everything as if that soul never happened. We call it the blink.

And what did I do in this partnership, you ask? Well, I assist in the
making of the cocktail. I help lift the patient onto the table,
sometimes being called on to help hold them down if we still need to
inject them. I’m a bigger dude so if there’s someone in the room
observing us I’m ready to intercept them, should they make any sudden
moves. Some people gain a conscience halfway through the operation and
try to stop it but trust me, once it is started it must be finished. You
do not want half a soul flapping around on the edge of the void. You
won’t like what it attracts.

It didn’t take long assisting the Surgeon for me to understand the
cruelty of the algorithm that governs our reality. You would be amazed
at what disappears about a person versus what after-effects of their
existence remain. You will never remember their face, or their voice, or
their name ever again but sometimes you will swear you saw them in full
in your dreams.

This job gives you a deeper appreciation for every single human being
currently alive, because the impact of removing any single one causes a
tangible change. The day following a surgery you will walk around
feeling like nothing is quite right, like every building you see is
slightly off by two inches. You will look at open patches of sidewalk
and swear there was a bench or a trash can there. You will have
conversations with loved ones and feel like some crucial piece of
information once shared between you was lost, even if neither of you
ever knew the erased. The butterfly effect of their absence stretches
further than you can ever imagine. That is the magnitude of a person,
and I promise you that neither I nor the Surgeon have ever taken this
lightly.

We have done hundreds of these operations by now, I believe. For obvious
reasons we will never know the true number. The way each person gets
erased is different each time, but we have organized them into three
different categories:

Some people are what we call clean cuts; the machine that they were a
cog in just plugs a slightly different cog into the missing hole and
continues as usual. It’s not out of the normal to go to those people’s
jobs to find someone with a similar haircut and build doing essentially
the same thing they did, to go to the house they shared with roommates
or a significant other and find an approximation living there and
everyone getting on more or less exactly as they had before. It’s as if
their entire life they were just a soda can sitting inside of a vending
machine somewhere, and the moment their number got plugged in and they
fell, the next identical can of soda slid forward to fill that space.

(These people are what I call low impact people, which I have been told
is an insulting thing to say. I tend to find that only low impact people
are insulted, for the record.)

Then there are the medium impact people, or as I call them, messy cuts.
These people might have had children or a pet or a blog or something
that depended on their continued existence. These ones are always rough
to revisit: most of the time the kids are totally gone but sometimes
they’re still around and they’re different. Like scary different.

(I think a lot of serial killers are just people who’ve had one of their
parents erased and on some level know it. Like they absolutely should
have disappeared in the refresh but due to some algorithmic oversight
they’re still here and they need to make it everyone else’s problem.)

A good example of a messy cut is this one woman whose husband we’d
deleted: she woke up the day following the surgery looking for him and
the kids they’d had together, sadly also deleted in the aftermath. She
no longer knew any of their names but she swore they existed and would
not drop it, alienating pretty much everyone in her life. She put up
flyers and did local news interviews attempting to start a nationwide
campaign to find them; she even got a police sketch artist to do fairly
accurate renderings. Her family promptly had her committed to a mental
asylum and to the best of my knowledge she’s still there to this day.

(Part of me did want to intervene and explain, to talk to her and let
her know that she isn’t crazy, that she really did have a family. But
ultimately I knew that it would just bring more attention to our
operation than we could afford.)

And finally, there are the deep cuts. These are the foundational people,
the ones you can’t take out without severely altering the nature of
society. Imagine you could snap your fingers and make it so George
Washington, Winston Churchill, or even someone like Adolf Hitler had
never existed. Reality would twist to fill the space, sure, but entire
countries would come out renamed (and borderlines redrawn) and there
would be new people to fill their space in history.

(If ever you’re learning about some place’s past and it feels hastily
improvised, it’s probably because someone formative in that part of the
world fell upon the knife.)

The Surgeon had one major rule: no deep cuts. No politicians, no movie
stars, no one with so much as a Wikipedia page. No one who had touched
the lives of millions. No one who had shaped opinions, captured hearts
and minds. The risk was far too great, he said.

And for a while, it was an easy thing for us to avoid.

“You know, I initially started this with pure intentions,” the Surgeon
told me while he was drunk one night. He had already done dozens of
surgeries by that point and told me he felt like a hundred different
people. He said that he felt his appearance warping significantly with
each blink: most of the time it was a new haircut, but sometimes his
skin color would change and at one point he even spent two weeks as a
woman until he did another operation.

“The Curse of the Knife is what I call it! Every time you erase someone
else’s identity you also erase a piece of your own. It’s difficult to
remember exactly how your life was before each blink because the way it
is now is technically the way it always was. Your life from before is
just a fleeting half-remembered dream and the new memories that come
flooding through supplant the old ones. A real freaking nightmare,” he
proclaimed between swigs.

Our goal was just to run a Mom & Pop-style endeavor where we offered an
ethical alternative to suicide. “Oh you don’t want to be around anymore
but you don’t want your parents to miss you? Try us!” was the general
pitch.

We would mostly service businessmen and wanted criminals. (A good amount
of the time they were the same thing.) We would have to route the money
through at least fifty various accounts and banking institutions or else
it would vanish along with the patient. Occasionally people came to us
asking us to erase someone else. Which the Surgeon wasn’t opposed to
doing but the fee was way higher and they had to catch the person
themselves.

(“We’re medical professionals, not hired thugs!” he’d scream, waving the
unsheathed knife around as a power move.)

These were always my least favorite surgeries; they’d kick and scream
until we injected them with the concoction and then we’d watch them give
in. All the fight drained out of their body but the way they’d stare at
you, damn. Even after they’d vanish you’d feel their gaze for the rest
of your life. No amount of blinks would ever remove that weight from
your conscience.

The Surgeon worked out a deal with a local criminal enterprise and
roughly twice a month we would be summoned to erase either someone who
owed a lot of money or was a witness to a pending criminal trial. They
wouldn’t say much about the person and we never asked. The Surgeon
taught them how to make the cocktail so the patient would already be
zonked by the time we got our hands on them.

It ran like clockwork for a few years. I participated in hundreds of
operations, a little piece of myself disappearing each time. I felt like
a book whose pages were being torn out one-by-one; I was richer than I
could have ever imagined. I could do anything I wanted to. But I was
dead-to-feeling.

(One thing I neglected to tell you earlier about the blink is that
sometimes you lose people. Like you’d do a surgery on a Tuesday and
swore you’d made plans with someone for Friday that you could no longer
recall any information about. You’d pass a stranger at the grocery store
or the mall and feel like maybe you two were something to each other
once. You’d constantly wonder if at some point in the past you had a
precious love in your life that you had forgotten, and your heart would
jump if you merely caught a glimpse of the embers. You’d look through
your phone and find pictures of yourself alone with maybe a bit too much
empty space on one side and wonder if there had actually been someone
else there alongside you, someone you’d shaken off years ago that you no
longer had any recollection of. The Curse of the Knife’s worst symptom
by far, if you ask me.)

Eventually the mafia outfit we were working with got busted by the FBI.
They stormed the safehouse and found the Surgeon’s contact information.
People started knocking on his door. He would call me at all hours of
the night swearing up and down that he’s being followed. He’d previously
been the calmest person I’d ever met, but now he seemed like an escape
convict: uncertain, paranoid, eyes darting around, reading every room,
ready to bail at any moment.

He told me somebody from the CIA visited him, telling him that they knew
about the knife. They knew what it did and wanted to hire him to use it
for them. There was a pretty popular presidential candidate gaining
steam and they wanted him deleted. The Surgeon refused, he didn’t want
that on his hands. The man just left without saying much else; the
Surgeon kept the knife on him at all times by this point and the agent
was smarter than to make any moves.

The Surgeon packed a couple bags and left his house for good, deciding
instead to stay in a series of hotels around the city. Every now and
then a random police officer or reporter would approach him in public
asking intrusive questions and he’d lure them somewhere else, somewhere
secluded. Somewhere where he’d answer anything they’d ask and get them
comfortable enough to let their guard down.

And then wham! He’d hit them with the syringe. An hour later I’d show up
with my truck and we would take them to wherever the operating table
currently was.

It carried on like this for about six months until one night where the
Surgeon showed up at my house. He begged me to come with him, saying
there was an urgent surgery we needed to do, it couldn’t wait. It had to
be now.

I stayed on my guard the entire drive there. Part of me was worried he’d
decided he could no longer trust me. We had been keeping the operating
table in an abandoned barn on the other side of the state and once we’d
parked it was still a seven minute walk up a large, rocky hill to get
there. Moving fifty pounds of lead across vertical terrain was a
logistical nightmare, but it had nothing on the feeling of walking five
feet ahead of him that night, knowing what he had on him. I didn’t want
to assume the worst about a guy who was my only friend in the entire
universe but I couldn’t help being on my guard. I stared down at the
dirt path ahead illuminated by my phone’s flashlight and I listened
intently for any quick moves behind me.

It didn’t help that he was dead quiet the whole time; usually he was
extremely talkative, gleefully pontificating about movies he’d seen or
big ideas he’d had. Tonight, however, he was a different person. Tired,
desperate, miserable. I could hear his shallow, frantic breaths behind
me as we walked. He was muttering something I could not make out over
the crunching of the leaves beneath our feet.

He did not make a move on the walk up but there was a full syringe on
the table when we arrived.

“Erase me.” He said, and injected himself.

I didn’t argue or protest. I complied. This man had been my sole
relationship for years but I knew what must be done; if we removed him
we could get the government off our backs.

While we waited for the drugs to kick in, he told me about the person
who gave him the knife. He had never really mentioned the guy before but
that night he told me all about him: what he liked, funny things he’d
said, precious moments they’d spent together.

He said this man was the one who warned him about the cost of the deep
cuts. About why we should never do them no matter the reward:

“These governments, they always use the knife on the freedom fighters.
That’s the only reason they could possibly want it. You ever read a
history book and wonder how an entire civilization fell into fascism?
Why a dictator rose up seemingly overnight? It’s probably because
someone took the knife and used it on every single person that spoke out
against them. And once every dissenting voice was erased, everyone else
just woke up from that final blink in a haze, wondering how they got
there, puzzled at where their fucking rights went. Human history is just
a series of disastrous deep cuts, and I can’t let another one happen on
my watch.”

The Surgeon confessed that he had used the knife on this man, and at
first, he didn’t feel like anything about it. Thought he had just done a
regular procedure. He’d completely forgotten the guy, lost all of those
memories.

But then, slowly, one-by-one, they’d resurface. He would remember a
moment he previously thought lost forever. Get a glimpse of his face, or
a chunk of a conversation they’d had.

“They come back, these people we erase. They return, after time. With
every single blink, a little bit of them bleeds back in. I believe that.
I’ll be gone for a long time but I’ll be back someday. I promise.”

I saw the pain in his eyes as I dragged the blade along the edges of his
soul. My hands moved on pure instinct while my gaze locked onto his
face. I stared at him, seeing his terror and his longing, and right
before I completed the outline I swear I watched a wave of relief pass
over him.

And he was right: I forgot about him for a few good years. I was pretty
wealthy by that point, managing a few properties so I didn’t need to use
the knife. I kept it safe, hidden away deep in my attic.

But then I made a few bad investments, and then the economy tanked,
causing the housing market to crash and I found myself in urgent need of
a large amount of money very quickly. I don’t want to act like my run of
bad luck was purely the knife’s will, but people who possess the device
do have a noted history of suddenly needing to use it.

So I opened up shop again. I put some feelers out and gained some
clients. I started doing surgeries once more. Started erasing people.
Good people, evil people, people who didn’t deserve it and people who
did.

And he came back to me. I’ll probably never remember his name but I
remember his words. I remember the impact they’ve had on me.

I’ve had a few different assistants since restarting but they keep
disappearing with the blinks. The Surgeon warned me this might happen if
I ever used the knife again, by the way. He said he couldn’t keep a
helper longer than two or three surgeries before they’d vanish
completely alongside the victim.

“No one ever stuck around until I found you,” he told me.

And that’s why I’m posting here. I have the government on my tail now,
and even though I keep abducting and disappearing the agents that follow
me, I can’t fully shake them. That candidate is running again and he’s
way ahead in the polls this time. Voter suppression can only do so much.

I need someone who’s ready and willing to take this knife from me. I
need someone without attachments, or at least someone who doesn’t care
about losing their current ones. I need someone who is capable of
deleting me. Someone who won’t disappear.

If that’s you, please reach out. Get a message to me however you can.

Much like the Surgeon before me, I’ve lost enough of the best parts of
my life and I’m ready to be gone. But I need to know I’m leaving a
soldier behind; someone who understands the weight of what they’re
taking on here. I need someone who will promise never to make a deep
cut, no matter how much money they are offered. No matter how much power
they stand to gain from it.

Someone who will carry me around the way I carry him.

Is it you? Are you willing to erase me?

Can you handle what comes after?
