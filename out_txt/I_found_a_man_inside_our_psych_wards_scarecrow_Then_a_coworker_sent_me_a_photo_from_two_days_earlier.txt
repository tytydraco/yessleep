The scarecrow was facing the wrong way.

Silvia stopped with her key still in the lock.

“Who turned him around?”

He stood in the last raised bed, near the hedge. Brown jacket, old
trousers, a hat on his burlap head. Usually he faced the hedge, with his
back to the door. That morning he was looking straight at us.

His arms were hanging down, too. Normally, under the clothes, he was
just a plastic frame zip-tied to a stake, with a crosspiece for the arms
and an old gardening glove tied to each end. The crosspiece slipped now
and then, and Silvia was forever fixing it. I assumed it had slipped
again. I was more concerned about a white object behind the second bed.
It turned out to be a plant label.

I’m a nurse on a locked psychiatric ward in southern Italy. Our
courtyard is three floors below the ward, out of sight of its windows.
Before the gardening group comes down, one of us walks through and
checks for anything thrown over the perimeter fence. Cigarette packets,
cans, things patients could hurt themselves with. We check the hedge,
too.

The other person waits by the door with the keys and the ward phone.

That Friday, six patients were upstairs with Nunzia, our head nurse, and
Irene, another nurse. Once I gave the all-clear, Silvia would call them
down.

She ran the gardening group. The scarecrow was hers, made with clothes
her father had worn in his vegetable garden. She called it the old man
and got annoyed when anyone pulled its hat over the green eyes she’d
painted on the sack.

The brim was down that morning.

“I’ll turn him around,” I said.

I checked the first three beds, picked up the plant label and put it on
the potting table. Then I walked to the far corner.

Up close, the jacket looked overstuffed. Straw poked out of the cuffs. I
thought somebody had been adding to it during Wednesday’s session.

I took hold of a sleeve to turn it back toward the hedge.

The glove caught my wrist.

I tugged once, thinking I’d snagged myself on something. Then the
fingers tightened, separately, and I felt the heat of a hand through the
fabric.

“Leave it where it is.”

A man’s voice, muffled by the sack.

I tried to pull away. He stepped down from the bed without letting go.

“Silvia.”

It came out too quietly. I said it again, and she ran toward me.

I wish I’d told her to stay at the door. Instead I’d called her over, as
if this were something she could help me sort out.

She grabbed my shoulder. His free hand caught her wrist, trapping the
coiled key strap beneath his fingers.

For a moment none of us moved. Silvia stared at the glove on her arm.
Then she made a sound I’d never heard from her and pulled back hard
enough to stretch the key strap.

He shoved her away.

She stumbled against the potting table. A tray fell, and the noise
seemed to get through to her.

“Inside,” I told her. “Lock it. Get help.”

She hesitated.

“Go!”

I watched her reach the door. When I heard the lock turn, I felt better
for perhaps a second.

Then he put his other hand above my elbow and twisted my arm across my
chest.

I bent to ease the pressure. He stepped backward, and I had to follow
him.

We’ve practiced getting out of wrist holds at work. I tried turning
toward his thumb, but he kept forcing my elbow up. My shoulder hurt so
badly that I couldn’t finish the movement.

I hit him with my free hand. My knuckles caught something hard through
the burlap. His head jerked sideways and he grunted, but he didn’t
release me. I swung again and caught his collar.

He dragged me another step.

I got my heel against the wooden border of the bed and braced there. He
pulled twice. The second time, something shifted in my shoulder, and I
heard myself begging him to stop.

He eased the pressure just enough for me to straighten.

Then he pulled again.

Silvia was behind the little wired-glass window, speaking into the
phone. I could see her mouth moving. I shouted for her anyway.

My heel slipped off the wood.

We went backward into the hedge. Branches scraped my face, and I grabbed
a handful of them. They bent toward us. I tried to hold on lower down,
where they were thicker, but he turned my arm again and my hand opened.

He hadn’t looked behind him once.

I thought we were about to hit the fence. Instead he backed through an
opening in it, pushing a loose flap of wire aside with his shoe. My
shoulder followed him through.

Beyond him was the service road.

I started screaming then. Until that point I’d been trying to get my arm
free. Now I was trying to keep any part of myself inside the courtyard.

I caught the fence with my free hand. A wire tore through the back of my
uniform. He pulled, and one of my feet came off the ground.

The courtyard door banged open.

Silvia shouted my name. Nunzia was behind her, coming down the path at a
run.

His grip loosened. I pulled against it, and suddenly there was nothing
to pull against. I fell backward into the branches.

By the time I got my head up, he was across the road. The brown jacket
disappeared between two maintenance vehicles.

I tried to stand and couldn’t make my legs do it. Nunzia kept telling me
to stay where I was, which annoyed me because I wasn’t going anywhere.

His hat lay under the hedge, the sack still inside it. The burlap must
have caught on the wire and torn off his head when he turned to run. By
then I was on my back in the branches. I never saw his face. Nobody did.

Silvia picked it up, then dropped it.

Below the painted green eyes, someone had cut two smaller holes. The low
brim had kept them in shadow.

She sat beside me while Nunzia called security. Her keys were still
around her wrist. They rattled every time she moved, so she trapped them
between her knees.

“I locked you out,” she said.

“I told you to.”

“I know.”

Security found the plastic frame on the other side of the fence, in the
long grass along the service road. The ties had been cut and the clothes
stripped off it.

Next to it, a patch about as long as a man had been pressed flat. Around
it lay empty water bottles, a lot of them. Some looked new. Others had
been in the sun so long their labels had faded white.

The gap in the fence was old enough for dirt to have collected on the
bent ends. One officer said that didn’t tell him when it had been cut.
He pushed the flap back into place, and it sat flush with the rest of
the wire. From inside, through the hedge, it just looked like fence.
There were no cameras covering that stretch of road.

Someone could come and go through there without ever entering the
hospital.

Silvia said the courtyard hadn’t been used on Thursday. Wednesday had
been the last gardening session.

“Was it checked then?” the officer asked.

She looked at me.

“Yes,” I said. “I checked it.”

At the emergency department, I had to ask someone to help get my uniform
off. My wrist was swollen and I couldn’t lift my arm properly. Nothing
was broken. They cleaned the scratches on my back, and I sat waiting for
the paperwork with one sleeve hanging empty.

I kept going over Wednesday’s check. There was very little to remember.
I’d walked the path, looked through the beds and checked along the
hedge. The scarecrow had been facing the right way. I was sure of that.

An officer asked for a photograph of the clothes. Silvia went through
the gardening group’s folder that evening and sent me a picture before
she sent it to the police.

It was from Wednesday.

We’d ordered brioche with gelato for the patients and staff that
morning. Silvia had decided we could eat outside before working on the
beds. The hospital café sent over a bag of brioche and the gelato in
tubs, packed in a Styrofoam box with ice packs. It was already hot by
ten, so Silvia set the box down in the only shade in the courtyard,
against the hedge at the end of the last bed, and filled the brioche
there one at a time so the gelato wouldn’t melt.

Irene had chocolate down the front of her uniform. One patient had
warned her that rubbing it would make it worse. She’d told him she knew
how to clean a shirt, rubbed it, and made it worse.

He’d laughed so hard he had to put his brioche down.

That was the Wednesday I remembered.

Then I saw the fourth bed.

The scarecrow was facing the hedge, as I’d remembered, but its arms hung
beside its body. The jacket was full across the back. The hat was low.

Silvia called.

“Look at his feet.”

I enlarged the picture. Below one trouser cuff, through a gap in the
plants, was the white edge of a shoe.

“Did you put shoes on him?”

“No.”

She sent a photograph from the previous week. Empty trouser legs hung on
either side of the stake. The jacket lay flat against the crosspiece,
sleeves stretched out.

I put the two pictures beside each other on my laptop.

On Wednesday, there was someone standing in the bed.

Silvia was still on the phone.

“I saw the hat,” she said. “The arms, too. I was going to fix him when
I’d finished with the brioche.”

“You didn’t.”

“No. Somebody needed the bathroom.”

Neither of us said anything for a while.

I’d spent the afternoon thinking that if I’d paid attention to the arms
on Friday, none of it would have happened. Silvia had noticed them on
Wednesday. If she’d reached up to push the hat back, her hand would have
been on his face, with the patients a few feet behind her. I was
grateful now that somebody had needed the bathroom.

After we hung up, more pictures came through. Silvia was sending
everything from that morning to the police, and she sent the photographs
to me, too.

There was a photograph of the tomato plants with one patient’s hand
holding a stem out of the way. Another of the first ripe pepper. Irene
had posed for a picture of the chocolate stain, one hand on her hip,
trying to look stern.

The fourth bed was in the background of several of them. You could make
out the hat between the stakes, or part of the brown jacket, and below
it the white corner of the Styrofoam box. Every one of us had gone up to
that box at least once. Silvia had stood at it for the better part of
half an hour, filling brioche.

I had to keep reminding myself that the shape above it was a person. I’d
known that shape in the corner for years.

Not everyone had. One of the men in the group had been admitted the week
before, and that Wednesday was his first time in the courtyard. While
Silvia was still filling brioche, he’d asked if the man in the corner
wasn’t having one.

Silvia had told him that was the old man, and that he was in charge of
the tomatoes. He’d nodded and gone back to his gelato.

I keep coming back to that. For once, somebody had looked at the corner
and seen a man. And the man in the corner had been close enough to hear
him say it.

Then I reached the picture Silvia had taken after somebody dropped their
gelato.

I remembered that, too. The patient had dropped it right by the box, the
moment Silvia handed it over, and he kept apologizing. Irene told him to
stop, it was only ice cream, and went to get paper towels.

In the photograph she was crouching beside the last bed, wiping the
paving stones. One knee was down, the other raised. Her mouth was open;
she was talking to someone outside the frame.

Above her was the brown sleeve.

The glove hung just behind her shoulder. She was close enough that when
she stood up, she’d have needed to step forward to avoid brushing
against it.

In every other picture, the hat faced the hedge. In this one it was
tipped down, toward the top of her head.

I called Silvia back.

“Did you send Irene these?”

“Not yet.”

I almost asked her not to. As if Irene would somehow be safer if she
never saw how close she’d been.

“We should tell her,” I said.

Silvia asked if I’d be there when she told her. I said yes.

I kept that photograph open after we hung up. I could remember Irene
kneeling there. I remembered someone asking for more napkins, and Silvia
counting heads to see who was still waiting. He’d been standing right
above the box while we argued over whether the café had forgotten the
pistachio.

I don’t know how long he stayed. When we left, he could have stepped out
of the clothes, or walked straight through the fence still wearing them.
We couldn’t see the courtyard from upstairs. There was nothing to keep
him there until Friday.

But he’d seen me do the check. He’d been facing the hedge. I’d checked
the hedge. I must have stood right in front of him, on the side with the
eyes.

He couldn’t have seen the door from there. He didn’t need to. He’d heard
Silvia’s keys. He’d heard me tell her it was clear before she called the
ward.

On Friday he’d turned to face us.

At first I thought he’d wanted a better view of the door. Now I think he
just needed to look wrong. Everyone knew the old man faced the hedge,
and whoever did the check would walk over and put him right.

I’d even said it out loud. “I’ll turn him around.”

The police kept the sack and the hat. As far as we’ve been told, they
still don’t have a name.

Silvia asked once whether they thought they’d recover the jacket.

She apologized before the officer could answer. He told her she didn’t
need to.

The courtyard stayed closed for three weeks while they replaced the
fence. The request for cameras along the service road is still waiting
for somebody’s signature.

When I offered to clear out the fourth bed, Silvia said she’d already
done it. She put herbs there, things the patients could use in the
cooking group.

The first morning we went back, she held out the keys to me.

“You take them.”

“You’re coming with me.”

“I know. Take them anyway.”

I put the strap around my wrist. We walked the path together, checked
behind the beds, looked through the hedge at the new fence. I made
myself stand in the corner long enough to see the whole of it.

When we got back, Silvia called upstairs.

The patients came down with Nunzia and Irene. There were seedlings to
put in, and one of the watering cans had split along the bottom. I went
to get another. By the time I came back, somebody was complaining that
the fourth bed still needed a scarecrow.

Silvia said the herbs would be fine without one.

The police had asked her for one more thing. They wanted her to go back
through the whole folder, every session since she’d first stood the old
man in that bed, and mark any picture where his arms were down.

She sent the ones she marked to the police. This time, she didn’t send
them to me.

I asked her once how many she’d marked. She said she’d given them all to
the police.

I said that wasn’t what I’d asked.

“The first one’s from the summer I put him out there,” she said, and
went back to the seedlings.

I thought about the crosspiece after that. Silvia had been fixing it for
as long as I’d worked there. Every few weeks we’d find the arms hanging
and the jacket sitting crooked on its frame, and she’d tut and
straighten him. More than once she’d had to replace the ties.

We’d always blamed the wind.

We still use the courtyard. The checks take longer, and we do them
together. Most mornings, once the patients come down, I can get on with
the group.

Wednesday is harder to leave alone. Friday, I can account for: his
hands, the fence, the door opening. Wednesday keeps coming back in
ordinary pieces. Irene rubbing at her shirt. Somebody asking for another
napkin. Me trying to finish my gelato before it ran down my hand.

I can remember where I stood and who I spoke to. I can even remember
being pleased that we’d managed to get everyone outside for once.

Then I look at the photograph of Irene and have to go through it all
again. Because when I remember that morning, the scarecrow is still
empty.

I’ve spent years of mornings in that courtyard. I remember all of them
the same way.

The old man is always empty.
