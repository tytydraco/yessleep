The memory of the last Halloween night I trick-or-treated starts around
six in the evening, 2019.

“Congratulations, you’re the sixth Freddy Fazbear I’ve seen tonight.”
The teenager pitched a mini candybar into my brother’s bag. “You’re all
real original.”

Dilly shrank down but I pushed in front of him. “Aw, that’s cool, you
can count past five! I bet your mom is super proud!”

His face colored and he slammed the door in our faces. I took my
brother’s hand, leading him off the porch step. “Don’t worry Dilly, your
costume is cool. He’s just sad he didn’t dress up.” My brother’s real
name was Dylan, but Dilly was our affectionate nickname for him. Hearing
it seemed to make him perk up.

“Aw, I told you not to go to the Hutchins’ house!” Nick was waiting in
the street, candy bag slung over his shoulder and his plastic Bumblee
mask atop his head. He was sweating; the night was cool with the cidery
bite of autumn, but his costume didn’t breathe. He’d already unzipped
the back a couple inches, his damp t-shirt crumpled underneath. “That
guy always makes fun of us when we ride by.”

Dilly tugged my pinkie. “Mona, you didn’t get any candy.” His voice was
soft, eyes set deep in the beaming Freddy mask. His hands were slightly
sweaty.

“It’s okay, there are plenty more houses.” I adjusted my glittery blue
dress. The shiny fabric and the tulle didn’t exactly line up, as the
costume was made for a girl a bit shorter, but ever since I turned
eleven, I was getting tall in all the weirdest ways. My hair only added
to the height, densely curled and dark. Even treated with product, it
was impossible to replicate the pretty little updo of Cinderella. At
least I wasn’t melting like Nick.

“We shoulda gone to my church’s truck or treat.” The last of our crew,
Emily, was sitting on the curb. She’d insisted on dressing as Jack
Skellington and she’d even persuaded her parents - probably her Dad, he
doted on her and did not understand footwear - to let her wear platform
shoes to add height under the legs. It looked cool when she stood
towering over us, but her feet were already sore. The white face paint
made her look washed out under the street light. “A lot less walking for
a lot more candy.”

“Well we can’t do that now.” I tried to ignore the churning of guilt in
my belly. “Remember, if our parents ask, Emily’s parents chaperoned.”

“And if my parents ask, Mona and Dilly’s parents chaperoned,” Emily
intoned.

“And my mom won’t ask because she’s too busy drinking pumpkin spice
schnapps,” Nick interjected. “Relax, it’ll be fine.”

I cast my gaze down the street. Jack-o’-lanterns reeked pungent and
sweet, their teeth and eyes going soft and gaping where squirrels and
birds scraped at the openings. The sky was overcast, making the bright
windows even more brilliant. Clusters of kids moved in colorful herds,
but their bags of candy had only a shallow lining along the bottoms. The
night was young.

“Are we ready to go to the bridge?” Nick’s voice broke my thoughts.

My brother looked up at me. “What’s at the bridge?”

“There’s a scary story told around there.” Emily hesitated. “It’s kind
of a game.”

Dilly quivered. I patted his arm.

“Don’t worry. I packed your ear buds. You can listen to something else.
It’s real quick, nothing’s actually gonna happen.”

This calmed him, and I fished the earbuds out of my glittery purse. If
our parents knew I’d brought Dilly out here without trusted adult
supervision, I was toast. I wouldn’t have internet privileges until
computers were holograms and robots cooked breakfast. But Nick had
really, really wanted to check out the bridge on Halloween night. And
Dilly…some of the kids at church were just total brats to him because he
was different.

My ears grew hot with indignation and I gently gave Dilly his earbuds.
“You’ve been really brave, you know? I’m proud of you.” 

He blinked up at me and finally lifted his mask. His dark eyes creased,
crooked baby teeth a sweet smile. I burned with the want to punch out
anyone that ever hurt him. I could fuss at him, but I had the privilege.
I knew him. “Thanks Mona.”

I set an episode of TMNT to play; Nick had his own phone and offered it
without complaint. I was so jealous. Dilly took my hand and followed
along down the street, listening to the turtles as he glanced around,
letting me lead him. Nick and Emily never made fun of Dilly. They just
let him come along, talked to him. Downloaded a couple episodes of
cartoons for him to watch if he started getting upset. I liked that
about them.

We helped Emily up and kept her balanced as we tottered along to the
corner. “So you gonna tell the story there or on the way?” I asked.

Nick held up a finger. “Wait until we’re there. Gotta have the right
ambivalence.”

“I think it’s ‘ambiance,’” Emily said uncertainly.

“That too.”

The neighborhood we trick-or-treated in was mine, Dilly, and Emily’s.
Nick lived in the next neighborhood and it could be reached via the
nearest main road, but there was another, quieter back road one could
take. It crossed over a creekbed, and when the rain was heavy, it could
be ten feet of water under the bridge. It had only flooded once in the
past forty years, though all of us were morbidy interested to see it
happen in our lifetime. We were forbidden to play in the creek, and we
heeded that warning. But nobody had ever forbidden us from walking onto
the bridge to peer over the rail. And the reason we wanted to be out and
about without adult supervision was because Nick had a spooky ritual
relating to just that creek.

It was eerily quiet, the closest streetlight well beyond the bridge.
Black branches cut into its pale haze, but it was just enough light
bouncing off the metal rails for the group to line up beside it and gaze
into the quietly churning creek below. I smelled leaves soaking and
rotting, mud collecting, winter coming. Chocolate would soon give way to
pumpkin, and then ginger.

“All right Nick,” I said, and Dilly did not react. He was content to
watch the water and couldn’t hear as long as we were quiet.

Nick cleared his throat and began, inject his voice with a rasp of
intrigue.

“Once, in this very town, there lived a little girl. She lived on the
wrong side of the tracks, outside of town - where, in fact, your
tale-teller lives - and she had few friends. Her father was known to be
violent and neglected her, but nobody ever helped her. Her mother had
left long ago, never even calling to check on her daughter.” He paused.
“Or maybe her mom was dead? I don’t know, there’s no mom is all.”

“Did you make up this story?” I asked. Most of these kinds of urban
legends started with abused kids, and Nick had unfettered internet
access. It wouldn’t have surprised me if it were a concoction of his own
making.

“No, it’s real. I mean, I really heard it somewhere. I just don’t know
all the details.” He got back into character. “On Halloween night, she
dressed up to go into the nearest neighborhood, hoping to get candy. The
poor girl had to make her own costume because her father wouldnt buy her
one or help her make it, even though stuff was super cheap back then.
But all the people of the neighborhood laughed at her costume. It was
clumsy, and no one gave her any treats because she looked so silly.”

“What was the costume?” Emily interrupted.

“Uh…I don’t know.” Nick’s voice dropped the drama again. “One of those
old monsters. Doesn’t matter, it was just kind of crummy. This happened
before the Halloween movie came out, so it was literally forever ago.”

Emily nodded. “Hm. Continue.”

“The girl headed back home crying, no candy in her bag and no Halloween
fun to cheer her. She reached this bridge and was crying so hard, she
didn’t see the lights of a car coming.”

I glanced at Dilly. He was bobbing his head in time with the music of
the episode, eyes closed. 

“The car hit her straight on. The impact sent the girl flying off the
bridge and into the creek below. Police were called out and the child’s
body was pulled from the water, cheeks still red from crying.”

Emily put a hand on her heart. “Poor thing,” she said softly.

Nick nodded. “But here’s the strange part. That girl so wanted to have a
fun Halloween that her soul couldn’t accept that she had died before
getting to experience it. So her soul stayed behind, lingering as a
ghost, angry at those who trick-or-treat and think nothing of those left
out. So if you come onto this bridge at night, there’s a chance…that the
child’s ghost might turn up.”

I rolled me eyes. But the thought of a waterlogged child in the road,
staring at us with dark eyes, made the night seem a little colder.

“The legend goes,” Nick continued, “that if you walk on the bridge, you
have to take one piece of candy and throw it into the creek. The child
will taste the sweetness of the night of treats and forget her anger for
a time, allowing you to leave in peace.”

“And if we don’t?” Emily asked, and her eyes were wider than before.
Either she was buying into it or having fun pretending to.

“You might not make it safely off the bridge,” Nick finished ominously.

We stood quiet for time, absorbing the story. “One piece of candy…we
going to do it?” I asked.

“I am. I think that’s the saddest story.” Emily opened her candy bag and
inspected the contents. “Um, do you think she had any allergies?”

“She’s dead, Emily. She can’t die from a peanut anymore.”

She glared balefully at me. “It might give her a bad memory! I’ll give
her a Milky Way. They’re my favorite.”

“You are too nice,” I said, but I couldnt keep from smiling. “Okay. I’ve
got…Smartees. Ugh. Why do they even sell these nasty, chalky things?”

“I think they’re okay,” Nick said. He pulled out some Laffy Taffy.

Dilly had noticed the movement and took out one earbud. “Is the scary
thing over?”

“Pretty much. We’re going to each throw a piece of candy off the bridge
into the creek. It’s…tradition.” I almost told him he didn’t have to,
but the most superstitious little part of me thought it safer for him to
do so. Just in case.

Dilly didn’t need to be persuaded. “Um…I don’t really like these,” he
said, pulling out a mini peanut butter cup.

Nick gasped. “Heathen!”

“Where did you even hear a word like that?” I asked.

“My Grandma called me that when I showed her the Creepypasta I was
writing. I think it means you’re crazy or something.”

Emily shook her head slightly but did not correct him. Dilly shrugged
and I fished around in my bag to find a sucker. It was bright green and
smelled tart through the wrapping. I wasn’t overly fond of green apple,
but it seemed to round out our flavor offerings with something sour.

Candy in hand, the five of us stood somber and nervous. “On three. I
mean actual three, not three-go,” Nick clarified.

We agreed. As one we dropped our pieces into the dark waters below. The
colorful wrappers disappeared immediately, no matter how hard we looked.
“You don’t think any fish will choke on them, do you?” Emily asked.

“Uh…probably not?” Nick looked around, as if expecting to spot a
creeping, sodden specter diving in for the sweets. “Thanks for coming
with me. It feels more fun to do this on our own.”

I knew what he meant. There was an electric excitement to being without
adult supervision, to hear a ghost story, to take part in Halloween
tradition. Even if it was probably a load of baloney. And we still got
candy. Looking around, I did not expect to see a figure, but the idea
that maybe, just maybe, a ghost was out there made the night feel dark
and dangerous. The shivers were pleasant and we were all a little more
inclined to jump and giggle at the wobbling shadows as we headed back
into the neighborhood.

We made our way back to the neighborhood and sought sweets with new
vigor. Our bags got heavier, the candles dipped lower, and Emily’s feet
ached more and more. After an hour we sat on the curb, letting her rest
again. Nick looked her over in concern. “You should take those off, your
feet are gonna hurt like crazy.”

She rubbed her ankles. “I didn’t bring any other shoes.”

“You can wear mine. I’ll walk in my socks,” Nick said, reaching down to
start to unlace his sneakers. Emily shook her head, though her eyes
flicked appreciatively toward him. “Come on, I run around barefoot in my
yard all the time. I’ll just walk in the grass,” he continued, offering
her his shoes. 

“That’s how you got hookworm,” I said, but he waved me off.

They were junky and worn - he’d had the same pair since last grade and
his toes were pushing against the front - but Emily slipped them on and
set her platform shoes aside.

“They’re kinda loose. But they feel a lot better. Thanks Nick.” He
grinned and I wondered if they’d ever get around to telling each other
they like-liked the other. Nick would have offered his shoes to anybody,
but I’d noticed the special little looks Emily and Nick shared. They
were always a second or two off each other, so they hadn’t caught the
other yet. I hoped it wouldn’t change the group if they did end up
together someday. Though Emily wasn’t allowed to date until she was
thirteen, and that was only in friend date groups. I might end up a
chaperone, I thought, displeased at the thought.

Dilly picked up the platform shoes, offering to carry them.”Look out for
dog doo,” he said seriously.

“Thanks Dilly, good call.” Nick jumped into the grass and sighed. “Nice
and cool.” We kept on, Emily tightening the laces and rolling up her
pant legs.

Fewer groups of kids were out now, the younger ones heading in. Our bags
hung heavier but we weren’t ready to let the night go just yet.

“I’ve been thinking about it, and…I think I’ve heard a version of that
story,” Emily said hesitantly. “It was different, but it was about the
same bridge.”

Nick turned to her, and rather than being offended at not having the
only spooky story, he looked eager. “What version have you heard? C’mon,
tell us!” he said, walking backwards across a yard, glancing behind him
to check for dog droppings.
I hesitated, looking at my brother, but Dilly seemed content enough to
work his way along a Twizzler, one cord at a time.

Emily nodded slowly. “A girl was walking home one Halloween night with
an empty pillowcase. She’d gotten no candy, found no friends, and her
costume made everyone laugh at how awful it was. A car hit her and she
flew off the bridge, into the full creek below.” Her eyes tracked a moth
fluttering in bewilderment against a light. “But that’s not the end of
her story. You see, the girl didn’t die.”

I frowned. “But…she got hit by a car?”

“Oh yes. Hard enough to knock her bones out of place and pop her lungs.
And the cold water burned all around her as she crashed into it.”
Emily’s eyes left the moth. “But she didn’t die. She was too angry. The
person who hit her had been at a Halloween party and drank themselves
silly before driving home. Her friends had made fun of her and left her
behind. She was so sad and angry that, when the time came for her to
die, her soul held on tight to her body and this world.”

I tried to imagine being so enraged that death itself couldn’t pry me
away from the world. I couldn’t picture it then. Being older, I think I
can now.

Emily pulled the words as if she didn’t know how to say them, like she
was afraid if she got one wrong, it would ve a terrible thing. “There’s
magic in the between places. In the cracks between life and death. And
she lingered there long enough to soak up that special magic. She didn’t
die, she changed. She flowed in the shadowy, wet dark and became a
monster.”

My brother stopped. “I don’t like this story,” he whispered. I realized
he’d been listening and tried to think of a way to distract him, but
Emily was already ahead of me.

“It’s all right. She didn’t become a whole monster. She swims in shallow
shadows and feels at home.” Dilly tilted his head as if trying to decide
if he was comforted or more perturbed.

I met Nick’s eyes. He seemed confused and fascinated in equal measure.
“Did anyone ever find the body?”

“The costume and body were never found. Only her candy bag washed up in
the reeds. It was a dirty old pillowcase.” Emily shrugged. “I don’t know
where I heard that version. I think some girls at church told it.”

“It would be church girls,” Nick said, but with amusement. Emily elbowed
him without force.

“Creepy. Wonder where they heard it?” I mused.

“Maybe one of them is the daughter of one of the friends! Maybe even
now, they’re stalked by the girl on Halloween night! Or maybe she
whispers the story in their windows at night!” Nick waggled his fingers
and I rolled my eyes.

Dilly held my hand. “Poor girl. I hope she liked the candy.” His eyes
were big and teary, and I knocked my chin against his head.

“Aw, if the story is true, I’m sure she loved it. But it’s just a
story.” I weighed my bag on my arm, gauging my loot. “What do you all
think? One more street?”

“Yeah. We can finish at the end of this one. Mrs. Wellers gives out
whole candy bars,” Nick said.

Dilly’s eyes widened. “No way! Real, whole candy bars?”

“Yeah, ever since she divored her husband two years ago and got a bunch
of money. He was a CEO or something, and she buys whole boxes of
Snickers.” Nick waved us on and Dilly looked to me. I grinned at him and
we set off, Emily clip-clopping in her too-big sneakers.

I thought of the angry girl as we received more candy, traipsing from
smiling face to smiling face. It was hard to imagine a kid getting no
candy at all on Halloween. Had her costume been that bad, or was she
from somewhere people hated? Was she poor? The neighborhood I grew up in
was warm and welcoming, middle class. All colors and kinds lived here.
How different must it have been, I thought. Our differences had never
harmed our friendships, but it wasn’t always like that decades ago.
Maybe it still was like that in some places.

A loud crack and a wail pulled me from my musings. Nick shouted and
Emily’s voice grew louder. “Whoa! What’s up?” I asked, turning. Emily
was on the ground, curled in, holding her leg. I gingerly pulled up the
black pant leg to see her ankle. It was purple and just touching her
foot made her shriek. We were far enough from the lit houses that no one
had come out - kids screaming with laughter was normal - so I shushed
her as gently as possible. “Emily, can you stand?”

She shook her head, choking on sobs. Carrying her was out of the
question. My geart began to sink. This was a case where parents were
needed.

“Mom and Dad will be so mad,” she whispered. “If they show up and Mona
and Dilly’s parents aren’t here…!”

Our parents would react no better. None of them would have wanted us to
have gone to the bridge to take part in a “ritual” of any kind,
especially not alone. The lie would be exposed. I turned to Nick
plaintively. “Do you think your Mom could come help?”

Nick’s mother, Natalie, was the kind of adult most likely to be swayed
into secrecy. She might be willing to help Emily get home without
letting on our parents hadn’t been there. Nick bit his lip in unease,
but as Emily sobbed softly, he pulled out his phone and made the dreaded
call.

I don’t know why Nick’s mom drank so much. I don’t know if it was
because of loss or anger or fear, a bad habit fed too long, a coping
mechanism. But as long as I’d known her, she’d always been the first to
hit up the liquor cabinet anywhere. But I’d never seen her drive drunk.

Not until that Halloween night.

She rolled over the curb as she pulled up and I realized this was a
terrible mistake. I looked to Nick, whose face had gone pale. The door
slammed and his mother walked steadily enough over to us. Her cheeks
were red and her curly hair unkempt, as if she’d been asleep on the
sofa. “Lemme see your ankle, hon,” she said, words tripping a little.
Emily keened, showing her.

“Nick,” I mouthed, “how many did she drink?”
He shook his head slightly. Natalie clucked her tongue looking over
Emily. “Good news, it’s not broken. But you sure twisted it good. Need
to put some ice on it. I’ll call your parents when we get to the house.”

I looked at the car. One wheel rested over the curb.

“Mom. You…shouldn’t drive. I think-” Nick began.

“I drove here, didn’t I?” Her voice had gained an edge. “You called me
out here and your girlfriend can’t walk. We’ll take her to the house,
get ice on it.”

Nick was quiet before trying again, more hesitantly, “Mom, I just don’t
want-”

She grabbed him by the shoulder and pulled him close as if to scold him,
but it took her a moment before her breath came out in a rush. “Listen.
You get in that car and shut up. Too tired to deal with this, and she’s
hurting. What’s she wearing your shoes for anyway?” He was quiet,
clearly torn on whether to answer or shut up. Apparently silence was the
right call, as she released him roughly, pushing him toward the car.
“C’mon, I’ll get you to a sofa.”

Emily allowed herself to be picked up, staring at me in a fervent
silence. I knew better than to get in a car with a drunk driver. But
Nick had to climb in.

“Miss Natalie, please. We lied to our parents about being out here with
adults. We should call them, we’re really sorry to have bothered you,” I
said, putting on my most contrite voice.

She paused and looked at me and her face had gotten redder. “You two get
in. I’m not leaving you kids unattended. Lying to your parents at this
age…”

I held tight to Dilly’s hand. He was obedient to a fault, and he was
already walking toward the car. He hdn’t noticed anything off, or if he
did, he didn’t realize how bad it could be.

It would be a short trip, I thought. And she wasn’t very drunk. She
walked pretty well. And I didn’t want to leave Emily and Nick alone.
Nick had never said his mom hurt him when she was sauced, but…

I climbed in with Dilly. Emily put out  a hand and I took it. Nick
climbed into the passenger seat, eyes fixed ahead, as if he could will
the car straight.

We rolled down the street, bouncing as the wheel left the curb. Natalie
cursed but seemed to regain the car. Most kids were heading inside, and
the road was sparse. I kept very quiet. Maybe if we didn’t distract her,
it would be fine. Nick didn’t look back at us, and Emily’s hand was hot
and sweaty in mine.

The radio played softly, and I wished I could turn it off. 

“Call us for all your plumbing needs! Because remember, Mr O’Neal has a
heckuva deal for cleaing blockages, drains, and any other plumbing
pains!” cheered a young woman. As the ad ended, I watched the side of
the road through my window. The lines wavered close and far, rising and
falling. The radio went on, and one ad in particular droned louder than
the rest.

“Remember folks, if you’re having a fang-tastic time this Halloween,
designate a driver. Nothing is as important as making sure our  ghosts
and goblins - big and small - make it home safe!” The voice was serious
and I stared at the radio, pleading for Natalie to receive an epiphany.

She shut off the radio. I kept my breath bottled inside.

We were coming up on the creek. I held Dilly’s hand. The car wove over
the yellow lines, correcting with jerky little scoots. Natalie cursed
again. I didn’t say a word. Dilly didn’t know what was going on, but I
felt his gaze on me as my hand tightened.

A pale blotch outside the window made me turn my head as we reached the
bridge too fast. One moment we were fine, on the road, and a pale little
figure draped in a dirty sheet stood watching. The eyes were cut poorly
and ringed in what looked like black marker, and it was the saddest,
most pathetic ghost costume I could imagine. White bare feet protruded
from the bottom, planted against the mud.

I don’t think I was the only one to see her. But I don’t think she
looked the same to Natalie. She gasped, pulling her whole body away from
whatever it was.

The wheels scraped the railing and the car jerked, and Natalie shrieked
as she overcorrected, lurching the opposite way. Nick yelled, jumping
toward the wheel and trying to grab it, but we were moving too quickly.
The rail was old and rusted and bent from too many close shaves, and the
car hit it hard, bashing us all around. Emily gasped in pain. For a
second we almost stopped, almost held back, before the metal groaned and
bent under the weight. The front of the car dipped and the back lifted
and I grabbed hold of Dilly and hid his face in my neck as we went over,
weightless for a sickening moment.

The car hit the water hard. My head hit the ceiling and I swear, if I
were any taller, my neck might have broken. Everyone shot up, shaken
like a soda can, water foaming up through the broken windshield. I
screamed, and I must have blacked out because after one second, the car
had righted itself and the water was around our shoulders. The car horn
bellowed and I panted, looking around. Nick was slumped across our laps
where he’d been thrown, unconscious. Dilly’s head rested against my
shoulder, heavy and still. Emily was curled up, all her limbs pulled to
her like a dying bug. And Natalie’s hair was festooned with mud and
twigs and broken glass.

I was the only one awake. I shook Emily, Dilly, Nick. The water seeped
in around us and I screamed. “Help! Help us!”

Something pale moved on the bank. I stopped, and long limbs trailed into
the water, wading close. It was taller now, too tall to be the little
child. Murky water churned around the thin legs and as it reached the
car, its arms and legs bent so it xould look into the car

The thing loomed. Her hair dripped straight and black as sodden thread,
hanging limp from beneath the dingy sheet. She sucked in and blew out as
her ragged breathing against the wet fabric rasped. I could see all her
naked body and a neck protruding from the sheet, but she was more like
autumn branched, bent and gnarled, than flesh and blood. If she stood
straight, she could reach the bridge above our heads.

I knew I had to try to get out of the car. But the sight of the monster
towering, hunching over the car as if she was ready to crack it open
like a crab leg and slurp out the meat inside, made my hands shake as I
unbuckled my seatbelt, then Dilly’s. The others’ heads lolled and the
horn still blared, Nick’s mom’s brow setting against it like a swollen
pumpkin. 
I felt water around my knees and sobbed, churning up tulle and mud as I
kicked at the seat in front of me, trying to wake the woman at the
wheel. I needed an adult. Even her. “Wake up, wake up! Please!”

Metal screamed and tore and the car lifted onto its side. I shrieked as
the others slumped against me, and the long-fingered hand ripped the car
door away with the ease of a pull tab on a soda. Water churned around me
as the car sank back down, water flooding in faster. I tried to crawl
across my friends, smacking them, trying to wake them.
Dilly alone stirred, stare vacant. Scratches along the right side of his
face wept red slowly, and he moved as if he were caught in molasses.
“…What happened…Mona?”

“The car crashed. Climb out your side, okay?” My voice trembled and I
wiped my face, trying not to show him I was crying. Water and dirt
smeared across my cheeks.

The head lowered so she was looking into the car door, sheet drenched,
her neck bending and popping long like a swan’s. Dilly froze. She
reached out with her terrible, bony arm and fingers with black
fingernails, crusting with moss. Dilly squirmed, whimpering when the
hand grabbed him. I grabbed onto him, sobbing, but the thing lifted him
out of the wreckage, out of my slick, shaking arms.

Then she gathered him up and carried him to the bank. Like an infant,
she set him safely out of the water, drawing away on her long, creaking
legs. I gasped with relief when he began to cry, hiccupping. If he had
breath to cry, he was probably okay.

The girl - I don’t know what else to call her - returned and held out
one horrible hand expectantly. I obeyed, grunting as I pulled Nick
forward a little more so she could reach him without knocking the car
around again.

She crawled about with her long, pallid limbs, depositing Nick on the
bank with Dilly. When she returned, she scooped up me and Emily and
didn’t bother about jostling the car. She pulled us out and set us on
the bank as water flooded the interior. 

She’d…saved us. 

I checked Nick’s breathing and then Emily’s. He’d gotten the worst out
the impact; his right arm was purple with bruises and the pattern of his
mask had pressed hard into his side, leaving a deformed Bumblebee
impression in livid red. Emily shivered but seemed unharmed but for her
ankle and some scratches. It was then I saw a mottled bruise in plum
starting on my arm, from my wrist to my shoulder. It began to hurt and I
tried to ignore it, still trying to wake the others.

The car gurgled and groaned, sinking deeper. I turned in realization.
The girl was perched in the water on the bank, watching it go down.
“Wait! Nick’s mom! She’s still in there!”

She turned her bed sheet head to me and said nothing. My eyes flicked
from her to the car. Then I began wading out into the water, dress
billowing up and sparkling in the dying rear lights of the car. 
Her head followed, neck long enough to keep pace without moving her body
an inch. I tried not to look under her sheet, cold water soothing my
bruise as I pushed through. “It’s her fault this happened.” A soft voice
creaked like a rope tire swing, rustled like dead autumn leaves. “Why
save her?”

I was scared, chest heaving, and the car kept sinking. I could see
Natalie’s face, water rising up her chin.

“Because-because! I can’t leave her!”

The black marker holes lowered to my eye level, hem of the sheet
settling on the surface of the water. “She would have killed you all.
She should die.” Her voice bubbled with anger, hot as tar and sticky as
syrup, clinging to the air like an echo.

I grabbed the passenger door and tried to pull it open. It didn’t give.
I groped for the lock, glass slicing along my arm and making me cry out.
I imagined snakes skulking across the water at the taste of blood. “I
can’t leave her! Nick loves his mom! I don’t want her to die alone in
there!”

She watched me struggle for a minute. Sighed a long, weary sigh.

“Only because green apple is my favorite.”

She pulled me away from the car roughly, yanking my arm almost out of
its socket, dropping me on the bank. I yelped, but she returned to the
car so fast, grabbing the door and peeling it loose like aluminum foil
off a hot dish. There was no care as she pulled out Nick’s mom, as if
she were pulling out a dirty towel, and she hefted her onto the bank.
Nick’s mom sputtered and thrashed like a fish, rolling onto her side and
vomiting water and something pumpkin-y. 

Blood stuck her hair to her face, and her eyes went big and wild as she
spotted Nick. “Nicky…Nicky! Oh, what have I…I didn’t…!” Her hands
scrabbled over his face, his chest, sobs bubbled out of her soaked
lungs. “Kids!”

“We’re alive. All of us,” I said, my arm boiling with fresh pain and
bleeding a thin sheet. Dilly was still sobbing and Emily was finally
starting to move, coughing. Nick’s mom looked at each of us, terror
growing and growing as she realized what she’d almost done.

“Natalie.” The wet sheet wobbled, and Nick’s mom started, jerking back
and spotting the girl at last. She froze, staring, uncomprehending. The
girl pressed her head against the side of Nick’s mom’s, and she
whispered something in her ear. She pulled the woman close, sheet sodden
as it squished against her ruddy cheek. The woman’s face paled and her
lips wavered, but no sound left them.

The girl drew her head back. She pulled up the sheet and I couldn’t see
her face, blocked from the side by the drape of fabric.

Natalie stuttered and blubbered and howled. “No! No, no, no! Never
again, never!”

The girl seemed satisfied. She let the sheet rest on her shoulders and
suddenly she was small again, just a little girl wet from the creek and
wearing a cheap ghost costume. She stood in water up to her ankles, a
rescued treat bag dripping mud. Her melancholy little sheet face turned
toward me.

Emily sat up, shivering. “Where are we? What…?” Her eyes fell on Nick
and she delicately patted his cheek. He stirred, sluggish, and began to
cough up gulps of water. Emily turned him on his side, rubbing his hurt
arm. “S’okay,” she said almost dreamily. White makeup streaked down her
face and black trickled from her eyes and the corners of her mouth.

“Hey!” A flashlight burned my eyes, making me raise a hand. “You all
okay? We heard a crash!”

Neighbors. I couldn’t make them out in the cloudy moonlight. More
flashlights came out and shoes waded through dead leaves.

“We crashed into the creek! We’re hurt!” I called, glancing at Nick’s
mom, still curled up tight like a pillbug, and then at the girl.

“Hold on, we’ll call the police!”

“My wife’s a doctor!”

“Get an ambulance over here!”

The voices were a noisy comfort. The girl headed into deeper water and I
followed her with my eyes as she stood still. She fished around under
her costume and pulled out a lollipop. The wrapper was dirty and the
stick bent, but she pulled it up under the sheet and popped it into her
mouth. She smacked her lips in approval.

“Thank you,” I said. My voice was weak but she heard me, because she
looked back at me, setting the treat bag on a bit of driftwood.

In the black holes, her eyes glinted with cloudy light like a fish’s,
the stick bobbing under the sheet as she whispered, “Happy Halloween.”

Then she was gone into the water. 

As far as I know, Natalie’s never touched alcohol again, even after she
got out of prison. The judge seemed flustered to have a defendant so
quick to confess and accept the prison sentence. Nick visits her every
week and lives with his Dad now, and he has new shoes every gear now. I
think she’s in AA in the prison, trying to change. What could have
happened still haunts her, and it will for the rest of her life. Maybe
that’s a good thing.

Nick refuses to drink alcohol, even though we’ll be legal in a couple
years. Emily is only too happy to do the same. They started going out
officially a couple years ago, and her parents are relieved they don’t
have to worry about either of them getting hauled in for underage
drinking.

Dilly refused to ever go trick or treating in the neighborhood ever
again. He likes the Trunk or Treats a lot better, and he always goes all
out for the ones at our church. Last year he handed out whole
candybars. 

I never told anyone about the girl. I couldn’t prove it happened and it
might have sounded like I was trying to get out of trouble since the
ritual technically worked. And boy, was I in trouble. Mom and Dad
grounded me for months, but every night they would peek into my room,
kissing the top of my head. I was fine with it. Dilly and me and my
friends were alive. I was grateful for that. Nothing else really
mattered.

I swing by the bridge every year. I bring a bag of candy and drop it
into the creek. And I always make sure to add extra green apple. Most of
the time I won’t see her. But I know she’s there. Because I still hear
stories about a girl in a ghost costume on the river bank that
disappears into the water when you take a second look. And once every
few years, I see a pale head bobbing under the bridge before slipping
away into the shadows, into the seams of the world that still pulse with
magic.
