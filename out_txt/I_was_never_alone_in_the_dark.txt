I’m 20 years old, and I’m still afraid of the dark. But that’s a bit
misleading, because nobody is truly afraid of darkness itself.

That primal fear comes from not knowing what’s out there. When it gets
dark and you lose the ability to see, your mind begins generating ideas
of what could be out there. No matter how unrealistic they may be, they
scare you. Those ideas build on one another and won’t stop until you are
“safe” within the light and can see again. But even then, those thoughts
still linger in the back of your mind.

Most people might say that I’m exaggerating. To most people, being
afraid of the dark is something you get over early in life. Most people,
however, never went through what I did as a child.

When I was a child, we lived in an older house. It was fairly large and
looked nice from the outside, but the inside showed its age. The wooden
floors creaked, you could see the wooden support beams on the kitchen
ceiling, and it had a very distinct smell that was hard to put into
words but very noticeable if you’d been out of the house for a while.
Its wiring was outdated, so even a light storm could knock out the
power. The tall trees around the house only made it worse.

Growing up, thunderstorms were the worst. If it stormed, it was highly
likely the power would go out, which filled me with dread. Plus, the
loud bangs of thunder always made me jump.

The worst part about power outages was how weird the house got.
Furniture seemed to move, like a chair facing the wall instead of the
table, or picture frames that were straight becoming a little crooked.
Sometimes they would stay like that when the power came back, and I
would know immediately. Other times, everything would go back to normal,
and the only reason I knew things had moved was because I’d bumped into
them while stumbling through the dark house, trying to figure out where
I had put the flashlights I was playing with earlier.

There was also this sound I would hear before things started to shift.
It would start off like the floors squeaking, but slightly drawn out.
Once things started to change in the house, the squeaking would become
more rhythmic, almost like the sound of a ratchet wrench.

The weirdest part was that sometimes, when the power was out and I was
searching for flashlights I could never find, I would notice the faint
smell of something sickeningly sweet. It was always accompanied by that
clicking, but I didn’t always smell it when I heard the clicks.

These things would scare me, but I’d always end up forgetting about them
shortly after the power came back on. Anytime I tried to tell my
parents, they just waved it off. They would always go to bed once the
power went out, so they never noticed anything strange.

It became almost like a routine. The power would go out, I’d wander
around aimlessly looking for flashlights, and then the house would start
creaking and popping until the power came back on. So I just started to
ignore it.

At least until that night when I was eight, when it wouldn’t let me
ignore it.

I was on the couch playing video games, listening to music on my iPod
Touch. After a while, my parents told me to pause my game so we could go
pick up dinner. Although it was dark out and it had been storming pretty
badly earlier, it had died down a lot since then, so I felt comfortable.
I was also really invested in my game, so I begged to stay home. My
parents eventually decided that it wasn’t worth fighting with me since
they would only be gone a little while, so they let me stay home.

But shortly after they left, the power went out.
I was more annoyed than I was afraid, but I got over it and started my
usual routine of walking around searching for my flashlights. I wished I
had plugged my iPod in so I had some light, but I always forgot to
charge it during the day and would plug it in before bed. Now the power
was out, and my iPod was at 4%, meaning I definitely didn’t want to
waste its battery.

I started hearing the creaking and then the clicking, but it didn’t
bother me too badly anymore. I had figured it was just the house making
noises at this point. Eventually, I started bumping into chairs and
walls until I bumped into something that felt like a pole. I felt around
and found out it was the kitchen table, but it was upside down, which
terrified me.

Up until now, I could explain all the weird things that would happen. A
picture being crooked? Maybe I was stomping around too much in the dark.
A chair facing the wrong way? I had probably left it like that. But the
kitchen table being upside down? I had nothing.

So I made my way back to the couch in a panic.
I decided I was just going to sit on the couch until my parents got
back. They should be back soon. I was too scared to do anything else.
After what felt like forever, I made it to the couch and sat down, but
immediately something was wrong. It was ice cold and felt like leather.
We didn’t have a leather couch. All our couches were made of fabric. My
safe spot didn’t feel safe anymore, so I immediately stood up.

The floor felt soft under my bare feet. It was comforting for a second
before I thought about it. Why was the floor soft? It had always been
hard wooden floors that squeaked.

WHY DID IT FEEL SOFT?

So I reached down to feel the floor with my hands.

It was carpet.

Okay, the table was weird, but I knew there was a table there before.
And the couch? Maybe I was just shaken by the whole table thing and was
imagining things. But this carpet? There was nothing in my mind that
could make carpet appearing on my wooden floors make any sense.
I felt absolute dread. Nothing made sense, and I was all alone with no
light, with no clue what I should do.

I started to cry.

I couldn’t stop crying until I heard something new. It wasn’t the
familiar creaking and clicking.

It sounded like a car.

The relief was overwhelming. My parents were home, and I wouldn’t have
to deal with this alone.

But it didn’t stop. It drove straight past my house. As it did, the
light from the car’s headlights swept through the room. The room looked
bigger than before, and the walls were the wrong color. My mom and I had
painted them pink ourselves, but now they looked almost green.

But there in the far corner, where the light could barely reach, I
caught a glimpse of what looked like a dog-shaped object before the
light quickly disappeared as the car drove away.

I froze.

I don’t have a dog.

There are so many words I could use, but none can express the feeling of
seeing that shape in dim lighting before being thrown back into
pitch-black darkness.

I couldn’t take it anymore. I fumbled for my iPod and turned on the
flashlight so I could see what that… thing was.
As I shone the light at that thing, I was horrified. It was a tangled
mess of legs. It was almost as if it had just one too many legs for it
to even come close to pretending to be something natural.

It had no feet or paws or anything remotely animal-like. They were
pointed nubs, as if it were a bug. And its skin—it seemed to glow from
the light of my iPod, but it wasn’t glowing. Its skin was just
translucent, ghost-like. Even the faint light I had made it shine.

It had a human-shaped head, but its face…
Where its eyes should have been were two empty holes that swallowed the
light of my iPod, leaving nothing but an empty void.

And then its smile. Dear God, its smile.

Blood-red lips stretched into an impossibly wide, open smile that made
it look jolly. Through the opening in its mouth, I could see rows and
rows of teeth that seemed to continue farther than my light could reach.
Pointed, jagged yellow teeth that were horribly misaligned on top of
each other in ways that were not possible.

Moments after the light hit it, its head tilted slightly, making its
smile even more haunting. The clicking and creaking stopped, and in
their place was a deep whirring. It was as if its teeth were rotating
around its mouth like the blades of a blender, grinding against each
other.

It slowly scuttled towards me, its legs bending backwards and over top
of each other. None of its legs moved in sync. Some dragged beneath it,
while others hung completely limp off to the sides.

It was getting closer. My mind was screaming to run and scream, but my
body wouldn’t listen. I felt completely petrified. All I could let out
was a quiet sob.

Then… nothing.

My battery had died.

The slow dragging of its appendages over the now-carpeted floor and the
deep whirring were all I could hear. I knew it was moving closer. As it
drew near, I felt its hot breath against my face, and the odor was
sickeningly sweet.

I felt three of its many appendages clumsily wrap around my body, its
breath hotter and sweeter as its mouth opened wider. I could feel its
teeth pressing against the top of my head. As it started to bite down on
me, I closed my eyes and felt a searing pain.

Then, again, nothing.

I slowly opened my eyes.

The power was back on.

Everything seemed normal except the floors. They were still carpeted.

I heard the front door open. My parents were home.
When my mom saw me, she screamed and ran to hug me, asking if I was
okay. I hadn’t noticed how badly my head was bleeding. I didn’t say
anything. I grabbed onto my mom’s arm as tightly as I could, not letting
go.

My parents carried me to the car and took me to the hospital. It looked
a lot worse than it was. The cuts from that thing’s teeth were shallow.
All they did was bandage my head, and we went home.

Once we got home, we went to bed. I slept in their bed that night.

In the morning, my mom asked me what happened, and I frantically
explained the chairs, the table, the couch, the carpet, and it. But they
seemed confused, so I explained again, slower, crying as I did. My mom
hugged me, then told me that the floors were never wooden. They had them
carpeted before I was born.

I was shocked. I told her she was wrong and explained about the old
creaky floors. She just said, “Okay,” and gave me a warm smile and a
hug. I felt better.

After that night, I refused to be alone. I wouldn’t let my mom even
leave a room without me. This has been a constant in my life ever since.
It doesn’t matter to me who it is—a friend, a family member, or my
girlfriend. No matter how small the task, day or night, I need someone
there.
Now, twelve years after that night, I still wake up with nightmares of
being eaten by that thing. I’ve tried to explain this to my girlfriend.
She knows something traumatic happened. I still have scars on my head to
prove that, and she knows I can’t be alone.

My girlfriend and I have moved far away from my childhood home, into the
mountains.

But she wants me to work through that, which is why she begged me to
stay home while she got dinner, saying that if I don’t try, I’ll never
get over this fear. It made sense. I was terrified of being alone, but I
loved her more than anything, and if this was important to her, I was
willing to try. I told her fine, and she left.

Not long after she left, the power went out.
There’s no cell service up here, so I can’t even call her.
I wrote this on my phone because I heard creaking, and I thought it was
just the house settling.

But those creaks just turned into clicks.
