I know how that sounds. Stick with me.

Mum loved magpies. She loved the patterns on them, that oily blue-green
sheen on the black feathers you only spot when the sun catches it, and
she loved how clever they are. Every time we saw one she’d give it a
little salute and tell me to do the same. I started at about six,
saluting them like a tiny soldier, dead serious.

We did loads of nature stuff together. Proper walks in the woods most
weekends, me and Mum out in front, Dad trailing behind with the flask.
She’d name every bird she heard and I’d pretend I could tell them apart.

She died on one of those walks when I was ten. Undiagnosed heart
problem, no warning at all. One minute she was pointing at something in
a tree and the next she wasn’t there.

I’ve been missing years of her ever since. Every big moment has had a
gap where she should be.

Dad was destroyed. Completely flattened. He stopped going on walks and
found it hard to look at me, which I’m fairly sure was down to me
looking like her. So I grew up quick. I learned to cook, I learned how
the boiler worked, and I learned to say “I’m fine” in a voice that made
people stop asking.

Now I’m in my twenties and still living at home with Dad, who barely
leaves the house. He told me once it didn’t feel right being out among
the birds and the trees without her. I never pushed it.

Then came the day of his diagnosis. Aggressive lung cancer. We were in
the kitchen and he was telling me, all flat and matter-of-fact, when a
magpie landed on the windowsill. It just sat there, head tilted,
watching us. Dad turned round, saw it and shooed it off with his hand.
Then he turned back to me and said, basically, that he was done for.

I wasn’t having that. I begged him to try the treatment, even though
it’s brutal, and he said it wouldn’t work. I told him I wasn’t ready to
lose another parent, and that was what got him. He agreed to try, for
me.

After his second round we got home and there was a magpie sat on our
doorstep, chirping away like it had been waiting for us. Dad lost it. He
was furious and properly distraught, like it was a cruel joke, and he
chased it off down the path. I got him up to bed and he was shaking.

Afterwards I went out the back with a brew and a magpie landed on the
grass not far from me. I’d put money on it being the same one.

I remembered Mum telling me once that robins are supposed to be dead
people coming back to visit. She’d laughed and said she’d rather come
back as a magpie, thanks very much. So I said hello to it, feeling like
an absolute lunatic. It hopped closer. Then it hopped onto the arm of
the bench, about a foot from me.

I started crying. I told it I was scared of losing Dad. It chirped a few
quiet little chirps and flew off.

I told Dad about it the next day and he waved it away. Said that was
just the sort of thing Mum would do, finding meaning in everything. He
said she believed in all that mumbo jumbo, and he just loved her. He
only ever went on those walks to be with her, and he never cared much
for the birds or the trees.

Then he apologised. For being so distant since she died. He said he was
so proud to see me turning into her, and that he’d missed her too much
to watch it happen. He said he believed this was the end for him, no
afterlife, and he’d never see me again.

I couldn’t take that in, so I pushed for more treatment. It made him
much iller. When it finished they told us there was nothing more they
could do to stop the spread. Dad said he was ready and that I’d be fine
without him.

I wasn’t fine. I went out to the garden and fell apart completely. The
magpie turned up again, chirping nonstop and flapping about right in my
face. I was so angry. I tried to shoo it away and it wouldn’t leave, so
I went inside and shut the door on it.

Dad was in the lounge, coughing up blood.

That was when I knew I had to let him go. I sat down next to him and
stopped fighting it.

A few weeks went by and the magpie kept turning up, sitting on the fence
while I did the washing-up or hung out the laundry. It just watched me,
like it was keeping an eye on things.

Then Dad got suddenly worse and ended up in hospital. I was holding his
hand when he looked past me at the window. The magpie was on the ledge.
He looked at it for a long time, and then he smiled, a proper one, the
first real smile I’d seen from him in years. He died like that, smiling.

I’m a science person. I don’t believe in reincarnation, and I know a
magpie is a magpie. But for those weeks it felt like a bit of Mum was
keeping me company, and I’ll take that. I haven’t seen that particular
bird since the funeral, and I couldn’t swear it was the same one each
time anyway.

I’ve started going on walks again, though. Through the woods, with the
flask, same as always.

There are two magpies I keep running into up there, and they’re always
together. They sit side by side on the same branch, shuffling closer
until they’re practically touching, then one starts chirping and the
other chirps straight back, like they’re in the middle of the longest
natter in history. Sometimes one hops down the path ahead of me, tail
bobbing, full of itself, while the other swoops in behind and gives it a
little peck on the head for showing off. They chase each other round the
oaks for no reason at all. Last week one of them presented the other
with a shiny crisp packet like it was an engagement ring, and I laughed
so loudly a woman walking her spaniel gave me a funny look.

They look so happy. Daft, loved-up, over-the-moon happy, the sort you
couldn’t fake even if you were a bird.

And know you’re all gonna say a magpie is just a magpie.

But I salute them every time.
