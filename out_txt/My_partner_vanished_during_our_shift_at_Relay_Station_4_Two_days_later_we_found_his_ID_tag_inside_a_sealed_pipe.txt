Never bypass the manual pressure relief valve on an isolated pump
station intake line. If line pressure builds past twelve hundred pounds
and the backup rupture disk fails, the cast manifold splits and floods
the lower vault in less than ninety seconds.
Wayne and I were working the four-to-midnight shift out at Relay Station
Four. It sits about twenty miles north of the timber gate along the
upper river basin. Our routine was always the same. We logged dial
readouts every hour on the hour, walked the fence line to toss off
fallen pine limbs, and kept an eye on the secondary runoff pit. Right
around nine o’clock, the wind died down flat. A minute later, the
primary intake gauge lost ten pounds of pressure without the pumps
cycling on to correct it.
I walked over to the telemetry cabinet and tapped the glass dial with my
knuckles. The needle didn’t move. Wayne grabbed the rechargeable lantern
off the wall dock, picked up his clipboard, and said he was walking down
to the intake shed to bleed the manual valve himself. He threw on his
high-vis vest, went through the double doors, and pulled the primary
latch shut behind him. Company rules said that door stays locked from
the inside whenever one guy is out on the yard.
Ten minutes went by. The digital clock on the wall rolled to
nine-fifteen. Looking out through the reinforced wire glass, the gravel
yard was empty under the floodlights. Then the needle on my intake board
dropped another eight pounds.
I picked up the company handheld from the desk dock and keyed the mic. I
called Wayne’s call sign three times. The speaker just gave back flat
dead air. No clicks, no static, nothing. I set it down and checked my
phone. The screen showed Searching with zero bars. I picked up the
landline receiver on the desk next to the monitor bank. The line was
stone dead. Not even an off-hook hum.
I didn’t like the feel of it. I grabbed the master ring and a four-pound
steel pipe wrench out of the locker by the breaker panel. I unlocked the
exit door, stepped out into the cold, and let the latch fall shut behind
me.
The yard smelled like damp dirt and diesel exhaust. The intake shed is
eighty yards west, right up against the rock cut. When I got to the
door, the padlock was hanging open on the hasp, unhooked, but the door
was pushed shut against the frame. I pulled it open and swung my
flashlight inside. Wayne wasn’t in there. His hard hat and his clipboard
were sitting right in the middle of the work table next to an open
socket set. The manual intake valve was turned half a crank open, but
there wasn’t a drop of water coming out of the bleed pipe into the floor
drain.
I stepped back out onto the gravel and turned back toward the main
station. Fifty yards out, right by the timber boundary fence, someone
was standing there. It was a tall, solid silhouette, completely
motionless. The person was squared up toward the building, shoulders
level, feet set in the stone. It didn’t shift weight. It didn’t lift an
arm. I put the flashlight beam dead on it. The light hit the chain link
behind it, but the figure stayed flat black. It didn’t bounce any light
back, and it didn’t throw a shadow against the fence.
I started backing toward the station doors, keeping my light on the
fence line. The figure didn’t flinch. I reached the concrete pad, looked
down for two seconds to stick the key into the cylinder, and turned the
lock. When I looked back up, the fence line was completely empty. The
gravel was undisturbed. The wire wasn’t shaking. There wasn’t a single
boot step or a branch snapping anywhere in the timber.
I got inside, racked both deadbolts, and threw the heavy steel safety
bar across the brackets.
At ten-forty, the intake gauge spiked hard. The needle slapped the brass
stop pin past twelve hundred pounds with a loud metal crack. A second
later, the intercom unit above the breaker panel clicked open. The
speaker let out a dull hum, and then I heard Wayne clear his throat. His
voice came through clean and dry. He told me to unbar the rear door and
come give him a hand at the intake shed because the bleed valve was
seized up on the threads.
I didn’t move an inch from the console. I had both hands wrapped around
that four-pound wrench. Right on the desk, three feet away, Wayne’s
two-way radio was sitting in the plastic charger with the red light
solid. The radio wasn’t receiving. The overhead speaker went off again.
It repeated the exact same sentence, using the exact same breath, the
same pause, and the same pitch.
Right after that, three heavy knuckles hit the outside of the steel exit
door behind my back. Then the intercom keyed again. This time it was my
own voice coming out of the wall. It was the exact sentence I had called
into the handheld an hour before, word for word: Station Four to Wayne,
check the bleed valve.
The metal handle on the locked door started turning down. The brass
latch ground against the steel strike plate. I looked right through the
four-inch inspection window. There was nothing out there except the
yellow floodlights hitting the wet stone. No face. No body blocking the
glass. The handle turned all the way down to the stop, released slowly,
and turned again. It did that three times in a row.
I stayed right where I was with the wrench up, watching the wall clock
until the readout clicked to midnight. The second the numbers flipped,
the intake needle dropped dead to zero. The handle on the door stopped
moving. The intercom snapped shut. The only sound left in the room was
the low buzz of the step-down transformer.
At five-fifteen in the morning, truck headlights hit the front glass. I
waited until I saw Miller get out of the company truck with his
paperwork before I lifted the bar and unlocked the door. The first thing
Miller asked was where Wayne took off to, since his personal pickup was
still parked over by the propane tank.
We walked the entire yard. We checked the pump shed, the intake house,
and every foot of the boundary wire. The chain link was tight all the
way around. No cuts, no bent posts, and no boot tracks in the mud along
the ditch. Wayne’s truck keys, his wallet, and his lunch box were still
sitting on his locker shelf in the back hallway.
The state troopers ran a full site check three hours later. They found
zero signs of forced entry, no marks on the doors, and the high-pressure
manifold was completely dry with no leaks. They wrote Wayne up as a
missing person who walked off into the woods on his own.
Two days after that, Miller brought in an outside pipe crew to pull the
intake apart. When they unbolted the three-inch check valve inside the
manifold shed, they found Wayne’s brass plant ID tag wedged flat across
the internal seat. The stamped numbers were clean and the ring was
whole, sitting locked inside a cast-iron line that hadn’t been unsealed
since the plant went in fourteen years ago.
