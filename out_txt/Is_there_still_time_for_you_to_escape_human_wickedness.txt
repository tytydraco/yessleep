Starting over is difficult, but sometimes necessary. It is a path that
stubbornly forces you to reflect on your present and bid farewell to
your past—a natural thing to do when you have not lived a life of sin.
That is my case.

I wish I had better words for this. I know what I have lived through
deserves them, especially if it is to serve as a lesson for future
generations. But these dry, solitary words are all I have now. The words
of a man who has failed and no longer belongs to the society he once
knew.

As a child, I was raised by the streets. Either I stole on the avenue,
or some shop took pity on me and gave me its leftovers. I carry the
pitying looks of every passerby with me. They hurt me as the sun warmed
my sorrows, and I missed them to the point of tears when the nighttime
cold took me in.

I knew no other way to survive than to steal. My gang friends taught me
to speak and write. I had no concept of the stages of life; I lived day
by day, like a stray dog.

Time took several members of the gang, including the two people I held
dearest among them. The latter happened during the kidnapping carried
out by the North Zone Cartel, forty years ago. We had spent several days
stealing their product, with no infrastructure beyond a trap house, a
hideout where we took refuge to get high and die. We were easy prey.

There, they forced me to work against my own gang, undercover. Only now
can I feel remorse for the people I turned in and the lies I told their
families. I just wanted to keep watching the sun rise and cool my face
with cold water from some faucet in those crumbling little plazas.

Nothing I have lived through over these past forty years is worth
telling. You can infer it all, if you pay close attention to my account.
The only thing I will mention is that, two years ago, I made a mistake.
I thought I could learn to live like everyone else.

I devoted myself to the church in the neighborhood where I had ended up,
worked at a community soup kitchen, and little by little, I began to
find peace. The most precious thing I had during those years was that,
for the first time, ordinary people believed in me. They believed in me
far more than I believed in myself, of course.

Then, one night in April, just a few months before I began writing this,
the long arm of crime reached into my life once more, coming to put an
end to my hopes. I had not called for it. I had not wished for it. I was
simply unfortunate enough to fall within its sights. For the first time,
I suffered firsthand what I had once done to so many others.

Heavy-headed and confused, I regained consciousness. I had fallen asleep
after drinking too much, as I often did to silence my memories. I
wondered whether it was real, and, sadly, it was. There were noises on
the other side of the house. Voices making no attempt to whisper, and
blows that were coming for me.

I wished I could drop dead right then and there, but my strong body
would not allow it. I had to be there.

My phone alerted me to a private message. It was a contact I could have
sworn I had deleted long ago. “Gabriela.” Caught somewhere between sleep
and a hangover, I once again wished I were not there, that it was all
part of a terrible dream.

She was accompanied by another man, taller and more muscular than my
new, peaceful routine had allowed me to become. I saw the hatred in his
expression, accompanied by a grimace of exertion, as he carried two
large black garbage bags, the kind used in apartment buildings.

I was forced to become the man my origins had made me. Once again, my
hands were stained with someone else’s blood. I felt nothing but an
urgency for it all to end, an urgency that turned into longing as the
long minutes dragged on.

The bags contained human remains. It was easy to recognize the arms and
legs of children as the other man pulled them out and placed them into a
barrel, its top completely open and filled with an improvised mixture of
concrete.

While that man broke apart and compressed the children’s remains, I was
tasked with digging holes and filling them with that macabre mixture. My
own house, now turned into a mass grave for organized crime, was as
broken and battered as my soul.

Eventually, we finished the job, and they left. Not before catching up
with me, though, and torturing me until I promised that, against my
will, I would continue living in that house.

Just a few feet from where I am writing this lies the anguish of those
children’s mothers. I can turn my head slightly and point out where the
three graves are, no matter how much I have renovated the house.

I have not gone outside in a long time. Fortunately, these good
Christians look after me, holding on to their belief that I have fallen
into depression. The truth is that I am utterly ashamed of being this
fraud, this miserable excuse for a human being. With every prayer,
sitting on those well-kept wooden pews, I have worked to change. I have
even imagined, for the first time, a peaceful routine. A decent life.

Now I know that once you cross the event horizon, nothing can escape the
dark evil of human nature.
