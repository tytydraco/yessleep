If you didn’t see my first post this is where it started

https://www.reddit.com/r/nosleep/s/o3eoExaADe

The man at the electronics store asked if I needed to keep an eye on a
pet.

“No.”

“Baby monitor?”

“Just a room.”

He showed me one that sent alerts to my phone whenever it detected
movement. I asked whether it recorded continuously.

“With a memory card, yeah. Otherwise you get event clips.”

“I need the whole night.”

He stopped trying to sell me the cheaper one.

I installed it on the bookshelf opposite the bed. Wide-angle lens,
infrared, audio. I checked the picture on my phone while standing in
each corner of the room.

On the screen, my bedroom looked like footage from a rental listing.
Smaller than it felt. Nothing hidden except the narrow spaces behind the
furniture.

The window catches were both open again.

I photographed them before touching anything. Then I shut the window,
locked it, and drew a pencil line across each catch and its mounting
plate.

I didn’t have a plan for what came after the recording. I just needed
something that would still exist when I was out of that room.

Jess called while I was formatting the memory card.

“You coming over?”

“Yeah.”

“You sound awful.”

“I’m not sleeping.”

“Then come now. I’ll make the couch up if you don’t want me waking you
when I leave.”

I looked at my bed on the phone screen.

“Can I ask you something weird?”

“Obviously.”

“When I’m asleep, do I hold my breath?”

“Sometimes. Then you do this big snort. I told you to get it checked.”

I sat down on the edge of the bed. On the screen, a tiny version of me
did the same thing.

“Why?” she asked.

“No reason.”

“Don’t do that.”

I rubbed my eyes, carefully avoiding the sore one.

“I’ll explain when I get there.”

I brought the photographs. I showed her my neck.

She didn’t say sleep paralysis again.

We watched the live camera feed from her kitchen until after three.
Nothing moved. When a moth passed close to the lens, I knocked my mug
over.

The room stayed empty the following night too.

On the third afternoon, Jess drove me home to meet the maintenance guy.
He checked the sash and replaced the catches. He showed us how one of
the old screws had stripped out of the wood.

“Could lift it from outside if you worked at it,” he said.

“From the fire escape?”

“Sure.”

Jess looked at me. Neither of us said anything until he left.

“You’re not staying here,” she said.

“I know.”

We packed a bag. She took it downstairs while I looked for my work
badge.

I found it in yesterday’s trousers, sat on the bed to put my shoes on,
and woke to Jess shaking my shoulder.

I hit her hand away.

She stepped back so fast she caught her heel on the laundry basket.

“Fuck. Sorry. Sorry, Jess.”

“You were asleep.”

The room was bright. The window was closed.

I’d been out for less than ten minutes.

That was how I ended up in the recording: not because I’d decided to
bait it, not because I wanted proof more than I wanted to live. Four
days later, waiting for a load of washing to finish, I fell asleep with
the ceiling light on and my shoes still tied.

When I woke, it was morning.

My first thought was that I’d finally slept.

Then I saw the window.

I left without the washing.

I watched the footage in my car, parked outside a supermarket that had
already opened. People were returning carts around me. Someone waited
for my space until I waved them on.

At 2:29:48, the window began to rise.

The pencil marks on the new catches rotated apart.

I replayed that part six times. The resolution wasn’t good enough to
show what moved them.

At 2:30:16, fingers curled over the sill.

I put the phone facedown on the passenger seat.

For a minute I watched a man transfer grocery bags into his trunk. One
bag split. An orange rolled under his car, and he crouched to get it.

I picked the phone back up.

It was inside.

The ceiling light was still on. There was no infrared glare to explain
its face, no darkness to make its arms seem longer. It lowered itself
headfirst, palms flat against the wall, and folded onto the floor.

In the recording, I slept with one hand tucked beneath my cheek.

I wanted to shout at myself.

It didn’t go to the bed.

It crawled behind the open bedroom door.

Only its fingers remained visible around the bottom edge. After eleven
minutes, its head emerged near the floor.

It watched me from there.

Then the closet.

I’d left the sliding door half open while packing. It pulled a shirt off
a hanger, placed it on the floor, and squeezed into the gap beside my
coats. For twenty-three minutes the only part of it I could see was one
bent knee.

I scrubbed forward, stopped, went back.

Under the desk, it folded its forearms across the chair rungs and rested
its chin on them.

My sleeping face turned toward it.

Its head lifted.

I rolled away.

It came out.

I began to notice that pattern. Not every time. Sometimes it stayed
after I moved. Sometimes it changed places while I lay perfectly still.
But it kept finding another angle on my face, another gap in the
furniture where I might look if I opened my eyes.

Behind the door. Between the coats. Beneath the desk where my feet went
every morning.

Once it flattened itself and slid under the bed.

I could only see a hand beside the bedpost.

Onscreen, my shoulders climbed toward my ears. My mouth closed. A few
seconds later I shifted toward the edge of the mattress.

The hand withdrew.

It emerged on that side instead.

I stopped the video.

There was a missed call from Jess across the top of my screen. I called
her back and couldn’t explain where I was.

“What supermarket?”

I told her.

“Stay there. I’m coming.”

“Don’t hang up.”

“Okay.”

I kept her on speaker while I watched the rest.

At 3:54 it settled beside my pillow.

For ninety minutes, it crouched there. I checked the timestamp, dragged
forward, checked again. Occasionally its fingers flexed against the
boards.

I slept through all of it.

Then its mouth moved.

The sound was low enough that I nearly missed it beneath Jess asking
whether I’d locked the car.

I turned the volume up.

“Awake?”

Jess stopped talking.

The voice was breathy and poorly shaped, but the word was clear.

——————————

“Did you hear that?” I asked.

“I’m turning into the car park.”

I didn’t play it again.

Jess took me back to her place. We brought the camera. I put it on the
cabinet facing her couch because I couldn’t bear the thought of another
gap in the footage, another stretch of time I would have to guess at.

She went to get my bag from the car.

I sat down to wait.

That was all it took.

When I opened my eyes, the light beside the couch was on and the room
beyond it was dim. Something stood between the coffee table and the
television, several feet away.

For a second I thought Jess had come back wearing her coat.

Then I saw its hands hanging beside its knees.

My eyes opened all the way.

It moved before I could take a breath.

There was a sharp scrape from the coffee table and its weight hit me
across the hips. I got my forearm over my face. Its shoulder drove into
it, knocking my elbow against my teeth.

I tried to roll off the couch.

It caught my wrist.

Not a scrambling grip. It took hold, pulled my arm down, and pressed it
into the cushion beside my head. Its other hand settled against my jaw.
I could feel each fingertip separately.

I shut my eyes.

Something struck my chest.

My eyes flew open again.

Its face was directly above mine.

The room had gone strangely quiet. I could hear air coming out of my
nose in little bursts. Its knee was on my thigh, and when I tried to
lift my leg, nothing happened.

It looked down at my shirt.

One finger hooked the collar and pulled until the fabric tightened
beneath my back. Then it set the tips of its other fingers against the
skin below my collarbone.

I felt pressure first.

Then it dragged them down.

I screamed into its face.

Its mouth opened. Its shoulders lifted, and for an instant I thought it
was going to scream too.

Instead, it waited.

I pulled against its hand hard enough that something in my wrist
clicked. It leaned closer, watching me. Its fingers moved back to the
top of my chest.

“No,” I said. “Please, no.”

It did it again.

I remember the lamp tilting in my vision. I remember trying to get my
heel onto the floor so I could push. After that there are pieces I still
can’t put in order: the taste of my sleeve, the scrape of upholstery
against my ear, my own voice sounding as though it came from elsewhere
in the house.

Then nothing.

The next thing I can remember properly is Jess on the floor beside me,
saying my name with her phone pressed between her cheek and shoulder.

“Don’t sit up. They said don’t sit up.”

I tried anyway.

Pain tightened across my chest and folded me forward. She caught me by
the shoulders.

“Where is it?”

“It’s gone.”

“Where?”

“I don’t know.”

The front door stood open. Her bag was in the hallway, its contents
scattered across the runner.

I couldn’t understand why she was crying. I kept asking how long she’d
been at the car.

“I told you,” she said. “I told you I was going to get your prescription
as well. You answered me.”

I didn’t remember answering.

At the hospital, a nurse cut away my shirt. I watched the scissors
because looking anywhere else meant I might see the doorway.

The wounds needed cleaning and closing. None went as deep as the doctor
had expected from the amount of blood on me. He asked what had caused
them.

I said I’d been attacked.

“By a person?”

Jess was standing beside the bed.

“We have a recording,” she said.

We did. The police took a copy. One officer watched about fifteen
seconds with the phone angled away from me, then asked who else had
access to Jess’s house.

I didn’t have an answer he could use.

I stayed awake through the cleaning, through the questions, through the
hours they kept me for observation. Every time my head dipped, I jerked
it up so hard my neck hurt.

It was the following afternoon before I watched what the camera had
saved.

Jess sat beside me at her kitchen table. Neither of us wanted to be near
the couch.

The recording showed me asleep for forty-two minutes before it entered
the frame.

Not from the front door.

It came out of the short hallway leading to her bathroom.

I stopped there.

Jess stared at the screen.

“The window in there doesn’t open,” she said.

We had nothing to add to that.

On the video, it stood by the television for almost six minutes. Once it
touched the dark screen with the back of a finger. Then I shifted on the
couch.

My face turned toward it.

I watched my eyes open.

The coffee table moved four inches when it launched itself. That was the
scrape I’d heard. A mug rolled off and landed on the rug without
breaking.

I muted the sound as soon as the first scream started.

Even without it, I could see what my screaming did to the thing. Each
time my mouth opened, it rose slightly over me. Its head made quick
movements, attentive in a way it hadn’t been before.

Then my free hand fell off the couch.

It stopped.

I went back ten seconds.

My hand fell. My face slackened.

Its fingers lifted clear of my chest.

There was no last swipe, no continued struggle. It released my wrist and
eased itself off me, placing its feet carefully between the fallen mug
and the coffee table.

Then it sat on the rug.

Its back rested against the couch below my knees. One hand lay open on
the floor. The other picked briefly at a bit of loose skin on its
knuckle.

I was unconscious beside it, bleeding through my shirt, and it looked
settled.

I dragged the playback forward.

“Wait,” Jess said.

Twenty minutes had passed.

My foot moved.

The creature turned its head.

On the couch, my mouth tightened. I could see myself trying to swallow.
My eyelids hadn’t fully lifted when it climbed back onto me.

This time it knew where to put its knee.

I turned the phone over.

Jess didn’t tell me I had to watch. She put her hand near mine, not
quite touching it, and waited.

There were three attacks.

I counted them because I needed to be certain. Three times it held me
down. Three times my body eventually went loose, and each time it
stopped within a second.

After the last one, it sat beside me again.

At the sound of Jess’s key in the front door, it got up and left through
the hallway.

She entered the frame carrying a pharmacy bag.

I closed the video before she found me.

For a while we listened to the refrigerator.

I’d thought it wanted me awake because sleep confused it. Because shut
eyes hid something it needed to see.

But it hadn’t lost interest when I woke.

It had lost interest when I couldn’t feel what it was doing anymore.

———————

I wrote that down and then crossed it out.

Unconscious = safe.

Too definite. Too easy to believe at three in the morning when I was
desperate.

Underneath, I wrote: “Stopped when I passed out. Three times.”

That was the beginning of the notebook.

Jess wanted me to stay in hospital. The hospital had already discharged
me. We argued about this in her kitchen as if one of us could change it
by saying the right thing.

“Then we go somewhere else,” she said.

“It came here.”

“So we don’t stay here either.”

“And when you fall asleep?”

She stopped.

I hated myself for saying it. I hated that she looked toward the
hallway.

I went home before dark. She drove me, furious, and waited while I
checked every room. Then she made me promise to answer the phone.

“If you don’t pick up, I’m calling someone.”

“Okay.”

“Not coming over. Calling someone.”

“Good.”

She hugged me carefully at the door. I kept my chest away from hers and
felt her notice.

I moved the camera into the living room. I left every light on.

For the first night, I sat at the kitchen table with the notebook and
watched the bedroom recording. The one from before the couch. I could
manage that one without shaking too badly.

I watched myself roll over. Scratch my cheek. Bend one knee and shove
the covers down.

None of those movements bothered it.

Once I moved so abruptly that it had to pull its hand away from my
elbow. It only waited for me to settle.

I wrote: Movement isn’t enough.

Then: Eyes.

I paused the couch footage on the last frame before it crossed the room.
My face was small, but the change was visible. Half-asleep confusion,
then recognition.

I didn’t play the next frame.

By five, my notes were barely legible. I’d been holding the pen so
tightly that my fingers wouldn’t straighten without help.

At seven I called Jess.

“Still here,” I told her.

“Have you slept?”

“Not yet.”

“You can’t keep doing that.”

“I know.”

I slept from nine until a delivery driver rang the bell at half past
one. Curled on my side, dressed, with my phone under my hand.

Nothing happened.

That afternoon I bought a cheap wristwatch with a vibrating alarm. The
sound of my phone waking me suddenly had become unbearable. I tested the
watch at the kitchen table until I could open my eyes slowly when it
buzzed.

It felt ridiculous.

I kept doing it.

The creature returned the next night while I was on the couch, watching
the camera feed on my phone.

I saw it on the screen before I saw it in the room.

A pale hand appeared around the living-room doorway.

My phone was upright against a cushion. My eyes were open. I was
breathing through my nose like a man sitting on a couch, because that
was what I was doing.

I shut my eyes.

Fast. No attempt to make it natural.

The floor gave a small creak.

I let my head fall sideways against the cushion and opened my mouth. My
pulse beat against the dressings.

Another creak, closer.

The phone was still playing a faint electronic hiss from the camera
microphone. I’d forgotten to mute the live feed.

I lay there while its own footsteps came out of the speaker a fraction
late.

It stopped beside me.

My left arm was trapped beneath my side. I could feel my little finger
going numb. I wanted to move it but couldn’t remember how quickly I’d
moved in the recording, whether a sleeping person would pull an arm free
or just put up with it.

Something touched the phone.

The hiss cut off.

A fingertip rested on my closed eyelid.

I stopped breathing.

It stayed there, barely pressing. I could feel a tiny tremor in the
finger, or in my face. I couldn’t tell.

I made the loose noise from the bed. It came out badly, caught at the
back of my throat.

The fingertip slid away.

I lay still until the vibrating watch woke me in daylight.

I had actually fallen asleep.

The phone was facedown on the rug. When I retrieved it, there was a
smear across the screen.

On the recording, the creature had watched me shut my eyes.

There was no possibility it had missed that. Its head was already
through the doorway. It had a clear view.

It stayed beside the couch for nine minutes, touched my eyelid, then
crouched behind the armrest until shortly before dawn.

I wrote: “Pretending worked.”

Below that: “It saw me.”

I didn’t know how to turn those two facts into something useful.

Maybe it couldn’t make the connection. Maybe whatever made it stop was
as simple as eyelids covering eyes.

Or maybe it understood perfectly.

I remembered its finger resting there. How little effort it would have
taken to lift my lid.

I put the notebook away. I still haven’t figured out what any of this
means. If it really thought I was asleep, then maybe I’ve found
something I can use.

If it knew I was awake and decided not to hurt me anyway, I don’t know
what to do with that.

Jess wants me to come over tonight. I told her I would, but I’m still
sitting at my kitchen table with every light in the apartment on. I
haven’t slept since yesterday morning, and the camera is still
recording. So far there’s nothing on it except me.

I’m going to try to sleep for a few hours while it’s daylight. I’ll
update when I can.

I don’t think I’ve been fooling it. I think it’s been letting me think I
have.
