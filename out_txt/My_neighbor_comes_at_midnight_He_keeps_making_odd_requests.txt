The woods behind the house were a complicated matter. Of all the places
the oddities came from, the ones that crawled out of those trees were
the ones you really didn’t want showing up. Anthony had made it clear we
weren’t supposed to go in there. That never stopped them from coming to
us.

It was a summer night, only a few months into my stay, when the knock
came. It wouldn’t have mattered if it had been the front door. This one
came from the back. I woke up hard, grabbed the pocket knife from my
nightstand, and went to answer it.

I opened the door and found a well-dressed man standing a few feet back.
At first glance he looked like an ordinary plain faced man, except for
the smile. It was wide, and every tooth was black, like they were
rotting in his mouth. He held out a hand.

“Hello, my friend. I just moved in recently and wanted to introduce
myself.”

I rubbed my eyes, still half-asleep.

“Yeah, man. Nice to meet you. You know it’s almost midnight, right?”

“Ah, yes. But I couldn’t move in without the proper formalities. I had
to make your acquaintance. I just moved in over there.” He gestured over
his shoulder into the solid black of the woods.

My throat tightened. Sweat started down my back.

“I appreciate the formality… Is there something I can do for you?”

“I was hoping I might borrow some sugar.”

I just stared at him. Of all the things that had shown up at this house,
this felt almost insultingly ordinary. 

“Yeah. Give me a second.” I shut the door, dug through the cabinets, and
came back with a mason jar of sugar.

“Will this work?”

He took it faster than I could pull my hand back. 

“Oh, how delightful! I shall return the jar posthaste.”

“Keep it. I don’t need it.”

“How pleasant. I appreciate your kindness, sir.”

He then tilted his head back. His neck began to stretch, the bones
cracking one after another as it lengthened. His jaw unhinged with a wet
pop and kept opening until it looked more snake than man. He raised the
jar and set it against the black teeth. The sound of those rotting teeth
scraping the glass sent a chill down my spine.

He forced the whole jar in. I watched the muscles of his throat work
around it until the glass gave way with a muffled crunch. Sharp edges
pushed against the skin from the inside like they might break through.
He swallowed hard, and his neck slowly folded back to normal.

“I appreciate the kind gesture, neighbor. I shall only be staying two
more nights, and will make only two more requests of you. I hope you
have a pleasant night.”

He turned and walked back into the trees without another word. I locked
the door, went to bed, and somehow slept.

The next night the knock came at the same hour. I opened the back door
and found him again. His clothes were tattered now, the fabric hanging
in places.

“What do you need tonight?” I asked. I sounded more tired than I meant
to.

“I was hoping you might assist me with a meal. I seem to have forgotten
to eat today.”

I could feel the bags under my eyes with how tired I was. I exhaled
deeply. 

I rubbed my face. “What are you looking for? We’ve got eggs, cereal… not
much else.”

He just looked at me.

“I wouldn’t ask much of you, kind sir. The meal I require is dead
flesh.”

I stared at him. 

“Did you just say dead flesh?” 

A flicker of irritation crossed his face. 

“Yes. Animal or human. As long as it is dead.”

“How long do I have?”

He glanced at his wrist. There was no watch; only a squirming tumor that
moved under the skin.

“An hour should be plenty, don’t you think?”

I didn’t need the full hour. Out here the roads always had something. I
found a possum flattened into the asphalt, gripped it by the tail, and
pulled. It came free with a wet tearing sound.

I returned to the man with the marsupial corpse in hand. I held it out.

“This work?”

He took the corpse and turned it over in his hands like he was
inspecting produce.

“I believe this shall provide the perfect meal!”

He started to turn back toward the trees when a large cockroach landed
on his shoulder.

“Oh, how wonderful. A new friend.”

He snatched it up and brought it to his right eye. He then began
pushing. The eye bulged sideways as the roach’s legs kicked. A thin line
of blood ran down his cheek. He kept pressing until the insect
disappeared into the socket with a soft, wet sound. He didn’t even
blink. He turned, and walked into the woods. I went inside and tried to
sleep.

The third night I was already waiting by the back door. The knock came
on schedule.

What stood there barely looked like the man anymore. His body seemed
held together by string and will. The left arm hung by thin cords of
muscle and stretched skin. The jaw was gone; only a long, swollen tongue
which lolled from the open lower half of his face.

“Hey…” I said, mostly to myself.

His eyes were dull. I wasn’t sure he even saw me. Then a voice came out
of him that didn’t belong to any human throat. It was deep and layered,
like something speaking through a borrowed mouth.

“In your bedroom you keep a locked box. Inside it is something precious
to you. Bring it to me.”

I knew exactly what he meant. At the bottom of my closet, in a small
locked box, I kept a few important things. One of them was a small pink
teddy bear I’d had since the day I was born. I got it and brought it to
the door.

He took it with both hands, like it weighed more than it should, and
pressed it to what was left of his face. He breathed in deep and
shuddered. 

“The taste of innocence… exquisite.”

He then came apart. The skin collapsed with a long hiss. Black liquid
ran out of the empty suit of clothes as a shifting mass of cockroaches
poured from the neck and sleeves and scattered into the trees.

I stood there for a while looking at the empty clothes and the dark wet
stain on the porch. I then went back inside, locked the door, and went
to bed. Whatever the house decided to send next, I’d deal with it. That
was the rule.
