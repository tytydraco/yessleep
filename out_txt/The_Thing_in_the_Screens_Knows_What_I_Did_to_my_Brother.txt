When I was eight years old, I watched something tear the arms off a
cartoon character while my mother folded laundry beside me. She didn’t
see anything.

I’m thirty-one now, and I’ve spent most of my life learning how to live
with that creature. For years, it existed only inside screens,
televisions, games, movies, videos and I told myself that no matter how
horrible the things it showed me became, there was still glass between
us.

Two weeks ago, it started appearing in my building’s security footage.

Last night, it knocked twice from the inside of my laptop screen.

Imagine a person who never finished loading. It is impossibly tall and
thin, with arms too long for its body. Some parts are perfectly
detailed: pores, fingernails, veins beneath gray skin. Other parts are
missing and filled with whatever is behind it, as if the world is trying
to occupy spaces its is behind it, as if the world is trying to occupy
spaces its body forgot to complete.

Its face never stays the same. Eyes shift to the wrong places. A mouth
appears where a cheek should be. Sometimes it looks like a skull wrapped
in wet skin; sometimes it borrows human features and loses them seconds
later.

Only one detail never changes: a scar cutting through its left eyebrow.

I don’t know anyone with that scar.

The first time I saw it, I was watching a cartoon we had recorded on
VHS. It was about two brothers traveling through space with a talking
dog, and I knew the episode by heart. That was why I noticed the figure
standing behind an alien tree.

I thought the tape was damaged until it tilted its head.

The cartoon froze, but the tape didn’t. Stars still blinked.

The creature moved closer without walking; every frame simply placed it
nearer. When it reached one of the boys, it pushed its fingers into his
mouth and pulled. His body opened like torn paper.

What came out wasn’t blood or organs but pieces of color which the
creature pressed into the unfinished holes in its chest.

Then it looked straight at me.

I screamed until my mother turned off the television. When she turned it
back on, the boy was alive.

After that, I saw it everywhere.

In a racing game, it stood in the middle of the track until my car hit
it, then the vehicle folded inward around its body like crushed foil.

In a children’s show, it held a smiling rabbit by the head and pulled
until its neck stretched across the screen.

During a movie at school, it pushed an actress into a wall and watched
while the plaster swallowed her. Nobody else reacted. My classmates
laughed while, on my screen, people screamed.

The only person I told was my younger brother, Tomás.

Tomás was three years younger than me, with hair so blond it was almost
white and a gap where one front tooth had been knocked out in a bicycle
accident.

He never saw the creature, but he believed me.
If I pointed to a corner of the television and whispered that the man
was there, Tomás would stare at the spot and ask what it was doing.
He wore a cheap blue plastic watch that had stopped working, and
whenever he wanted me to follow him, he tapped its face twice with his
index finger, as if we were late.

Two taps, then he would run and expect me to follow.

When I was eleven, our house caught fire.

For most of my life, my memory of that night began in the backyard. I
remembered standing barefoot on wet grass while smoke poured from the
roof, my mother screamed Tomás’s name, and a firefighter disappeared
inside.

They found my brother much later.

He died before he reached the hospital.

Everyone told me that children hide during fires.

They said he must have panicked, gone into the small storage room beside
the kitchen, and become trapped by the smoke.

I accepted that explanation because the alternative was a blank space in
my memory.

After Tomás died, the creature disappeared for almost a year.

When it came back, I was watching a comedy with my mother. It walked
through the apartment door as if it were part of the cast, placed both
hands on an actor’s head, and squeezed until the face collapsed between
its palms.

My mother laughed at a joke.

On the version I was seeing, the creature killed everyone in the scene.

From then on, it never really left.

I stopped playing games at fifteen and movies at seventeen. In college,
I invented excuses whenever friends invited me to the cinema.

Then it began appearing on my phone too, peeling skin from a singer,
pushing makeup brushes through a woman’s eyes, dismantling an animated
child piece by piece.

Eventually I discovered a rule that made life possible: it needed
someone inside the screen.

A character, an actor, a person in a video.

Empty screens were safe.

For years, I built my life around that rule.

Then, two weeks ago, it changed.

I work for a company that manages commercial buildings, and one night I
had to review security footage after a reported theft. While
fast-forwarding, I saw myself enter an elevator.

The creature stepped in behind me.

I paused the video because I remembered that moment.

I had been alone.

In the footage, it stood so close that its chest nearly touched my back,
and when the doors slid shut it lifted one hand and tapped its wrist
twice.

I rewound the recording.

It did it again.

Two taps.

Tomás’s gesture.

That night I dreamed about smoke, and when I woke up, twenty years of
missing memory came back all at once.

Tomás and I had been fighting before the fire.

He had broken a box of porcelain figurines that belonged to our
grandmother, and I chased him through the house. He ran into the storage
room beside the kitchen.

I slammed the door and locked it from the outside with a small key.

I meant to leave him there for five minutes, just long enough to scare
him.

Then I heard my mother coming home and panicked because I knew I would
be punished, so I put the key in my pocket.

Not long afterward, the fire started.

We never knew exactly how.

I remember the smoke now.

I remember my mother dragging me outside.

Most of all, I remember the firefighter kneeling in front of me in the
yard and asking, “Is anyone else inside?”

I knew Tomás was in the storage room.

The key was still in my pocket.

But I was terrified of admitting what I had done, so I shook my head and
said no.

I only told the truth when my mother began screaming his name.

Until then, the firefighters had been searching the bedrooms.

They lost minutes.

Maybe ten.

Maybe fifteen.

I have never had the courage to find out.

There is one more thing I erased: while my mother pulled me toward the
front door, I heard Tomás pounding on the storage room door.

I heard him, and I kept running.

The night I remembered, I opened my laptop and said his name.

The screen flickered.

The creature appeared in front of the storage room door and tapped its
wrist twice.

The next morning, I went to my mother’s house and found the old computer
we used as children.

Inside was a folder called TOMAS.

My mother swore she had never created it.

There was one file.

When I opened it, our old kitchen appeared, not quite like a recording
but like a memory filmed from somewhere inside the house.

I watched my eleven-year-old self chase Tomás.

I watched him run into the storage room.

I saw my own hand close the door and heard the key turn.

The creature stood in the hallway, watching.

Then the fire began.

The image shifted to the backyard, where the firefighter knelt in front
of the child version of me and asked if anyone else was inside.

Behind her, the creature’s face changed from mine to Tomás’s and then to
the face of a boy I had never seen before, a boy with a scar through his
left eyebrow.

I started crying and said everything out loud.

I admitted that I locked the door, that I had the key, that I lied to
the firefighter, and that I heard Tomás pounding while I ran outside.

As I spoke, the creature began to fall apart.

Pieces of its body dropped away like dead pixels.

Its face split open, and underneath was my own face at eleven years old.

There was no scar.

The child opened her mouth, but instead of a scream I heard a fire
alarm, followed by two knocks.
Then the screen turned white and the computer shut down.

For four days, the creature was gone.

I watched an entire movie and nobody died.

I opened random videos.

Nothing happened.

I played a game for two hours without seeing it once.

For the first time in twenty years, I wondered if my psychiatrist had
been right.

Maybe the creature had always been guilt given a body by a memory I
refused to face.

Maybe confessing had killed it.

Then my mother found an old tape recorded three days before the fire.

Tomás was sitting in front of the television playing a video game while
I did homework in the background.

Halfway through the recording, the creature appeared inside the game.

The camera had captured it.

I paused, rewound, and watched again.

It was still there.

That had never happened before.

Then Tomás stopped playing and stared directly at it.

He could see it.

The creature raised one hand and tapped its wrist twice.

Tomás smiled and copied the gesture.

“Can you see it too?” he asked.

From the background, my younger voice answered, “See what?”

The creature killed his character, tearing it open and filling one of
its empty arms with pieces of the body.
But afterward it didn’t look at Tomás.

It looked past him, directly at the little girl sitting in the
background.

At me.

As if it already knew what was going to happen.
I closed the video.

The screen went black, and I heard two knocks behind me.

Not from the computer.

Behind me.

In the reflection, the creature was standing in the room, outside the
screen, almost completely formed.

Tomás stood beside it, still eight years old, still missing his front
tooth.

He didn’t look afraid.

He pointed at the creature’s face, where the scar cut through the left
eyebrow, and shook his head.
I understood.

The scar had never belonged to him.

The tapping gesture had.

The faces the creature wore came from somewhere else.

Some were mine.

Some belonged to people I had never met.

Maybe that is what it has been doing all this time: collecting faces,
gestures, memories, scars.

Maybe every fictional character it killed taught it how bodies work, and
every person it haunted left something inside it.

Now I see real people dying on screens.

My mother on my phone.

My boss in security footage.

My neighbor on television.

Yesterday, during a video call, I watched the creature break my
therapist’s neck while she continued speaking normally.

They are all still alive in the real world.

For now.

I’m writing this on my mother’s computer because my laptop stopped
working.

The screen keeps flickering.

In the reflection, the creature is standing behind me, and its face is
changing between Tomás, my mother, me, and that unknown boy with the
scar.

Now it has stopped changing.

It is wearing my face, but its hands are burned black all the way to the
wrists.

Mine aren’t.

Not yet.

Tomás is beside it, pointing at the screen.

At first I thought he was pointing at me, but he isn’t.

The creature isn’t looking at me either.

It is looking at the camera.

At whoever is reading this.

It has raised one hand and pressed two fingers against its wrist.

One tap.

Then another.

And now it is waiting to see if you follow.
