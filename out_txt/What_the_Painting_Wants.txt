Notes for Chapter 3 – Qualitative Methods

March 3, 9:47 PM

Introduction to Qualitative Social Research (by Philipp Mayring)

Central question of the text: What is the procedure for qualitative
thinking? What steps need to be observed?

Key terms and concepts: Five postulates form the structure of
qualitative thinking

1.  The research object is always a subject; it is both the starting
    point and the goal of the investigation. The subject’s own
    perspective is foregrounded (subject-orientation)
2.  A description of the object always comes first
3.  Interpretation is necessary because the object is never fully
    self-evident
4.  The object is studied in its natural, everyday environment, to
    minimize distortion
5.  Generalizability of results is not automatic. Individual cases must
    be justified

Nothing substantial has really been published on this painting since
Snyder’s 1985 essay.

While digging through the institute archive I came across two oddities
I’m noting for later. A 1998 institute newsletter announces a
dissertation titled “On the Iconography of the Painting’s Interior Space
in Las Meninas” — it was never published, and on the only surviving copy
of the newsletter, the author’s name has been rendered illegible by a
water stain. A 2011 conference program also lists a talk, “Reading the
Interior: A New Approach to Las Meninas” — in the published proceedings
afterward, the paper is simply missing, no substitute, no footnote, no
explanation. Both times, as far as I can reconstruct it, unlike Snyder,
Foucault, or Searle, the subject wasn’t perspective or geometry, but
what’s actually going on inside the room itself. Odd that those two, of
all people, never finished. Probably a coincidence.

Starting point: what every introduction to this painting explains first,
the punchline I learned myself in my first semester: we, the viewers,
are standing where King Philip IV and his wife stood while Velázquez
painted them. The mirror in the background shows us. We’ve taken their
place, which is why everyone in the room is looking at us.

That’s the version everyone starts with.

10:14 PM

Small marginal note first, because it won’t leave me alone: the dog in
the foreground lies, in every reproduction I know, with its head on its
paws, half asleep. In the high-resolution Prado file I got hold of
today, its head is raised a few degrees. I compared the files. It’s the
same scan. Probably a compression artifact. Moving on.

11:02 PM

Joel Snyder’s 1985 essay actually works out the perspective of Las
Meninas — orthogonals, vanishing lines, a floor plan of the room. His
conclusion: the correct point of projection, the spot from which the
picture is perspectivally constructed, is not opposite the mirror at
all. It sits well to the right of it. If we stood at the geometrically
correct spot, we wouldn’t see ourselves in the mirror at all. So does it
reflect the actual royal couple standing in front of Velázquez — or does
it reflect the already-finished double portrait on the huge canvas?

The story everyone tells about this painting — we are the king, we are
the queen, we are the ones meant — is an illusion the painting produces
in us, but not one that’s actually built into its geometry. Snyder puts
it this way: “This illusion is not in the painting; it is in us.”

I’ve been sitting in the same spot for an hour and I notice I’m getting
cold. My mouth is dry … The heating is on. Going to get something to
drink … I think I’ve got a few more hours ahead of me, better keep
myself hydrated!

11:47 PM

Second thought, which follows from the first, and is actually worse: if
the king and queen really did stand directly opposite the mirror, as
tradition claims, then Velázquez — standing between them and the mirror
while he painted — would have blocked their view of it with his own body
and the enormous canvas. They could hardly have seen themselves. The
mirror supposedly made for them was, for them, effectively invisible.

So: vantage point, viewer’s position, the royal couple, and the mirror
image can’t simply all be collapsed onto one single point …

I looked in the archive today for a conservation report on the original,
just out of curiosity. What I found was a note from the eighties
recording “anomalous wear marks” at the exact spot of the painted mirror
— microscopically fine scratches, as if from repeated touching, on a
painting that has hung behind glass for decades. Marked as “unresolved.”
No one ever followed up on it.

Kind of creepy … A mirror made for no one, that someone touched anyway.

March 4

12:31 AM

It gets worse, and this time it’s almost verbatim in the text — I read
it three times to make sure I wasn’t misunderstanding it. Snyder argues:
even the painted Velázquez himself — the figure with brush and palette
looking straight out at us — if he turned around and looked into the
mirror, could not see what we see in it. He would only see himself.

Which means: no one inside the depicted scene can ever see what’s
visible in the mirror. Not the king. Not the queen. Not the painter. It
exists for no one who lives in that room. Only for us. Only for a gaze
that comes from outside that reality.

I don’t know how you’re supposed to fit a sentence like that into a
paper without it sounding like something other than art history. “And by
the way, the function of the royal couple is that only we can see them”
— how absurd …

1:16 AM

I just found again the note that’s been bothering me most since reading
the essay. Snyder quotes Foucault writing about the mirror: in its
depths there ought, by rights, to appear the face of the chance
passer-by as well as that of Velázquez himself. But because both are
already present within the picture — one to the right, the other to the
left — neither the painter nor the viewer can appear in the mirror.
“Just as the king appears in the depths of the looking-glass precisely
because he does not belong to the picture.”

I’ve read that sentence four times now. Only what doesn’t belong to the
scene can appear in the mirror. Presence rules out reflection. Only the
absent, the outsider, is made visible. So it’s really about looking at
the finished painting. No one inside the depicted scene — not the dwarf,
not the Infanta, not Velázquez himself — could ever glimpse what’s in
the mirror. The dog might point to this too, or symbolize it, even. Its
eyes seem closed; you can’t see them from outside, and you can’t tell
where they’re looking. The mirror with the royal couple exists only for
us, for a gaze from outside the depicted reality. Only for me …

An image inside an image, made for no one who lives in this world.

I’m calling Julie on video now after all, maybe she’s still up …, just
to have someone on the line. I can screen-share the scan and ask her
what she sees in the mirror.

1:58 AM

Julie says I look pale, asks if I’ve slept since yesterday. I keep
writing while she stays on the line.

There’s a point I’d missed before, because everyone who writes about
this painting stares at the mirror as if hypnotized — myself included,
for the last seven hours. The mirror does sit at the pictorial center of
the painting, where our eye is automatically drawn. But the actual
geometric center of the canvas, the point where the room’s perspective
lines really converge, is somewhere else: upper left, at the doorframe,
directly above the bent arm of the man in the doorway in the background.
In shadow, barely noticeable, of no visual interest whatsoever.

The painting deliberately steers us away from its own center. It shows
us the mirror so that we won’t look at the door.

The man there is named José Nieto, chamberlain to the queen. His entire
official duty at court, according to the household ordinances, was to
attend to her “in order to open doors, whenever he is so ordered.”
Nothing else. A man whose sole function was opening doors, forever
frozen at exactly the spot which this painting — if you take its own
geometry seriously — makes into its true center. Why such a prominent
position? Is he coming, or going? Was he instructed by the queen to open
the door? For whom, if everyone else is looking the other way … almost
as if he’d opened it for me, for the viewer … Behind him, through the
open door, light is falling in that, according to the actual floor plan
of the Galería del Mediodía, shouldn’t be falling there at all — at that
spot in the Alcázar there would only have been a dark stairwell. In
every scan I’ve opened tonight, it’s gotten a little brighter back
there. Probably just the screen brightness adjusting automatically.

I’ve opened the scan several times tonight. I keep feeling more and more
that the figures are looking straight at me. I don’t think Velázquez is
painting the royal couple, either … the palette in his hand shows the
colors of the Infanta’s dress, not the colors of the reflection in the
back. But that would mean the artist and the whole group, dog included,
are standing in front of one gigantic mirror, and the artist is painting
from that. The door-opener seems to be opening the door for whoever is
looking at the painting — but that’s exactly where the viewer is
standing … which is to say, me … Which would mean there’s really only
one mirror in the whole picture-world … So why don’t the figures look at
their own reflection, at themselves, but at me, behind the mirror?

2:35 AM

Last find, and then I’m calling it a night, I promise myself. Mirrors
like this one belonged, in seventeenth-century Spain, to their own
literary genre, the espejo de príncipes, the “mirror of the prince.” A
mirror that doesn’t show outward appearance but an ideal, exemplary
image — a reflection that, literally, “can be seen only by the inner
eye, a reflection whose source is not, and cannot be, corporeal.”

A mirror with no bodily source for what it shows.

3:12 AM. Last entry.

Julie hung up. She says I’m talking nonsense and should go to sleep,
also that my camera’s frozen and she can’t hear me properly anymore.

I know now what the painting wants, and in the end it’s the simplest
thought of the whole night, assembled out of a bunch of small, correct,
verifiable facts: the mirror only shows what doesn’t belong to the
scene. Whatever is present cannot appear in it. That applies to the
royal couple. But it also applies to me. Which means whatever lies
between the two is another place … something like a room you only pass
through. I need to keep thinking about this tomorrow, this could really
turn into an important paper!

The room has gotten cold, as if there were a draft — but the window’s
shut, and the door to the hallway is on the other side of the room, not
behind me. Hopefully I’m not coming down with something now too, and for
some reason it smells like dog in here …

I’m going to lie down for a second—
