I didn’t cry the day my husband was cremated.

At the Universal Funeral Parlour in Hung Hom, I greeted relatives,
answered calls, and checked which folder held which documents.

People took my hand and told me how sorry they were. I nodded and
thanked them.

I heard the same words so many times that, eventually, I stood up
automatically whenever anyone approached.

In his funeral photograph, Jayne was wearing that blue shirt. When I
took it, I’d told him he was grinning like an idiot.

“I’m looking at my wife. What do you expect?” he’d said.

Now everyone said I’d chosen a lovely photograph.

I hadn’t had a proper night’s sleep in days. There were things to hand
over at his company, funeral arrangements, insurance forms, calls from
the police, and court appearances I’d already been scheduled for.
Everything needed an answer from me.

I’m a prosecutor with the Department of Justice. I’m used to dealing
with problems.

That day, I kept dealing with them until GIGI tugged at my clothes.

She stood beside me, holding her stuffed rabbit in her other hand.

“Mommy, where’s Daddy?”

The relatives nearby fell quiet.

I crouched and straightened her collar. There was nothing wrong with it.
I just needed something to do.

“Daddy’s gone away on a business trip. Somewhere very far away.”

“When’s he coming back?”

My hands stopped.

“Mommy doesn’t know yet.”

She thought for a moment, tucked the rabbit under her arm, and reached
for my handbag.

“Then let’s call him.”

I gently caught her hand.

My phone was in the bag. His number was still in it. So was the voice
message I hadn’t been able to listen to.

I didn’t know what to say.

Someone called from behind me. There was a document I needed to check. I
stood, held GIGI’s hand, and told her to sit with Grandma for a little
while.

She didn’t make a fuss. But after a few steps, she looked back.

I lowered my eyes to the paperwork and pretended not to notice.

Later, when the last relative asked if there was anything else they
could do, I opened my bag and checked everything again.

As if finding one unsigned form would mean he wasn’t completely gone
yet.

But there was nothing.

Everything was done.

Four-year-old GIGI fell asleep in the car on the way home. I carried her
to bed, took off her shoes, and went to sit in the living room.

I only meant to rest against the sofa for a moment. I hadn’t even put my
phone down. But my eyelids grew heavier, and once they closed, I
couldn’t make them open again.

I don’t remember when I fell asleep.

When I opened my eyes, my head was resting on a school desk.

There was a scratch in the surface. I remembered it. Part of a math test
lay beside me.

Someone picked my jacket up off the floor and draped it over my
shoulders.

I looked up.

Jayne was sixteen.

He startled, looked away, muttered that he’d just been passing, and
hurried off.

We weren’t together yet. Back then, he could barely manage a whole
sentence while looking at me.

I watched him walk away and couldn’t help smiling.

Then someone pulled at my hand.

“Mommy, I’m hungry.”

I woke up.

The living room had gone dark. A gray blanket had been tucked around me.

I assumed GIGI had put it there.

The fridge was full of food he’d portioned out: marinated pork chops,
ground meat, chopped vegetables. Every container had a date on it in his
crooked handwriting.

Jayne used to say I treated the kitchen like a crime scene. I’d take
everything out and forget to put any of it back. So he prepared things
ahead of time.

He’d told me to check the dates. Once something was past its date, I was
to throw it away. No feeling bad about wasting it.

I stood in front of the fridge and suddenly couldn’t remember what day
it was.

The pork chops had three days left.

He didn’t even have tomorrow.

After we ate, GIGI and I sat together in the rocking chair in our little
garden. The moon was full. She rested her feet across my legs.

“Daddy comes home when the moon is full,” she said.

I stroked her hair.

“With Daddy away, you’ll have to help look after your clumsy mommy. You
even put a blanket over me earlier. What a sweet girl.”

She shook her head.

“It wasn’t me. Daddy did it.”

“Yes. Daddy used to help Mommy with lots of things.”

She looked at me as if she wanted to say something else. Then she just
leaned against me.

I didn’t ask.

I wanted her to be right so badly that I couldn’t let myself believe
her.

A sentencing hearing in a manslaughter case had already been fixed. It
couldn’t be moved. Before I’d had time to recover from the funeral, I
had to return to the High Court as scheduled.

The defendant had pushed her husband during an argument. He’d fallen
down the stairs and died. She had pleaded guilty to manslaughter and
accepted all the facts.

After the defense made its plea in mitigation, I stood. I only meant to
add one thing.

“He was already trying to walk away. Why did you have to keep pushing
him?”

“I’m guilty,” she said, looking down.

“If you’d just listened to him—”

“I’m guilty. I’m not making excuses.”

I stared at her. My chest felt unbearably tight.

“Did you ever think about the fact that he would never come home again—”

“Counsel.”

The judge’s voice cut me off.

Defense counsel was already standing. The junior prosecutor beside me
was watching me, too.

“The relevant facts have been established,” the judge said.

I lowered my head and sat down.

The hearing continued. I stayed on the same page of my file, never
turning it.

Afterward, my junior reached for the case papers.

“Please, take a few days off. You’re exhausted. We can handle things.”

I wanted to say I was fine.

In the end, I just let go of the file.

The second dream came a month later.

I was studying for my university exams.

Jayne sat beside me, keeping me company. He was already nodding off. I
told him to go home. He insisted he wasn’t tired.

In the dream, I went to the bathroom.

When I returned, he was taking apart the lid of my insulated cup,
fitting a little rubber ring into it.

That cup had been leaking for ages. I’d always thought it stopped
because I’d finally learned to screw the lid on properly.

When I woke, the lid of the cup I’d been using for years had been fixed.

The tools were at the far right corner of the dining table, the
screwdriver resting on a tissue.

That was how he always left them.

GIGI said Daddy had spent a long time fixing it the night before. He’d
told her not to touch the tiny pieces.

I checked the locks. I called my mother and asked if she’d been over.

She hadn’t.

I still thought I might have done it myself.

You can forget things when you’re that tired.

The third time, I dreamed about an argument I’d forgotten years ago.

We’d only just started dating. I was refusing to speak to him over
something trivial and gathering my things to leave.

He wrote an apology on a scrap of paper, folded it into a little shirt,
and slipped it into one of my shoes by the door.

When I bent down to put my shoes on, I saw a corner of paper sticking
out.

I pulled it out and couldn’t help laughing.

“Are my shoes your mailbox now?”

He stood beside me. When I finally spoke to him, he smiled too.

The little shirt had a corner missing from its left sleeve.

He’d folded it wrong the first time. After that, he kept making it the
same way, mistake and all.

It became our private agreement.

Whoever wanted to make up would leave a paper shirt in the other
person’s shoe. Whoever was ready to forgive would move it to the window.

When I woke up, there was a little paper shirt on the table.

No writing.

A corner missing from the left sleeve.

GIGI was eating cookies. I asked who had folded it.

“Daddy.”

“You saw him?”

She nodded.

“He made two. The first one wasn’t very good.”

There was another, badly folded piece of paper in the wastebasket.

I sat there for a long time.

That argument had happened before our daughter was born.

We’d never kept any of the shirts. I’d never told anyone about them, not
even my closest friend.

I started checking the calendar.

All three dreams had happened on nights with a full moon.

I remembered what GIGI had said.

“Daddy comes home when the moon is full.”

At the time, I’d thought she just missed him.

Now I was beginning to think she might be telling the truth.

I wanted to find out for myself.

On the next full moon, I asked my mother to keep GIGI overnight.

She assumed I needed some rest and didn’t ask questions.

I put the paper shirt in one of my husband’s shoes.

They were still by the door. I’d never been able to put them away.

Standing there, I felt ridiculous. By day I demanded evidence in court.
By night I was talking to an empty pair of shoes.

Finally, I said quietly, “If it’s you, you remember what to do.”

I hadn’t been drinking. I hadn’t taken sleeping pills.

I sat on the sofa and waited.

Eventually, I fell asleep.

In the dream, Jayne sat beside me. He was the age he’d been during our
marriage, wearing that old T-shirt with the stretched-out neck.

I asked why he wouldn’t speak.

He just looked at me.

When I woke up, it wasn’t light yet.

The paper shirt was gone from his shoe.

It was by the window.

I ran outside. The garden was empty.

But the rocking chair, which had been still, moved a little.

“Jayne?”

No answer.

I went closer and touched the back of the chair.

It felt warm. I didn’t know if that was just the warmth of my own hand.

That was when I finally cried.

I said so much. Whatever came into my head. I repeated some things.
Others I couldn’t get all the way through.

I told him the washing machine was broken and I didn’t know how to fix
it. That when GIGI had a fever in the middle of the night, I’d sat in
the car downstairs for ten minutes with my hands shaking. That his
clothes were still in the wardrobe. I hadn’t moved a single one.

Then I talked about the day of the accident.

We’d argued.

I’d just finished work and was too tired to talk. He called to ask about
dinner. I snapped at him for asking me about every little thing and told
him to stop calling.

Half an hour later, I regretted it.

I texted him to come and get me.

He never arrived.

The time the police later gave me was exactly when I’d been standing
downstairs, waiting for him.

“If I hadn’t asked you to come, it wouldn’t have happened.”

I knelt beside the chair.

“If I could go back, I wouldn’t be with you.”

The wind stopped.

“Then you wouldn’t have to die.”

The chair stopped moving too.

Before dawn, I heard a faint sound from the side of the house. Like
someone walking past.

I went after it.

There was water on the steps from the night before. I didn’t see it.

After my foot slipped, all I remember is the moon sliding out of sight.

When I woke up, I was in a hospital bed.

My mother sat beside me.

She was much younger.

She scolded me for not watching where I was going, even as she touched
my forehead and asked if it still hurt.

“Where’s GIGI?” I blurted.

“Who’s GIGI?”

I stared at her. Before I could answer, I noticed the date on the
television news.

I threw back the covers and rushed into the bathroom.

A teenage girl looked back at me from the mirror.

I froze, then leaned closer.

That face.

Wasn’t that me, years ago?

I touched my cheeks. My fingers were trembling.

My mother followed me in, saying I’d just had a fright and should get
back into bed. I stayed where I was, staring at the mirror.

How could I be back in the past?

If it really was 2003, Jayne was still alive.

But what about GIGI?

Where was my daughter?

That night, I sat in the hospital bed and wrote down everything I could
remember. My husband’s name. My daughter’s birthday. Our address. The
date of the accident.

When I wrote about the rabbit GIGI slept with, I cried until the paper
was soaked.

I didn’t know how to get back.

But perhaps if I could save her daddy first, everything would be all
right.

The solution seemed simple.

Don’t fall in love with him.

When I returned to school, I changed seats.

Jayne stopped in the aisle with his books in his arms. He saw me sitting
on the other side of the room and said nothing.

I avoided the activities we’d once done together. I took a different
route home. I refused the things he offered me.

He didn’t follow me around asking why.

When I made it clear I didn’t want him near me, he stepped back.

But sometimes I’d look up and catch him watching me.

That look hurt.

I knew it too well.

I’d seen it from the kitchen doorway. Outside the delivery room. In the
middle of the night when he tucked our daughter in.

For years, I kept avoiding him.

I’d thought that if we never dated, if we never married, there would be
less to lose.

Then one day I saw a blue shirt in a shop window and stood on the
pavement crying.

I knew I still loved him.

So I decided to find him someone else.

My closest friend was willing to have dinner with him. She thought Jayne
was a lovely person. I arranged several meetings and always asked what
they’d talked about.

Eventually, she refused to go again.

“You’re the one he likes.”

I said that wasn’t possible.

She looked at me. “Every time you ask about him, you look more miserable
than he does. What are you trying to do?”

I asked Jayne to meet me.

I was cruel that evening.

“Jayne, listen to me.”

“You can’t fall in love with me. Don’t even think about it.”

“You and I are never going to happen. Do you understand?”

He listened without interrupting.

I turned to leave.

Behind me, he asked, “Did GIGI tell you Daddy comes home when the moon
is full?”

I stopped.

People were walking past. Traffic was loud.

Suddenly, I couldn’t make out any of it.

I turned around.

The young Jayne stood where I’d left him, his eyes red.

“I never thought you’d come here.”

I asked him so many questions.

The blanket. The cup lid. The paper shirt.

The things I’d said in the garden.

He knew all of it.

At last, I touched his hand.

It was warm.

I held on tightly, afraid that if I let go, he’d become that funeral
photograph again: the blue shirt, that ridiculous grin.

He told me that after he died, he could go home on nights when the moon
was full.

GIGI could see him. When I was asleep, he could come closer and do a few
of the things he used to do for me.

The rest of the time, he came back here.

To our past.

He didn’t know why. He didn’t know how long he could stay.

“You knew who I was from the beginning?”

He nodded.

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

He looked down at our hands.

“You said you didn’t want to be with me.”

I wanted to argue. I couldn’t get the words out.

“I wanted to see,” he said, “if you’d really be happier without me.”

I hugged him.

He hugged me back.

After so long, I could finally hear him breathing beside my ear again.

I told him I only wanted him to live.

He said he’d chosen to come and get me that day.

He’d wanted to make up too.

“Asking me to come doesn’t mean you made that car hit me.”

Friends and relatives had said that to me before.

This was the first time I heard it.

We got together again.

I still studied law. I still did the work I wanted to do. He never asked
me to give it up, and I didn’t give everything up to prove I loved him.

But some things changed.

He stopped saying everything was fine when it wasn’t.

Once, when I canceled a date at the last minute, he told me he’d been
waiting downstairs for a long time. That it really did hurt.

I went back to see him.

When we argued, I stopped hiding in my work and waiting for him to come
and make things right.

Before we married, he folded another paper shirt.

I teased him for still not being able to get it even after all those
years.

He showed me carefully.

Leave a little corner missing from the left sleeve.

Give the right one an extra fold, like the floppy ear on GIGI’s rabbit.

At the mention of our daughter, we both went quiet.

Later, in this life, she came to us again.

The first time I held her, I cried so hard at the sight of that familiar
little face that a nurse thought something was wrong.

I knew there was another four-year-old GIGI waiting for me in the world
I’d left.

I couldn’t stay here forever.

But the days passed. There were meals to cook, a child to pick up, work
to do. We still got tired and impatient. We still argued over little
things.

Living those days once already didn’t make them matter any less.

One night, I came home late from work and found them both asleep at the
dining table.

Jayne still had the unfinished food labels in his hand.

I didn’t wake them.

I finished writing the dates, put the food in the fridge, and draped a
jacket over his shoulders.

Half asleep, he caught my hand.

“You’re home?”

I told him I was.

2026 arrived.

The closer we got to that date, the more frightened I became.

Once, I reached for Jayne’s arm. I was sure I’d touched him, but my
fingers closed on nothing.

I froze.

He turned and took my hand. His palm was still warm.

I told myself I was too anxious. I’d simply missed.

On the day of the accident, we didn’t go out.

We turned off our phones and put the car keys in a drawer.

GIGI drew pictures in the living room. He and I sat at the dining table.

I kept looking at the clock.

The time of the accident came and went.

He was still there.

Another hour passed. He asked if I was hungry.

I laughed through my tears.

That evening, for the first time, I told him we should get a wider
rocking chair when we were old. One big enough for us.

He said we would.

A few days later, my hand closed on nothing again.

He was sitting right beside me. I reached for his hand, and my fingers
landed on the armrest.

I tried again at once. His hand was solid in mine.

“What is it?” he asked.

I shook my head and said nothing.

Then, one night, moonlight fell across his hand, and I saw the grain of
the table.

Through his skin.

I switched on the light.

The wood grain disappeared.

His hand lay on the table as it always had.

He looked down at it too.

Neither of us spoke.

But it kept happening.

Sometimes, when he stood by the window, I could see the frame through
his shoulder. Then he would turn, and there would be nothing to see.

I started leaving the lights on all night.

Eventually, even the light couldn’t hide it.

He sat in front of me. I could just make out the back of the chair
through his clothes.

“Yan.”

I didn’t answer. I reached for his hand.

This time, I could only feel it if I held it very gently.

“I think this is as far as I can go with you.”

I shook my head and tightened my grip.

My fingers closed on air.

He was still sitting there, looking at me.

Slowly, I loosened my hand and tried again.

This time, I could feel him.

I didn’t dare squeeze.

The accident hadn’t happened.

But I finally understood that he still had to leave.

On the last night, he asked if we could sit in the garden.

We went outside together.

The moon was full.

He sat in the rocking chair and asked if I regretted it again.

I knew what he meant.

A long time ago, I’d knelt beside another chair and said that if I could
go back, I wouldn’t be with him.

I shook my head.

“I’d still choose you. Even if I had to do it all again.”

“Good,” he said.

I still cried.

Living those twenty-three years again hadn’t taught me how to let him
go.

But I no longer wished those years away.

He told me not to shoulder everything alone. To ask for help when I
needed it. He reminded me that GIGI could put on her own shoes now. I
didn’t need to crouch down and do it for her every time we were rushing
out.

He always remembered things like that.

I held his hand and said I would.

As dawn approached, his hand began to fade in mine.

I curled my fingers around it. There was only air.

His outline grew fainter.

Through him, I could see the back of the rocking chair.

Then the chair was empty.

I didn’t reach for him again.

I sat there until the sun came up.

The next time I opened my eyes, I was back in the hospital.

My mother was older again.

She held my hand and said I’d finally woken up. That I’d frightened her
terribly.

I asked where GIGI was.

At home, she said. Being looked after. Then she scolded me for still not
watching my step at my age.

I listened to her scolding and cried for a long time.

Twenty-three years hadn’t passed here.

When my daughter threw her arms around me, she was still four.

Back home, I found a little paper shirt by the window.

A corner was missing from the left sleeve.

The right had an extra fold, like the floppy ear of a rabbit.

It was the fold he’d taught me later.

I put it somewhere safe.

That night, I finally played the last voice message my husband had left
while he was alive.

It was only ten or so seconds long.

He asked how much longer I’d be. Said the pork chops were marinating.
Reminded me to eat them the next evening.

When it finished, I didn’t delete it.

GIGI and I still sit in the garden.

Sometimes she says Daddy came by. That he helped her find her rabbit, or
told her not to leave cookie crumbs in bed.

I don’t interrupt her anymore.

On the last full moon, she sat beside me, looked up at the sky, and said
it again.

“Daddy comes home when the moon is full.”

I pulled the blanket over her legs.

“Yes,” I said. “He’ll watch over us, just like the moon.”

She leaned against me and kept telling me about kindergarten.

After a while, she stopped and looked at the empty chair beside us.

“Daddy says you remembered the blanket this time.”

I didn’t turn around.

I looked down at my daughter and tucked her bare foot under the blanket
too.

Then she kept talking.

And I kept listening.
