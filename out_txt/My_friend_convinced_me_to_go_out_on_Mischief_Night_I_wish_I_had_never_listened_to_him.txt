The jack-o’-lantern didn’t last long in Bobby’s hands before it lay
smashed into an orange pulp on the pavement. Its demise didn’t satisfy
him for long; he still had plenty more chaos to unleash upon our sleepy
little town before sunrise. It was Mischief Night, after all, and he
intended to live it to its fullest.

I had known Bobby all my life, and I had never seen him act this way
before. He was usually a good kid: respectful in class, friendly to his
peers, did well in school. I was surprised when he suggested that we go
out for our first ever Mischief Night, but knowing him, I figured it
wouldn’t have amounted to much. I thought we’d T.P. a house or two,
maybe even throw an egg here and there, and be done with it. As far as I
was concerned, I’d be home in time to watch an hour or so of cartoon
Halloween specials before bed.

But I was wrong.

Looking back on it, I realize that it was probably Bobby’s daily
pressure to be a goody two-shoes that caused him to act out so much that
night. He had spent his whole life being a model child; a single night
of mischief and debauchery must have felt so cathartic to him. It’s a
shame what happened to him in the end. He definitely didn’t deserve his
fate. All he had wanted to do was blow off some steam.

Of course, I’m partially to blame for what happened that night. I was so
stunned by Bobby’s behavior that I felt unable to talk any sense into
him, and so I simply went along with his little tirade of terror, lest
he suddenly decide to start taking his aggression out on me. I wish I
had done something. I wish I had done anything.

“Come on,” Bobby said after sending the jack-o’-lantern to its doom.
“Let’s hit that one next.”

He pointed to a house farther up the street. It was a large, old
building; a worthy sentinel to stand watch over the street’s
bramble-ridden dead end.

“Don’t you think we should be getting home soon, Bobby?” I said,
speaking up for the first and last time that night. “We’ve got school in
the morning, and if I’m not home soon, my parents won’t let me go
trick-or-treating tomorrow.”

Bobby sneered at me. “Who wants to go trick-or-treating when this is so
much better?” he said. “And anyway, aren’t you having fun?”

“I am!” I said sheepishly.

“Then come on!” he said. “We’ll hit that house, and then we’ll go home.”

Bobby, deciding this to be the end of our discussion, took off up the
street before I could respond. I reluctantly followed after him, if only
to make sure he didn’t get himself into too much trouble.

I caught up to him as he stood in front of the house, staring at our
next mark. The house rested atop a slight hill, which leant to its
foreboding appearance. A bright full moon floated in the sky above the
house, cascading its light upon the thin walkway of stone stairs leading
up to the building’s wraparound porch. Bobby offered me only the
briefest glance of acknowledgement before continuing up the stairs.
Feeling as though I had no other choice, I hurriedly followed after him.

We were both panting lightly by the time we reached the top of the
stairs. Bobby allowed us a brief moment’s rest before he approached the
front door with me at his heels. The door was tall—taller than almost
any door I had ever seen—its top coming to a soft point that stared down
upon us with an air of equal parts elegance and defiance about it.
Resting on the porch in front of the door was a single jack-o’-lantern.
It was far more meticulously crafted than any of our other victims had
been that night, complete with careful, finely-dressed carvings that I
found impressive even at that young age.

Bobby, on the other hand, was not nearly as enamored with the
jack-o’-lantern as I was. He hardly gave it a glance before he
approached it, took it into his hands, and smashed it into an
unfortunate mush against the old wooden planks of the porch.

“Alright,” I said, “now can we please get out of—”

The front door began to groan in protest as it came open, disappearing
inward toward the newly revealed darkness of the house. From beyond this
threshold materialized the tall, thin frame of a man who at the time I
thought looked ancient, but likely wasn’t too much older than sixty. He
seemed to have been conjured by the shadows beyond the doorway, and
spoke with a powerful voice that demonstrated his undisputed dominion
over the realm of gothic darkness that was his home.

“Enjoy smashing jack-o’-lanterns, do you?” he said. His tone existed
somewhere between good-natured and absolutely furious.

“Yes, sir,” Bobby said, “I mean no, sir. Sorry, sir.” He had immediately
regressed to his normal self, as if the man’s presence had broken a
powerful spell that had been placed over him by the terrible influence
of Mischief Night.

“Yeah,” I added quickly, if only to make my own repentance known. “We’re
sorry, sir. We never should have come here.”

The man shook his head. “I suppose your parents would not be placed in a
merciful mood upon hearing of your misdeeds.”

Some persistent coals of Mischief Night seemed to stir back to life
within Bobby as a flash of defiance returned to his voice. “Well then
it’s a good thing you don’t know who we are, isn’t it?” He turned to
look at me. “Come on, let’s get out of here. There’s no way this old man
will be able to keep up with us if we run away now.”

The man immediately said both of our full names, along with our
addresses. His voice clapped like vengeful thunder in my ears. After a
few moments, I managed to conjure a few words. “How did you—”

“I have not lived here for this long without learning a thing or two
about my neighbors,” the man said. He smirked, and I once again
struggled to determine if he was amused or angry. “Regardless, I do not
think you two will be getting away as scot-free as you may have
originally believed.”

“Please don’t tell our parents, sir,” Bobby said, Mischief Night once
again losing its grip. “They’ll kill us if they find out what we’ve been
doing tonight.”

The man stroked his silvery beard while contemplating my companion’s
plea. “Very well,” he eventually said, “but you must complete a task for
me first.”

Bobby frowned. “What do you want us to do?”

The man stepped onto the porch, clearing the way into the house. The
sight of the unguarded threshold filled me with a sudden sense of dread,
as if the man had been the only barrier between us and whatever lived
beyond that sinister doorway.

“You must come inside and carve me a new jack-o’-lantern to replace the
one you destroyed. Only then will I promise not to inform your parents
of what you have done.”

Bobby and I shared an uncertain glance. After a few moments, I spoke. “I
don’t know…”

“You can always leave and return home if you so wish,” the man said,
“but your parents will know of your misdeeds before you arrive.”

“Alright, fine,” Bobby said sharply, trying desperately to maintain the
facade that had already been shattered into nothing. “Let’s just get
this over with.”

He stepped forward and walked past the man, disappearing into the house.
I once again followed after him. A sudden chill overtook my body as I
passed in front of the man, but I didn’t pay it much mind. I was too
focused on keeping up with my companion.

We found ourselves in a large, poorly lit foyer. Dim lamps adorned the
sections of the walls not occupied by paintings, sconces, and a series
of other decorations. A crimson rug runner lay sprawled from the
entrance and climbed up the stairs resting at the far end of the room.

“Please proceed past the stairs to the dining room,” the tall man said
from behind me.

Startled by his sudden words, I twisted around to see him standing in
front of the closed entrance. The outside world had officially been
stolen from us, and the only way we had left to go was forward.

“You shall find your project waiting for you on the table,” he
continued. “Once you have finished, you are free to leave.”

I turned around and followed Bobby past the stairs and into the dining
room.

The space was just as ornately decorated as the foyer had been. It was
slightly better lit than the previous room thanks to the large
chandelier suspended from the ceiling, which was studded with small,
flickering candles. An oblong wooden dining table was situated in the
room’s center, surrounded on all sides by articulately carved chairs of
the same material. Resting on the table in front of one of these chairs
was a single, almost picture perfect pumpkin. Several knives of varying
shapes and sizes lay lined up on either side of the squash, along with a
deep ceramic bowl.

The tall man followed us into the room. He pointed a long, bony finger
at Bobby. “You are the one who committed the sin,” he said, “so you
shall be the one to atone for it. Your ally in debauchery will watch as
you complete your task.”

“I don’t think I can carve a jack-o’-lantern as fancy as the one I
smashed earlier,” Bobby said.

“Most cannot,” the man said. “I will judge your work based on the amount
of effort you put into it, not based on the look of the finished
project. All I require is that you craft a jack-o’-lantern with a proper
face, including two eyes, a nose, and a mouth.”

“Sounds easy enough,” Bobby said. He took the seat in front of the
pumpkin, and I took the one next to him.

Bobby studied the tools of his craft for a few moments before selecting
a knife. He raised it toward the outer wall of the pumpkin.

“First you must open its top and remove all of its innards,” the tall
man said, now seated at the head of the table. “Surely a lad your age
knows how to carve a pumpkin.”

“Of course I do,” Bobby said, sounding offended by the implication. He
turned his attention to the top of the squash and began slicing a circle
around its stem. After the top was pulled free, he set to work on
removing the newly exposed medley of seeds and guts, which he placed
into the bowl with a series of wet, sloppy splats. When he was done, he
began carving the face, beginning with two triangle eyes, then moving on
to a nose of the same shape, and finishing with a jagged mouth. He
placed the chunks of displaced squash into the bowl with the discarded
innards, put the top of the pumpkin back into place, and then turned his
creation around to show it to our host.

“There,” Bobby said. “All finished. Are you happy now?”

The tall man scratched his bearded chin for a few moments before shaking
his head. “A quick glance through the thing’s mouth shows that you left
a seed inside. Your task is not complete until that error has been
corrected.”

Bobby frowned. “There’s no way,” he said before he turned the
jack-o’-lantern around and glanced in through its mouth. “I don’t see
anything.”

“Take off the top and look if you don’t believe me,” the man said.

Bobby attempted to pull the jack-o’-lantern’s lid back off in order to
investigate, but the thing would not budge. “It’s stuck.”

“Then you will have to go in through the mouth and feel around for it.”

“It’s just one seed, old man,” Bobby said. “You really can’t let it go?”

The man shook his head. “I am afraid that I cannot honor our deal until
the seed has been removed.”

Bobby sighed. “Fine. But after that we’re out of here.”

He gently slid his hand into the mouth of the jack-o’-lantern, careful
not to damage the structure of the opening. After feeling around for a
few minutes, his skin making a wet, squishing sound against the inner
walls of the pumpkin, he frowned. “I don’t feel anything.”

“Surely it is in there,” the man said. “Keep searching.”

A few more seconds passed. “I’m telling you I don’t—”

The jack-o’-lantern’s jagged mouth suddenly slammed shut, throwing up a
spray of Bobby’s blood as its teeth crushed his hand and tore through
his flesh. Bobby screamed; he tried to pull his hand free of the
squash’s hungry maw, but it was clamped too tight; he only managed to
take the thing with him as he stood up from his chair and staggered away
from the table. The pumpkin lurched forward and chomped down again,
eliciting fresh screams from Bobby as it decimated his wrist. Again it
moved, and again it chomped, continuing upward until it had climbed
beyond Bobby’s elbow. There was not enough space within the pumpkin’s
hollowed out cavity to contain that much of Bobby’s arm, and yet there
was no sign of the lower half of my companion’s ruined limb.

“It hurts!” Bobby wailed. “It hurts!” He turned to look at me with
pleading eyes as he flailed his arm all around, spraying blood on the
table and nearby chairs. “Help me!”

I was already on my feet and backing away from the horrific scene. Part
of me wanted to run to my friend’s aid, but another part of me—a much
louder part—shouted that I needed to run. It told me that I couldn’t do
anything to help him; that at this point all I could do was save myself.
And I listened.

As I turned to go, I quickly glanced at the tall man. He remained seated
in his chair, showing no intention of stopping my escape. He only
offered me a satisfied smirk as I rushed toward the exit, Bobby’s
horrific screams rattling in my ears all the while.

His voice suddenly became muffled as I passed through the threshold into
the foyer. Against my better judgment, I briefly turned back to look
into the dining room. I couldn’t see Bobby from the doorway, but the
room’s chandelier cast his shadow onto the wall directly opposite the
door. I call it his shadow, but by that point it certainly belonged far
more to the pumpkin than to Bobby. There wasn’t very much of my
companion left.

I didn’t turn back around again after that. I only ran. I ran through
the foyer, out the front door, down those narrow stairs, and all the way
back up the street. I kept going even as that wretched house disappeared
into the darkness behind me. I ran and I ran until my lungs began to
burn and my legs screamed for me to stop. I couldn’t even think about
slowing down until my home was within view.

I rushed down my driveway, but I stopped before reaching the house’s
entrance. Resting on the porch, right next to the front door, was the
single jack-o’-lantern that I had carved several days earlier, and which
I had paid little mind to since. Now it suddenly terrified me, and for
good reason.

Because from its gaping mouth came the horrible sound of Bobby’s eternal
screams.
