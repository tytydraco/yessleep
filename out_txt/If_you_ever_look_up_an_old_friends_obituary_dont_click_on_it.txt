It had been a year since Dennis’ service. You know that ride-or-die
childhood friend you had? Well, Dennis was mine. He and I were
inseparable, but when we got to middle school, Dennis’ parents’ divorce
hit him like a freight train, and we drifted apart almost immediately.

Dennis was still a good guy; we’d nod at each other in the hallways all
through high school until he got expelled. I went off to school out of
state, and besides the occasional Instagram post, I didn’t really think
about the guy.

That was until the drunk-driving accident and the phone call my mom
relayed to me one autumn night.

“The service is Wednesday, honey, if you can make it.”

“Yeah, Mom, I’ll see what I can do.” I’m not proud to admit it, but
after the initial shock, apathy took hold more than anything.

My girlfriend, Rita, had just moved in to start the semester, and
Tuesdays were one of our favorite date nights at Miguel’s for $2 tacos
and $8 margaritas. You really don’t know how good you have it until you
leave college. Needless to say, the 6-hour drive, a 12-hour round trip,
for someone I barely even knew anymore was not top of mind.

From what I heard, I missed a shitshow. Mom tried to talk it up, but
Anthony, my confidant on Thanksgiving Eve, was more vulgar.

“Dude, it was bad. Dennis’ mom and dad couldn’t keep it together, not
even for the service. Rich (Dennis’ dad) was doing everything he could
to show off his young piece of ass while Amy (Dennis’ mom) was still
reeling from a hangover. It was good to see some of the guys, but you
didn’t miss much.”

“Honey, why don’t you check on Dennis?” Mom had suggested to Rita and me
after Christmas Mass a month later, Rita making her first impression on
the rest of the family, while we visited my grandpa at the cemetery. I
did it begrudgingly; Dennis’ headstone was much newer than the rest, his
grass just starting to sprout.

I didn’t think about any of it much. Rita and I eventually split, her
taking the dog while she let me keep the couch. I sulked into
graduation, barely getting my degree before settling a few hours closer
to home, in a job I hoped was transitional at best, but the job market
was abysmal.

It was October before I knew it, a full four months post-grad, when, for
some reason, that day, the 3rd, made me think of Dennis. It had been a
year. I snooped on Facebook and found Dennis’ mom, Amy. She’d posted
that morning: “Missing my little boy extra hard today.” His Instagram
had been archived, and his contact on my phone, still sporting an email
from before he and I got phones and could only text on iMessage, was all
I had of him. I don’t know why, but I looked up his full name and
clicked on his obituary.

It didn’t load up right away, but soon I was met with a smiling picture
of Dennis at a skate park. I didn’t recognize anyone in the background;
again, we didn’t run with the same crew, but I sat there reading his
obituary.

Whoever wrote it must not have known Dennis very well. Generic was an
understatement.

After skimming over the year he graduated and what car he was driving
when he T-boned the tree, the site suddenly glitched. Pop-ups took over
my screen. I tried to click the Xs but they were too quick. They jumped
all over my screen with just Dennis’ smiling face, the only part of the
screen untouched. My computer started making the Windows error sound as
other tabs I had opened closed in the browser, and for a split second I
coulda sworn, maybe it was just my eyes, Dennis’ face turned ever so
slightly, and he seemed to stare right at me as my computer suddenly
went black.

My computer took a minute to start back up, and retracing my steps
revealed the website was completely gone. A Google search later, and I
was down a rabbit hole about how obituary sites are commonly targeted
for malware against unsuspecting elderly people. Nice. I had fallen for
the same tricks as a bunch of hapless boomers. Annoyed at myself, I put
Dennis out of my mind, but I made sure to download any anti-malware
software I could find and concluded that whatever it was did no
permanent damage to my computer.

The next day at work was uneventful. My boss rode my ass for some report
I had forgotten to do, and Carol, the only coworker my age who just
happened to also be an absolute bombshell, dropped the unfortunate
detail that her boyfriend from UW wasn’t just a college fling but
something really serious.

After work, I made my normal slop, ground chicken and rice, and rewarded
myself with some COD on my Xbox. I paused a round of Zombies to make
peace with my toilet, and when I returned, I heard the peaceful tunes of
Minecraft playing on the Xbox, start screen open. My first thought was
some kind of intruder.

I scanned the entire apartment, kitchen knife in hand. Shower curtain
pulled aside. Closet door yanked open. Even the pantry was checked. All
clear. I ended up back in front of the TV, picking up the controller as
if it were a piece of evidence. It was now split-screen with both
players on Creative. I didn’t even know you could play split-screen with
two players, but assumed it was some sort of Xbox glitch.

Looking back, it was completely asinine to chalk it up to some sort of
glitch, and part of me didn’t believe my own lie. I force-powered off my
Xbox and retreated back to my bedroom, doomscrolling on my phone until
it was time for bed again. I remembered the one hiding spot I’d missed
and begrudgingly checked under the bed. All clear. I went to bed that
night unnerved but still collected; the next night I wouldn’t be so
lucky.

After realizing Carol would be nothing but a fantasy, I kept my
distance. Bryson, probably the third-youngest person in the office after
Carol and me, invited me out for Thursday Night Football, and he and I
met at Buffalo Wild Wings after work. Even though he was on the younger
side in the office, Bryson was still old at 28 and left me at halftime
to go meet his ex-wife to pick up his son for his weekend time.

I finished my drink and had better luck than usual with the cute girl
behind the bar. I didn’t get her number or anything, but she smiled at
me longer than any of her other customers. When I got back to my
apartment, I was ready to open the door and collapse on the couch when I
stopped in my tracks after I heard a faint beep and humming noise on the
other side of my door. I felt my pulse throbbing in my hand as I pressed
it against the door. I grabbed my phone, flashlight on, as I pushed open
my door to find it dark and seemingly empty. In my living room, which
doubled as my TV room and office with my computer setup pushed against
one wall, something beeped to life.

I turned on the light and saw pieces of paper littering my floor. My
printer had been busy, and when I flipped over the pages that fluttered
on the floor, I felt my throat close up. It was Dennis. He was flipping
off the camera in one and posing on a ski lift in another. He had his
arm around a cute, shorter girl sporting a sleeve of tattoos and was
shirtless and posing on a beach in another.

I had seen these photos. They were on his archived Instagram, and my
printer had printed them over and over and over again.

I forced my printer to shut down, gathering the pages and throwing them
in the trash. The malware had clearly been more than harmless, and
whoever had hacked my computer was tormenting me with my old friend’s
death.

Maybe it was the alcohol, but that night I cried in bed. I thought of
Dennis and me, back in Cub Scouts, the same Little League team, even our
plans to be world-famous YouTubers. I didn’t necessarily miss him; I
didn’t even know him, but the tears fell nonetheless.

I was late to work the next day. Fridays were calm around the office,
and after avoiding my boss’ desk and doing my best to make it look like
I hadn’t just slipped in, an hour late, I made my way to the IT desk.
Tyson liked to keep to himself, but if you got him talking about coding,
or hardware, or whatever goes into a computer, he wouldn’t stop.

I told Tyson about what had been going on. How I went on a shady website
and my computer shut down on itself. Tyson chuckled to himself, very
much under the impression my website was some sort of adult website
rather than the obituary site I was afraid to mention. He asked me about
switching default passwords and updating firmware, and when I pleaded
ignorance, he theorized that some sort of hostile agent had taken
control of my home network. He advised me to unplug my computer and
printer from the socket, and he would stop over on Monday to fix it.

I thanked him profusely; house calls were well beyond his obligation as
a coworker, and I promised to buy him lunch. He waved me off, and I went
home that day at peace. Damn. I had forgotten to mention the Xbox glitch
too, but figured it was all related. The sun was setting when I got
home. My apartment parking lot was emptier than usual. I parked in my
assigned spot, #204, and walked up the two small flights of stairs to my
apartment door. I remember looking out, noticing the clouds, and
assuming it would rain soon.

When I opened the door, my computer screen was already on. Had I
forgotten to turn it off? The room smelled musky, like something foul
was inside, and I was on guard to be ambushed by the same imaginary
intruder from a few days ago.

I grabbed my phone, ready to call the police, when a video started
playing on my computer.

“Hey everyone! This is D-Fox and his boy, uh, K-Crew, and welcome to our
Minecraft let’s play series!”

My heart stopped. We never uploaded them, or at least I know I hadn’t.
Dennis must have uploaded it years ago without telling me. The screen
was on YouTube, the channel a faceless amalgamation of letters and
numbers for a username. The video was private, posted 13 years ago. You
would think the video would be showing our characters in the game, but
no. Instead, it was both me and Dennis, or at least our 9-year-old
versions, side by side on the tan shag carpet in his old house, our
massive laptops letting our eyes just peek out from the top.

The room was dark, our faces illuminated in blue by the laptops.

“Today we finally defeat the Ender Dragon!” Dennis said
enthusiastically. I started laughing in the video. “K-Crew’s excited…”
Dennis beamed at the camera, pushing his laptop down, revealing his
smile. But his mouth and teeth were too big for a 9-year-old. His mouth
took up half his face, almost like it was his adult teeth already. And
his smile. It was unnaturally big, uncanny. And there I was next to him,
still laughing, slamming my laptop down because I couldn’t stop
laughing. My face contorted. Red. Purple. I looked like I was in pain. I
tried to click it off. The mouse, it wouldn’t work. I was panicking. My
younger self, reaching out towards the camera, begging, beckoning, for
anything, for help. I tried to pause it. Nothing. I got down on my
knees, ready to unplug it, but it wasn’t right.

The printer, the computer, the lamp. All were already dangling,
unplugged. I didn’t think. I slammed my palm against my secondary
monitor, shattering it against the floor. The video stopped. I grabbed
for my phone. It wasn’t in my pocket, not on the desk. I’d had it in my
hand a second ago. Where?

Ring, ring, ring.

It was coming from the kitchen. Had I been in the kitchen already? I
wasn’t thinking about my safety; I should have run out of my apartment,
but I went for it.

Rita.

I hadn’t gotten around to changing her contact background, as I saw her
kissing my cheek for the first time in months on my phone screen.
“Rita?” I was confused.

“I don’t know what kind of sick game you’re playing, but you better stop
it before I press charges.”

“Rita, what are you talking about?”

“Don’t do that; this is what I was scared of when we broke up,” she said
between tears. “I tried to make it easy, but Becky told me you might be
a psycho!”

“Rita,” I repeated, taking a seat at my kitchen counter, “what the hell
are you talking about?”

“The box,” Rita tried to compose herself, “the one sent from your
hometown. Did you really think I wouldn’t know who sent it?”

I started to panic. “Rita, I didn’t send anything; something…terrifying
is happening, and I don’t want you to be involved. Did you already open
it?”

“Yeah. I don’t want pictures of you as a kid and your old jackets and
socks. I–I still might have feelings, but we can’t.” I sat in stunned
silence. “Pictures are weird, you in school, at the pool, you-.”

“Rita?”

Rita’s voice dropped an octave. “Why is there one of that kid’s
headstones?” I nearly jumped out of my skin when I heard the faint
humming and beeping of the printer in the other room. Every bone in my
body told me not to see what it was, but I couldn’t stop my feet.

“Rita, I think I’m….we are in danger.”

“What?” I heard a faint knocking from the other side of the phone.

“Rita, was that you?”

“The door.”

“Rita, don’t answer that.”

“Why am I in danger?” she pleaded. I got to the printer, paper already
on the floor.

It was a picture. She was blonde. It was grainy, so it was hard to tell,
but her outfit. I knew it. Her back was to the camera. She was standing
at a white door, with something I couldn’t quite make out pressed to her
ear.

A phone.

“Rita, they’re behind you!” I screamed before I heard Rita screaming in
terror. Then the phone call dropped.

I didn’t even know which police to call. I settled on a wellness check
where Rita lived, and an hour later, when Rita was reported missing, I’m
sure I was the prime suspect in her disappearance.

The police said the local force should be here any minute to take me in
for questioning and, God willing, protect me from whatever is going on.

But that was 16 hours ago, and I haven’t heard from them since. The sun
still hasn’t finished setting. For the first fourteen hours, I sat in
silence, kitchen knife at the ready, every piece of technology in my
house smashed to pieces, besides this phone I’m writing to you on now,
waiting to be whisked away by the police, yes, to certain arrest, but
also to safety.

It’s been too long. I know now they are not coming. For the last 2
hours, all I can hear is my tortured laughing from the other side of the
front door, as whatever is making it seems to be getting closer and
closer.
