Whenever I think of summer, I am most prominently reminded of the
oppressive August heat of the countryside, where my aunt and grandmother
lived. My cousin and I would go to stay with them over the summer, it
was a lush suburban area on a hill, by the seaside, surrounded by forest
and a couple other houses. We were free to explore and do as we pleased
with the other kids around, as long as we didn’t wander into the forest.
We took advantage of this freedom as much as we could, before we had to
go back to the smog covered storm of colour and sound in the city. This
part of the coast has always been known for its temperamental weather
and so sometimes we would need to find ways to amuse ourselves inside,
with board games and card games. My grandmother had one of those old TVs
with antennae in the living room, where she would watch drama series. In
front of her we would be sprawled out on the carpet with books, and
markers, and card houses, and drawings.

Those were my favourite days, the hum of the TV in the background, my
books in front of me, my cousin next to me writing something, our
grandmother sitting peacefully with the crossword on her lap,
occasionally following her show and keeping an eye on us.

It became harder to visit when my family moved away, suddenly we
couldn’t go every summer, my cousin was busy, so was my aunt, my
grandmother had to move somewhere closer to the city, because she needed
easier accessibility to healthcare. Free time was scarce and the effort
needed to go to the countryside grew largely absent. 

What happened the summer before I moved away however, will stay with me
forever. My cousin and I were both eleven years old, at the time, we
were both fairly well-behaved children. Although I think he preferred
his own company, back then, and I was unbearably hyperactive. I talked
too loud, jumped everywhere, bounced off the walls, and I suspect most
of my enthusiasm came off as obnoxiousness rather than excitement. I
understand now that I was probably kind of hard to put up with,
especially for someone as reserved as him.

It might be unsurprising to learn that we bickered regularly, I don’t
remember it ever becoming very serious, but they always left me in a
foul mood and he would storm upstairs into my aunts room for space. On
one such day, instead of sulking inside, under my grandmothers watchful
eye, I decided I would go for a walk to cool off.

Around ten minutes into my walk Scraps joined me, and I decided that we
would go for a stroll on the beach. Scraps was one of our neighbourhood
dogs, since we were far out from the city and surrounded by forest,
hyenas and foxes stealing food or raiding bins had been a problem. The
solution the people of the neighbourhood community came up with had been
to adopt street dogs. I’d known Scraps for ages, had fed him, played
with him, he was the largest of the three dogs we had. He’d often stand
guard in front of our house and ferociously bark at intruders and
unwelcome visitors from the forest, alike.

He was large and had sandy light fur that was always slightly mangy, he
strolled next to me as I loitered towards the seaside, through the old
path, covered with trampled shrubbery and down the worn stone steps. It
was a fairly nice afternoon but the beach was empty. I understood why,
when I payed closer attention. Despite the nice weather, I could see
dark storm clouds in the distance, turning the waves from small hills,
into valleys and mountains, highlighting the water with white, wild
foam.

Despite this, the claws of the ocean hadn’t yet reached the shore and
when Scraps excitedly dashed towards a stick, spraying sand everywhere,
I followed. We played fetch for a bit, ran around, he barked as I dipped
my toes into the icy, frigid waters. I then started heading towards the
rocky area of the beach, where I would go seashell hunting in the
shallow pools with some of my friends. I was slightly annoyed when I
realised Scraps wasn’t following, I called out to him, gestured, pointed
at the rock. I knew he liked playing in them, splashing around and
trying to catch crabs, but he wouldn’t budge. 

I think I made a noise of frustration, and said things along the lines
of “Why not?!”, “C’mon Scraps!”, “Here boy, get over here,” to which he
responded by plopping down and turning his face towards the horizon. I
looked with him and saw that the storm was much, much closer than it had
been. How long had it been? How long had we been on the beach? I was bad
at keeping track of time at the best of circumstances, but while
distracted things always got away from me, like water slipping through
cupped fingers. Scraps was looking at me again as if to say it’s time to
go, and I glared at him, just to be contrarian. 

“Fine, let’s go, then,” he followed me as I headed for the stairs. We
were halfway up the stairs when the wind picked up erratically, spraying
me with salt water, and I turned around to look at the ocean one more
time. At first, I didn’t quite understand what I was looking at.

It was as if the sea had expanded to engulf the sky, or some sort of
giant wave was heading towards the sore, so big I couldn’t see where it
ended, no matter how high up I tried to look. The wall of water shifted
and elongated as the wind continued to pick up. Scraps, was barking at
me from the top of the stairs, as I hung on to the railing to withstand
the gusts of harsh wind. I couldn’t look away, it felt like there was a
bubble in my chest, right on my sternum, making it hard to breathe, yet
filling my lungs with so much air I thought my chest would burst. I
couldn’t think of anything, my mind was completely empty as my eyes
tried to convey whatever was happening, to my brain, but it made no
sense.

I’ve reflected back to that moment many a time since, trying to pin down
what was happening or even what it was I was looking at, but my mind was
completely blank, I wasn’t thinking anything. In all honesty, there
aren’t any strong emotions of terror colouring the memory, despite the
total incomprehensibility of that scene. I remember feeling awed, as if
my spirit were separate from my body, and I could see from afar how tiny
I was, compared to this impossible body of water. It continued to change
and twist into strange shapes, causing the water on the shores to slosh
forwards, like water spilling over the brim of a bucket. 

I watched blankly and slack jawed as it started to expand, I didn’t know
how that was possible, it looked as if it was trying not to just cover
the sky, but to devour it. I bent my neck and looked up, but I couldn’t
see anything other than the water, there were a couple beams of light
filtering through. The ocean was shifting, writhing, stretching and
trying to become something. For a long time I didn’t have the words to
describe what I saw that day, even now the closest thing I’ve seen to
the shapes the ocean showed me are fossilised coral reefs. 

It was so mesmerising and so close, all I had to do was walk towards the
shore and I could take a closer look, understand what exactly it was I
was seeing. I took a step forward only to feel a sharp biting pain on my
calf. Scraps had bit me, I cried out in pain and the moment was over.
There was an immense sound, like the shattering of a thousand windows or
maybe the striking of a thunderbolt right next to me, I turned around
and it was gone. The sky was back in its place, the ocean was alive with
angry waves, and the storm had arrived. While I had been standing there,
the tide had risen so far that the waves were now yanking at my flip
flops. I cried out again as Scraps let go of my leg and started yanking
at my sun dress. The waves had reached my feet and the force of the tide
caught me off guard as I was trying to scramble up the stairs. I felt my
flip flops slip off but I didn’t even care. I ran all the way up the
path, thorns sticking into my feet, and then I was running on the stone
brick road, and then on the grass, where I tripped and slathered my
yellow, torn dress in sticky, tar-like mud. It started raining
ferociously, as if the sky had opened up, but once again, I didn’t
stop. 

I imagined I could feel the rain drops on my skin, each shifting into
little coral branches and trying to grab me, drag me down into the dirty
muddy water puddles. I don’t think I’ve ever ran as fast as I did then,
the way back was a thirty minute walk uphill, I ran it without stopping.
I got back and started banging on the door of the house, my grandmother
opened it. I was in an absolute state, crying, yelling, talking
incomprehensibly about the thing that ate the sky. The commotion must
have alerted everyone in the house and soon my aunt and cousin were
there, they got me inside, near the fireplace with a blanket and that’s
all I recall. I don’t remember anything else, until my parents came back
to pick me up next week. 

According to my aunt I was next to that fireplace for almost two days
until they could get me to say or eat anything. My cousin says I did
talk, but only once and only to him. Apparently I’d told him, in the
softest whisper imaginable, “It wanted the sky,” he’d asked me what I
meant but I hadn’t replied. My grandmother doesn’t really talk about
what happened, but I remember soon after, she thought me how to light a
fire, she was also the only one that could convince me to take a bath.

Nowadays I don’t need help with that sort of thing, but I don’t like
going to the seaside and I hate pools with a passion. Nothing like that
has ever happened to me since, but whenever I look into the surface of a
lake or the ocean for too long, I feel like I can see something  swaying
in the surface, like seaweed, swaying and dancing to an unknown rhythm,
calling me down, down into its dark endless horizon.
