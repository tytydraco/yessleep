MAURA:

She was the new sous chef at Nook Nook. Thirty-one, red-haired, freckled
across the bridge of her nose and down both forearms, with the lean
build and calloused hands of someone who’d been working kitchen lines
since she was old enough to hold a sauté pan. She had a tattoo of a
whisk on her left wrist and a scar on her right palm from a carving
knife slip she described with the same casual pride a soldier might use
for a bullet wound.

I didn’t really like her. Not at first. She was loud in the
kitchen—screamed at the line cooks, threw things when a plate came back,
once broke a cutting board over the prep counter because someone
mislabeled the mise en place. But her food was extraordinary. Even the
staff meals she threw together—the ones she made for the crew from
whatever was left over—were better than anything I’d ever eaten in a
restaurant. She had the thing that separates a good cook from a great
one: she could make you taste her intention.

And he loved her food more than anything I’d ever offered.

The first time Maura cooked for me—really cooked, a Sunday dinner at her
apartment, just the two of us, a braised short rib with horseradish
cream and roasted root vegetables—I brought a carton home and fed Him
and he went so still afterward that I thought, for one glorious hour,
that it was over. That He’d been satisfied so completely that whatever
mechanism animated Him had finally wound down. That I could put Him back
in the box and live a normal life.

He didn’t go quiet, of course. But the hour of silence was intoxicating.
Sixty minutes without the voice, without the constant low-frequency hum
of hunger, without Him scratching his voice in my dome with orders and
wants.  It felt new. It felt clean. It felt like pain though.

So I kept seeing Maura. Not because I loved her—I didn’t—but because she
was the only thing that kept Him quiet.

The cover story worked beautifully with her. On our third date, I told
her about “my process”—the puppet, the creative expression, the
therapist’s recommendation. I braced for the reaction Charlotte had
given me: confusion, concern, the slow withdrawal. Instead, Maura leaned
across the table and put her hand on mine.

“I think that’s really brave,” she said.

“Brave?”

“Most men would never. They’d just bury it. Drink it away. But you’re
actually sitting with your feelings. You’re—” She paused, choosing her
words. “You’re letting yourself be vulnerable. I admire that.”

I felt a flush of something—shame, maybe, or its cousin, the awareness
of being admired for a lie. But I also felt Him, in the bag at my feet,
radiating through the leather. He either liked her or just her food. It
didn’t matter. I liked her. Maura came back to my place that night. She
saw the puppet and instead of flinching, she walked over and studied his
face.

“He’s beautiful,” she said. “In a creepy way. Does he have a name?”

“I call him Paxton. It means Peace” I don’t know why I lied, but in some
ways it was true. He brought me peace. Of course that should be his
name.

“Paxton,” she repeated, and I swear—I swear—his glass eyes shifted a
fraction of an inch toward her voice. But the light was low and Maura
didn’t notice.

I told myself it was a shadow.

She stayed the night. In the morning she made breakfast—a shakshuka with
perfectly poached eggs and a sourdough toast with homemade ricotta—and I
saved a portion and fed him after she left, and the satisfaction that
came through was so intense I had to sit down.

“Keeps her,” He said.

The arrangement worked for four months. Maura moved in. She cooked every
night—elaborate, beautiful meals that she photographed for her Instagram
and that I dutifully praised before scraping a portion into a takeout
container and disappearing to “do my work with Paxton.” Maura didn’t
just tolerate it. She encouraged it. She’d even plate His portions
separately, with the same care she gave her regular dishes.

“How’s he doing tonight?” she’d ask, as a joke, the way you’d ask about
a pet or a child.

“He loved the branzino,” I’d say, and she’d beam. She thought I was
healing. She told her friends about me. 

“He’s the most emotionally honest man I’ve ever dated,” she’d say, and
her friends would look at me with a kind of awed respect, as if my
willingness to carry a puppet around and talk to it in private made me
some evolved species of man. But they only saw the surface. The
sensitive man. The brave man. The man who wasn’t afraid of his feelings.

But if they could see what happened at midnight—me on the couch, the
puppet on my hand, the jaw working, food disappearing into nothing, his
voice critiquing the seasoning—they’d have called the police. 

When Maura was off or sick or visiting her mother in Reno—those were the
bad nights. Without her food, He grew vicious. I’d come home with
takeout from whatever restaurant was open, and He’d reject it—jaw
clamped shut, the warmth that was once my everything intensifying into a
pain that radiated from my hand up through my arm and into my shoulder.
And sometimes it was a deep bone-ache that no amount of ibuprofen could
touch. 

On the worst nights, the pain became something else: a compulsion. My
right hand would move on its own, the fingers flexing and clenching,
reaching for things—knives, glasses, the edges of countertops—with a
force I couldn’t override. I’d grab my own wrist with my left hand and
hold it still, and the struggle would last minutes, my body at war with
itself. My mind had waved the white flag months ago, so now it was a new
war. A war with my mind, but also Him in my mind.

“Hungry. Hungry. Hungry.”

I would fantasize about doing it. Just a kitchen knife and cutting
board. I’d send the knife away to one of those mail away sharpening
companies. Then I’d drink. Drink hard. Open the like new knife. And yeah
you get it. I would think about. Cleaving off your limb is not easy, but
like anything, you think about it enough you see the way. 

This happened a dozen times—the nights without Maura, the pain, the
involuntary movements—and I knew then, with the clarity of a man who has
run out of options, that I needed Maura in my life permanently. Not for
love. For survival.

He agreed.

And Maura—god help her—thought she was dating the most emotionally
available man in Las Vegas.

THE VOICE:

It happened in stages. First the food critiques, delivered in that dry,
accented whisper while I fed him on the couch.  Then, he had opinions
about Maura.

“Hates me,” he said one night. “It’s her flaw. Tell her stop hating.”

“I’m not going to tell my girlfriend that my puppet thinks she hates
him.”

“Then I wills.”

He didn’t. Not then. But the threat was there—always there—and I
understood it for what it was: a reminder that He could speak without
me, that my hand inside Him was a courtesy, not a requirement. I’d seen
the jaw move on its own, the glass eyes track across a room when no one
was touching him. He tolerated the arrangement because it suited him,
not because he needed it.

“You know what you are?” I asked him one night, both of us on the couch,
the TV muted on a Celtics game.

“What I am is hungry. Gets the crab cakes.”

“Are you—alive? How are you…you?”

“I’m Paxton. Gets the cakes.”

A searing pain ran up my arm. Like a hot coal on my nerve, but grinding.
I fell to the floor. I looked up at his bent neck on the couch, staring
down at me. His smile seemed to grow wider.

I got the crab cakes.

SCORSESE:

Maura found out on a Thursday.

I’d gotten careless. The feeding schedule had become so routine that I’d
stopped closing the bedroom door. Maura was always asleep by eleven, and
the feedings happened at midnight, and she’d never once woken up during
one. But that Thursday she’d had a stomach bug and was up at
twelve-thirty, shuffling toward the kitchen for ginger ale, and she
heard us.

Not the feeding. The conversation.

We’d been arguing. It had become the usual. I wasn’t taking his shit. He
wasn’t taking mine. We co-existed though out of necessity. She walked
in. It was a man sitting on a couch at midnight, arguing with a puppet
and the puppet was talking back.

“Steve.”

I turned. She was backlit by the hall light, her face in shadow, one
hand on the wall.

“Is it—” She stopped. Swallowed. “Is it talking?”

“Maura—”

“I can hear it, Steve. I can hear the voice. It’s not yours. It’s—that’s
not you.”

On my hand, I felt him go still. Then, his head turned toward her. On
its own.

“Privacy please.”

She didn’t scream when he said it. I’ll give her that. She just stood
there, her face cycling through expressions so fast it was like watching
someone flip through channels: confusion, horror, disbelief, more
confusion, and then—worst of all—understanding. The kind of
understanding that comes when a hundred small mysteries suddenly resolve
into a single, enormous, terrible truth.

The sensitive man. The brave man. The man who wasn’t afraid of his
feelings. All of it—a show. A shit-show, really.

“Steve,” she said. Very calm. Very quiet. “What the fuck is going on??”

“You talk to me. Not him.” He said.

She turned and rushed out. She came back three days later. I don’t know
why—maybe the same  reason people in haunted houses, the need to
understand, to confirm that reality is as broken as it appeared. She
asked me to show her. I did. She sat on the armchair across from me
while I put my hand inside him and let her hear him speak. 

He was charming—warm, funny, self-deprecating in a way I’d never heard
from him. He complimented her cooking. He asked about her mother. He
told a joke about a rabbi and a sous chef that actually made her laugh,
and the laugh horrified her more than the talking had. She took the
puppet off my hand. Held it tenderly. Inspecting.  Then—

“How long?” she asked.

“Almost two years.”

“Is that why Charlotte left?”

“Yes.”

She looked at the puppet on my hand. His glass eyes looked back.

“What does it want?”

“It’s a he.” I said. “Call him Paxton.”

“Ok…what does Paxton want?”

“Food,” I said. “That’s all. He just wants to be fed.”

This was a lie. I knew it was a lie. He knew I knew. But Maura needed
something she could understand, something manageable, something that fit
into the framework of a world where puppets don’t talk and men don’t
fall apart. Food she could do. Food was her language.

She lasted two more days. She cooked for both of us—knowingly now—and
there was a cold stillness in between the words spoken. The intimacy
between us was gone. She couldn’t look at me without looking at Him, and
she couldn’t look at Him without something in her face collapsing. She
said good-bye and that’s when it first happened-

“Take him off, for fucks sake.” She wanted to hug goodbye, but made a
point of me doing it without being inside Him. My hand tightened,
unconsciously.

“It’s fine. Come here.”

I went in for a hug and she pushed me back.

“I’m serious. Just take that thing off. It skeeves me out.”

I pause. Look down at Him. He’s shaking his head. I obey her wish. Just
for s second I tell myself. I hug her when—

“OWWW!”

She rips away from me. Holding her back.

“It bit me! It fucking bit me!”

Then she left. For real this time. Slamming the door. She took her
knives—the good ones, the Japanese steel she’d brought from Reno—and her
cast-iron skillet and her box of spices, and she drove away in her Civic
with the broken taillight.

“Good riddance,” He said.

“You had to bite her?”

“We done’s with hers. We’s find better.”

But we didn’t find better. Not right away. And in the week that
followed—seven days without Maura’s cooking, seven days of me trying to
substitute with takeout from lesser restaurants—he grew savage. He
demanded I keep my hand inside him at all times, and when I refused—when
I physically held my right wrist with my left hand and said “No”—my arm
went numb from the elbow down, dead as a piece of wood, and stayed that
way for six hours.

He was taking control. 

Soon it spread throughout my whole body. He was able to control it all.
Not suggesting. Not persuading. Controlling. My hand, my arm, my legs,
my back, the muscles in my forearm and bicep, all of it becoming less
mine and more his with every passing day. I caught myself walking to the
kitchen one night with no memory of deciding to get up. I caught myself
dialing a restaurant on my phone with my right hand while my left hand
gripped the couch and tried to stop it. The hand that held him was no
longer fully my hand.

And then Maura called. Not me. Her friend. The friend married to a cop.
And she told her friend everything.

I saw the cruiser pull into the parking lot on a Wednesday afternoon,
and something inside me—the part that was still Steve, still rational,
still capable of self-preservation—knew that this was a line being
drawn.

He knew it too.

“She has to go,” he said. Quietly. Almost sadly. Like a doctor
delivering a diagnosis.

“No.”

“She’s going to tell someone who believes her. And then they’ll come for
both of us. And you’ll never feel this again.” My hand warmed. I could
feel the click of connection, the rightness, the wholeness.

“Well. Do you want to go back to being empty?”

The cop knocked on the door, I’d answered—Paxton tucked in the closet,
my hand shaking at my side—and I’d been calm and clean and articulate.
No problems. No domestic issues. Maura and I had broken up amicably. The
cop nodded and was going to leave but then asked—

“Can I see him?”

Fuck. She told him. I had no choice. But He knew better than to attack a
cop. He’d play dead, the cop would shame me, and I could go back to
Ramen feeding the wooden doll who owned me.

“Sure, hang on.”

I handed him Paxton. He looked at Him with such derision it burned
bright. Turning over Paxton like a perv. He went to put his hand in and
I stopped him-

“Don’t.”

“Why?”

“He doesn’t..”

I stopped myself. I just went to take it back, but the cop held firm. 

“Let go, sir.” He commanded. I refused. That’s when the gravity I
experienced for so long stretched its legs in a different way. Paxton
sprung upright in his hands. Smiling. He loafed over, inconspicuously
onto the forearms of the cop.

“Holy shit is this an RC or what?”

Then he screamed. Not a scream of stepping on a stone. But a quiet steam
being released and a deep howl of pain kind of yell. The cop buckled.
Blood pooled fast on my entry rug. His wrists slopped down onto the
ground. His other hand was burger meat in seconds too.

Paxton gnawed on his stumps for long after. But first I shut the door
and put Paxton on his throat. I’m not a fan of police and authority
figures as a default setting, but I’m not a monster. Paxton would open
up his throat like an open-faced sandwich and hopefully put him out of
his misery quickly. 

As I dragged his body in and closed the door, in the deepest and most
exhausted part of myself, the truth of what was coming creeped in.

Maura had to go too. He was so right.

Mob movies are usually full of shit. Scorsese’s films are operatic in
their violence, and only because the worlds are so fully realized that
the murders feel like inevitable crescendos. But you don’t actually
watch someone get killed and think, Yeah, that’s probably how it
happens. Because it’s not. It’s uglier and smaller and more
administrative than any movie prepares you for.

He knew how. Of course he did. He’d been thinking about it since the day
Maura heard us in the shower, running scenarios the way a chess engine
runs permutations—calculating, optimizing, eliminating variables. I was
just the body. The vehicle. The hands.

“The kitchen,” he said. “Lots of things to use in the kitchen.” Maura
came over when I called. She was curious but also giddy to see what the
cop did to me. Boyo, was she wrong.

I won’t describe what happened in Maura’s apartment. I owe her that
much. I’ll say only that he directed my right hand with a precision that
terrified me more than the act itself—here, now, this angle, harder—and
that my left hand, my own hand, tried to stop it, clawing at my right
wrist, pulling, fighting, a civil war waged within me. But his hand was
stronger. He is strength. He is all my strength now.

When it was over, I sat on Maura’s kitchen floor with my back against
the stove and my right hand still inside him. He was quiet like the
first day I met Him. Then I vomited on the tile. I crawled to the
bathroom. Inside I saw the shower curtain with the blue anchors. The
drive out to I-15. The caliche and the shovel and the bats and the moon
he’d planned for.

And then Vegas, and the fight, and our bet. Him and me. The only us
there’d ever be again.

CHARLOTTE:

We met at the piano bar. It was always our favorite.

New York New York was just as glitzy as the other casinos, but the piano
bar really did feel like a real New York bar—dark wood, low lighting, a
guy in a vest playing Billy Joel with more soul than Billy Joel.
Charlotte and I had spent a dozen nights there during better times,
drinking manhattans and pretending Vegas was Manhattan and that we were
the kind of couple who’d last forever.

I’d left Paxton in the car. This was our new deal—our bet. Jake Paul’s
fight. I’d picked the underdog and won, and his end of the bargain was
silence. One night without. One night of being just me. The last meal
would hold him.

She wore red. Her hair was shorter.

“I don’t know why I even answered your call,” she said. “Let alone
agreed to meet you.”

“Hey, Char.”

“What do you want?”

“I’m sorry about how things turned out. I miss you.”

Her eyes betrayed her posture. Moisture built around her irises and she
sighed—a long, deep exhalation that carried years of accumulated grief.
I reached for her hand. She pulled it away.

“I should go. You look good. I need to go.”

“You were right. I was wrong. I want you to know that I know that now.”

“It’s too late for that. And it’s not about right and wrong. It’s way
more than that.”

She got up. I was running out of time. I needed Charlotte. Not just
because I missed her—though I did, achingly, the way you miss a version
of yourself that no longer exists—but because without Maura, without her
food, he was starving. But so was I. I needed help. And a starving Him
meant a dangerous him, and a dangerous him meant someone else would get
hurt. Charlotte had access to restaurants. Charlotte had taste.
Charlotte, in his calculus, was a supply line.

But I needed this to be real. I needed one thing in my life to be what
it appeared to be.

“Let’s go for a walk,” I said. “Just walk with me.”

Charlotte paused. We looked at each other for a long time—long enough
for the piano player to finish one song and start another.

“Okay,” she said. “I’d like that.”

We walked from one end of the Strip to the other. The past was covered
at length but never in detail—both of us skating over the worst of it,
acknowledging the wreckage without examining the individual pieces. The
future was the main course. Could we try again? Did she want to? Did I?
What would be different this time?

“The puppet,” she said, halfway across the Bellagio bridge. “Do you
still have it?”

I hesitated. On the Strip below us, a fountain erupted in synchronized
jets, the water catching the light from a thousand hotel windows.

“Yes.”

“And the—the talking to it. The feeding. All of it.”

“Yes.”

She was quiet for a long time.

“I don’t understand what you have with that thing. I probably never
will. But I understand that it’s real for you, and I’m tired of
pretending I don’t feel for you still. I’m…drawn to you.”

I could have cried. I almost did. Because she meant it—I could hear it
in her voice, see it in the set of her shoulders—and because she was
wrong, so utterly and completely wrong, and I couldn’t tell her. She
thought I was a man with an unusual coping mechanism. She didn’t know I
was a man with a leash around his wrist, a dead cop at home in the
closet, and a murder in the desert and a puppet in the car whose glass
eyes were probably watching us through the windshield right now.

Our clothes flew off the moment we got back to the apartment. I prayed
she wouldn’t go into the living room and see Paxton or he’d say
something mean and end such a magical night. I felt me for a moment.

I laid Charlotte down on the bed and drew the blinds closed. The room
went dark except for a sliver of neon from the Strip, painting one wall
in alternating pink and gold.

We were aroused immediately. There were no cobwebs, no dusty habits. We
knew each other’s bodies the way you know a house you grew up in—every
corner, every creaky floorboard. It went on like clockwork, the old
rhythms returning as if the two years apart had been two days.

Until she shrieked.

“What the FUCK!”

“What? What’s wrong?”

“Something BIT me!”

Charlotte was wailing, both hands clamped on her upper thigh, blood
seeping between her fingers. I scrambled to turn on the light. In the
brightness I could see it—a half-dollar-sized wound on her inner thigh,
the skin torn in a ragged circle. And next to the wound—or rather, where
it used to be—a smooth, pink patch of new skin.

The mole was gone.

And at the foot of the bed, on the carpet where I had not placed him,
where I had not carried him, where he had no physical means of arriving,
sat Paxton. Upright. His jaw open. His carved teeth smeared with
something dark. His glass eyes fixed on Charlotte with an expression
that, in the low light, looked almost tender.

“Oh Jesus. Oh Jesus. What did you do?” I screamed at him.

Charlotte looked at me and then at the puppet and then at the blood on
her hands.

”It’s my mole,” she gasped. “Something bit my mole right off.”

The blinds rattled as the desert wind came through the cracked window.
We didn’t speak for the rest of the night. She slept eventually—or
passed out. I sat in the corner chair and stared at Paxton on the
carpet, his jaw now closed, his glass eyes reflecting the neon from
outside.

I stared at Him. His face gave nothing away. It never did.

In the morning Charlotte left in a hurry. She was shaking, barely making
eye contact, gathering her clothes from the floor with the frantic
energy of someone fleeing a crime scene. The door slammed behind her.

From the living room, his voice:

“Boyo. Hungry hungry hungry.”

I had no words. The love of my life had walked out the door and I was
alone again—alone with him, alone with whatever was left of me, alone in
an apartment that smelled like blood and sex and the particular
emptiness I’ve come to know.

I went to the fridge and poured a glass of milk. Chugged it straight
from the carton.

“Boyo. Boyo. Boyo.” His chaffing growl of a voice uttered.

I stood at the counter, milk running down my chin. Rage boiled from
within. I was gonna do it. I was going to. Kill him. Or myself. Just…to
end it. End it all. then—

“Come back in. You’ll feel better.”

And the worst part—the worst part of all of it—was that he was right. I
would feel better. My hand was already moving toward him before I
finished the thought. Charlotte called three days later. I almost didn’t
answer.

“The wound healed,” she said. Her voice was flat, clinical. “Completely.
There’s no scar. The mole is gone and it looks like it was never there.”

“Char, I—”

“And I had the strangest dream last night.”

I waited.

“I dreamed I was hungry,” she said. “Not regular hungry. Hungry like
I’ve never been before. And everything I tried to eat tasted like
nothing. Cardboard. Paper. Nothing. And then I went to this restaurant—a
tiny place, no sign, no menu—and they brought me a plate of something I
couldn’t identify. And when I tasted it, Steve—” She paused. “It was
like hearing music for the first time.”

My hand tightened around the phone. On the shelf across the room, Paxton
sat in his usual spot. But something was different. His jaw was slightly
open. His glass eyes were bright—brighter than I’d ever seen them.

“And when I woke up,” Charlotte continued, “I went to this antique shop
on Fremont. There was something in the window.” She hesitated. 

“A puppet. A little one, in a blue dress. And when I picked it up and
put my hand inside—Steve, I can’t explain it. It felt—”

The line was quiet for a long time.

“Yeah,” she whispered. “Like that. Quiet. And warm…Is that like yours?”

Across the room, Paxton’s jaw opened wider than I’d ever seen it. Not to
eat. Not to speak. 

To smile.

And from the phone, very faintly, I heard another sound—a second voice,
higher than his, softer, coming from somewhere near Charlotte’s end of
the line. 

A voice that said, in a tone I recognized instantly:

“Hungry.”

I looked at Paxton. He looked at me. His glass eyes were warm.

“See?” he said. 

I closed my eyes. My hand began to shake.

“Char,” I said. “We need to talk. And I need—”

She cut me off—

“We all do. Just the four of us. Are you free for lunch?”
