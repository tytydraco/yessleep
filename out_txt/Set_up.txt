Ever since I was little, my dad always liked scaring me and my little
brother. All of my earliest memories with my dad were of the stories he
would tell me. Unfortunately, he had the habit of telling them at the
worst possible moment, which was often a point of contention with my
mom. In fact, I’m writing this after coming back from a family camping
trip in which a story of his caused us all to sleep in shifts, but
that’s a story of it’s own. One of the more famous stories in my family
is one from when I was no older than 5 years, one my cousins would
torment me with endlessly when I was younger. I’ll do my best to retell
it, but this is my first time writing and my uncle Joe is a much better
storyteller than me.

It was bed time, mom and I had just finished our nightly routine, I held
her hand as she walked me to my room. She started to tuck me in when I
started putting up a fight, I don’t remember why, it doesn’t matter I
guess, but at that point dad walked in.

“What’s going on here little Missy?” He said in a boisterous and jolly
tone.

“I don’t wanna sleep daddy,” I protested with a giggle.

“Well that just won’t do now would it lil’ missy?” He responded, bending
down to my level as his voice changed more to that of a deep southern
accent. My dad always loved doing voices, whenever we asked him about it
he would always say he just found them fun. It was probably the same
reason he learned so many different languages. He often joked that my
mom was the smarter one, seeing as she had the college degree, but we
all knew he was a few IQ points away from genius. Both of my parents
knew English and Spanish, seeing as they grew up in California, and they
met while learning ASL. However, while my mom stopped after that and
became an interpreter, my dad kept going.

“Don’t get her riled up now honey, I was just about to tuck her in,” my
mom protested as my dad proceeded to pick me up and do exactly that.

He swung me about and slung me over his shoulder, burying his face in my
rib cage and tickling me. I pushed against him and laughed much to my
mother’s dismay.

“Ya basta! She needs to sleep, she has school tomorrow hon,” she
protested.

My father relented, placing me back under the covers and kissing my
forehead. “Simma’ down now, we on’ wanna get a Peeker now do we?” He
said with a playful southern tone.

“MARK!” My mom yelled at my dad, obviously reaching her breaking point.

“What’s a Peeker?” I asked, curious, partially out of rebellion towards
my mother.

At this, my mom groaned and covered her face with her hand. “This is
exactly what I didn’t want,” she chastised my father.

“I’ll tell you some other time baby,” he told me in response, attempting
to correct his mistake.

“Noooo,” I whined, “daddy please, I’ll go mimiz if you pleeease tell me
please.” I gave him a quivering lip and doll eyes, at which point he
hung his head, then turned to face my mom with a sigh. She rolled her
eyes, threw her hands up in frustration, and left the room.

Dad looked in my direction, but not at me. He focused his eyes out the
window behind me, a weird smile on his face. He bared his teeth and bit
his lip, his eyes focused like lasers for one second longer before they
snapped like chicken bones. They focused on me now, a dark brown pool of
muddied dread.

“Peekers,” he began, “aren’t like any other monster you can think of.”
His voice was back to usual, albeit a little more weary. His face was as
while explaining why it was wrong for me to push my brother into the
pool last month. He was going to terrify me, but it was all too funny
for him to keep a straight face. This was not malicious, however, my
father was not a sadist. Rather, he was, like many fathers, a man who
didn’t know when to end the joke.

“All the other monsters,” he continued, “chase you or eat you or hide in
the dark waiting for you to slip up, catch you off guard. But Peekers?
Peekers are smarter, they wait till you haven’t slept. They wait until
you’ve seen something scary. They want you so sleepy you can barely
stand, so spooked you’re shaking. They want you weak so that they can
swoop in and drag you away without any trouble.”

He paused, letting me soak in what he just said, the wind blew and bent
the trees. It seemed to push against the very foundations of our house,
increasing the pressure in the room as he continued.

“Then, and only then, will they get bold enough to show themselves.
They’ll paralyze you - that means you can’t move - and then, finally,
come out of hiding to get you. They will only do that when they know
they can get you. Other than that, best you’ll get is a quick glance out
of the corner of your eye.”

By this point I was already terrified, holding my blanket a little
tighter against myself. “What do they look like?” I quivered out,
unaware of what horrors would come next.

“Depends,” he responded, “they will always pick the best form for the
job, never human, always humanoid.”

“Humanoid?” Please keep in mind I was like five, my vocabulary wasn’t
great.

“It means that it looks kinda like a person, but not enough where it IS
a person.” I nodded as if I understood, in truth I hadn’t any idea what
those words meant. “So it has a head, two arms, two legs, all the stuff
that makes it look human, but it isn’t. It could have really long arms,
or stumpy legs like tree trunks. It could crawl on fours with an extra
long spine, snapping and cracking and popping. It could look like a
shadow or just a face or even like…” he turned his head slowly back
towards the window. His eyes widened, the moment lasted ages, and yet
only a second or two. He raised his finger to point out the window as a
look of fear was cast upon his face. “Like that.”

I spun my head to face the window, my eyes frantically scanned the
darkness beyond the glass. I turned back to face my dad, and his face
was so close, our noses were practically touching. He had a wide-eyed,
creepy grin on his face, and was breathing abnormally.

Before this next bit, I remind you again that I was five years old.

I screamed, I cried, and worst of all, I wet the bed. My dad was
cackling laughing, up until that last part. My mom ran into the room,
panicked, until she saw us.

“AY Mark,” she walked to scoop me up, smacking my dad on the back of the
head as she passed. “Por eso te digo que no la asustes. She’s like me,
pobrecita can’t handle scary stuff.” She rocked me up and down, patting
my head and shushing - until she felt my soaked onesie.

“Mark, are you kidding me?” She groaned. My dad had already started
changing the sheets and barely turned to face her as he meekly
responded.

“I know, I know, I’m on it already.”

Usually, that’s about where the story ends in my family. My uncle will
say that, rag on my dad, and laugh as he shakes his head. Maybe my
cousin will say it to poke fun at me wetting the bed. My brother will
use it to call me a “scaredy cat” despite the fact he wasn’t even there
when it happened, or old enough to remember it if he was. Unfortunately,
that’s not where the story ends for me.

That night, and many others since, I held my stuffed rabbit extra tight,
seeking comfort. Despite the fact that I slept between my parents, I
could not sleep. I would see shadowed faces staring at me from the
window, waiting for me to sleep. Faint white dots bore holes into me
from dark vents above us. Those same fingers would wrap around corners,
or over the foot of the bed. I could hear it behind me, breathing
through a gnarled set of sharp, angled teeth. Its maw would open and
snap its jaws around my skull.

A blink of the eye, a shift in my vision, and it was gone. The lights
would come on, the shadows would disappear, the breath on my neck would
stop.

When I was younger, I would pray to God that He may guard me from this
evil. I would beg Him to make the monsters go away. I was small, and
stupid. For all His power, His supposed goodness, He would not
intervene. No crucifix to ward away the devil, no nightlight to cast
away monsters.

I grew up with this fear, this monster, following me wherever I go,
never able to rest. Everyone saw me as uptight, skittish, paranoid even,
but I knew better. I knew that the second I let my guard down, the bear
trap would snap around my neck.

How easy would it have been to tell someone, anyone, and maybe they
could help me. Surely, someone might believe me, somebody who cares and
loves me might save me from this. So I did, I told the person I trusted
to love me more than anyone else. Needless to say, my mom was not
thrilled to hear this.
