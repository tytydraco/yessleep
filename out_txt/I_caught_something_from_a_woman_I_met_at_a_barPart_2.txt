I said I’d update when I could, so here I am. It’s Sunday morning now, a
little over twenty-four hours since I hit post. I haven’t really had a
chance to sit down and process any of this until now. Most of yesterday
was spent being moved between rooms, having blood drawn, answering the
same questions over and over, and watching increasingly concerned people
pretend they weren’t concerned.

The paramedics arrived maybe a minute after I posted. I heard them
before they knocked, which still bothers me. There were two of them, a
man who looked like he was probably in his fifties and a younger woman
carrying most of the equipment. The older guy was talking to the
dispatcher when they came through the door, and I remember being able to
hear both of their hearts before I could make out what they were saying.
The woman put a monitor on me while the other one asked questions. When
the leads were attached, the machine immediately started making noise.
She checked them, moved one, replaced another, then pulled everything
off and started again. I asked what was wrong, and she told me one of
the leads probably wasn’t making good contact. The second attempt didn’t
work either.

The older paramedic checked my wrist, then my neck, exactly like I’d
been doing before they arrived. He didn’t say anything at first, but I
could hear his heartbeat speed up. That’s becoming something I’ve
noticed about people now. They can keep their faces completely neutral,
but apparently nobody teaches your heart how to lie.

They took me downstairs on a stretcher even though I kept telling them I
could walk. The morning sun had started coming through the windows in
the stairwell, and the little bit that touched my face brought that
pins-and-needles feeling back almost immediately. It wasn’t worse than
the day before, but one of the paramedics noticed me turning away from
it and pulled the sheet a little higher over my shoulders. The ambulance
ride was mostly more of the same. They tried an automatic blood pressure
cuff three times before switching to a manual one. I don’t know what the
final reading was because neither of them told me. The younger paramedic
kept looking from me to the monitor like she was trying to decide which
one was lying to her.

At one point, she told me to breathe normally, and that’s when I
realized I hadn’t been. I don’t mean I was holding my breath. I wasn’t
breathing. I had been lying there listening to them talk, and at some
point my body had apparently stopped doing it automatically. I didn’t
feel short of breath. My chest wasn’t tight, and no panic told me I
needed air. Once I noticed, I inhaled and then deliberately waited.
Thirty seconds went by, then forty. I stopped counting past a minute
because the older paramedic asked me another question, but I still felt
no urge to breathe. I started doing it consciously after that because
not breathing while being taken to the hospital for not having a
heartbeat seemed like one problem too many.

The emergency room was worse. They hooked me up to another monitor
almost immediately, and I watched three different people decide the
equipment was broken. One nurse replaced the leads. Another brought in a
different machine. Eventually a doctor came in with a portable
ultrasound and pressed it against my chest. He looked at the screen for
a long time. I couldn’t see much from where I was lying, but I could see
enough of his face to know he wasn’t getting the answer he wanted. He
moved the probe, added more gel, and tried again from a different angle
before asking me how I felt. I told him fine.

He asked if I felt dizzy, confused, nauseous, weak, short of breath, or
like I might pass out. I said no to everything. He asked if I’d taken
anything, and when I said no, he asked again, but phrased it
differently. Prescription drugs, stimulants, recreational drugs,
supplements, anything someone might’ve slipped into a drink. I told him
about the missing time Tuesday night and said I honestly didn’t know if
I’d been drugged.

That sent everything in a different direction for a while. Toxicology
came back negative for everything they tested. They did more bloodwork
after that. Honestly, the blood draw was probably the most normal thing
that happened all day. The nurse found a vein, filled the tubes and
labeled them like normal. My blood looked exactly like blood. Normal
color, normal consistency, nothing strange about it that I could see. I
remember watching it fill the tubes and feeling weirdly relieved because
at least one part of my body was apparently still doing what it was
supposed to.

Then the results came back normal. Not “mostly normal” or “nothing
immediately concerning.” Normal. The doctor actually used the word
healthy. Blood counts, electrolytes, kidney function, liver function,
blood sugar, everything he went through with me was sitting right where
it should be. He said if he’d been looking at the bloodwork without
knowing who it belonged to, he would’ve assumed it came from a healthy
adult with no obvious medical problem. I asked him how that was possible
when my heart barely seemed to be doing anything. He didn’t have an
answer.

They repeated some of the tests because apparently that was easier to
believe than the results. The second set came back the same. As far as
my blood was concerned, there was nothing wrong with me. The strange
part is that the needle marks disappear quickly. The first few lasted
maybe an hour, and the ones from last night were gone when I checked
this morning. The wounds on my neck are still there, though. Those don’t
seem interested in healing at all.

By early afternoon I had a cardiologist, an infectious disease doctor,
and another doctor whose specialty I don’t remember all asking
variations of the same questions. Nobody has given me an actual
diagnosis. I’ve heard dehydration, abnormal cardiac conduction, toxic
exposure, infection, metabolic disorder, and several words I had to
Google after they left. None of them seem very convinced by their own
explanations.

One doctor eventually showed me part of the ultrasound. I’m probably
going to explain this badly, but he said there was movement in my heart,
just not enough movement to explain what the rest of my body was doing.
There were occasional contractions, but nothing resembling a normal
rhythm and nothing that should have been maintaining normal circulation.
Then he told me my brain and kidneys showed no signs of being deprived
of oxygen. I asked him how that was possible, and he said, “That’s what
we’re trying to determine.” That has basically been the theme of the
last day.

They tried feeding me around noon. Broth, crackers, gelatin and some
kind of nutrition drink. The broth tasted like hot water with salt in
it. The crackers tasted like paper. I couldn’t even finish the nutrition
drink because it left this thick artificial sweetness in my mouth that
made me feel sick. I forced down some of the broth anyway, and about
twenty minutes later I threw it back up. The nurse asked when I’d last
eaten a normal meal. I told her I hadn’t really been able to since
Wednesday. She looked at my chart and asked how I still had as much
energy as I did. I didn’t have an answer.

They put me on fluids after that, and I went through two bags without
needing to use the bathroom once. That bothered the doctor enough that
he ordered more tests. Everything keeps bothering them without actually
producing an explanation.

The worst part about being in the hospital isn’t the tests, though. It’s
the smell. There is blood everywhere. Not literally. The hospital is
clean. That’s almost what makes it worse because I can smell it through
things now. Someone gets an IV three rooms away and I smell that tiny
amount left on the gauze afterward. A nurse walked past my room carrying
one of those red biohazard containers yesterday and I could tell it was
coming before she reached the door.

There was an older man across the hall who had blood drawn sometime
yesterday afternoon. I couldn’t see what they were doing because his
curtain was closed, but I smelled it. My mouth started watering
immediately and my stomach cramped. I gripped onto the side of the
mattress until they finished. I’m trying not to think too much about
what that means.

They eventually moved me away from the main emergency department. I was
told they needed the bed, which makes sense because the ER was packed,
but the room I’m in now is different. It’s farther down a quieter
hallway and doesn’t have a window. I didn’t ask for that, but one of the
nurses said it would probably be more comfortable because of the light
sensitivity I’d reported. That also makes sense. Most things that have
happened here make sense on their own. When I put them together, they
stop making sense.

A nurse working last night especially bothered me. I don’t know her name
because I don’t remember seeing her badge clearly. She was probably in
her late fifties or early sixties, gray hair pulled back, completely
ordinary looking. She came in sometime after midnight to check my IV and
take my temperature. By then it was 92.7. She looked at the thermometer
and didn’t react, which stood out because everyone else reacts. Some try
to hide it. Some check the machine again. One nurse actually said, “What
the hell?” before remembering I could hear her. This woman just wrote
the number down.

Then she asked about my appetite. I told her it was terrible. She asked
whether smells were bothering me, and when I said some were, she looked
at me for a second and asked which ones. I don’t know why, but I didn’t
want to tell her about the blood, so I said it was mostly food. She
nodded and changed the IV bag.

Then she noticed the wounds on my neck. At least I think that’s when she
noticed them. She stopped moving for maybe half a second. It was such a
small reaction that I probably wouldn’t have thought anything of it two
days ago, but I’ve become very aware of people hesitating around me. She
asked when I’d gotten them. I told her I noticed them Wednesday morning,
but whatever happened probably happened Tuesday night. She nodded again
and that was it. No questions about animals. No questions about whether
another person bit me. She didn’t ask anything the urgent care doctor
had asked. She just finished what she was doing and told me to try to
rest.

I didn’t really sleep. I don’t seem to do that anymore. I can lie there
with my eyes closed, but my brain never seems to switch off. I spent
most of the night listening to the hospital. I can tell which nurses
wear softer shoes. I know one of the elevators has a faint squeak every
time it stops on this floor. Some machine somewhere down the hallway
clicks once every forty-three seconds.

Mostly I hear people. Heartbeats are becoming easier to separate from
everything else. I can tell when someone is walking toward my room
because their pulse changes slightly with the effort. I can tell when
someone is nervous before they speak. I can tell when someone has just
had coffee because their heart seems to beat harder and a little faster.
I know how insane that sounds.

Around three this morning, two nurses were talking at the station down
the hall. I wasn’t trying to listen at first, but it’s become hard to
avoid hearing things. One of them mentioned me. I couldn’t hear every
word because other people were moving around, but I heard my room number
and something about cardiology still not having an explanation. Then the
nurse from earlier said something I couldn’t quite make out. The other
nurse asked, “You’ve seen this before?”

The older nurse paused before saying, “Not exactly.” That was all I got
because someone rolled a cart past my room and drowned out the rest.
Maybe she meant she’d seen someone with similar cardiac problems. Maybe
she meant severe hypothermia. Maybe it had nothing to do with me at all
beyond whatever she’d read in my chart. I’m trying very hard not to turn
each weird conversation into something more than it is.

Around five they drew more blood. The nurse who did it this time took
three tubes. Everything about the draw looked normal again. I watched
her label all three at the bedside. About an hour later the doctor came
back with more results, and somehow they were still normal. He seemed
more irritated by that than anything else. He went through them again
and eventually said, “On paper, you’re healthy.”

I asked him if healthy people usually had a body temperature in the low
nineties and no real heartbeat. He stared at me for a second and said,
“No.”

This morning they let me shower, and I finally got a good look at myself
under bright bathroom lights. I look terrible and healthy at the same
time. I don’t know how else to explain it. The sunburn is completely
gone. My skin is paler than it used to be, but otherwise it looks good.
Better than good, actually. A scar on my chin I’ve had since high school
is noticeably lighter, and a small cut on my hand from last week is
completely gone. The wounds below my jaw are the only things that
haven’t changed.

My eyes look different too. Not a different color or anything dramatic.
They just look clearer. I stayed there looking at myself for long enough
that the nurse eventually knocked and asked if I was okay. I told her
yes.

When I came back into the room, the older nurse from last night was
standing beside the bed. I hadn’t heard her come in. She was looking
through the notes clipped to the end of the bed. She asked how I was
feeling, and I told her hungry. That was probably the first completely
honest answer I’d given her.

She looked at me for a second longer than I expected, then asked if the
hospital food still tasted bad. I told her everything tasted wrong. She
asked if I’d tried anything since the broth, and I said no. Then she
asked if anything had tasted normal. I almost told her about the blood
from my gums. I don’t know why I didn’t. I just said no. She nodded like
that answer meant something to her, but maybe I’m reading too much into
it. She wrote something down and said she’d let the doctor know.

Before she left, I asked whether she’d really seen someone like me
before. I told her I’d overheard the conversation at the nurses’
station. She just smiled a little and said hospitals see strange things
all the time. I asked what she’d meant by “not exactly.” She thought
about it for a second before saying she’d seen similar symptoms under
different circumstances. When I asked what circumstances, she shook her
head and told me she couldn’t talk about other patients.

I apologized and told her I wasn’t asking for a name or anything. I just
wanted to know if somebody else had ever been awake and talking with
numbers like mine. She didn’t answer that. She just said, “Your doctor
is the person you should be asking about your condition,” and left.

I stayed there for a while after she left, thinking about what she’d
said. I don’t know why it bothered me so much. Maybe it was nothing.
She’d probably worked in hospitals longer than some of the doctors had
been alive. Still, when she came past my room again later, I caught
myself watching her.

The doctors are supposed to come back sometime this afternoon with more
test results. They still aren’t talking about sending me home, which is
probably for the best considering I technically still don’t have a
heartbeat. My temperature was 91.9 the last time they checked, and I
still feel completely fine.

The hunger is getting worse, though. That’s the part I’m having the
hardest time explaining to them because calling it hunger doesn’t really
feel accurate anymore. My stomach isn’t growling and I don’t feel weak.
It’s more like a space somewhere inside me that keeps getting larger.

A few minutes ago someone down the hall had blood drawn. I knew the
second the needle went in because I could smell it from here. The older
nurse happened to be standing at the nurses’ station when it happened,
and I could see her through the open door. I must have reacted somehow
because she looked over at me. She didn’t seem alarmed or confused. She
just watched me for a second, then glanced toward the room where they
were drawing the blood.

After that she walked over and quietly closed my door. She didn’t say
anything about it. Maybe she remembered what I’d said about smells
bothering me and was trying to help. That’s the obvious explanation, and
right now I’m trying very hard to stick with obvious explanations.

The doctors should be back soon. Hopefully one of them finally has an
answer for me. Until then, I’m trying not to think about the fact that I
can still smell the blood from down the hall even with the door closed.
Whatever they’re putting in the IV isn’t helping the hunger at all.

A little while ago I opened Google because there was something I wanted
to search. I typed about half of it before I felt like an idiot and
deleted it.

I’m not typing it here either.

I’m starving. I’ll update again when I know more.
