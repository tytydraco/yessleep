[Part 1]

The police have finally seen fit to issue a statement on the murder in
the Lett’s hotel. Mom cannot help but enjoy the strange excitement of
her friends who call often to ask if I’m okay but really just want to
know the details. They’ve all seen the news story unfolding across the
local channels. The man who went missing from room 416 was an
out-of-town businessman who made a small fortune selling dental
supplies. Based on the blood splatter, they are convinced he is dead.
But nobody knows where he is, and the authorities are desperate for more
information.

His name is Marcus, and as I sit and watch the news with Mom, I begin to
smell something disgusting. It is cold and fetid. So pungent it makes my
eyes water. I look around but no one else reacts, which is insane
because this isn’t a mild fragrance. This is what I’d expect to smell if
I opened a flooded basement full of rotting corpses.

“You sure you’re okay with me watching this?” Mom asks when she notices
my discomfort. “They’ll be on to another story any second now, so it
isn’t long.”

“It’s okay Mom,” I grumble, then try to focus on something else–like my
fingernails all chewed and cracked–but it isn’t long before I glance
back up at the latest picture of Marcus. He’s accepting an award at
another conference. His smile–the same one in every photo–is cold and
reserved, his hair blond and thinning. He is a well-groomed man in his
mid-fifties who is a little chubby around the midsection. When I look at
him, the smell grows stronger than ever before and I have to bite my lip
to stop the tears in my eyes rolling down my cheek.

“Mom,” I say, swallowing the bile in my throat. “Actually, can we watch
something else?”

“Of course,” she says comfortingly, then picks up the remote and flicks
to another channel. 

There is a man in a dog costume on the TV. He is squatting on all fours
and bobbing up and down eagerly as a woman stands beside him, holding a
leash around his neck. She is talking calmly, but I don’t hear what
she’s saying right away. All I can see is that nightmarish mask staring
right at me.

The man is smiling behind that mask. I cannot see his face, but his eyes
tell me he is grinning ear-to-ear. 

A second later the shot changes to show a dog. A real dog. And text
scrolls past asking for donations to a local dog shelter. They want me
to adopt Minnie who is a kind of pitbull mix. A moment later there is a
dalmatian being hugged by the same woman who tells me all about him. He
is called Dylan and is only eight months old and needs another home. 

The commercial lasts another minute, and I remain paralysed the entire
time. The smell is growing worse. Stronger. Closer. 

The people onscreen tell me all about the other dogs, and at the end
they cut to a final shot of them together. Dylan and Minnie and
Tinkerbell (who is an ironically named Great Dane) and Kelly and Rufus
and Tintin and, finally, a full grown man in a dog outfit called Marcus.
The entire pack rolls around in a jumbled mix of wagging tails and
panting breath. 

No one onscreen or in the room beside me acts like there’s anything
strange about the fact that one of the dogs is a human being wearing
articulated foam and fabric to look like a dog.

“Oh I do miss Lucy,” Mom says sadly once the commercial finishes. “Ever
feel like you could handle another one?” she asks Dad who has been
staring at his TikTok feed for the last hour straight. 

“Getting a dog is easy,” he says without looking up. “It’s letting them
go that’s the kicker.”

Mom sighs. 

“I guess you’re right. I still think about Lucy every day.”

They both cry out in surprise when I throw up over the floor.

-

It is October and raining and my Mom and I are walking out of the
supermarket with a trolley full of groceries and she is telling me about
the candy she wants to put out for the trick or treaters. I ask her if
she remembers that time my friends and I wanted to watch Chucky and she
insisted she stay in the room because she was worried we’d get too
scared, but she was the one who got so frightened by the talking doll
that we had to turn the TV off and then just to be extra safe she made
us all say some prayers. 

“I hated that awful looking doll,” she says before putting her arm
around my shoulder and squeezing just as Dad rolls up. He always goes
ahead to get the car ready so Mom doesn’t have to spend long in the
rain. He even pops the door for her, and she slides into the passenger
side seat while I go behind the car to load groceries into the trunk. 

They must think I can’t hear all the way from the back because they
start flirting with each other.

“We can always drop the third wheel off and go for a drive,” Dad coos as
I load the shopping in the car. “Remember that little cove we’d go to. I
can make you feel young again.”

“Dad!” I cry out. “You’re a seventy year old man!”

Mom and Dad keep giggling like school children, and once I place the
final bag I shout out, “You two need to act your age!” before slamming
the trunk closed. 

Marcus is squatting on the roof of the car, his face mere inches from
mine. The second my eyes land on his blood soaked fur I can smell him.
Damp decay. Rotting flesh all slick and cold, like roadkill in a
rainstorm. 

I scream and trip over my own feet, landing painfully on the asphalt.
The man looks down at me and tilts his head like a curious dog. Before
anyone comes to my aid, he slides off the roof and gallops into the
darkness on his hands and feet.

-

It is around 3am and I need the toilet. I turn on the lamp by the side
of my bed, and slide my feet into the pair of slippers I keep by my bed.
They are slightly damp, and I think Lucy loves chewing my slippers as I
shuffle, bleary-eyed and half-asleep, to the toilet. Growing up with a
purebred husky meant Mom nearly lost her mind dealing with dog hair, so
she had all the carpets taken up and now it’s just hardwood floors. I
don’t like cold feet, so every Christmas, Mom makes sure to get me a
pair of slippers. They always look like some kind of animal, and this
year they’re two little pandas. 

As I pee, I think of that time I was maybe thirteen and Lucy stole a
pair I particularly loved. They looked like Alpacas and she had me
chasing her round the yard for nearly thirty minutes. She was so proud
of her little heist I couldn’t be mad. Every time I stopped she’d stop
and look at me with her puffed up snout full of my footwear and her
giant feather-duster tail would wag excitedly and my heart would swell.

Once I’m finished, I hurry back to the warmth of my bed and climb under
the covers. Wearing slippers that were a little crisp from constant
chewing was part of our life as a family. Damp heels were just the price
you paid for having a dog like Lucy in your life.

My heart hits my stomach at terminal velocity when I remember that Lucy
died six years ago. 

Every sense wakes up at once and I am plunged into reality like someone
falling into icy waters. My hands grip the duvet and instinctively pull
it towards my chin. My legs stiffen. My heart races. After several long
seconds spent listening to the near-perfect silence of my room at 3am, I
roll over onto my stomach–wincing at the bed springs, too loud–and lean
my head just a little over the side. 

There is only one little panda looking up at me.

The fur along its face and the heel of the slipper glistens in the lamp
light. It looks like an enormous slug has crawled over its
black-and-white ears and eyes.

A brown gloved hand reaches out to take it. It is smeared with jammy,
black blood, and suddenly I can smell him again. A reeking pestilence.
The revulsion is electric. Physical. I am propelled out of my bed by an
instinctive need to get away. When I turn to my bed his face is staring
up at me from the darkness. He has lifted the dog mask enough to let him
slobber over my slippers. Cracked blue lips peel backwards and yellow
human teeth gnaw pathetically at the panda. He is grinning excitedly.
Sniffing and snorting like a pig. Like a dog licking itself. Licking its
genitals. Over and over. The kind of mindless onanism you see in
asylums. The kind that sickens and distresses.

He recedes into the darkness, gloating and drooling, just as Dad bursts
into the room.

-

The storm is torrential and the hotel nearly empty when I arrive for my
shift. Enough time has passed since the murder that Mr Morrison is no
longer schedules Callie and I to always work together, so I arrive in
the dark just as she’s leaving. Both of us hold our blazers over our
heads as makeshift umbrellas, and the rain is so loud I can barely hear
her. She screams something about horseshit weather and something about
the new bus boy she’s been flirting with, and I laugh and try to ask her
if her shift was quiet but neither of us can stick the weather for long.
We say our goodbyes and I’m soon inside the cloak room, patting my
uniform dry with a towel.

Before I sit down at reception, I transfer a taser from my bag to my
pocket where it hangs heavy like a brick. Mom gave it to me after she
caught me trying to sneak a kitchen knife to the cinema. I didn’t tell
her about the man in the dog suit, but I didn’t have to. She didn’t ask.
She just told me it’s what she used to take out with her when work took
Dad out of state. It’s old, but she says it still works. When I ask how
she knows she laughs and tells me it’s why she’s been limping a little
all morning.

The shift starts slow. A woman checks in around 11pm, her wet hair
clinging to her forehead as she stands, dripping and tired, waiting for
me to find her key. One guest calls to arrange a wake up in the morning.
Another orders room service from the bar. 

Around 1am I get a call about a broken television from a man on third.
He sounds a little spaced out. We stock a generous minibar here at
Lett’s, and there’s a very real chance the guy’s up there trying to
change channels using the AC remote, so I don’t bother calling
maintenance. I go up on my own, patting my pocket with the taser a
couple times in the elevator just to make sure it’s still there.

It definitely isn’t visible when I knock, but the man opens the door and
glances downwards anyway like knows what I’ve got in my pocket. It’s
awkward, but neither of us acknowledge the gesture.

“Are you here for the TV?” he asks.

“Yes,” I answer brightly. “We had a call–”

“It isn’t working.”

He’s a tall man and the room is dark. I have to look up to see his face
that is cast in heavy shadow almost level with the door frame. I can’t
say what colour his eyes are, or even what expression is on his face. 

I desperately want him to turn on the light, but he hasn’t even put his
keycard in the wall to turn the power on.

“I’d like to watch some TV,” he says dully.

“O-o-okay,” I stammer politely. “Maybe if you put the keycard in that
little slot there it’ll–”

“Can you turn the TV on for me?”

“I think the problem sir is that the keycard isn’t in the slot so the TV
and lights won’t turn on and thinking about it the phone won’t work ei–”

“Oh this?” he asks in a slow stupor before pulling out a keycard from
some back pocket and sliding it into the nearby slot.

Lights turn on. The AC whirs into action. The man’s face is revealed. He
is mid forties. Handsome if a little weather-beaten. The face of a
bartender in a crime thriller maybe. He smiles simply and points at the
TV for third time.

“Been trying all night,” he says a little more cheerfully before
stepping back into his room to search for the remote. I cannot see a
suitcase or any other personal effects and the bed is well-made. “I
can’t get it working,” he adds.

“Well if you find the remote and show me the problem I can–”

He looks over his shoulder and frowns. 

“How are you gonna fix it over there?”

“Sir I–”

“Ah here it is,” he says while grabbing at something under the bed and
then sitting on its edge to point a little black remote at the TV.
“See?” he adds with a kind of stupefied frustration. “Nothing I press
gets it to work. Can you please just take a look? I can’t sleep without
the TV on.”

His eyes are pleading. I think about the taser in my pocket and step
inside. The door swings shut on its hinges without me touching it. The
sound of it clicking startles me, but the man does not react. 

“I’m Alex,” he tells me gently. “Come sit.”

He pats the bed next to him. Breath shudders in my throat. I stifle a
whimper and ask, “Can you show me the problem with your TV?” while doing
my best to sound firm and calm. 

“Of course,” he says, then points a remote at the screen and presses a
button but nothing happens. 

“Is it plugged in?” I ask.

“I don’t know,” he shrugs. “Doesn’t look much like any TV I’ve seen. How
would I know to fix it?”

The screen is wall mounted, and I can see the man sitting in its
reflection, his features despondent like a bystander at a funeral.
Desperate to get out of here as quickly as possible, I reach forward to
to fumble round the back of the TV so I can try and find the socket, and
my face is pressed so close to the black glass I lose sight of him in
the reflection. My nervous hands struggle but I soon find what I’m
looking for. I jam the loose plug back in and step back, turning quickly
to get that man back in my line of sight.

“There you are, Sir,” I say in while brushing dust off my uniform. “Try
now.”

The man does not look up. His face is hidden as he stares at his feet,
his shoulders shaking. His body heaving. I flinch when a kind of keening
howl emits from him. Wet and dry. A smoker gasping for their final
breath. 

An older man sobbing like a child.

“I’m sorry,” he says after that long and terrible gasp. “It just hurts
is all. Hurts real bad.”

He looks up at me with a tear-stained face. The kind of shellshocked
expression you see in old war photos. He sniffs long and hard before
continuing: “You know, he brought his car in to my garage three times in
as many weeks? Took me a while to get the hint.”

“Sorry I don’t understand?” I reply, edging slowly towards the door.

“It isn’t unusual for a clean cut guy to show interest in someone a
little rougher than them. Just about every time it happens, that fella
will have had a daddy who did something blue collar. Welder.
Electrician. Builder.” He laugh, his eyes redder than lit coals. “But it
still took me by surprise when he asked me out for dinner. Not beer,
mind you. That’s usually how it goes when a doctor or lawyer decides to
try it on with someone like me. But not him. He asked me to dinner.”

“Sir can you please try the TV now? I think I got it working,” I say,
and I cannot stop the desperation from slipping into my voice. I am
right by the door now. Close, so close. 

I have to fight the instinct to ask his permission to leave.

“Six weeks later,” he continues ignoring me. “And I was lying on some
basement floor, paralysed but awake after he’d gassed me up with
something strange he had lying round in big cannisters. Don’t know what
it was but I felt everything as he started sliding knitting needles
between my knuckles.”

I do not try to speak this time. My hand simply drops down and brushes
my pocket to feel the heavy outline of the taser. The tall man sees me
and this time, the gesture does not go unacknowledged. His face darkens.
His voice is terrible. 

“That won’t help,” he tells me, and my blood runs cold. I fumble for the
doorhandle behind me while the man stands up and somehow he is barely
contained by the room. He stoops just to keep his shoulders from
touching the ceiling and then he moves and God, he’s quick. So quick my
eyes can’t quite put together the path he took from A to B. There was a
chair between us. Yes there was. 

But he didn’t go around it. 

He is suddenly in front of me, so tall he blocks out the light and
darkens my little corner of the world. He looks down and there is only
the sound of the storm and a chill in the air that reminds me of room
416.

“I came a long way,” he says while taking my hand. His skin is cold. Ice
cold. He moves my arm with such strength that I fear fighting back might
result in a broken bone. Soon he has my hand in front of him, and he
takes something from his pocket and goes to place it in my palm. Only my
hand is a closed fist and seeing this makes him snarl and lean forward.
His teeth are vile. Rotten and yellow and all wonky. Rotten teeth. A
corpse’s teeth. 

“When I was a boy the nuns told us about Mary. You know about Mary don’t
you? She gave birth to little baby Jesus.”

His grip tightens and I nod. 

“They said Mary was God’s favourite. Same way some of us would have a
favourite hero on the radio or TV. Sister Angela asked me who my
favourite hero was and I said Batman. And she asked if that meant did I
hate Superman and I said oh no sister I loved them both. But Batman was
my favourite. And she said exactly. God loved us all. But Mary was his
favourite and she always had been. That could happen cause God knew us
all from before birth. Knew all our souls. And before Mary was even
born, God knew he liked her the most.

“Now I don’t know what God saw in Mary, but I know why Satan likes
Marcus. The sticky tarry shape of his soul. And I guess he’s pulled
enough strings that Marcus can hang around a little longer so he can
take a final victim. One for the road, so to speak. But he don’t got
forever. And that means you don’t either. Time’s ticking. You got
minutes, not days, you hear?”

He tries to place something in my hand once more and when my fingers
won’t budge, he slaps me hard across the face and I cry out. My ears
ring and my jaw aches and I cannot help but open my hand and then the
tall man is shoving something into the palm. 

It is cold and wet and made of plastic. 

He lets me go and steps back. We both stare at the rubber ball he has
placed in my hand, and then he speaks with a voice deeper and darker
than a coal mine.

“Marcus likes his games.”

There is a flash of lightning and with a terrible clunk every single
light in the hotel goes out and I am left standing alone in an empty
room that smells of freshly turned soil. 

-

I am still holding the ball when I open the door. The corridor outside
is quiet, and so pitch black that most of what I see looks like abstract
shapes painted in blue static. I know there’s a wall and a floor. But
I’m not even sure that’s what my eyes are seeing. I might be filling in
the image with what I remember about the corridor. The boundary between
what’s in my mind and what’s out there is a little fuzzy. There could be
something in front of me–something inches from my face–and I wouldn’t
know. But I do know I’m not alone in this corridor. There’s something in
the air. A trace of a familiar odour. 

Marcus.

I can’t hear or see him but he’s there. He might be waiting in ambush or
scouring the halls searching for some trail, but I can’t just stand here
forever so I take out my phone and turn on the flashlight and there is
the immediate, almost explosive, sound of footsteps sprinting towards
me. I shine the light quickly left to right and even though the beam is
weak and the light is faint, there is no one there. He must be getting
nearer though. Any second and I’m sure he’ll be there. This is not the
quiet brisk walk of a man closing the distance. The beat of his feet on
the heavy carpet of the hotel floor brings to mind an athlete in a 100m
sprint. Arms pumping. Legs pounding. He never appears. At least not in
the light. I try to hold myself in one place waiting to see if, by some
desperate mad hope, I’m wrong and it isn’t Marcus, but when my ears and
nose tell me he’s almost within arm’s reach I cannot help but cry and
shrink back into the room. 

The door closes just in time for Marcus to slam into it, rattling the
entire frame. He wastes no time thrashing against it. He kicks and slaps
and punches and lunges and the whole time he barks like a junkyard dog
threatening a passerby. Like one of those pitbulls that are all muscle
and teeth, hurling their body against a thin wire fence the second they
see you, rattling the heavy chain and padlock that keeps the gate
closed. The sound is like gunfire in my nervous system, but I stifle any
cries or sobs because I don’t want him to hear me.

The sound is furious, but it is not forever. I stand there shaking,
nervously biting one knuckle, and eventually, Marcus begins to calm. It
isn’t immediate. The barking slows to a grunting. Then a groaning.
Snarling. There are long seconds where he just sniffs the door with
great big performative huffs, his movement tracing the outline of its
frame. And then, when I think it must be finally over, I hear an unzip
and a trickle of fluid.

He’s pissing on the door. 

I am forced to stand there for a long thirty seconds as the stink of his
urine fills the room, and when he’s finally done he leaves, panting
happily all the way down the corridor until I can no longer hear him out
there. When I am sure that it won’t give me away, I turn my phone light
back on and stare dumbfounded at the darkening stain that creeps beneath
the door and inches towards my feet. It takes everything I have to hold
back the tears that threaten to shake my entire body. 

It takes a few long, slow minutes before I am able to will the muscles
in my body to relax. After a deep breath to steady my nerves, my fist
loosens and the rubber ball I’ve been squeezing this whole time inflates
and lets out a long flatulent squeak. 

Marcus immediately begins to bark and yelp with joy. I can hear him
running back towards me. Back to start it all over again.

I will not stand there a second time. I can not endure it all over
again. So I take the taser from my pocket, turn it on, then throw open
the door and hold it out in front of me. 

The sound of his footsteps is on me within seconds. I thumb the trigger
on the taser, timing it to the best of my ability just as his terrible
masked face appears in the blue glow and he launches himself towards me.
There is a moment where the taser is atoms away from contact with his
stinking rotting fur and my heart skips a beat. This is the moment when
he will be sent jolting, shivering, writhing to the floor like the
pathetic little man he is. This is the moment I am sure Marcus will be
bitten back.

Except nothing happens.

Like a ghost, Marcus passes through the taser. Through my hand. My arm.
And me. And just before he disappears there is a split-second where we
are nose to nose, and I see it clear as day. 

He is smiling.

-

Marcus knocked me on my ass and now I am sitting on the floor, balancing
precariously with my phone still held in one hand. There is the sound of
receding footsteps as Marcus, invisible in the beam of the phone’s
light, flees the room. There is no taser in sight and I know
instinctively Marcus has stolen it. 

But he has left me the ball, so I take it and climb to my feet before
approaching the door stealthily. Using the light to peer down the
hallway prompts another explosion of footsteps. He is coming and fast
but this time I give the ball a squeeze and throw it as far as I can
down the other side of the corridor. Then I switch off my phone light,
step back into the room, and listen as the ball squeaks down the
hallway.

Marcus barrels past. I cannot see him but I hear him running like a
freight train and I smell him too. It is the stench of meat turning
green. It is powerful but fleeting, and when it fades along with his
footsteps I know with certainty he has taken the bait.

Remembering the man from room 408 and the way he closed the distance all
those weeks ago, I resist the urge to run and instead walk at a steady
pace. But it is dark and I can barely see my feet, and as I take each
step I keep thinking about Marcus and this game we’re playing. Surely
he’s got the ball by now, hasn’t he? And I’ve played this game before.
We all have. It’s fetch. I threw the ball and he chased but it doesn’t
end there. There’s a second half to it. He’s coming back and that
knowledge is pressing down on me like storm clouds building pressure
over the land below. I want to twitch. To turn. To jump and run. I’m
that rabbit again with a twitching nose waiting for the dog to break its
cover. I am prey and he is predator. 

The tension inside of me is unbearable. It might as well be a small bomb
inside my chest. I would give anything to be free of it. To hand it over
to someone else so I could feel normal again. My nervous system feels
like a computer that’s on the verge of crashing. My legs and arms move
but they do not feel like my limbs. They feel like someone else’s. I
feel like a ghost trapped inside my own body. It takes everything I have
to make my legs move but it’s a fight. What would happen if I lost that
fight? I would just stop. I realise now just how powerful the
freeze-response really is. It isn’t just for small furry things. It has
clung on for millions of years and still lives in humans to this day. It
is very possible that I could just lose control and stand here in the
pitch black of this powerless empty hotel waiting for something in the
dark to come and find me.

This terrifies me. It isn’t enough that I’m fighting Marcus. I’m
fighting myself as well, and a million biological systems that operate
on pulses and twitches and spasmic firings without any consideration of
me, up here in my head, trying to keep the whole thing moving.

Shivering. Dissociating. Close to total breakdown. I keep walking and
the hallway takes a hard turn and I see the faint ghoulish glow of the
green Exit sign. It marks the start of the stairwell. The emergency
lighting beyond the door is such a relief. It pushes back the madness
prickling at the back of my mind and I find thoughts of dead rabbits and
hungry dogs fading in its presence. I start to feel like myself again.
But it seems impossible to me that Marcus has not taken up the chase
yet. Time has stretched on too long for me to pretend otherwise. And
sure enough, I open the doorway and pull it shut with a quiet click and
then turn on my phone and yes… 

He’s there. 

He does not rush me or try for the handle. He just looks with a tilted
head. A rubber ball clutched between his jaws. I cannot stop myself
studying him, and I realise he is unchanged since I first saw him in the
lot. At first I think that he must be rotting, but why would he? He
isn’t a physical presence. His real body is decomposing somewhere, isn’t
it? Rotting in a body of water. Turning to soup in a suit made of
articulated foam and fur. And I’m not stupid. I’ve known this whole time
where he is. And now the storm must be flooding those pipes and I bet
that by morning he’ll be flushed out of his hiding hole. Out of the
dark. That’s why he’s rushing to this conclusion. That’s why he’s come
tonight in this way. Once his body’s found after the storm, it’s over
for him.

Who says I have to wait for the storm? 

As I think the words Marcus changes. He stands fully upright and takes
the ball out of his mouth before reaching for the door. I do not have
time to lock it, so I simply turn and flee, racing down the steps.
Taking them two or even three at a time. One floor. Two floors. Three,
Soon I am at the bottom, one doorway away from reception. I can already
hear everyone in there, milling around and muttering. Safety is inches
away, and I instinctively check behind me and when I see nothing there,
I look upwards to see the green silhouette of the spiral staircase lit
by the emergency lighting. For a moment there is nothing, but then
Marcus appears and looks down at me and I swear to God he is different
now. He is a man. A whole man. There’s no game to this anymore. He isn’t
playing. Those eyes, smiling until this moment (always fucking smiling)
are full of rank and cold intelligence. Assessing the distance.
Assessing the time he has left.

I am convinced he will begin the chase again but something else happens.
He squints. Recalculates. Reconsiders. 

And then he simply disappears. 

-

Mr Morrison is in reception and shouting for everyone to stay calm. He
has driven here all the way from his house just to take care of the
situation after he called the desk phone and no one answered. When I
explain to him I was stuck in total darkness on sixth, he seems to
faintly register the disarray I’m in before quickly asking, 

“Well what were you doing up there? No one’s booked in on that floor!”

Hearing this and realising that he’s going to find some way of punishing
me for being absent from the desk even though he’s the one who always
chews us out for wasting maintenance’s time, I decide that I’m going to
look for another job straight away.

I tell him this and he guffaws quietly before turning and focusing on
the crowd of irate guests. I know he’s probably had his fair share of
people quit on him. There even were rumours that one of the chefs he
fired took a crap on his desk. But I still expected a little bit than an
eye roll. Although, just as I take out my phone to call my dad to come
get me, he does remark, 

“If you’re looking for that friend of yours she went outside.”

My heart sinks as this sentence registers. I cancel the call and ask
him, “What do you mean? What friend?”

“Callie,” he says.

“She went home!”

“No she’s been hanging out with Darren all night, hasn’t she? You can
tell her she’s another one who won’t be employed here for much longer if
she keeps distracting the kitchen staff during work hours.”

“Where is she now?”

“I told you!” He throws his hands up in the air and then points at the
doors to the parking lot. “I sent her outside. As long as she’s here she
might as well make herself useful and we had at least one guest fumbling
around out there. I could see him walking around by the grass, probably
drunk out of his mind, so I told her to go get him.”

He keeps talking but I don’t hear him. I don’t hear anything. I simply
push past him and make for the doors.

I burst into the darkness and see a flash of lightning briefly light up
the lot. Callie’s car is nearby, parked up. She must have told me on the
way in that she was going to go hang out around back with Darren, but I
didn’t hear her. This whole time she’s been milling around and once
Marcus realised he couldn’t get me, he decided to target my friend. 

I scream Callie’s name but there’s no response, so I turn on my torch
and march into the darkness. The rain soaks through my clothes in less
than a minute, but I continue pushing deeper into the grassland which
has grown barren in the late Fall. The bushes that dotted it have died
back to brown and thorny shrubs and heavy rainfall has drowned the grass
leaving a muddy plain. As I run, my feet churn the sodden Earth until it
suddenly sucks one foot down into a quagmire and steals my shoe as I
drag the ankle free again. 

I am losing control of this situation. I sweep my light in a 360 degree
arc and there’s nothing but rain falling in great sheets that catch the
beam of my flashlight. I scream Callie’s name again but there is still
no response. I can only keep on stumbling around until suddenly the
ground disappears and I fall over onto my hands. I manage to keep a hold
of my phone, but where the mud swallows my hand up to the wrist I lose
all of its light and am left completely blind and hurt. I have no choice
but to roll over into the mud and feel it smear up my back and under my
blouse where it slithers icily across my skin, Finally, I pull one hand
free then clumsily try to clean the phone. 

Eventually, the light returns and there, just a dozen feet away, lies
the stormpipe. 

Callie is curled up in its mouth and her eyes are vacant but open. She
looks so small to me in that moment. Her pale legs pulled up to her
chest as she strokes and coos at something cradled in her arms. 

I crawl over and see what she is holding. Even over the rain, I can hear
Callie singing to it. She seems drugged. Mad. Whatever is happening, I
am sure that her mind does not belong to her. Not fully. No one in their
right mind would hold that in their arms with such loving, tender care.
I have to help her. I have to get her away from this.

Despite everything, I hesitate.

It’s Marcus. But not the one who’s been chasing me. This is the real
Marcus, recognisable only by his trademark fursuit, and made of flesh
and blood.

His corpse is monstrous. The dog mask has gone, washed away, and now I
can see the skeletal face of a revenant. Tissue clings desperately to
bone revealing a deeply grotesque and exaggerated face, and his eyes
have long since been eaten by either bugs of bacteria. And the mold! I
didn’t know mold grows directly on human flesh. But why wouldn’t it?
Marcus’s face has patches of green and blue fur sprawling across raw
bone and meat. It’s strangely violating to see a human face like that. A
reminder that what often turns our bread to mush is waiting for its
chance to get at us as well. It just needs our immune system to take a
break and our body temperature to fall low enough. 

And the suit Marcus was wearing, soaked for weeks and months in
liquefying fat and flesh, has begun to rot in a strange and hideous way.
The suit has absorbed Marcus, letting him seep into the microscopic
spaces between its fibres. It is probably no longer possible to separate
it from the man underneath. Not even with the careful touch of a
surgeon. And Callie is stroking it! Him. Over and over. Blank glassy
eyes staring at nothing as she coos and sings and mutters, 

Good boy. Good boy. Good doggo. Cute doggo. What a pupper. Who’s a good
boy?

The platitudes are revolting. I cannot let this go on for a moment
longer. I reach out and grab Callie’s arm and pull her away from the
body. As she rolls behind me I go to help her back to her feet when
lightning flashes once more and I see another Marcus standing over us,
at the top of the shallow embankment. 

I realise that this is the spirit somehow divorced of the flesh that
lies just a few feet away from me.

He is smiling again.

“Fuck you!” I scream before grabbing his rotting corpse and pulling it
out of the pipe. 

Marcus disappears from the hill as my hand makes contact with the dead
body. As I drag it out into the light, the head rolls disgustingly on a
weakened neck. It leans so far backwards that a great tear in the suit
becomes visible and I see that this cut goes deeper than fur. It is the
fatal blow that killed Marcus. It has to be. I can see vertebrae. 

Suddenly, the state of room 416 makes a great deal of sense, and I
cannot stop myself from smiling as I imagine how Marcus’ final moments
play out. His last victim got him good. Dug a blade so deep it didn’t
just cut, it tore through his ligaments and arteries, brutalising
Marcus’ oesophagus and shredding his voice box. Good, I think as I dig
my heels into the mud and pull with every ounce of strength I have, but
my joy is short lived.

In the fray, Marcus’ head suddenly rolls forward and in the darkness, I
feel teeth clamp around my arm. 

I scream and hear a playful tittering. 

Marcus’ ghost is in the tunnel. He has one arm on his body and he begins
to pull. His corpse still has its teeth sunk into my arm and all of a
sudden my feet begin to slide. I cannot get enough purchase to stop him.
And I cannot tear my arm free! I smash at the corpse’s head with my left
arm. I strike it over and over but it does nothing except make those
cold and slimy jaws bite down harder. I’ve never felt pain like this.
His incisors have been driven into the muscle of my arm the same way a
shovel gets stamped down into soft Earth. And the more I struggle the
more it is like his mouth is worrying at my arm the way a dog worries a
dead sheep. 

And all the while, Marcus is grinning at me from the mouth of the
tunnel. 

And I know. 

Yes, I know. 

If he gets me in the darkness, Callie will wake up tomorrow and I will
be found drowned in that hole. They will say it was an unfortunate
accident. Hell, Mr Morrison might even lose his job for being such an
asshole. But my parents will be told my death was due to drowning in a
storm but that isn’t true.

I’ll die with that dead son of a bitch leaning over me and gloating. I
will die in the dark as his final victim. 

One for the fucking road. 

He keeps pulling. 

I keep sliding. 

The darkness is so close that I can feel it and behind it, somehow… yes,
impossible, but it’s there. I can hear screaming. Not the screaming of
one or two. But the screaming of a million. A billion. The desperate
pained and tortured shrieks of every soul of every sinner born and died
and even those yet to die. It’s cold in that pipe. Yes. So cold it
begins to burn. 

But I will not give up. I stare down at the wound in Marcus’ neck and
realise there is less than a few inches of meat and bone hanging on. A
knife would make it easy, but I don’t have a knife. I only have my one
free hand. 

I reach into the cold and wet flesh of his lacerated throat, seize on
his spine and grip it as hard as I can. My fingers slide between the
vertebrae, gripping a spinal cord so decayed it feels like I am
squeezing an over-ripened banana in my fist. Then, plucking
individually, I am able to break the last of the tendons holding one
segment onto another.

Something changes in Marcus’ eyes as he realises I am close to freedom.
He screams something. Words. Actual words. The one and only time I will
ever hear his human voice. 

“You bitch!” 

The words strike me as hilarious as I tear his head free from his body
and throw myself back from the mouth of the pipe. 

Laughing and crying, I land in heap on top of Callie and the final thing
I see before Marcus slips back into the darkness are the eyes of a man,
furious and denied.

The photos, found clutched in the hands of a headless Marcus by police
the following morning, do not exonerate the man from room 408. He is
still guilty of killing someone, but the charges are lowered
significantly and he is no longer facing the death penalty. It is
possible that without the photos, which Marcus only let slip in a moment
of lazy arrogance as he prepared a sedative in the hotel bathroom, he
would have gone unchallenged even in death.

Despite everything, Marcus is unlikely to go down in history. His kills
were surprisingly few, and most of the lurid details have been
suppressed from any reporting. Callie tried courting a couple true crime
podcasts but they were not interested. Seven kills across thirty years?
Not enough. Not for them. 

But they don’t know Marcus. 

But I do. 

And I think, to someone like him, the world must have seemed very
strange. He had no empathy. No kindness. I can only imagine the little
boy he once was, dead behind the eyes and full of only a quiet but
burgeoning curiosity about the meat beneath his classmate’s skin,
staring at Christmas trees and football games and cheering crowds and
laughing couples and being unable to understand any of it.

But on some level, I think he might have understood how even those
things could be done in a selfish way. How gifts can be given to
manipulate or love can mimicked to satisfy lust. But I bet there was one
thing in this world he could never understand. 

Something he could only interpret as a flaw, as a joke to be ridiculed
and mocked.

Something to be worn as a costume.
