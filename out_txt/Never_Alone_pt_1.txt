I pulled over on KY-259 just past the Leitchfield city limits because
the Check Engine light had been staring at me for twenty minutes and the
temperature gauge was climbing like it had somewhere better to be.

Grayson County at 11:47 p.m. on a Tuesday is mostly black fields and the
occasional pair of deer eyes. My phone said I still had three bars. That
felt like a lie.

The day had already been a slow-motion car wreck. Double shift at the
clinic in Elizabethtown because two nurses called out. A patient who
wouldn’t stop screaming about something living in the vents of her
apartment. My own car’s AC dying sometime around mile forty of the drive
home. By the time the engine started making that wet, metallic cough, I
was running on caffeine and pure spite.

I killed the ignition. The sudden quiet was worse than the noise. I sat
there with both hands on the wheel like the car might still somehow save
me if I just believed hard enough. Then I did the adult thing and popped
the hood.

The night air smelled like wet leaves and something faintly sweet,
almost like antifreeze mixed with rotting apples. My phone flashlight
cut a weak cone across the engine bay. Everything looked the same as it
always did. No steam. No obvious leaks. Just the soft ticking of metal
cooling down and the far-off sound of a single pair of tires somewhere
behind me that never got closer.

I checked my phone again. Still three bars. I opened the Maps app just
to see how far the nearest open gas station was. The blue dot
representing me sat perfectly still on the thin gray line of 259. Then
it blinked once and jumped about half a mile north of my actual
location.

I frowned, locked the screen, unlocked it. The blue dot was back where
it belonged. Probably just a glitch. Rural Kentucky GPS is not exactly
surgical.

That’s when the headlights appeared in my rearview.

They were low and yellowish, the kind of old sealed-beam lights you
still see on farm trucks around here. They came around the last curve
slow, almost hesitant, and stopped about fifty yards behind me. Engine
idling. No one got out.

I waited. Counted to thirty. The lights stayed on.

I told myself it was just some local who didn’t want to leave a woman
alone on the side of the road. Good manners. Southern hospitality. The
kind of thing that still happens out here.

I raised one hand in a small wave without turning around. The headlights
flicked off.

Complete darkness swallowed the road behind me.

My stomach did something unpleasant. I reached for the door handle, then
stopped. Getting out felt wrong. Staying in felt worse. I locked the
doors instead and killed my own interior lights.

Thirty more seconds. Nothing. No footsteps. No second set of lights.
Just the soft chorus of insects that never quite stop in October.

I decided to call my sister. Voicemail on the first ring. I left a
message that sounded calmer than I felt: “Hey, it’s me. Car’s acting up
on 259. I’m fine, just sitting tight. Call me back when you can.”

When I hung up, the blue dot on the map had moved again. This time it
was sitting in the middle of a blank field to the east of the road. No
roads. No landmarks. Just open nothing.

I stared at it until the screen timed out.

Something tapped the passenger window.

Not a knock. A single, precise tap, like a fingernail or a small stone.

I turned my head slowly. The window was empty. Just my own dim
reflection and the black shapes of trees beyond it.

Another tap. Same spot. Same weight.

I didn’t scream. That felt important later. I just sat very still and
watched the empty glass, waiting for a face or a hand or anything that
would make the moment make sense.

Instead, my phone buzzed once in my lap.

One new text from a number I didn’t recognize.

It said only:
You’re not supposed to stop here.

The headlights behind me flicked back on.

They were closer now.

I didn’t answer the text.

My thumb hovered over the screen like it might bite. The number had no
name, no area code I recognized. Just ten digits and that single
sentence sitting there in the dark of the car like it had always
belonged.

The headlights behind me brightened. Not the gradual brightening of
someone rolling closer. One clean step up in intensity, the way stage
lights do when someone hits a switch. They were maybe twenty yards away
now. Close enough that the yellow beams filled my entire rear window and
turned the interior of the Civic the color of old paper.

I kept both hands visible on the wheel. I don’t know why that felt
important. Some half-remembered tip from a self-defense video, or maybe
just the animal part of my brain trying to look smaller.

The passenger window received another tap. Same exact place. Same polite
little click.

This time I turned my whole body toward it.

Nothing.

Only my reflection, pale and wide-eyed, and beyond it the black wall of
trees. The reflection looked wrong for half a second—like the angle of
my face didn’t quite match the way I was sitting—but then the headlights
behind me flickered and the glass went normal again.

My phone buzzed a second time.

You were supposed to keep driving.

I locked the phone and shoved it under my thigh. The map was still open
underneath. I could feel the heat of the screen through my jeans. When I
glanced down, the blue dot had moved again. It was now sitting inside
the tree line to my right, about forty feet off the road, perfectly
still.

I told myself it was a glitch. Rural GPS. Atmospheric interference. The
kind of thing that happens when you’re exhausted and the universe
decides to pile on.

Then the driver’s side window tapped.

Not the passenger side this time. My side. Right beside my ear.

I flinched hard enough that my shoulder hit the door. The sound had been
softer than the others, almost careful. Like whoever—or whatever—was out
there didn’t want to startle me too badly.

I forced myself to look.

The glass was empty. But the trees on that side of the road looked
denser than they had thirty seconds earlier. Closer. The kind of optical
trick you get when you’re tired and the dark starts rearranging itself.

The headlights behind me went out again.

Complete black.

In the sudden absence of light I realized the insects had stopped. The
whole stretch of 259 was holding its breath.

My phone lit up under my leg with a third message. I didn’t want to
look. I looked anyway.

Kelly.

Just my name.

No one local called me Kelly except my sister and two people at the
clinic. Everyone else used Kel or the full first-and-last when they were
irritated. The number still had no contact info. No previous messages.
Nothing.

I hit the call button on pure reflex.

It rang once.

A soft click.

Then a voice that was almost mine, but pitched half a step too low, like
it was being played back from a slightly warped cassette.

“You weren’t supposed to see the map do that.”

The line went dead.

I dropped the phone. It bounced off the cup holder and landed face-up on
the passenger floorboard. The screen stayed lit, map still open. The
blue dot was no longer in the trees. It was inside the car with me,
overlapping the little arrow that was supposed to be my location.

Outside, something heavy shifted on the gravel shoulder. Not footsteps
exactly. More like weight being carefully redistributed. The way a
person moves when they’re trying not to make the stones crunch.

Or the way something larger moves when it’s deciding whether the stones
matter.

I reached for the ignition with a shaking hand. The key turned. The
engine caught on the first try, smooth and ordinary, like nothing had
ever been wrong with it.

I didn’t look in the rearview.

I just put the car in drive and rolled forward into the dark, tires
whispering over the asphalt, the blue dot on the abandoned phone still
sitting perfectly still in the passenger footwell as the road began to
curve.

I didn’t look back.

The Civic’s headlights cut a narrow tunnel through the dark as I pushed
the speedometer past fifty. The road curved gentle and familiar—I’d
driven this stretch a hundred times—but the trees on either side felt
tighter than they should have, like the night had decided to lean in.

My phone stayed face-up on the passenger floorboard. Every few seconds
the screen lit on its own, casting a cold blue glow across the empty
seat. I didn’t reach for it. I already knew what it would show.

The blue dot was still inside the car.

I forced my eyes forward. Leitchfield was only eight minutes away if I
kept this pace. Streetlights. People. A gas station that stayed open
past midnight. Normal things.

The engine note changed.

Not the earlier cough. Something subtler. A low, uneven rhythm under the
usual hum, like a second heartbeat trying to sync with the first. The
temperature gauge sat perfectly in the middle. The Check Engine light
stayed off. Everything looked fine.

Then the headlights dimmed.

Not failed. Dimmed. As if something had stepped in front of them for
half a second and then moved aside. The road ahead went soft and gray
before the beams recovered.

I tightened my grip on the wheel.

In the rearview, nothing but black. No headlights. No shape. Just the
dark swallowing the road behind me the way it always did out here.

My phone buzzed against the floor mat.

I ignored it.

It buzzed again. Longer this time.

On the third buzz I risked a glance down. The screen showed a new
message from the same unknown number.

You can’t outrun the map.

The blue dot on the open Maps app had left the car. It was now traveling
ahead of me on the road, moving at the same speed I was, a perfect
digital twin leading the way by maybe thirty yards.

I slammed the brakes.

The Civic skidded to a hard stop in the middle of the empty highway. My
seatbelt locked across my chest. The phone slid forward and hit the
underside of the glove box.

Silence rushed in.

No engine noise. I had killed it without thinking. No insects. No wind.
Just the soft tick of cooling metal and the sound of my own breathing
that was too loud, too fast.

I looked out the windshield.

The road continued straight for maybe a hundred yards before the next
gentle curve. Ordinary Kentucky blacktop. Ordinary dark.

But the trees on the right side of the road had changed.

They were closer. Not by feet. By judgment. The kind of wrongness you
notice in the corner of your eye and then can’t unsee. Branches reached
farther over the asphalt than they had any right to. The gaps between
trunks looked deeper, like doorways that led somewhere the map didn’t
cover.

And standing in one of those gaps, half-hidden by a crooked pine, was a
figure.

Human-shaped. Roughly my height. Still.

It wore something pale over the upper half of its face—cloth or bone or
both—and the rest of it blended into the shadows so completely that I
only knew it was there because the darkness around it was shaped wrong.

It didn’t move.

It didn’t need to.

My phone lit up one more time on the floor. I didn’t want to read it. I
read it anyway.

Never alone, Kelly.

The figure in the trees tilted its head the exact same way I do when I’m
listening hard.

Then the headlights behind me came back on—close, bright, and yellow—and
the engine of my own car turned itself over without the key.
