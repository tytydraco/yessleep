I’ve been thinking about telling this story for a long time. I have been
writing and deleting it more times than I can remember. I am still
cautious about telling you, but I have to let it out.

This happened a while ago in my hometown, a small village. When I say
small, I mean houses scattered so far apart that you could walk for
hours without seeing another house. There were stretches of farmland,
dense forests, and narrow paths connecting the few places.

There was no electricity in our part of the village. Once the sun went
down, the darkness was absolute. On clear nights, the moonlight helped
us find our way around. But when it was cloudy, you could barely see
anything beyond the reach of the lanterns.

I grew up in the city with my father, who moved to find work. We would
occasionally return to our village to visit my grandmother, uncle, and a
few other relatives, but those visits were usually short. Our village
was particularly difficult to reach during the monsoons. Our houses were
built on stilts, and travelling required us to take a boat. The only
place not submerged was the main highway connecting the village to
neighbouring towns. My father never took me there during the rains,
partly due to the commune.

My grandmother used to tell me stories about something called a Baak.
According to her, it loved fish, lived around water, and would mimic
human voices. She said it wore the skin of the people who followed the
voices. I believed it was just one of the countless other stories she
told me and assumed it was tales to keep kids from wandering around and
getting lost.

However, I always remembered two things about the older people from our
village. They always carried small bundles of mustard seeds and a little
folding knife. Even the ones who laughed at the stories seemed reluctant
to leave home without them, especially during the monsoon.

I didn’t understand why my father was so uncomfortable about taking me
back to the village during the rains. My father had lost his job, and we
had no choice but to move back in the middle of the monsoon. My father
was anxious the whole journey, constantly looking out the bus window and
hoping for the rain to stop. We got off on the main road near our
village, where our uncle was waiting for us with his boat. The journey
to our house was quiet and uncomfortable. We reached home, and I could
tell neither my grandmother nor my uncle was happy to see us.

I enjoyed the next few days despite the isolation. During the day, I
would go fishing with my uncle. But as evening approached, my
grandmother would lock every door and window. She would make sure we had
enough firewood, food, and water inside the house so that we wouldn’t
have to leave. At night, she made me sleep beside her. She would keep
the bundle of mustard seeds and knife under the pillow.

She told me to never open my eyes if I heard someone calling my name in
the darkness, no matter how familiar the voice sounded. I was to remain
still and keep my eyes shut.

I was also to never open the door when she wasn’t around. I remember
thinking she had become unusually paranoid since our arrival. I was old
enough to understand that people in the villages had their superstitions
and didn’t question them much. I followed her instructions because I
didn’t want to upset her.

At this time, my father spent his days looking for work in nearby towns.
Every morning, my uncle would take him by boat to the main road. My
father would travel from there, while my uncle would come back home.
Normally, my father would be back before nightfall. Just before sunset,
he would call out to my uncle from the main road, and my uncle would
take the boat to bring him back.

That particular morning, my father had left for town and was expecting
to stay overnight. My uncle dropped him off like usual and came back
home. My grandmother had gone to visit a neighbour that afternoon and
was supposed to return before dark.

My uncle decided to take a nap since he didn’t have to pick my father up
that evening, and I also made myself busy. My grandmother had not come
back, and the rain was relentless throughout the day. The rain had
drowned out almost every other sound, just the raindrops hammering on
the roof.

I was beginning to feel sleepy when I heard someone calling from
outside. It was my father’s voice calling for the boat. I remember
feeling confused more than anything. He called out again in the exact
same voice and exact same tone, like a voice recorder playing the voice
on a loop. When he called out again, something made the hair on my arm
stand up. I hurried to the next room, where my uncle was sleeping. I was
frightened and didn’t want to be alone.

My uncle was still half asleep. I watched him wake from his half-sleep,
still disoriented, and turn towards the door. It was as if he heard my
father’s voice and instinctively responded without giving the matter a
second thought. Before I could process what was happening, he unlatched
the door and swung it open.

There was something at the door, right in front of my uncle. My uncle’s
body blocked half of it from my view. It was slightly taller than my
uncle, its frame looming over him. I could not bring myself to see what
it actually looked like. I couldn’t move. I could only stare as that
thing lunged forward and seized my uncle. And before I could register
what had happened, they were gone, both of them.
