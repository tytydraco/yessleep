Devant le Roi.

That’s my family’s motto. It’s carved over the front door of the house I
now own, cut into the face of the signet ring that arrived in a padded
envelope six weeks ago, and painted in flaking gold leaf over the
doorways of the east wing. It means before the King, in the old sense.
In his presence. In the room.

The Corvalles were never the richest family in France, or the oldest,
but for a century and a half they stood closer to the throne than almost
anyone. A Corvalle was gentleman of the bedchamber to Louis XIV. A
Corvalle handed Louis XV his shirt every morning at the lever, the
ceremony of the King’s rising. A Corvalle stood in the room when Marie
Antoinette gave birth.

When the Revolution came, the families that had stood nearest the King
were the first to the guillotine. Not one Corvalle was arrested. Not one
lost a single acre. Their château in the hills of Burgundy came through
the Terror with its windows unbroken.

I didn’t learn any of that from my mother. My mother never talked about
her family at all.

When I was small, she came into my room every morning while it was still
dark, sat on the edge of my bed, and laid her palm over my eyes. She
stayed until it was fully light. I’d wake under her hand and lie very
still, because I could feel her holding her breath, letting it out in
tiny, careful sips, like someone hiding in a closet. When I asked why,
she said it was a family thing, and changed the subject so fast it felt
like a door closing.

Once, when I was twelve, a friend’s dad got us up before dawn to go
fishing. When I told my mother, she crouched in front of me, took my
face in both hands, and said, “Did anyone ask you to say something?” She
asked three more times, slower each time, watching my mouth. Then she
made me open it and looked inside, the way you’d check a toddler for a
swallowed coin. Her thumb on my lip was shaking.

For most of my teens I thought she was crazy. I’m not proud of that.

She died three years ago, at fifty-four. A neighbor found her in our
kitchen, in a chair she’d dragged to the window. She’d torn the curtains
down, rod and all, and pulled the nails out of the plaster with her
fingers. Three of her fingernails were still in the wall. Her hands were
clasped at her chest. The coroner put her death at around six in the
morning. Sunrise that day was at 6:04. I looked it up later. I wish I
hadn’t.

The man from the funeral home called a few days afterward. In twenty-two
years, he said, he’d never seen anything like it. My mother had bitten
her tongue almost completely through, in two places, front and back,
until it hung by a strip of muscle. Her jaw had locked so hard around it
that two of her molars had cracked.

The letter came from a notaire in Autun named Maître Garnier. My
great-aunt, Héloïse de Corvalle, had died at ninety-seven, the last of
the family besides me. Garnier attached a list of the others, the way
you’d attach an invoice.

My grandfather drowned in a pond four feet deep; they found him on the
bottom, kneeling. My uncle Thibault walked into the forest behind the
house in 1989 and was never found. My cousins Anne and Mathilde vanished
from their car on the A6 in 2004. It was found idling on the shoulder,
doors locked from the inside, both seatbelts still buckled.

“Vous êtes le dernier,” he wrote. You are the last.

I should have thrown the letter away. Instead I booked a flight. I
wanted to know what my mother had been sitting at that window waiting
for.

Garnier picked me up at the station, a soft, sweating man in a good
suit, and drove into the hills of the Morvan with both hands on the
wheel and the radio off. Eventually, without looking at me, he said,
“During the Terror, the committee in Autun sent men up to the château to
arrest them. Twice.”

“And?”

“And then they stopped sending men.” A kilometer later he added,
quietly, “The second time, they were found on the avenue the next
morning. Eleven men, kneeling in the gravel, facing the house. The ones
who could still speak would not say what they had seen.”

I asked how Héloïse died. He didn’t want to tell me. Eventually, he did.

The caretaker, Madame Bataillard, found her in the tower room at the top
of the house, where the window had been shuttered and nailed for as long
as anyone could remember. Someone had pried the shutters open with a
fireplace poker, and the poker lay on the floor, bent nearly in half.
Héloïse weighed perhaps forty kilos.

She was kneeling in front of the open window with her hands clasped at
her chest.

I didn’t say anything. I was thinking about my mother’s hands.

She’d been kneeling for days, the doctor said. Long enough that the skin
of her knees had worn away, then the flesh, until she rested on bare
bone, and the oak beneath her had worn into two shallow hollows. Every
one of those mornings, Madame Bataillard called up the stairs, and every
morning Héloïse answered, clear and calm, that she was fine and wished
to be left alone.

“The doctor was certain of one thing,” Garnier said. “By the last of
those mornings, she had been dead for at least two days. Her eyes were
open. And her lips were burned. Only her lips, as if she had kissed a
stove.”

He stopped at the bottom of a long avenue of lime trees and handed me a
ring of iron keys and a small envelope. “She left this for you. It is
the only thing she left.” Then he reversed the whole length of the lane
rather than turn around in front of the gates.

The envelope held a single card, in a hand so shaky it looked like it
had been written in a moving car: Ne réponds pas. Tu n’en auras pas
besoin.

Don’t answer. You won’t need to.

I couldn’t decide whether it was meant to comfort me.

The château sits at the end of the avenue, three stories of
honey-colored stone gone grey at the edges, its slate roof so steep it
seems to be listening. Ivy has taken the east wing, and when the wind
moves it, it moves all at once, like the flank of something breathing in
its sleep. Every window on that side is shuttered and nailed. Some of
the nails are new. Some are rusted so deep into the wood they look like
they grew there.

Halfway up the avenue I noticed the pale gravel was uneven: pairs of
shallow dents, side by side, every few meters, all the way to the front
steps. As if someone had stopped, over and over, to kneel.

Madame Bataillard had left the lamps on and a note on the kitchen table.
The east shutters were to stay closed. And, underlined twice: if anyone
knocks after dark, do not go to the door, and do not speak.

I checked every bolt on the ground floor twice. Then I said “Okay” out
loud, just to hear a voice. A house that size should echo. It didn’t.
The word just stopped, as if someone had taken it. That night I dreamed
of a road. Pale dust running straight across an empty country to a
horizon far too close, under a light that came from nowhere. Nothing had
a shadow. Then came the sound of footsteps, thousands of them, rolling
toward me like weather, and the procession came over the horizon.

They were my family. Men in powdered wigs, women in high lace collars, a
little boy in a sailor suit. Thibault with pine needles in his hair. My
grandfather with pond water running from his sleeves. My cousins side by
side, their seatbelts still strapped across their chests, torn loose at
the anchor and trailing in the dust. They were all smiling the same
wide, fixed smile, and every one of their mouths was burned white,
blistered and healed and blistered again.

Behind them came others, far too tall, and so thin they stooped as they
walked, like men passing under a low beam. I couldn’t see their faces. I
had the feeling that if I tried, the trying itself would be noticed.

Between them they carried a length of heavy red cloth that stretched
back along the road and over the horizon. It was soaked, and it left the
dust dark where it dragged. In places, shapes pressed up against it from
the inside, like faces under a wet sheet, all moving their mouths the
same way. I understood then that it was a train, the hem of a coronation
robe, and that something was wearing the other end.

I couldn’t see it. I could only see the horizon lifting slowly in the
middle, the way a sheet lifts when someone sits up beneath it, and along
the top of that impossible curve, a row of points, like the tines of a
crown.

Every head in the procession turned to me at once and drew in a breath,
the way a choir does just before it begins.

Then a hand closed on my wrist. It was my mother at twenty, the only one
not smiling. Her jaw was clamped so hard the muscles stood out, and
blood ran from between her lips in a thin, steady line. When she spoke,
the words came out thick and wrong, because her tongue was in pieces and
she was holding the pieces in.

“Ne dis rien.” Say nothing.

In the morning there were four dark bruises on one side of my wrist and
one on the other, where a thumb would go.

I found the gallery that afternoon: a long room on the east side,
windows nailed shut, lit by electric sconces that buzz faintly.
Thirty-one portraits hang down one wall, one for each head of the family
since 1631.

I walked the length of it three times before I saw it.

In the oldest portrait, far behind the sitter, in a doorway at the very
back, there’s a pale vertical smudge. You’d take it for a curtain, or a
flaw in the varnish.

In the 1700s, the smudge stands in the middle of the room, taller than
the doorway behind it should allow. It’s narrow, and bent at the top,
like something stooping under a ceiling.

The portrait from 1794 is the only one painted outdoors. The sitter
stands on the front steps, looking pleased with himself, and the pale
shape fills the doorway behind him. On the avenue beyond, small enough
to miss, eleven figures kneel in two neat rows, facing the house. The
painter gave each of them a tiny dark O of a mouth.

By the 1800s, it stands just behind the sitter’s chair, and the painters
have given it long white fingers. In my grandfather’s portrait, from
1958, only its hand is visible, resting on his chair. I counted the
knuckles on the index finger. There are five.

In Héloïse’s portrait, the hand is on her shoulder, and the arm runs up
past the top of the frame. She’s smiling. It’s the smile from the road.

I took her portrait down and pried off the backing with a butter knife.
The arm continues onto the folded margin of the canvas, the strip no one
was ever meant to see, up over the top bar and down the back, and the
canvas runs out before the arm does. Somebody stood behind that canvas
and painted a part no one would ever look at, because they believed it
mattered that it was there.

After Héloïse there’s one more frame, holding clean white canvas. The
brass plate beneath it has my name on it. It has the same dull, thumbed
patina as all the others. Whoever put my name there did it long before I
was born.

My hands shook so badly I missed the hook twice, hanging her back up.
Afterward I stood in the front hall looking at flights. But it was
already four, and Madame Bataillard was buttoning her coat and wouldn’t
meet my eye, and walking that avenue alone at dusk felt worse than
staying. One more night, I told myself. A painting is just a painting.

Last night, they knocked.

I woke at 3:40 to knocking at the front door, two floors below. Not a
fist. Something long and heavy, struck against the stone threshold. At
the first blow I reached out and hit record on my phone. French theaters
keep a tradition called les trois coups. Before the curtain rises,
someone backstage strikes the floor with a wooden staff: a fast, rolling
run of blows, a pause, then three slow ones. The audience falls silent,
because they know it’s about to begin.

I heard the rolling run. Then silence. Then the three.

Tok.

Tok.

Tok.

The pipes stopped ticking. The wind stopped in the limes. It felt like a
room full of people who had all stopped breathing at once.

I did not go to the door. I want that on the record. I lay with the
blanket pulled up to my eyes like a child, biting the inside of my cheek
until I tasted blood, and I did not go to the door.

Then I was lying somewhere else.

The recording is two hours and fourteen minutes long. For the first
forty minutes it’s only my breathing. At 4:22 I get out of bed. My
footsteps are slow and perfectly even, like someone walking in a
procession. The bedroom door creaks. Then nothing.

At 4:35, faint but clear, the recording picks up wind, and lime leaves
moving overhead. Then the crunch of gravel under someone’s weight, the
careful sound of someone lowering themselves onto it. Then again, a
little farther off. Kneeling, and rising, and kneeling, moving away down
the avenue.

The phone never left the nightstand. The bedroom is on the second floor,
on the west side of the house.

At 4:51, I start speaking.

It’s my voice, but projected, as if I were addressing a great hall. It
isn’t French. I don’t think it’s anything. Some of the sounds come from
places in the throat words don’t go; some are spoken on the inhale. It
has the cadence of a coronation, titles read out one after another, each
ending in a soft, wet click. It goes on for nineteen minutes without a
breath. Around the twelfth minute, I say my own name, clearly, in my
ordinary voice, the way you’d give it to a receptionist. Then the other
voice picks up again.

I don’t remember any of it. Whatever answered, it wasn’t me deciding to.

At 5:10, the house answers.

It begins quietly, from the walls and the floor under the nightstand: a
few voices, then dozens, then far more than the house could hold, all
murmuring the same word, low and reverent, the way a congregation says
amen. One of them, I’m almost sure, is Héloïse: an old woman’s voice, a
half beat ahead of the others. Eager.

Underneath them, with headphones on and the volume all the way up,
there’s something else. A deep, slow sound you feel in your teeth more
than hear. It sounds like something very large shifting its weight a
long way off. It sounds like something sitting up.

At 5:43, the voices stop.

For the last thirteen minutes there’s almost nothing. Room tone. And,
very close to the phone, someone breathing. Holding their breath, and
letting it out in tiny, careful sips.

I know that breathing. I lay under it every morning until I was nine
years old.

I woke at seven in the tower room, on my knees in front of the window
where Héloïse died. The tower door had been locked the day before, and I
didn’t have the key. This morning it stood open, with the key in it, on
the inside.

The shutters were closed. I am more grateful for that than for anything
in my life.

But every nail had been pulled out of them. They lay in a neat row along
the sill, sorted by size, each one straightened. The shutters were held
closed by nothing at all.

My knees were raw and full of pale gravel from the avenue, ground so
deep I spent an hour digging it out with tweezers. I cried the whole
time, and not from the pain. I just couldn’t stop.

The front door was still bolted from the inside, both bolts and the
chain.

Then I looked in the mirror. My lips are blistered and split down the
center, as if something small and very hot had been pressed against my
mouth and held there. Printed into the blisters on my lower lip, like a
wax seal, is a short row of tiny points.

Madame Bataillard didn’t come this morning. She isn’t answering her
phone.

I went to the gallery last. I knew I would. The last canvas isn’t white
anymore. There’s no paint on it, but it has gone grey in a long, narrow
shape down the center, bent slightly at the top, the way a wall fades
around a picture that has hung in the sun for years.

I’m writing this in the kitchen with every lamp on and my back against
the wall. A taxi from Autun is coming at nine. My bag is by the door.

I always pictured my family standing beside the King, at the King’s
elbow, handing him his shirt. The motto doesn’t say beside. It says
before. In front of.

On your knees, facing him, when he rises.

I’ll post again when I get the chance.
