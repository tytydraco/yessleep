Marisol arrived while they were preparing to move Sam to the hospital.
She came around the ambulance at a run and stopped when she saw him, as
though she’d expected the man on the stretcher to be someone else. His
face was swollen, and there was dried blood along his mouth. She knelt
beside him without touching him at first. Sam looked at her and made an
embarrassed attempt to wipe his face with his injured hand. “I’m sorry,”
he said. Marisol told him to shut up, but the words came out so gently
that he began crying again. She put her palm against his forearm and
waited until he reached for her. Then she leaned forward and held him
with both arms, careful of his hand, while the paramedic gave them a
minute.

I stayed a short distance away. I could hear Sam trying to explain about
the people who hadn’t come out, apologizing in bits and pieces whenever
he caught his breath. Marisol told him he didn’t have to explain any of
that yet. He asked whether the girls were at home, and she said they
were with their neighbor, arguing over whose turn it was to choose a
movie. That made him close his eyes for a moment. When he opened them,
he asked whether she’d told them he was coming back. She said she’d told
them he was alive. It was enough for that hour, though neither of them
seemed to know how to make the relief fit what had happened.

Before they loaded him into the ambulance, Sam caught my sleeve. “They
don’t keep everybody in the rooms you’ve seen,” he told me. “Those
people behind the door, they’re the last ones. They’re taking them
somewhere else because you’ve got the plant shut down. The woman in
charge told the workers to get the herd moving.” He looked back toward
the roof of Coldwater, where smoke was drifting from the damaged dock.
“Bess said they used the lower freight passage when they couldn’t keep
the pens here. She said she’d heard the wagons going at night. You need
to tell Reese before they get too far.”

I told him I would. He kept hold of my sleeve long enough to make sure
I’d heard, then released it and let the paramedic raise the stretcher.
When the ambulance doors closed, Marisol put a hand on the back of the
vehicle and stood there until it moved. She turned to me with an
exhausted expression. “You got him,” she said. I shook my head. I wanted
to explain how many people had helped, how close we’d come to losing
him, and what Bess had done for the girl. Marisol looked at my face and
stopped me. “He’s out,” she said. “You can tell me the rest later.”

I looked at the photographs Nora had sent while I walked back inside. In
one picture a bird stood on the overturned boat, barely visible unless
you enlarged it. I asked whether she had waited for it to land. She
wrote back, “Twenty minutes. You never would have lasted.” Inside the
south dock, investigators had secured the processing room. The chain
hung motionless above the floor, and the officers had covered the two
women who had died. A medical examiner was working near the counter
where the ribbon still lay. The man who had treated Sam’s hand was
sitting against the wall in restraints, asking whether anyone intended
to complete the afternoon count. When he looked at Bess’s covered body,
he seemed genuinely distressed. He said she had always been difficult
about instructions near the end of a service. He also wanted somebody to
know he’d given her pain medication that morning. Vale listened long
enough to record his name, then asked another investigator to take over.
Behind her, a board listed the victims by identification number. I asked
her to write Inez’s name beside 06-E, as I had promised Nell. She did.

Reese met me beside the heavy inner doors. Through a small inspection
panel we could see the floor descending into a long passage with rails
set into it. Fresh wheel marks ran through the dust. We heard metal
wheels moving far below us, followed by the thin sound of voices calling
to one another in a language I didn’t know. Reese already had officers
guarding every known exit and teams looking for another way into the
passage. Teams were searching beyond the southern property line, but
nobody had found the other end of the route.

I told him what Sam had said about the last herd. Reese looked through
the panel again, and for a while neither of us spoke. We had stopped the
slaughter schedule. Sam was alive, and people who had been waiting to
die were on their way to hospitals. Yet somewhere beneath our feet, the
Housekeeper was moving the remaining captives away from the only place
we knew how to reach them. One of the officers called from the loading
area that another survivor had been found behind a storage partition,
and Reese went to help. I stayed at the panel until the sound of the
wheels faded. When I turned away, I could still see the place where Bess
had fallen between the carts. She had wanted to know whether buses still
ran.
