I took this job to understand a man I barely remember. Thirty years ago, my father spent one fire season in a tower just like the one I'm in now. “Solitude with a view”, he called it in his letters.

He never came home that season.

I found the posting on a job board. Applied before I let myself think twice. I told my mother it was for the pay. She didn't believe me.

They stationed me on the western ridge. My supervisor ran through the routine fast, like he wanted to be gone before dark. Light the lamp every dusk. Set it in the west window. Never skip a night. 

“Keeps the watchers from going mad out there,” he told me, like a man reciting a rule he'd stopped questioning a long time ago.

The trail up took six hours. Switchbacks cut through an old burn scar. Black trunks stood like something that died mid-scream. Grief looks the same in a forest as it does in a person. 

The tower was smaller than I'd pictured. Steel legs bolted into bedrock, forty feet of ladder climbing to a single glass room with windows on every side, built so a person could watch the world catch fire from any angle they chose. A cot. A woodstove. A logbook with a cracked spine. A radio that hissed low and constant, like something breathing on the other end of the silence.

That's when I saw it.

Across the valley stood a second tower, close enough that I could just barely pick its shape out of the dying light. Same steel legs. Same glass room on top. Same catwalk. My supervisor's clipboard listed it as decommissioned. 

Decommissioned didn't stop its lamp from catching that evening. Right on schedule. Right as mine did. 

I keyed the radio that first night. Static. I tried again at dawn. Nothing. I lit the lamp every dusk. Pressed my face to cold glass. Waited. Thirty seconds later, a small gold light would bloom on the far ridge, like it was waiting all day just to answer me. I told myself there must’ve been a watcher doing the same lonely job across four miles of black timber. 

I couldn't tell you when my days stopped feeling ordinary, only that I noticed it had already happened. I'd stare at the logbook, unsure if I'd written that morning's entry or only meant to. 

I’d talk out loud just to hear a voice fill the room. Then I'd forget I'd spoken. Flinch at the sound of it coming from somewhere behind me. I’d argued with myself over which shelf held the matches. I always lost.

More than once I woke standing at the window, lamp already lit, no memory of striking anything. My fingertips smelled of sulfur. My knees ached like I'd spent hours kneeling on the floor, praying to something I hadn't agreed to believe in.

I started to wonder if the isolation did something structural in my skull. A hole you can't climb back out of. 

Was this what happened to him?

One night I tripped over a loose board by the stove. It had been rocking under my heel since the day I arrived. I pried it up.

There was a note, folded into a square worn soft as skin. I knew the hand. My mother still had a shoebox of his old letters. I read them enough as a teenager to know the slant.

Five words, pressed hard enough to read from the back of the page.

***THERE IS NO OTHER TOWER***

I laughed. Of course there was another tower. I'd watched its lamp with my own eyes for weeks. My father must have written this in whatever fever the mountain fed him. The same fever eating through me now.

A man can go so long without another voice that his mind starts inventing enemies.

I took the binoculars outside. The lens found the far ridge easy. I'd aimed it there every dusk for weeks. 

*Something* was walking it.

At first I thought it was the other watcher. Two arms. Two legs. The general shape of a human. But something was deeply wrong with its movements. Each rickety joint landed in the wrong place, like it learned the idea of a person secondhand, by someone who hated us. 

Its head sat tilted at an angle no neck holds, like a doll snapped back into its socket wrong,

It turned to face me. Dead across four miles of black timber, like it had waited this whole time for me to look up from my own unraveling and finally notice it was there.

It lifted one arm. Waved, like it knew I would come to it eventually.

I lowered the binoculars. Stood very still in the dark.

Something shifted in the glass in front of me. In the reflection. Right behind my own shoulder. I didn't turn around.

Some doors you just don't open 

I stopped lighting the lamp at dusk. I couldn't tell you when I stopped. Some nights the light in the far tower comes on anyway. I just stand there and watch it burn.

Tomorrow I hike the trail across the valley. I need to know what happened to my father. I need to understand.

If you're reading this, please don't come for me.
