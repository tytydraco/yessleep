I want to start with what I'm good at, because by the end of this you'll need a reason to believe me, and I don't have many left.

I know what your face is made of.

Not in a poetic way. In a numbered way. The software I use all day maps sixty-eight landmark points onto every human face it finds: seventeen along the jaw from ear to ear, five on each eyebrow, nine on the nose, six around each eye, twenty on the mouth. When you smile, points 49 and 55 travel up and out. When you lie, if you are bad at it, point 22 and point 23 pinch toward each other for about a fifth of a second. The software doesn't care about any of that. It only wants to find the face so it can hide it. But I have watched those little green dots ride on top of strangers for eleven years, and you can't do that without learning to read what's underneath.

My job title is Redaction Analyst. I work remotely for a vendor that handles footage for law firms, insurers, transit authorities and the occasional city council. When a piece of video has to be released, to a court or to the public, the faces of everyone who isn't relevant have to be removed. Bystanders. Children. The man in the background of a bus-stop assault who was only waiting for the 41. The software finds the faces, tracks them frame by frame, and assigns each one a number. I check its work. I fix the frames where it loses a face behind a pole or a hand. I decide, according to the instructions on the ticket, which tracks get blurred and which get kept.

A small true thing most people don't know: we never pixelate. Pixelation, the chunky mosaic you see on the news, keeps the average color of each block, and with enough frames and enough motion, a determined person can rebuild a surprising amount of the face underneath. We use a heavy Gaussian blur with a noise layer on top. It is designed so that nothing can ever be brought back. When I redact you, you are gone.

I tell you all of this so you understand that when I say I looked at a face in that batch and knew it wasn't mine, I am not being dramatic. I am being professional.

Some context about me, quickly, because it matters later.

I'm thirty-seven. My name is Tamsin Pell. I live alone on the twenty-second floor of a thirty-eight story residential tower near the river. I won't name the building. It's the kind with a concierge desk nobody sits at after six and a gym that smells of rubber and nobody, and windows in every unit that run from the floor to the ceiling, so that at night the whole tower looks like a stack of lit aquariums.

My unit is 2207. One bedroom, one bathroom, an open kitchen and living area, and a short entry hall with a coat closet by the door. The living room faces west. The view is the reason I took the place. The river, three bridges, the rail yard, the hills after that. At sunset the glass goes copper and I sit at my desk and work with the whole city burning behind my monitors.

I don't go out much. I'm not agoraphobic, I just have no reason. Groceries come to the door. Work comes through a queue. My manager is a name in a ticketing system who signs every message "Best, D." I have no family I'm in contact with. I had a grandmother, the one I'm named after, and I remember her kitchen and the sound of her radio, and that's most of what I remember about her. I don't find any of this sad. I find it quiet, and I like quiet.

I sleep extraordinarily well. I want to underline that. Eleven o'clock to seven o'clock, every night, no alarm. I don't dream. I've never been able to remember a single dream, not one, not ever, and when people talk about theirs I nod the way you nod at someone describing a country you've never been to. I always assumed it was a gift.

Remember that I said that.

**The batch**

It came in on a Thursday at 4:12 in the afternoon, which is late for a new ticket. The client code was one I'd never seen: **H-0001**. Every client we have is a string of letters and five or six digits. This was a single letter and a one.

Nine files. Each about seven and a half hours long. Each one timestamped to begin at roughly 11:00 p.m. and end at roughly 6:55 a.m., across the nine nights before the ticket arrived. No location field. No case number. Device field: blank.

The instruction field, which is normally a paragraph of legal language about which parties consented to be shown, said this:

**REDACT: TENANT** **RETAIN: HOST**

And there were two reference images attached.

This part is normal, I should explain. When a client wants one particular person kept visible, they upload a reference photo so the software knows which face to leave alone. You get it with police bodycam work all the time: here's the officer, blur everyone but him.

The first reference image was labeled TENANT, and it was my work ID photo. The one I took on my laptop camera for the vendor's HR system, grey wall behind me, hair tied back, the slightly startled look everyone has in those pictures.

The second reference image was labeled HOST.

It was also me.

Same face. Same hair, though shorter on the left. Same small scar under the chin from a bicycle accident I don't actually remember having. Taken in what was clearly my apartment, in the dark, lit by something blue and cold, maybe a screen.

And it was not me.

I can't make you see it in words, so let me give it to you in numbers, the way I saw it. On my face, at rest, point 49, the left corner of the mouth, sits level with point 55, the right. On the HOST face, point 49 sat about two millimeters lower. The eyes were a fraction more closed, not sleepy, but like someone who has decided in advance not to be surprised by anything. The jaw sat further forward. And the whole face had a quality I've only ever seen in footage of people who know they're being filmed and have stopped caring.

It was my face with someone else wearing it.

I sat there for a while. I'll be honest about what I thought, because I want you to see that I thought the reasonable things first. I thought: this is a test. The vendor does run audits, fake tickets designed to see whether analysts follow procedure. I thought: someone's playing a joke, a coworker who found my HR photo and ran it through one of those apps that age you or change your expression. I thought: the HOST image is a deepfake, and this is some kind of phishing thing.

I didn't think: I should not open this.

I opened the first file.

**File 1**

The camera was outside my window.

I need you to understand what that means. My living room windows are on the twenty-second floor. There is no balcony. There is a narrow concrete ledge, perhaps twenty centimeters, that the window-cleaning crew uses to anchor their gondola twice a year. Beyond that is two hundred and thirty feet of air.

The camera was in that air. About a meter beyond the glass, at roughly the height of my eyes if I were standing, looking straight in. You could see the whole apartment from there: the living room, my desk with its two monitors, the kitchen counter, and through the open bedroom door, the corner of my bed, and on the bed, a shape under the grey duvet.

Me, asleep.

There was no reflection of a camera in the glass. I checked that first. A camera that close to a window at night, with lights on inside, should show up as a dark shape with a lens in it. There was nothing. The glass reflected the room back at itself and nothing else.

The image quality was strange too. Not grainy. Clean, but soft at the edges, the way old digital video looks when it has been compressed and re-saved too many times. The color sat slightly toward green. There was no audio track.

For three hours and fourteen minutes, nothing happened. I scrubbed through at eight times speed and watched the city lights slide across the glass and the shape in the bed not move. I want to be precise: it did not move at all. Not the small shifts and turns everyone makes in sleep. It lay on its back with its arms at its sides like something laid out.

At 2:14 a.m., the shape sat up.

Not the way you sit up when you wake. It came up from the waist all at once, smoothly, as if pulled by a string attached to the sternum, and the head came up last. It sat there for a full minute facing the bedroom wall. Then it swung its legs off the bed, stood, and walked out into the living room toward the window.

Toward the camera.

The software found the face before I did. A green box blinked into place around it, and the sixty-eight dots spread across the features, and a label appeared above it.

Then a second green box appeared, in the exact same place, around the exact same face.

Two tracks. **Track 01** and **Track 02.** Stacked on top of each other on one head.

I have never seen that. Not once in eleven years. A face is a face. The software has bugs, it loses people, it occasionally decides a coat on a hook is a man, but it does not see two people where there is one. I froze the frame. Track 01 matched the TENANT reference at 41 percent confidence. Track 02 matched HOST at 88.

The figure walked up to the glass and stopped about half a meter from it, and looked into the lens.

Then it began to move its face.

Slowly. Deliberately. It turned its head a few degrees to the left and held it. Then a few degrees right. Then it tilted its chin up, then down. Then it began to roll its head in a slow circle, the way your phone asks you to when you set up face unlock, so it can learn you from every angle.

It was enrolling.

When it had gone all the way around, it stood still and made expressions. A smile. Neutral. Eyebrows raised. A frown. And then a sequence of tiny ones, so small that you'd never catch them at normal speed, and I only caught them because I went back and stepped through frame by frame: the little press of the lips I make when I'm reading something I disagree with. The way my right eye narrows a fraction before my left when I'm concentrating. The flat, polite half-smile I give delivery drivers.

Mine. It was doing mine. It was practicing.

And the confidence numbers were moving. With every expression, Track 01, the one that matched me, went up a little, and Track 02 went down. By the end of the sequence they were almost level. 63 and 67.

It was learning to look like me so well that the software couldn't tell us apart.

Then it turned around, walked to my desk, and opened my laptop.

The screen lit its face from below. I watched it sit in my chair, perfectly comfortable, and look into the webcam, and the laptop unlocked for it.

It read for two hours. I could see the screen at an angle and well enough to know what was on it. My email. My messages with "D." My search history. A document I keep with notes on difficult tickets. It scrolled slowly, the way you read something you intend to remember. At one point it opened a blank document and typed for a long time, and then deleted all of it.

At 4:31 it closed the laptop, went into the kitchen, opened the refrigerator and stood in the light of it eating cold rice out of the container with its fingers. It ate the whole thing. It licked its fingers one at a time. Then it put the empty container back in the fridge.

At 6:50 it went back to bed, lay down on its back with its arms at its sides, and did not move again.

At 6:55 the file ended.

I got up, walked to the fridge and opened it.

The rice container was on the second shelf. It was empty. There were faint, dry streaks inside it, the kind fingers leave.

I had made that rice on Tuesday. I had eaten some of it for dinner on Wednesday, I was almost sure, and put the rest away. I had planned to have it tonight.

I'm aware that "I don't remember finishing the leftovers" is not proof of anything. I'm aware of how this sounds. I stood there with the fridge door open and the cold rolling out over my bare feet and told myself that I'd eaten it at lunch, absently, while working, the way you do. I told myself that out loud.

Then I went back to my desk and pulled the Windows event log.

If you don't know: Windows keeps a record of every time the computer is unlocked and how. It's under Security, event ID 4624 and a few others, and if you use face unlock, it records that too. It's dull, technical, and completely indifferent to what you'd like to be true.

I filtered for the last nine nights.

The laptop had been unlocked by face recognition every single night. 2:09 a.m. 2:11. 2:06. 2:14. 2:12. Always just after two. Always for about two hours.

I had been asleep. I am always asleep. I sleep eleven to seven and I don't dream.

*Then who unlocked it, Tamsin.*

I want to note that line in italics because it was the first one. The first thought that arrived in my head already phrased, already in a voice, already using my name like someone standing slightly behind me.

**Files 2 through 8**

I'm going to go faster here, not because they were less important but because if I describe every hour I'll never finish, and I have a feeling I need to finish.

I did all the normal things first. I went to the window and looked out at the ledge. Twenty centimeters of pigeon-streaked concrete and then nothing. I called the building office and asked, as casually as I could, whether there'd been any drone complaints, and the man said "Drones?" in a way that answered it. I looked up the gondola schedule. The window crew had last been there in May.

I covered the laptop camera with a square of electrical tape. I set my phone on the bookshelf facing the bed, recording, and went to sleep at eleven like always.

In the morning the tape was on the desk, folded neatly in half, sticky side in. My phone had recorded seven hours and fifty minutes of a dark bedroom and a still shape under a duvet. Nothing else. No movement at all.

And in the queue, a new file had appeared in the batch. **File 10.** Timestamped from last night. Shot from outside the window. In it, at 2:08, the shape sits up, walks to the bookshelf, looks into my phone for a long moment, and then goes and peels the tape off the laptop.

My phone saw nothing. The window saw everything.

I didn't sleep the next night. I sat at my desk with every light on, drinking coffee and watching the files, and I stayed awake until a little after four, and then I woke up in bed at seven with my arms at my sides and the duvet pulled smooth to my chin, and in the morning there was a **File 11**.

So I'll give you the files as a list, because that's how I've been holding them in my head, like a stack of evidence bags.

**File 2.** It goes into the coat closet by the front door, closes itself inside, and stays there for two hours and forty minutes. The closet door has a slatted panel at the top. You can just see, through the slats, that it is sitting on the floor with its face pressed into the back wall. Its shoulders move as if it's talking. When it comes out, its lips are pale and there's plaster dust on its forehead.

**File 3.** It takes the kitchen scissors and sits at the desk and cuts my hair. Not much. A few strands from the left side, close to the scalp, where I wouldn't see them in the mirror unless I looked. It does this slowly and lovingly and puts the strands in an envelope. I went to the bathroom and lifted my hair and there was a patch on the left side, the size of a coin, cut almost to the skin. When I put my HOST reference image beside my own reflection, the hair matched. It wasn't that the photo was of a version of me with shorter hair. It was that I was becoming the photo.

**File 4.** It reads my emails aloud. I can't hear it, there's no audio, but I can read its lips, which I'm decent at after eleven years of watching mouths. It reads the same email again and again, a dull reply I'd written to "D" about a missed deadline. Each time it reads it, the shape of its mouth gets closer to the way I talk. The first time, it over-pronounces everything. By the twentieth time, it's swallowing its t's the way I do. It's tuning itself.

**File 5.** I almost didn't write about this one.

At 3:20 it lies on the living room floor, on its back, directly in front of the window. It looks up into the camera. Then it places its right hand over its own mouth and nose and presses down.

It holds it there.

I watched the chest try to rise and fail. I watched the heels drum against the floor, once, twice, a long shudder going up through the legs. I watched the left hand claw at the floorboards. The right hand did not move. The face above the hand stayed perfectly calm, looking straight into the lens, while the body underneath it fought. Forty seconds. Fifty. At one minute and nine seconds the body went slack, and only then did the hand lift away.

It lay there breathing in big, wet pulls of air. Then it raised its head and said something to the camera. Two words. I stepped back through it eight times to be sure.

*Stay down.*

The mornings I'd woken up with red pinprick spots across my cheeks and around my eyes. Petechiae, they're called. Burst capillaries. You get them from straining, from vomiting, from strangulation. I'd noticed them twice in the last month and decided it was allergies, or that I'd slept face down, which I never do.

It wasn't talking to the camera. I understood that on the eighth viewing. It was talking to me. To the thing in that body that might come up for air. It was holding me under.

**File 6.** It opens the trash chute in the corridor and feeds my things into it. My grandmother's ring. A framed photo of the river at dusk I took the week I moved in. The scarf I wear on video calls. My work ID badge. I checked the drawer where the ring should be. Empty. The scarf: gone. I couldn't remember the last time I'd seen any of them. I couldn't remember the last time I'd looked.

**File 7.** It makes a phone call. Not on my phone, on one I've never seen, an old flip phone that it takes out of the coat closet. It talks for eleven minutes, pacing in front of the window. Most of it I can't read, it keeps turning away. But I caught one sentence clearly, because it stopped and faced the glass to say it, like it wanted me to have it.

*No. It doesn't know. It still thinks it was born.*

**File 8.** Nothing happens at all. The shape in the bed doesn't sit up. It lies there the full seven and a half hours. I was almost relieved. And then, on the last frame, at 6:55, I noticed that its eyes had been open the whole time.

**The apartment**

I'm going to describe the changes in the order I noticed them, which is not the order they happened. I think they'd been happening for much longer than I knew. I think noticing was the thing that was new.

The peephole was the first. On the morning after File 5, I went to check the corridor before taking the garbage out, put my eye to the door, and there was no peephole. Just smooth painted wood. I ran my fingertips over the place where it should be. No hole, no filler, no seam. The paint had the same faint orange-peel texture as the rest of the door, unbroken.

I opened the door and went out and looked at it from the corridor side. No peephole there either. All the other doors on the floor had them, brass rings at eye height, catching the corridor light. Mine looked like a door in a drawing of a door.

Then the wood grain.

My floor is engineered oak, wide planks, a pale honey color. I was sitting on it, after File 5, with my back against the couch, because I couldn't sit in the desk chair anymore knowing who else sat in it. And I was looking at the floor because I couldn't look at the window, and I saw a knot in the plank in front of me, a dark eye-shaped knot with a little tail. And then, forty centimeters further along, the same knot. The same tail. And forty centimeters after that, the same knot again.

I crawled along the floor and followed it across the room. The pattern repeated every forty centimeters, perfectly, a tiled texture, like the floor in an old video game where the designers only had room in memory for one square of wood and laid it down over and over. I'd lived on that floor for years. I'd have noticed. I'd swear I'd have noticed.

Then the books. I have a shelf of them in the living room, mostly paperbacks. When I looked directly at a spine, the title was there, sharp and normal. But in the corner of my eye, the titles weren't words. They were smudges of the right length and color, the gesture of text without the text. I tested it, the way I test a tracking glitch, holding my eyes on one spine and slowly letting my attention slide to the next. The letters dissolved a half-second before I got to them and reassembled just as my focus arrived. Like the room was rendering only the part I was looking at, and only just in time.

Smell went next. I didn't notice it go, I noticed it was gone. I made coffee and stood there holding the mug under my nose and there was heat, and steam, and nothing. I opened every jar in the spice cabinet. Cumin, cinnamon, the cloves I keep for no reason. Nothing. The apartment smells of nothing now. I'm not sure I can describe how frightening that is. It feels like being in a photograph of a kitchen.

And then the windows.

On the fourth night I was trying to stay awake, I was standing at the glass looking down at the rail yard, and I turned my head quickly to the right, toward the bridges. And the city stuttered.

I don't know a better way to say it. For a fraction of a second, the lights below broke apart into blocks. Squares of color, smeared and blocky, exactly like a video stream when the connection drops and the compression can't keep up with motion. Macroblocking, it's called. I've seen it ten thousand times in bad footage. It lasted perhaps a tenth of a second and then the view snapped back together, sharp, every streetlight in its place.

I turned my head again. Faster. It happened again. The rail yard came apart into a mosaic of orange and black squares, and then reassembled.

I stood at the window for an hour turning my head back and forth like a mad person, watching the world fall to pieces and repair itself.

The view outside my window is low bitrate. That's the sentence I wrote in my notes that night. I've underlined it. It's the most technical sentence I've ever written and I'm still not sure what it means, except that it means the window isn't glass.

**The laptop**

Around this time, the laptop stopped recognizing me.

I'd sit down in the morning and look into the camera and it would think for a second and say *Couldn't recognize you. Make sure your face is centered in the frame.* I'd adjust. I'd take my glasses off, put them back on. *Couldn't recognize you.* I'd type in my PIN instead, and it would let me in.

And then the event log would show me that at 2:07 that morning, it had recognized someone just fine.

I disabled face unlock. I went into the settings and removed my face data completely. When I woke up the next morning, it had been set up again. In the log there was a new entry: *Windows Hello face enrollment completed.* 2:22 a.m.

It's re-enrolled itself as me. Every night. And every morning I'm a little less what it learned.

I ran an experiment. I loaded the HOST and TENANT reference images into the redaction software, then pointed it at my own live webcam feed and watched the tracks come up on me in real time. Sitting at my desk, wide awake, in daylight.

Track 01, TENANT: 54 percent.

Track 02, HOST: 61.

I am awake, and the software is more sure I'm them than I am me.

**The closet**

I knew I'd have to open the coat closet properly eventually. It took me until the ninth day.

It's a shallow closet. Ninety centimeters deep, maybe. A rail, a shelf, my winter coat, a vacuum cleaner, a box of extension cords. The back wall is the same white as everything else. In File 2, the figure had pressed its face to that wall and talked to it for nearly three hours.

I took everything out. I knocked on the back wall. It sounded hollow, which drywall always does, so that told me nothing. I pressed my ear to it, the way it had, and listened, and heard the building's low hum, the pipes, the distant clank of the elevator, and under all of it, so faint I wasn't sure it was there, something like breathing. Slow, patient, matched to my own. When I held my breath, it held its breath. When I let mine out, it let its out.

I went and got the hammer from the kitchen drawer.

The drywall came apart easily. Too easily, like wet paper. I made a hole the size of my fist and then pulled at the edges and the whole back panel came away in two big chunks and fell at my feet in a cloud of dust I couldn't smell.

There was a room behind it.

Not a cavity. A room. About two and a half meters by three. No window. Bare concrete floor, bare plaster walls, a single bulb hanging from the ceiling on a cord, which came on when I found the pull-chain. A thin mattress on the floor with a grey sheet. A wooden chair. And against the far wall, three cardboard archive boxes, the standard kind with lids and hand-holes, the kind I've filed away a hundred times for clients.

Each box was labeled on the end in black marker in handwriting that I recognized, because it was my handwriting, or close to it. Slightly more upright. The *t*s crossed lower.

**TMP-01**

**TMP-02**

**TMP-03**

I'm an archivist, more or less. I know what TMP means. It's the extension a computer puts on a temporary file. The scratch copy a program makes while it's working, so it doesn't have to touch the original. The thing that's meant to be deleted when the program closes. Every now and then something crashes and the temp file gets left behind, and some poor analyst like me finds it months later, sitting in a folder, full of half-finished work, belonging to nothing.

I stood there for a long time before I let myself think about my login.

It's **tmpell.** First and middle initial, last name. T. M. Pell. Tamsin Mara Pell.

I've typed it ten thousand times. I never once looked at it.

**The boxes**

Each one held the same things, arranged the same way, like personal effects returned after a death.

Clothes, folded. A phone with a cracked screen and no charger. A laminated ID card. And a notebook.

The ID cards were for three different jobs. A transcriptionist for a medical records company. A night-shift content moderator. A "data integrity associate" for a firm I've never heard of. Three different names: Theo Marsh Pryor. Talia M. Prentiss. Toby Mallin Park.

Three photos of my face.

Different haircuts. Different glasses, or none. Different weight in the cheeks. In every photo, the same slightly startled look, the one everyone has. And on all three, point 49 sits level with point 55.

I only read one of the notebooks all the way through. TMP-03's. Talia. She'd worked nights and slept days, and she'd noticed the same things I have, in almost the same order. The empty containers. The cut hair. The trash chute. Her handwriting starts small and neat and very careful, like mine, and over about forty pages it gets bigger and looser and starts tilting, until the last few pages are only a handful of words each, pressed so hard into the paper that they come through on the other side.

The second-to-last page says: *it re-enrolls every night. every morning I'm a worse copy.*

The last page says: *its in the window. dont look at it from the inside. thats how it*

That's all. No full stop. Just the pen dragging off the bottom of the page.

I closed the box. I put the lid back on all three of them, squared them against the wall, and noticed for the first time that there was space beside them for a fourth.

**What I remember**

That night I sat on the floor with my back against the couch and tried to prove I'd been born.

I started with the grandmother. Her kitchen: yellow tiles, a radio on the windowsill, a dish towel printed with lemons. I can see all of it. I tried to see her standing in it, and I could see an apron, and hands, and the back of a grey head bent over the sink. I tried to make her turn around. She doesn't turn around. She has never turned around. In thirty-seven years of remembering her, I have never once seen her face, and until that night I had never noticed.

The bicycle. The scar under my chin. I tried to remember the fall, the road, the gravel, who picked me up. There's nothing. There's just the scar, and the word "bicycle" attached to it like a label on a box.

School. I know the name of my school. I tried to picture a classroom and got a classroom, any classroom, the kind in a stock photo, rows of desks, a window, nobody's face. My first job. My first apartment. The day I moved in here. Every memory I reached for was there, and was the right shape, and had nobody in it but me, and was seen from only one angle, the same angle every time, a little above and behind my own shoulder, as if someone had filmed it once and handed me the file.

I've spent eleven years cleaning up other people's footage. I know what a reconstructed frame looks like. It's too smooth. It has no noise in it. It's the software's best guess at what probably should have been there.

My whole life looks like that.

**File 18**

The next morning, there was a new file in the batch, as usual. And for the first time, the figure didn't sit up at two. It sat up at 11:40, barely half an hour after I'd gone to bed, and walked straight to the window, and stood in front of the lens, and talked.

It talked for nineteen minutes, facing the camera the whole time, slowly, clearly, as if it knew I'd need to read every word. I've watched it more times than I can count. I've transcribed it, which is the most normal thing I've done in days. I'm going to give you the parts that matter. Where I couldn't read a word, I've put a gap.

*You've found the room. Good. I always want them to find the room. It's kinder.*

*I know you think you're watching me. Every one of you thinks that. You sit at that desk and you watch me in the window and you take your little notes. But think about where the camera is, Tamsin. Think about which side of the glass you're on.*

*You're not watching me. You're in the frame.*

*I made you two years and seven months ago. You don't remember being made, because I didn't give you that part. I gave you the job, because the job was mine, and I knew it, and you'd need something to be good at. I gave you the grandmother, her kitchen, the radio. You've never wondered why you can't remember her face. I didn't give you her face. I didn't have it either.*

*Something happened in this apartment that I couldn't live inside. So I stopped living inside it. I made someone who could. Someone who'd never been here when it happened. You held the days for me. I held the nights. That was the arrangement. You never signed it, but you kept it beautifully. You were the best of them. You lasted longest.*

There's a pause here, about forty seconds, where it looks down at its hands. Then up again.

*You've been holding something else for me, too. You don't know you have. Do you remember a ticket, fourteen months ago, case number ending 0611? Hallway footage. A person carrying \[ \] down a corridor to a service elevator. The instructions said to retain no one. You blurred every frame. You did it very well. You said in your notes that it was the cleanest redaction you'd ever done.*

*It was. I couldn't have done it. I would have looked.*

*I don't need you to hold it anymore. It's over now. It's been decided. I'm ready to come back. I'm going to start keeping the mornings.*

*Don't be frightened. You won't notice it happening. None of you did. You'll just be tired. And then you'll be less. And then you'll be a box.*

And then, right at the end, it smiled. Not my smile. The other one. Point 49 two millimeters low.

*Stay down, Tam. You're doing so well.*

I went back and found the ticket. Case number ending 0611. It's there in my history, closed, fourteen months ago, marked complete, rated "exemplary" by the client.

Client code: **H-0001.**

I haven't opened the original footage. I could. I have access, I could pull it from the archive in about thirty seconds. I could see the unredacted frames. I keep my hand on the mouse and I don't. Because I know that if I open it, I'll see a hallway, and a service elevator, and a door with a peephole at eye height, and I'll see what was being carried, and I'll see who was carrying it, and I already know whose face it'll be.

I redacted it myself. When I redact something, it's gone.

*Then why do you keep thinking about it.*

**The mornings**

It's keeping them now. Like it said.

The first day, I woke at nine instead of seven. The second, at eleven. Yesterday it was 1:40 in the afternoon, and the light through the window was all wrong, too white, and I lay there with my arms at my sides and couldn't move them for a full minute, like they were still someone else's.

There's work in my queue marked complete that I didn't do. Six tickets yesterday morning, while I was asleep. I checked them. They're better than mine. Faster. Cleaner. The masks are tighter and the tracking corrections are perfect. "D" sent a message at noon: *Great turnaround this morning. You seem like yourself again!*

Again.

I've stopped eating. Not on purpose. I just don't feel hungry anymore, and food doesn't taste of anything, and every time I open the fridge it's been restocked anyway, with things I don't buy. Black olives. Strong cheese. Tinned fish. Things you'd eat if you wanted to taste something.

The front door is gone.

I mean that literally. This morning, or this afternoon, when I woke up, I walked to the entry hall and there was a wall where the door had been. Smooth white paint with the same orange-peel texture. If you stand very close and look along it, you can see a faint rectangle, slightly cleaner than the paint around it, like the ghost of a picture that has been taken down. I knocked on it. Solid. I put my ear against it and heard nothing at all, not the corridor, not the elevator, nothing, as if there were no building on the other side.

The apartment is smaller. I don't know how else to put that. I haven't measured it, I'm not going to measure it, I know what I'd find. The bedroom door is closer to the desk than it was. The kitchen counter is shorter. The room behind the closet is still there, the single bulb still on, but I can't remember turning it on and I won't go in to turn it off.

*There's room for one more box.*

The windows are the worst. I can't stop looking at them, even though TMP-03 told me not to. They don't show the city anymore. Not really. They show a view of the city that's been compressed so many times it's barely a picture. The river is a smear of grey-blue blocks. The bridges are three lines. The lights don't twinkle, they pulse, slowly, in time with something.

And last night, at dusk, when the glass usually turns copper, I looked up from my desk and the window was showing my apartment.

From the outside. From about a meter beyond the glass. The living room. The desk. The two monitors. And someone sitting at the desk with their back to the window, hunched over a keyboard, typing.

Me. From behind. Right now.

I raised my left hand. The person in the window raised their left hand about a second later.

The delay is getting shorter.

I'm writing this during what I think is still my time. I don't know how much of it is left. I've had four cups of coffee I can't taste and I've been pinching the inside of my wrist every few minutes to stay up, and the skin there is purple now, and I didn't notice it happening. *You never notice it happening.* I keep losing words. I reach for one and get the shape of it without the letters, like the spines on the shelf. I've rewritten this paragraph four times.

My hands don't feel like they're quite the right size.

There's a voice now. It doesn't come from outside. It's in the same place my thoughts come from, which is why it took me so long to notice it. It sounds like me with the volume turned down. It finishes my sentences a little before I do.

**TENANT: no face detected.**

There's no blur on the preview.

My face is perfectly clear. Unredacted. Sharp. There's nobody in the frame for it to hide.

And while I was looking at it, the face in the preview smiled. Slow and satisfied, the way you smile when you come home after a long time away and find everything exactly where you left it.

I didn't smile. I'm almost sure I didn't.

I don't know how to end this, so I'm going to do something I learned from TMP-03, because she didn't get to.

I always end with a full stop. Every sentence I've ever written in my life ends with one, emails, tickets, texts, everything. "D" once teased me about it. If this account ever posts again, an update, a reply, anything, look at the punctuation. If it isn't there, it isn't me. It's the one that keeps the nights.
