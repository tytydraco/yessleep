Simon got the call around midnight. I rolled closer to him, draped my arm across his chest, and listened by the receiver. It was the penitentiary. A guard had discovered Celeste sprawled out on the floor of her cell, cold, pulseless. His mother was dead.

I smiled.

She finally got what she deserved.

When Simon was young, Celeste trapped him in the cellar of his childhood home. 

Confined him to a windowless room with only a mattress, a bucket, and a record player, and every day was identical: listening to the same record on an endless loop, dawn till dusk, from the moment he woke up to the moment he fell asleep. Celeste melted the vinyl before the police could get their hands on it, so only Simon knows what it sounded like. He doesn’t like to talk about it, though, and I love him to death, so I don’t pry.

When the call was over, he threw his feet over the edge of our bed and put his head in his hands. Honestly, I expected indifference. Or joy. Something vaguely positive.

Simon, though…he wept.

Fat tears and convulsive sobs, well into the early morning. I held onto him, silent and warm. Didn’t ask questions. There’d be time for questions later.

Then, the package arrived.

I found it lying on our front stoop. The return address was Simon’s childhood home. Holding the small brown envelope felt like molding a snowball without gloves: a burning cold, phantom needles digging into my palm. I tore it open and pulled out its contents. There was no letter; just an antique cassette player, shaped like a shoebox with sleek gray plastic and a little handlebar below the buttons. I popped the cassette compartment.

Empty.

I slid the player under my armpit and marched over to the garbage can. Simon had endured enough. This thing, whatever it represented, would surely reopen old wounds. I lifted the lid and held the player over the hole, but my fingers refused to let go.

Was this right?

Could I really make this decision for him?

The woman never hurt Simon. Not physically, at least. When the police rescued him, he was healthy. Scared and vitamin D deficient, yes, but well-nourished, hydrated, and intact. Celeste’s public defender argued that Simon could have escaped any time because she kept the door unlocked. Maybe she had been a loving mother, despite her delusions; I had no idea, and I suppose that’s the point. Only Simon and Celeste knew what transpired in that windowless room, but apparently, there’d been enough love to shed tears over.

I sighed, slid the player back under my armpit, and brought the hellish totem into our home.

“So, what is it? I mean, I know *what* it is, but why do you think she had it sent…” Simon asked, carefully setting it onto his desk.

“Couldn’t tell you. Whoever’s managing her affairs didn’t send an explanation, or maybe it’s just coming separately - “

He pressed play.

A *snap* echoed through the room, followed by the mechanical whirring of the flywheels spinning without a tape.

Simon’s demeanor shifted.

He shot upright, rigid, flushed. His expression was a disturbing mixture of elation and terror, a sharp grin with bulging eyes.

“What’s that?” he shouted.

I jumped in place, heart spasming.

“I…I didn’t say anything.”

His gaze darted between the cassette player and me, pupils dilating, spreading over the whites of his eyes like an oil spill.

“I’ll just…” I started, reaching out, “…store this in the attic for now…”

“No!” he screamed, snatching the machine and clutching it against his breastbone, nostrils flaring. The whirring meshed seamlessly with his labored breathing; I couldn’t distinguish the two.

Confusion suddenly clouded his features.

“Sorry, Kat. I’m feeling a bit off.”

He loosened his grip on the machine, but only slightly.

“But…uh…I’ll hold on to this. For now.”

I nodded.

Forced what I can only imagine was the world’s flimsiest smile.

“Yeah, okay. Just…just let me know what you need.” I said, stepping out of his office.

There was a pause, and then he said:

“Right, right. That makes sense.”

He was staring at the cassette player, cradling it in his arms like a newborn.

The next few days, I was nervous. Constantly, incessantly nervous. Simon works from home doing graphic design, but I work nine-to-five at an accounting firm, so every evening I’d be a wreck driving home, terrified about what could be waiting beyond our front door.

But every night, he was normal.

Cooking. Cleaning. Laughing.

Acting like his usual self.

Slowly, with caution, I began to unwind.

Felt like I had my best friend back.

It was nice.

But it was fake.

And with each passing moment, the truth became harder and harder to deny.

Something was wrong.

The changes were small at first, small enough that my conscious mind couldn’t grasp them, but the oldest bits of my nervous system, the prehistoric holdovers, they could sense the danger swelling around me, even if they couldn’t give the danger a name. It wasn’t just Simon, either; the entire house was different. Polluted. Mutating.

Then, one night, I was lying in bed, eyes fluttering, thoughts loose, and a question popped into my brain.

Where *the hell* was the cassette player?

My eyes burst open.

I looked over. Simon was turned away, motionless.

“It’s around,” he groaned.

“…what?”

“The cassette player - it’s around.”

I struggled to breathe. Dread dripped through my lungs like hot tar.

I hadn’t said a damn thing.

“You know, just…don’t worry about it.”

He yawned, shifted, and stilled. I stared at his back, at Simon, this dark statue resting in the blackness, and I wondered:

was he actually sleeping, or was he just pretending?

Were his eyes wide open?

I didn’t check. I couldn’t. I lay awake and listened to him breathe, convincing myself that everything was fine.

Simon wasn’t changing; he was grieving.

I must have asked where the cassette player was out loud.

There wasn’t a soft whirring in the walls. It wasn’t beneath the floorboards, or coming from inside our closet, or radiating from my husband’s skin.

I was just hearing things.

The following morning, I was a mess. I couldn’t think straight. My movements were bipolar: restless and twitchy when the anxiety flared, deadened and lurching when the sleep deprivation took hold. I stumbled through my routine. I reached toward my wardrobe but missed the handle. I nearly killed myself on my way to breakfast, lost in a haze while stepping a little too far forward, the ball of my foot just catching the stair’s edge.

But the strangest part?

The trip from our bedroom to the kitchen felt longer.

I’ve lived in this house since I was a kid, inherited it from my parents when they passed. I *know* the layout. My body has internalized every inch of that place, and I’m telling you, it was longer.

When I got downstairs, he was at the table, stirring a cup of coffee, staring out into space.

“Where is it, Simon?” 

He turned his head and smiled. Revulsion dug its fingers into my heart.

My husband was…different.

His lips were too thin. His usually hooked nose was slightly blunted. His green eyes were unnaturally bright, a synthetic hue, neon and piercing.

“Where’s *what?* Are you alright, Kat? You don’t look - “

“Cut the shit.”

His smile ballooned.

“It’s right there.”

I scoffed, preparing to really lay into him, but then he gestured toward the center of the table, and dear Christ, there it was, whirring, spinning, and empty.

It hadn’t been there a second ago, right?

“You…we need to get rid of that thing, Simon.”

I walked across the kitchen, knelt beside my husband, and placed a delicate hand on his shoulder. Immediately, his demeanor shifted. Arrogant and malicious to scared and harmless in the blink of an eye. Even his facial features reverted to something akin to normal. His body was trembling. I slipped my hand up his neck and pivoted his chin, pulling him toward me. I talked slowly.

“I’m going to take this…thing…away from us.”

He shook his head. His voice was shivering like a lapdog in the snow.

“No, no, no, you can’t do that, I have to listen so we stay together, you can’t throw it away…”

I felt my composure slipping, but I focused. 

“Okay…well…I’ll hide it.” I said, stroking his cheek. 

“Hide it?”

“Yes. I won’t throw it away. I’ll just…put it somewhere safe.”

My hand crept towards the cassette player. He tracked the movement, but I pressed my other hand into his jaw, guiding his attention back to me.

“I’ve always wanted what’s best for you, right?”

As soon as I touched the machine, a jolt of static flooded through my arm, crawling along my bones. 

I did not let go.

“Trust me, love.”

His trembling slowed.

“I won’t throw it away. I promise.”

A lie, but a necessary one.

Gradually, he nodded.

“Yeah…yeah, okay.”

I took a deep breath. 

It was now or never. 

I grabbed the cassette player and headed for the front door, determined to get the machine away from our home.

The *thump* of Simon shooting upright caused my spine to twist. 

Before I could speed up, he brushed past me and stood at the door, positioning his body between me and the exit. 

I recalibrated. 

Took a sharp right turn and began climbing the stairs, gesturing for him to stay put. Thankfully, he obliged. I ascended quickly; the terror was slipping through. By the time I reached our bedroom, I was hyperventilating. 

Where could I hide it? 

I bolted across the room, dropped to my knees, and ripped a small lockbox out from beneath our bed. The player just barely fit inside. I secured the lock and shoved the box back under. A burst of dizziness hit me like a tidal wave. I rested my head on the comforter, letting the sensation run its course.

The lockbox was only a temporary solution, if it could even be considered a solution at all. 

A permanent fix would require incapacitating Simon.

I scrambled to my feet, the fragments of an idea crystallizing in my mind. I pulled my nightstand drawer open and dug through the clutter until I found a bottle of prescription-grade sleeping pills. The label was worn, eroded to the point where I couldn’t read the name of the drug, but I gave it a quick shake and the contents rattled. Plenty left. I unscrewed the cap and dropped the key to the lockbox inside the bottle. 

When I returned downstairs, Simon was still standing at the door. I attempted to focus on the task at hand; he’d only lower his guard if I acted normal. 

Still, I felt my thoughts drifting. I couldn’t help it. 

My mind kept replaying the two trips: from the foyer to the bedroom, and from the bedroom back down to the foyer.

They were different.

The second trip was much, much longer. 

The upstairs hallway seemed to expand along with my movements, unfurling before my eyes, silently adding more doors and floorboards with each step, as if the invading presence had become tired of hiding or no longer felt it necessary. I’ve never experienced fear like that before. It was primal. All-consuming. But when I finally made it back to the foyer, I saw Simon, and I swallowed the fear.

I would not let it take him.

So, I forced a smile, declared I’d be calling out sick from work, took his hand, and pulled him into the living room. 

We spent the day watching old spy thrillers on the couch. During that time, I noticed that contact had a sort of grounding effect on Simon. As long as I was touching him, he seemed to remain normal, his eyes their regular greenish hue. I was his anchor. 

But the measure was just a stopgap. 

As soon as I let go, his body would begin to distort, and the breathless whirring of the cassette player would resume, swirling around us like invisible smoke.

Dusk came faster than I expected. 

The pills were burning a hole in my pocket. 

I needed to act before it was too late.

I excused myself and began walking toward the kitchen, fast but not hurried, carefully maintaining the veneer of normality. I peeked over my shoulder. He was already changing. His lips were visibly thinning. Deep wrinkles accumulated across his face, clawing through his skin. He didn’t move from the couch, but he kept his bright eyes glued to me. Once out of sight, my pace turned frantic. I rushed over to our liquor cabinet and threw it open.

I staggered back, gripping my chest, disbelief surging through my veins.

The cabinet was empty, and it was dark.

The inside seemed to go on for miles.

A musty stench slinked from the crevice, and if I squinted, if I really concentrated, I could appreciate something distant in the darkness, dragging itself closer -

I slammed it shut.

“You alright in there?” he called out.

“Y-yes!” I shouted, beating back the nausea.

I stood before a massive wall of small doors that shouldn’t have been able to fit in my kitchen. The cabinets were multiplying. The house was changing. I looked up. The ceiling hadn’t grown to accommodate the changes, but when I looked back at the wall, it somehow appeared even larger than before. I couldn’t understand. I keeled over, clutching my temple. My brain was boiling. I fought the urge to tear at my skin. 

Footsteps began emanating from the living room, thunderous but languid. The overhead lights flickered. Bulbs sizzled and popped, but they did not dim; their exposed nerves kept firing, battering me with harsh phosphorescence.

“You alright in there, Kat?” His voice was twenty octaves too low.

I grunted, steeling myself, ripping open cabinet after cabinet, discovering only darkness, hole after hole, a bevy of black pores defiling my childhood home.

Simon was closing in. 

His footsteps had become deafening. 

I opened what felt like the hundredth door.

And I lucked out.

I grabbed a half-filled whiskey bottle by its neck.

“Are you suuuuure you’re alright in there, Kat?”

Simon began to round the corner.

I didn’t look.

I didn’t hesitate.

I aimed the bottle where I imagined his head was about to be, and I swung.

A sickening *crash* echoed through the air,

and everything stopped.

Simon collapsed.

The kitchen was plunged into darkness.

Lines of sticky warmth drizzled across my wrist.

I let go. The shattered bottle clattered against the tiled floor. 

I looked around. The cabinets were normal. The lights had quieted. And my husband lay in front of me, motionless, his hair slick, glistening with blood and booze.

Muted sobs bubbled up my throat. I knelt down. His breathing was shallow, but it was there.

Stepping over him was agonizing.

I couldn’t stop, though.

This was my chance.

I bolted through the foyer and up the stairs, dialing *911* on my cell as I ran. The dispatcher’s voice crackled through the receiver. I screamed that my husband needed an ambulance as I barreled down the second-floor hallway.

*What was I going to do with the accursed thing?*

I tore the lockbox from its hiding place and threw it onto the bed.

*Bury it?*

*Burn it?*

*Hand it to a priest?*

I ripped the pill bottle from my pocket and unscrewed the cap, dumping its contents into my shaking hands. Tablets ricocheted off my damp palm, then the key. I jammed it into the lockbox.

*Right now, it doesn’t matter. I don’t need a plan. I’ll figure it out once I’ve gotten the machine away from Simon.*

The lid creaked open.

There was nothing inside. 

“Mom said you’d try to do that.”

Simon loomed in the doorway, naked as the day he was born, dried blood staining his cheeks. The cassette player was resting against his sternum. He had his arms wrapped tightly around the machine, which was still empty, playing nothing, whirring ceaselessly, whispering terrible things I couldn’t hear.  

“I didn’t want to believe her.” He sniffled. His eyes were glassy and burning bright green.

“I said: 'YOU don’t know Kat. She wouldn’t lie to me.'” 

He uncrossed his arms. The machine did not fall. It did not move an inch. Moonlight streamed from a nearby window, illuminating him. 

The cassette player was fused to his chest. 

Thin red vessels swam through mounds of cauterized skin, adjoining flesh and plastic, stitching it all together.

“But here you are, dead set on throwing her away, when you promised me you wouldn’t.”

“Simon…I…”

“Don’t even say it!” he screamed.

I crept forward, reaching out. Contact had brought him back before.

“I can still fix this…” I whimpered.

He retreated through the doorway, backpedaling with small, scurrying movements, never breaking eye contact, never blinking.

I followed, gaining speed.

“Simon! Please, God, wait!”

Halfway down the hall, he twisted his arm behind his back and clasped the handle of a tiny linen closet. My legs pumped wildly. My heart struck a desperate message against my rib cage. 

I was his anchor. 

I could bring him back.

I just needed to get to him. 

The door creaked open. He squeezed inside.

I angled my outstretched hand, grasping for Simon.

My fingers grazed the handle’s cold metal. 

The door shut.

I threw it back open.

I should’ve seen my husband, contorted into a space no larger than a high school locker.

Instead, I saw stairs. 

They were faintly visible in the hallway’s moonlight. The silver outlines of wooden planks descending into darkness, accompanied by a splinter-infested handrail. A cool draft seeped from the opening. The air smelled of mud and rot. I knew what I was looking at. Implicitly, I understood what was beyond the door.

A cellar.

If I entered the darkness, if I worked my way down these stairs, I’d surely find a windowless room at the bottom.

A honey-sweet noise emanated from deep within, so quiet that it was nearly imperceptible. It coaxed me over the partition. My head couldn’t have been more than a few centimeters over the edge, but that was enough, I heard the music so clearly, and I saw her, a wide-eyed face etched into the blackness, brimming with a gentleness that could easily be confused for cruelty. 

Celeste began to race up the stairs. 

I reflexively jerked back and slammed the door shut.

I stood there for a moment, frozen, entangled in a perfect silence. 

Slowly, I opened the door.

All I saw were a few stacks of neatly folded pillowcases.

The whirring was gone.

Simon was gone.

Some time later, sirens blared in the distance. 

The ambulance had finally arrived.

When the medics asked where my husband was, I didn’t have the willpower to lie. I just pointed to the front door and then sat back down on the curb. 

So, they searched the house. 

Didn’t find anyone, of course. 

Simon’s blood was still in the kitchen, but it wasn’t fresh; it looked like an old stain, something that’d occurred months or even years before that night. I assumed that stain would get me arrested for Simon’s murder. It was still his blood, after all.

But then they asked me one simple question:

“What’d you say your husband’s name was, ma’am?”

“It’s - “

I paused.

I tried again. 

“His name is - “

Nothing.

I racked my brain. Scoured my memories.

No matter how hard I tried, though, I couldn’t come up with his name. 

I can see his face, clear as day, but his name? 

It’s gone. 

Vanished along with him, his mother, and any proof he ever existed to begin with. I have no photographs of him. His side of the closet is empty. “Simon” is just a placeholder; the label eases the pain. Whatever his name was, all I have left of him is a stain and some memories. The memories of our lives together. Of a pitch-black cellar. Of a song oozing from the darkness. I only heard a second of it, but the song is indescribably beautiful. Imagine the sound of your child taking their first breath, multiply that joy by a thousand, and maybe you’ll scratch the surface of what I experienced. 

I can still hear it, too. 

The song drifts through my vacant home, curling around the ruins of my life, taunting me, testing my limits, but I will not fail him, not again.

So, every night, I stand in front of the linen closet, terrified and penitent. 

I beg.

I open the door.

I shut it.

I wait. 

I beg.

I open the door.

I shut it.

And I know one day, they’ll take me. I just have to be patient.
