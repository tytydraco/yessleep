*My grandma died recently and I was left to take care of all her junk. Which was fine by me as I could snoop around old diaries and reminisce over old photos. But, I found something I'm compelled to share with you all. Its a page from what I believe to be my great grandfathers journal. For privacy reasons, I changed and left out certain names he wrote. The writing was in cursive so I did my best to decipher it, so sorry for any errors.*

I suppose to begin, I must state: I am not a writer, nor do I plan to be one. To put it simply, I find the activity to be rather burdensome. I’d rather leave my thoughts to collect dust in the empty attic of my mind. But something happened to me today, something that compels me to…dissect an older memory.

When I was younger, 12 to be exact, my friend Erik and I thought ourselves to be great explorers. Often roaming around the lofty mountains or the abandoned farmlands that rotted in the barren fields. We’d hunt gophers with slingshots, swim in rivers, and see who could climb the highest on Ol’ Henry. The last venture, however, was quickly banned after Erik had slipped and fallen to the earth. I can still recall the sound of his arm snapping on impact. I also recall how I had beaten his old climbing record and was sure to tell him after the doctor set his arm.

Our mothers had chewed our ears off and forbade us from ever climbing the old white pine. We quickly nodded and obeyed them for a full three months until Erik's arm was healed, and he demanded a rematch.

Despite my clear recollection of the event, I can only remember it took place in early autumn.

“What do you and Erik plan on doing?” My mother asked that fateful day.

I rolled my eyes, but my mom didn’t notice. She was too busy trying to break the tough bread to dip in her soup.

Me and Erik had planned to meet at Ol’ Henry, not to disobey our mothers this time. No. We had it in our minds to play mountain men and catch ourselves a deer. Gone were the juvenile days of pebbles and gophers. We were going to prove ourselves to be men. I didn’t have a father to take me hunting, and Erik's father was too busy playing business with the other hoity toity men in our small town.

No matter. We could learn ourselves. We had read plenty of cowboy books and used instructions from an old survival handibook to fashion ourselves bows and arrows.

“We're going deer hunting, Mom, I told you a hundred times already!”

“Will.” Her head shot up, brown eyes piercing and alight with a threat. *You want to try that again*, they said.

I felt my ears redden. “Uh- I- Me and Erik are going deer hunting.”

She continued to study my face, but I quickly broke eye contact and stared at my own soup. Swirling the potatoes around the creamy mixture with my silver spoon. The sound of metal on porcelain echoed throughout the small cabin.

“Hm.” My mother hummed. “Just be home before sunset. And if it rains-”

“I'll come back. I know, I know.”

My mother nodded, and we continued to eat in silence.

Once I finished, I cleaned my bowl and quickly made my way out the door. As I passed my mom in the kitchen, I gave a quick goodbye. Her mouth was full of stale bread, so she could only wave. I wish I had said more. But I was too excited. I ran with the energy of a boy ready to prove himself. Erik and I were going to kill a deer and bring it home for our mothers. We agreed to split the meat and the antlers. Erik wanted the skull, and I agreed so long as I got the pelt. 

By the time I had made it to the canyon's mouth, my cheeks ached from a constant smile. A cold wind blew down from the entrance, rustling the golden leaves of the cottonwoods. The wind found my face, pinching my nose and cheeks like a presumptuous old lady. The clouds above were still gray and draped upon the mountain tops like a thick blanket. I ignored the sight; the beauty I had grown used to was easy to ignore.

I had continued my jog until my shoe got caught in a particular deep patch of mud and nearly sent me prone. Patience was never my strong suit, but I would never live it down if I met up with Erik caked in mud. I shifted the bow on my shoulder and brought my satchel of arrows to the side of my hip and continued along the narrow path. Bushes brushed against me, dampening the sleeves of my thick sweater. My boots collected the mud like my mother does tea sets, and much of it was kicked up onto the back of my pant legs.

I was becoming bored. I was maybe a mile ‘til.

I started peeling my ears to the sounds around me. The river that had carved the canyon babbled somewhere to my left. It wasn't tremendously impressive, even with the recent rain. There was a symphony of bird calls, and a cacophony of crackling leaves and crossed squirrels. I remember hearing a particular bird call, one I knew well. A simple whistle, a quick rise and fall in tune, it was the black capped chickadee. Lovely little bird. I took to starting a conversation with the creature as I walked. When it called, I’d mimic it. *Springs here,* was how my mother taught me.

*Springs here,* the bird's voice had grown closer.

“Springs here,” I replied.

We continued until I was sure the chickadee had to be in the branches about me. But the small bird remained hidden in the canopy of warm orange leaves.

“Springs here”

I waited a moment, and then another. Nothing.

As a matter of fact, everything was hushed. The wind blew softer, the trees only whispered, and the animals were silent. Silence was always dangerous in the forest. It meant a threat was about, prowling, hunting. I froze.

Slowly, I turned in a circle. Letting my eyes dart between the web of branches. Jumping whenever the wind blew a tree too roughly, or a sound I swore to be a snapping branch boomed in the quiet. The minute crept slowly like a snail escaping a jar of molasses.

*Spri-prings h…ere.*

The sound tripped my heart's steady beat. But like a conductor tapping his baton, the whistle restarted the forest orchestra. Magpies continued their squabbles and squirrels resumed their scurrying. Everything was back to normal. Almost.

*Sp-rings he-re.*

I didn't respond. I justified it by saying I was simply bored, not wanting to admit to myself I was scared of a bird. Men weren’t so easily scared.

​

By the time I'd made it to ol’ Henry, I had long forgotten my unease. Erik was already there, hanging upside down in the tree. He swung precariously over the pine roots, which spread across the forest floor like lightning. It was these roots that made the tree the perfect meeting spot, as they prevented anything from growing within the radius, giving us plenty of space to play. The wide branches gave us shade in the summer and shelter in the winter.

“Finally!” Eric cried, dropping from the branch and landing with all the grace of a newborn fawn. “I’ve been waiting for hours!”

“No, you haven’t,” I said defensively.

“Ya-huh.”

“You said we’d meet after lunch.”

“No, I said during!” Erik huffed. He folded his arms over his thick wool coat. That, along with his scarf, told me his mom had more than a sway on his outfit then he. “I brought you a sandwich and everything.”

“Well, I’ll still eat it,” I said.

“I already did.”

I punched his shoulder, and he punched mine back.

“Did you see any deer on your way up?”

Erik shook his head. “No, I even walked along the river.”

“Did you hear anything…weird?” I asked, trying to keep a laissez-faire tone.

“No. But the river was loud.” Eric walked around Ol’ Henry and picked up his bow where it lay camouflaged with the other branches. “Why?”

“Oh, I just heard something, but it doesn’t matter.” My fears felt just as stupid on my tongue as they did in my head. 

Erik fidgeted with his bow and placed his sling bag awkwardly over his shoulder, adjusting it so none of the arrows poked or proded his side. He didn’t seem to register my vulnerability since he didn't have a jab at the ready.

He gave a proud huff and nod once everything seemed right. Then, turning to me, “*Alright, partner, we’re losing daylight*!” He said with a Southern twang, mimicking ‘Two Life’ Jim from our favorite cowboy book.

“*Let's go find ourselves a buck!”* I responded, echoing the accent.

The two of us made our way down to the river, wading along its bed. There was no plan, nor strategy. I'm sure a quadriplegic raccoon with a slingshot would have had better luck than us.

“What's the biggest animal you could beat in a fight?” Erik asked.

“Hmmm.” I contemplated, picking up a stick and swinging it back and forth. “I don’t know. It depends. Do I have a weapon?”

Erik thought only a moment, obviously having not thought too deeply about his own hypothetical. “I say…Recess rules.”

“What if I brought a knife to recess?”

“No weapons. You know Sister Braithwaite would find that knife quicker then she’d sniff out the devil. Then she’d beat your knuckles red and blue.”

“It's black and blue.”

“Not if you're bleeding.”

“Ok, recess rules, no weapons…” I continued to think. Swinging the stick, back and forth, back and forth. “I think maybe a coyote, or a bobcat. Bobcats aren’t that big, right?”

“No bigger than a hunting dog.”

“Then a bobcat,” I declared confidently.

“Really? Not even like a horse?” Erik asked.

“No way! Didn’t you hear what that horse did to Mr Mayberry?”

“No. What happened?”

“It kicked his chest in; my mom told my aunt it killed him instantly. Instantly!” I chucked the stick towards the river and watched it get whisked away.

“Hm.”

“What about you?”

“A mountain lion.” He answered. Obviously having thought deeply about his answer.

I laughed, “No way!”

“I could! All I would have to do is get all big and scary, maybe get a stick and wave it around-” he picked up his own branch and began to swig it like I’d done earlier..”-maybe poke it once or twice and then it’ll run off.”

“Well, maybe, but didn’t you say beat?”

“Yeah, but you don’t have to kill something to beat it. You just need to get away!”

“Oh… Well then maybe I could take a horse.”

“I’d get a spot for you right next to Mr Mayberry.”

We laughed and continued to chat to one another, hoping to happen upon a buck. But our boyish voices, loud and shrill, scared any creature within a 5-mile radius. Not to mention we walked downhill, the wind blowing our scent and acting as yet another warning sign. But I digress.

We were simply boys wishing to prove ourselves to our imaginary judges. I to my mother, who has proven to love me no matter what, and Erik, whose father saw the boy the same way one saw dirt on their dress shoes

“I don’t understand; it's autumn. Don’t they come down in autumn?” Erik whined. We had been wandering for a while now. I felt a deep chill in my bones, regretting not dressing like Erik, who hopped around rock to rock. Seemingly unaffected, while I trembled in my sweater and overalls.

“Maybe we should head home, come back tomorrow. They’re probably just hiding after the rain.”

Erik huffed, looking ahead, his pale eyebrows pinching together. He obviously disagreed but was finding the best way to dissuade me.

“How bout we split up for an hour and then, if we don’t find any tracks, we meet back up at ol’ Henry?”

I wrinkled my nose. Even with my underdeveloped brain, I could see that his plan was flawed. Though I lacked the emotional maturity to understand why he was so desperate to continue this hunt.

“I want to go home, Erik.” Unlike him, the wind had long since left my sails. My excitement gone as soon as I found the task to require patience.

“Oh, come on, Will! Quite acting so yellow bellied,” Erik snapped.

I felt heat rise up my neck. “I’m not!”

“Then prove it! You’re- you’re all talk, but you never *do* anything!”

I couldn’t even fathom where this attitude had come from, so I took to clever retorts.

“I do not!”

“You said you could beat up Thomas, but- but you ran. You ran away!” Eric placed his hands on his hips. “Now we're *both* the saps at school.”

“You're still upset about that?”

“Yes! I looked stupid in front of the whole class. Plus the shiner Thomas gave me really hurt.” He rubbed his eye, as if it still did.

“I really am sorry about that, but it was one time-”

“Nuh-uh. Sawmill.”

I folded my arms protectively over my chest. “You said you wouldn’t bring that up.”

“And you promised we’d hunt deer.”

I couldn’t deny that. “I'm not a yellow belly!”

“Then act like it!”

“Fine!” I said, stomping away.

“And don’t be late this time.”

I waved my hand at him, grumbling as I walked up river and Erik continued his way down. The wind was stronger now, stinging my eyes and bringing them to tears. I decided to take my chances in the brush. Both to avoid the wind and maybe find some old trail. Maybe find a deer. Maybe shoot it. Maybe brag about it to Erik. Prove myself. I wasn’t a coward.

I remember always being jealous of Erik, despite loving him like a brother. He was tall and attractive in the eyes of the other schoolmates. He had the curly golden hair and bright blue eyes one would see in the angel paintings at our church. No angels ever looked like me. He was smarter than me, richer than me and–most painful of all– braver than I ever would ever be. I still think that. Erik is a good man, and while I blamed him for so long for keeping me in the woods, I know now it was my own self consciousness.

I scoured the woods for any sign of an animal. I’d stare at broken twigs, indents in the earth, suspicious rock piles. Anything. I even stared at a rabbit skeleton. Looking at its skull which was missing its lower jaw and a few teeth. I willed its bones to give me some hint. But of course, nothing.

I began to pick up old sea shells, the white swirls easy to spot amongst the brown. My mother had told me this land had once been a great ocean, long before our ancestors had settled here. She never talked much about “our people” but I never asked. I hated the sadness that stained her eyes anytime she gave their stories. Maybe these shells would make her smile.

I was picking up my 5th shell when my eyes spotted a familiar bean-like pile. Scatt! I quickly knelt down, ignoring the mud seeping through the denim. The dung wasn’t too wet, so it had to be recent. I scanned the ground for any indents, smiling when I saw hoof prints. Eureka!

Slowly, quietly, I followed the trail. Excitement was again invigorating me.

It wasn’t long before the trees thinned out and I found myself in a large clearing with reeds and grasses reaching my knees. Plenty of room for grazing. The deer had to be close! I took to squatting and waiting, mimicking the cowboys in my book. I let my eyes dart across the field, scanning for any movement in the grass or treeline. Nothing.

I glanced down at the hoof print, hoping it held the answer. It was then I noticed the blood. The small speckles were bright and now impossible to ignore.

“What?” I muttered under my breath.

Slowly, I stood, eyes locking in on the next splatter of blood just ahead of me. I followed the new trail further and further into the clearing. The amount only grew until I found its source.

A deer. *The* deer.

Well, it wasn’t anymore. Now it looked more like a hunk of meat that lost a battle to a shotgun and had four men line dance on its corpse. Ribbons of flesh dangled from its protruding bones. Its legs were twisted at such an odd angle, I could only guess which were the front or back ones. Its innards were empty, as if vultures had long since scavenged despite the carcasses' freshness. I couldn't tell where its neck was, but I found its head a few feet away. It looked as if it was ripped clean off, though his antlers remained. 8 points, nice and healthy. A shame. The head seemed to be the most intact part of the deer, except for the fact that it didn’t have its lower jaw, only dangling muscle and patches of fur. It was likely somewhere else in the field.  Coyotes seemed to be the most logical culprit. They like to play with their food, which is probably why the deer's body looked so bad compared to its head. It was a simple and logical explanation.

So why did something feel off?

I stared at the poor creature's head, avoiding the lifeless eyes that seemed to stare back. The gears in my head turned, turned with what little wisdom I had. Eventually it clicked.

Despite the gore and mud, there were no signs of coyotes. No paw prints, no fur, no patches of flattened grass where a group would lie. If there was only one, maybe it could hide its trace better. But I didn’t think a single coyote could take a young buck. Maybe the buck was sick?

A cry cut off my thoughts. The sound was short, guttural, and not human, like a crow's squawk. Definitely a deer's warning cry. Maybe one from the bucks' herd, warning me to back away.

It gave another cry, and I could tell it was coming from the trees just ahead of me. I shrugged my bow off my shoulders and knocked an arrow, an action that took much longer than what I had in mind.

The loud sound echoed through the clearing again. And again. And again. Each time, the gap between the cries shortened. This made something very clear to me. The sound never changed in pitch. Never changed in length. Never wavered. It was as if it was on repeat. I stopped my pursuit, but it was too late.

From the treeline, a figure ran. A human figure. It appeared naked except for what appeared to be a thick white shawl. I stood frozen to the ground, not able to do anything but stare as the creature made its way towards me. It stumbled, but not once did it stop its pursuit. I knew it wouldn’t stop its pursuit. I knew it wanted me.

That's all the motivation I needed.

I turned and ran. Pumping my arms and quickening my pace. I could hear its footsteps gaining on me and what sounded like a bunch of wooden spoons clacking into one another. My heart pounded its way up my throat as the horrible feeling of something behind me shocked my muscles. The same feeling that was only a whisper you hear when alone in the woods or in a dark house, now was a siren in my head.

It's behind you. It's behind you. Run. Run!

“Erik! Erik!” I cried in desperation. My voice shrilling and cracking like embers to a dying flame. “Mom!”

I was nearing the forest's edge when a force hit my back like a speeding train. I fell to the ground, pain exploding in my face as it met the earth below. I gave out a desperate sob as I wriggled and squirmed, but it was all hopeless against the pressure on my back. It became clear to me at that moment that I would die. I couldn’t move, I couldn’t fight; I would die here. I let out another sob when I felt a big hand clutch my shoulder, the pressure releasing from my back for only a moment as *it* flipped me over.

I will never forget its face.

It was like the beginning of an awful clay sculpture of a man. Chunks of flesh stretched unnaturally over its skull, eyes seeming to fall out of its loose eye sockets. It had no lips, exposing red gums and the whitest teeth I had ever seen; its lower jaw was held by two strips of wet flesh. It wore what can only be described as a shawl of jaw bones and teeth that ran all the way down to its stomach. Pieces of flesh and tendons were braided like ropes and used to tie the bones together.

His complexion was ashen, like an old corpse, but his body looked well fed.

His dark irises bored into me, his head cocking as if waiting for something. I stared back, vision blurred from my tears. I never prayed more earnestly than I did in that meadow. I prayed for the vile creature to leave me alone. I prayed for Erik. I prayed for my mother. Help her move on from losing her son.

It was then that what little hope I had in God left my soul, like a candle being snuffed by an exhale.

The damn thing opened its mouth, its shawl clanking against itself. Out of his mouth came a scream. Not a scream of threat and violence as you’d expect from a predator. But a young girl.

“*MAMA*!” It sobbed. “*MAMA HELP! MAMA I DON'T WANT TO DIE*!”

Over and over, the voice rang. Like the deer's cry, it seemed to be on a loop. Same words. Same emotion. Same vocal crack on the word help. But despite the emotion, the thing's face didn’t move. Not even its mouth.

After a few repeats, its real mouth fell open, and it let out a pained gasp before shaking its head, and a new voice rang out.

“*What is that? John! John, RUN!*”

It continued its call for a shorter period before, once again, giving a pained gasp and releasing a new voice.

This one was a bird, the tune clearer and ever familiar.

*Springs here.*

*Springs here*.

I began to hiccup, the sobs wracking my body painfully as I finally spoke.

“What do you want? What do you want?” I cried.

My words were like a match to a puddle of oil. A flame of malice alit in the creature's eyes, and its hands shot to my face.

Hot red pain overtook every thought as the creature dug into my flesh, slicing my skin like warm butter. The sound was like that of biting into an orange, wet and loud. I felt its fingers dig into my mouth, pulling at my teeth. Some were pulled out as easily as one would pull a weed from a garden. I knew what it wanted, even in my pained delirium. He yanked at my jaw, an electric shock temporarily replacing the sharp stings around my mouth and gums. I heard a crack, certain the thing had finally got what it wanted. 

A veil seemed to separate my mind from my body, muffling the pain. But through my ringing ears, I heard the creature use various voices to scream. I felt something tug at my arm, and I fought back in a feverish panic until a familiar voice pulled me from my fog.

“Come on, Will! Go, go, go, go!”

I could only groan as Erik helped me to my feet, and together we ran out of the field. Or more so, he ran and dragged my limp body along with him. I couldn’t register what had happened, or what was happening. Past and present muddling together in a slurry of pain and confusion. I could feel my consciousness failing. The edges of my vision were darkening. My mind was drifting. I didn’t remember why me and Erik were running. But I knew I shouldn’t stop.

I don't remember fainting, only the sound of my name being screamed from Erik's lips.

The rest of the events were shared with me by Erik and my mother.

When I had lost consciousness, Erik lugged me over his shoulders with his skinny, boyish arms. I don’t know how he was able to pick me up, as I was a full head taller than him at the time. But I will forever be indebted to him. Erik had heard my cries and raced to me, finding me in the clearing with that thing. He told me, without hesitation, that he jumped on the figure and drove his bow into its stomach.

He carried me all the way to my house and fetched the doctors while my mom did her best to stop the bleeding. She said she couldn’t even recognize me. My face just a mess of flesh and blood. The doctors came and sutured my face enough for me to look somewhat human. But it took me a week to gain consciousness, and even then, all my memories seem to be wrapped in a thin layer of silk. I can make out certain shapes, but the details evade me.

It took me a few days before I could remain awake without morphine to dull the pain. It took me weeks to get over the fact that I couldn’t produce a single sound. I’d cry and beg, but only chokes and gasps escaped. The *thing* never needed my jaw, even though it tried. I can still remember how it tugged and pulled.

After a month, I was nearly normal. Only tainted by the trauma of what I faced.

No one believed what me and Erik saw. They assumed some crazy backcountry fellow saw an innocent child in a forest and decided to attack. The doctors guessed my muteness was a result of a combination of brain and mental trauma. Promised me their bottom dollar that I would speak again. Still waiting for that payout.

I don’t think it was a man. No, I know it wasn’t a man, and Erik did too.

We grew apart, the two of us. Him because he couldn’t separate the bloody mess on the meadow floor from the scarred boy before him. And I because I didn’t make any effort to prove him wrong. I distanced myself from everyone while simultaneously trying to force things back into my comfortable mold of normalcy. But it was like shaping a rock with a cookie cutter.

My mother and I switched roles. She worked while I remained the homemaker, no one wishing to have a dumb, ugly boy as their employee.

Years passed, and I grew older.

I found a girl who was ok with the scars and silence, and we married. I built us a house far away from the canyon. Happy to finally be able to afford to get away. My mother came with us, helping raise our two children. I began a carpentry business, making cabinets and such, and my wife and mother helped around the house. For the first time in a while, I felt content.

Until today.

It happened during dinner. My wife prepared a delicious meal with the ingredients from my mother's garden. While my wife and mother chatted, I noticed my son and daughter were playing with their food. They looked as upset as they did when I didn’t let them have a second slice of cake.

I asked them what was wrong, using a language my mother and I had made up involving various hand signals. My children looked at one another when my daughter spoke up.

“We went in the woods today.” She confessed.

The table grew silent as we all stared. It was a rule that they weren’t allowed anywhere near the mountains without an adult. My wife was just about to chastise them when my son spoke up.

“We know we weren’t supposed to and were sorry, but we heard something and-” he

trailed off, looking rather pale.

“What was it?” my mother inquired gently.

“We heard a boy; he was screaming. I think he was hurt, but we couldn’t find him anywhere,” he replied. Voice trembling

My daughter, with tears in her eyes, looked to us and, through hiccups, said, “He just kept screaming, ‘*What do you want? What do you want?’”.*
