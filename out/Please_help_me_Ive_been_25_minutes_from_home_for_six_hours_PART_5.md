“I’ve waiting your whole life to meet you, kiddo.”

Murmur stopped so abruptly that the leash pulled against my wrist.

The man stood just beyond the reach of the parking lot lights. I could see he was wearing a black “armor” motorcycle jacket, dark pants, and his hands were in his front pockets.

After everything I’d seen, a person moving should have been a relief.

Instead, I found myself watching his shoulders. Waiting to see whether his head would turn without them.

“Stay there or I will fucking shoot, I swear to god fucking test me”.

He stopped.

“Who the fuck are you?”

“Cherinko.”

I waited for the rest.

He somehow felt that covered all my possible questions..

“Is that a first name? A last name?”

“I’m Jeffrey Cherinko but my friends call me by my last name, and we are friends.”

The name bothered me before I understood why. There was something familiar about it.

Then I thought of the tattoo on my ankle.

He glanced down at it as if he had heard my thought in that moment.

“Good,” he said. “You’re starting to put things together.”

“No. I’m starting to decide whether I need to run or cap you”

Murmur pressed against my shin. He wasn’t barking. He stared at the man with his ears drawn back and his mouth firmly closed.

“I’d like to walk with you. Explain a few things, if it helps you can point that gun at me the entire time”

“Explain them here in the well lit parking lot”

He looked toward the parked truck, then at the phone in my other hand.

His expression changed.

“Somewhere quieter.”  
   
“And who the fuck are you worried will hear us, I don’t know if you noticed but the world has paused.”

There was no traffic. No conversation. Just wind moving through the trees and Murmur’s nails scraping once against the pavement.

“What part of this is loud?”

“Bring the dog,” he said.

Then he turned and walked toward the overlook.

I stayed where I was long enough for him to stop and look back.

I wish I could tell you I made a smart, carefully reasoned decision to follow him.

Mostly, I couldn’t bear the thought of watching the only other moving human being I’d found disappear into the dark.

So I followed.

Far enough behind that he couldn’t grab me without giving me a head start.

He stopped near a stretch of rock overlooking Tucson.

The city was spread below us in thousands of lights. From up there, it looked alive. People should have been sitting in traffic, arguing over dinner, doing mundane people things. I wondered how many of those lights were shining on people who hadn’t moved in days.

Cherinko stepped closer to the edge.

I didn’t.

Absolutely fucking not.

I’d survived the hospital. I wasn’t about to die because a mystery mountain man wanted to show me a view.

He noticed the distance between us.

“Comfortable there?”

“Delighted actually”

He slid one hand toward his jacket pocket.

“Don’t.”

He stopped immediately, then withdrew his hand slowly, empty.

“All right.”

My heartbeat was so hard I could feel it in my hands and feet. I kept trying to work out the distance to the truck without taking my eyes off him.

Could I get Murmur back there fast enough? Would I have to pick him up?

What if shooting him didn’t do anything?

“I’m a program manager,” he said.

That was such an ordinary sounding answer that I laughed.

“And what the actual fuck does that even mean?”

“For a medical research program within IARPA.”

I stared at him.

“I-A-R-P-A,” he added.

“Spelling it isn’t helping, what is IARPA?”

“Intelligence Advanced Research Projects Activity.”

“Great. Government. That really clears up why I found a room full of tortured dogs.”

He glanced at Murmur.

I shifted in front of him.

“Don’t look at my dog.”

Cherinko returned his attention to me.

“I’m surprised you don’t recognize the name. Given your time in Reston.”

The wind caught the back of my shirt.

For a moment, I barely felt it.

“What?”

“Reston, Virginia.”

“I know where Reston is, how the hell do you know I was in Reston?”

I didn’t like how patiently he waited. He stood there with a slightly smug smirk on his face.

“Ryan,” I said.

He nodded.

“What does Ryan have to do with this?”

“What did he tell you he did?”

“He was a contractor. An ex-Marine. MARSOC guy. He worked on directed-energy weapons.”

“Did you know who he made them for?”

“He was a contractor,” I repeated, louder this time, as though the second attempt might make it mean something different.

“That describes an employment arrangement. It doesn’t tell you who benefits from the work.”

I looked out at the city, then immediately back at him. I didn’t want him out of my sight.

“Are you telling me my ex-boyfriend was part of Lighthouse?”

“Yes.”

“Did he know that?”

“Not necessarily.”

“Oh, good. So you can join this fucking thing accidentally.”

“Most people contribute to something smaller than the whole. A contract. A study. A piece of software. Sometimes themselves.”

“And what was I? His fucking assignment?”

Cherinko was quiet for a moment.

Then he asked, “Do you remember matching with him?

I remembered.

I remembered sitting on my phone, swiping through faces with the detached despair that dating apps tend to create in a person.

I remembered Ryan’s profile, and not being able to clear his card from the deck. Everytime I swiped it off, it popped back up. I wanted to go to the next person so I finally swiped right and it matched.

“I thought the app was glitching.”

“What happened when you swiped left?”

“He was still there.”

I’d thought it was funny once we started talking.

A stupid little story about how persistent his profile had been. How even Tinder apparently thought I should give him a chance.

I felt something turn over in my stomach.

“You did that?”

“Your profiles were adjusted.”

“Adjusted how?”

“Enough to make each of you more likely to respond to the other.”

“And if I didn’t?”

“You remember what happened.”

I stared at him.

There was a moment where I tried to pretend this all wasn’t real. Just a coincidence somebody had learned about and was now using to scare me.

Then he said, “The same thing happened on his end.”

The wind kept moving around us.

“You kept putting us back.”

“Yes.”

I gave this horrible little laugh that didn’t feel like it belonged to me.

“You fucking arranged a relationship through Tinder? Wow some real hardcore top secret government shit.”

“We created an opportunity for contact.”

“Oh, go fuck yourself, this is insane, you’re fucking insane”

He didn’t react.

“What was the point?”

“We needed you in Virginia. It was time for you to progress. We needed you watched and safe and we needed sustained proximity between your device and his.”

I looked at my phone.

All those nights it had sat beside his on a kitchen counter. Charging near his bed. Connected to the same Wi-Fi.

“What did the phones do?”

“Established continuity. Allowed observation. There were things we needed to monitor over time.”

“What kind of vague ass answer is that? What things?”

“You.”

For a few seconds, I couldn’t speak.

I kept thinking of ordinary memories. Getting coffee. Watching television. Being annoyed about something that seemed important then.

I hated him for making me inspect them.

“Was any of it real?”

“The relationship was yours, how meaningful it was is only something you could determine.”

“Lighthouse,” I said. “Is that your medical program?”

“The agency gives me a title. Funding. Access to certain people and facilities.”

“And Lighthouse?”

He looked past me toward the road.

“Lighthouse is why I’m here.”

That answer made the entire mountain feel less safe.

I stood up, keeping Murmur close.

“Where’s my husband?”

“I can’t give you that answer tonight.”

“Can’t or won’t?”

“This can drive you insane or make you something better than human, it almost killed me a few times, I’m trying to help you. I know what you want.”

“No, you don’t. Because if you did, you’d stop talking like you’re handling a customer complaint.”

He let me stand there shaking.

Then, very quietly, he said, “I have seizures, too.”

I blinked at him.

The change in subject was so abrupt that for a second I thought I’d misheard.

“Since I was in college. I got hit in the head playing football.”

“Congratulations, do we get matching friendship bracelets now?”

“I’m trying to explain why they chose certain people.”

I could feel the cold getting through my clothes now. I wanted to go back to the truck. But I also wanted to keep him talking.

“Did you know Keppra used to be used as a nootropic?”

“No.”

“In some of the work I was involved in,” he said. “We were interested in more than seizure control.”

“Okay.”

“You don’t believe me.”

“I’m standing on a cliff with a stranger who says the government catfished me. I haven’t gotten around to reviewing your pharmaceutical credentials.”

He smiled.

“Have you ever thought of a seizure as a reset?”

“Electroshock,” I said. “They induce a seizure.”

“Under controlled conditions.”

“Very fucking controlled conditions. That seems like a relevant detail, my seizures were not an exciting feature. They fucked up my life.”

“I know.”

“How do you know?”

For the first time, he looked away.

I couldn’t tell whether I’d embarrassed him or whether he was deciding how much more to say.

“There have always been accounts,” he said eventually.

“People who described visions. Voices. Sudden certainty. Experiences they had no language for except the language available to them.”

I said nothing.

“Joan of Arc. Religious visionaries. Some have even speculated about Jesus.”

“Oh, fantastic. We’ve reached Jesus psychosis.”

“People have looked back at those accounts and suggested seizure activity.”

“People can suggest whatever they want about somebody they’ve never examined.”

“Yes,” he said. “They can.”

“Then why bring it up?”

“Because we began with the same question. Whether some of those experiences had a neurological mechanism. Whether a small number of people could perceive  
something the rest of us couldn’t.”

“And you decided seizures gave people psychic powers.”

“We decided there were things worth investigating. Tell me what happened before yours,” he said. “Before you lost awareness.”

I didn’t answer.

“The sudden clarity. The feeling that several thoughts were arriving together, but you could understand all of them.”

My stomach felt sick.

“And afterward,” he continued, “the hunger. Then those periods when thinking seemed easier than ever?”

“Stop.” I was fighting back tears now.

“Things connecting faster than you could explain them to anyone else.”

“I said fucking stop.”

He did.

I hadn’t told Ryan all of that. I wasn’t sure I had ever managed to tell a doctor all of it either.

But I had written it down.

Late at night. On my phone. To Della.

Cherinko watched me realize it.

I wanted to throw the phone into the dark.

Instead, I held it tighter, remembering my sickness when my phone wasn’t well, i wasn’t well.

“Have you heard of Robert Monroe?” he asked.

“No.”

“He developed a program in Virginia. The Monroe Institute.”

“Of course it’s in Virginia.”

“There’s a physician there I work with. We’ve had military personnel go through the training.”

“Veterans?”

“And Active duty.”

“What kind of training?”

“Attention. Altered states. Learning to recognize when your perception is changing, and how to remain oriented when it does. Transcending dimension if you’d believe it.”

I looked down at Murmur.

He was staring at Cherinko again.

“Does the training come before or after the part where the hospital empties and people’s heads turn backward?”

“That’s part of why you need it.”

He said it so calmly. Like I’d shown up to a driving lesson and complained about the traffic.

“There’s a process called Hemi-Sync,” he continued.

“Audio designed to encourage synchronization between the hemispheres. That will be part of what the doctor does with you.”

“You’re going to fix this with headphones?”

“No.”

“Because I’m going to be pretty fucking upset if the answer to all of this is a premium Spotify subscription.”

“We need you to learn control.”

“Over what?”

He looked at the phone again.

“Over how much of you is available,” he said.

For a moment, neither of us moved.

Then I asked, “Available to what?”

“That’s a conversation we should have in daylight.”

I looked behind me at the pitch dark trail back to the lit parking lot.

When I turned around, Cherinko had moved away from the edge. I stepped back so fast that Murmur startled.

Cherinko stopped.

“I’m not going to hurt you. You need to trust me. We’ll begin here tomorrow morning. A few days of preliminary work. After that, you’ll drive to the institute.”

I stared at him.

“You’ve already decided that for me huh?”

“The physician is waiting.”

“And he’s awake? Moving around?”

“Yes.”

“Because he’s Lighthouse?”

“Yes.”

“How many of you.. us are there?”

“Enough to help you get where you need to go.”

“That is an incredibly creepy fucking answer.”

I looked toward Tucson again. At all those lit windows.

“You want me to drive across the country.”

“Yes.”

“Through that.”

“Yes.”

“And what happens if I don’t?”

He took a slow breath through his nose.

I noticed then that he looked tired. Not distracted or mildly worn out. Tired in a way that seemed to have settled permanently into his face.

“I don’t think you understand the amount of preparation that went into getting you here.”

“Then you should have sent an itinerary.”

“You weren’t selected recently.”

“Well aren’t I lucky and special? How recently?”

He didn’t answer.

I thought of my mother’s story and the missing time. The way she always told it like something strange that had happened to her, with me being the cause.

“What did you do?”

Cherinko held my gaze.

“It cost over a million dollars to make you.”

“Make me? Do you mean treat me?”

Nothing.

“Study me?”

He looked almost sorry, then looked down at the ground away from me still silent.

“Then somebody got ripped off. I would have sent me back.”

He didn’t smile this time.

I stared at him, waiting for the explanation that would make it less awful but this time there wasn’t one.

Under my hand, Murmur’s heart was beating fast. I’d crouched again without remembering doing it. My fingers rested against the place where I knew the scar ran beneath his fur.

The room with the cages came back to me.

The treadmills and he labels.

My hair taped to a document.

SUBJECT MATERIAL.

ANCHOR CLASSIFICATION: PARENT.

“What am I supposed to be?”

“You’re a person.”

“Apparently a very expensive one.”

“Yes.”

“Did I ever get a choice?”

He looked down.

“We’re done for tonight,” Cherinko said.

To be honest he was sort of right, I did want to understand but I also felt like any more information and my brain would split in half and I’d loose what remaining grip on reality I had left.

I started backing towards the path.

He let me.

“Tomorrow morning,” he said. “Just after sunrise.”

I kept walking backward until the uneven ground nearly took my ankle out from under me. Then I turned and hurried toward the truck, listening for footsteps behind mine.

I got Murmur inside first, then I climbed in and only now did I realize how fast my heart was beating. My hands were shaking so badly that I missed the charging cable on the first two attempts.

I sat there with the engine running, staring through the windshield at the path we’d taken.

Cherinko didn’t come back.

After a minute, I opened the navigation app.

The search field already contained a destination.

The Monroe Institute.

Virginia.

I hadn’t typed it.

I stared at the route, then changed the destination to home.

Twenty-five minutes.

I changed it back.

A route stretched all the way across the country. A departure time. An arrival time and perfectly reasonable number of hours.

From the passenger seat, Murmur gave a low, uncertain whine.

“Yeah,” I whispered. “I know.”

I closed the route.
