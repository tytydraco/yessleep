Do you enjoy apple picking? A lot of people seem to suddenly develop a hobby for it as soon as autumn starts. They go the whole year without so much as thinking about apples, and then the moment the breeze gets chilly and the leaves start to turn orange, they’re loading baskets into the car and heading to the nearest orchard.

When I was a kid, apple picking was never something my family felt the need to get involved in. We had three scabby little apple trees in our front yard that provided more than enough apples for us to eat. Every year, my grandpa would sort through the apples that fell to the ground and bring in buckets of apples for my grandma to cut up, season with cinnamon, and put in the freezer for future apple pies.

I don’t remember seeing Grandpa ever pick apples off the branches, he only gathered the ones that had already fallen. That’s the trick. Apple trees are generous, they give plenty of their apples for free, why be greedy and try to take more than they’re willing to give?

I learned that lesson the hard way, but my brother learned it harder. 

I was eleven when it happened. My brother James was thirteen and as good as a grown up as far as he was concerned. He was “too mature” to play baby games like tag and hide-and-seek with me anymore. He was much more interested in sitting at the end of the driveway and polishing the skateboard he didn’t know how to ride in the hopes that a girl would think he was cool. 

They didn’t. But plenty of boys asked him where he got the board and his story of how he found it got bigger and bigger every time he told it.

‘I found it at the bottom of that haunted well!’

‘I was walking through the woods and found it sticking out of the crack in a rock, just like Excalibur!’

‘I went on a trip to California and met Tony Hawk and he gave it to me!’

He found it in a junk pit in the middle of a hayfield. He cut his knee open on a rusted saw blade getting it and had to get a tetanus shot that he whined about for days afterward. 

This particular day in late September we had just gotten home from school. It was a little too chilly to sit at the end of the driveway, but James didn’t want to go inside yet. He hated seeing Grandma waiting for us in the kitchen instead of Mom.

I was cold and wanted to go inside, but I felt bad leaving James pacing around the front yard by himself, so I stayed with him, keeping warm by jumping along the faded outline of hopscotch squares drawn on the driveway. Meanwhile, James shuffled around nearby, kicking pebbles against the side of the house. 

“I’m hungry,” he said eventually.

“I’m sure Grandma has dinner ready,” I offered, knowing it was probably the wrong thing to say. 

James had been four when our parents died and he swore he remembered every detail about them. He had resisted living with our grandparents from the start and never stopped. He refused to believe our parents were dead. Looking back, I can say that he was probably a troubled kid who never allowed himself to grieve. But back when I was eleven, James just looked like a petty, obnoxious boy who refused to accept anything good.

Maybe he was, I’ll never know now.

But when he screwed up his face in disgust at my suggestion of eating the dinner that Grandma had most likely lovingly prepared, my temper got the better of me.

“You’re so stupid….”

I’d meant to mutter it under my breath, but Jame’s head snapped in my direction anyway.

“What’d you say?”

“I said you’re stupid!” I snapped, “You’re standing outside in the cold because, what? You don’t want to eat food that was made for you? Just because it was made by Grandma? What did she ever do to you?”

James scoffed, “You’re too young to understand.”

“I’m only two years younger than you!” I was screaming now, “If I’m so young then why are *you* the one acting like a baby?! If you’re such a grown up then go make your own dinner!”

Now James was mad. His face was red and he clenched his hands into fists. “I will!” he screamed back at me and stomped away toward the apple trees. Grandpa had gathered up all of the good apples off the ground the day before so James kicked around at the rotting remains left behind before turning his eyes up to look at the branches. 

I guess I should explain that the apple trees in our front yard were wild apple trees. They didn’t grow the big, juicy apples that you find in the store. The apples on these trees were smaller, mostly green with streaks of red when they were ripe, and tasted extra tart, almost sour. The trees themselves were old and twisted with scabby bark that looked like the flaky calluses on Grandpa’s feet. Little green apples crowded the branches, too small and green to be any good to eat.

All except for one. In the topmost branches of the tree farthest from the driveway was a single ripe apple, fat and red, heavy enough to make the thin branch bend slightly under its weight. 

James scowled and jumped at it, falling laughably short of reaching it. 

I moved closer, still fuming from our fight, and laughed at him, “*See*?! You’re too little to even get an apple!”

James' expression turned black. I had never seen anyone look so hateful and angry before that moment, and still haven’t since. He took a step toward me, but a soft thump caught his attention. An apple had dropped from the tree, smaller than the one he had been jumping for, but perfectly ripe. 

James sneered and brought his foot down on the apple with a furious stomp. “You don’t get it!” he screamed, “Nobody gets it!” He pulled his foot back and kicked the trunk of the apple tree so hard he must have broken toes. “Everybody got over it! Everybody expects me to get over it! Everybody expects me to be happy! Well I’m *not* happy! You hear me?! I’m! Not! Happy!”

He kept kicking the tree over and over until the bark chipped away and he hit the bare wood underneath. 

What happened next is painful to remember and hard to explain.

James finally finished his tirade against the tree and turned to me like he was finally going to head into the house. But he didn’t move.

His eyes went wide and I followed his gaze down to his feet were dozens of fibrous lines wrapped around his ankles like black skeletal fingers. I remember thinking that I had seen those thready lines before when I helped Grandma move one of her plants to a bigger pot. She had shaken the plant out of its pot and carefully massaged the tightly bound roots until they hung in loose, dangling fingers just like the ones that now gripped my brother’s legs.

“...Wendy…?”

It happened so fast I feel like I didn’t actually see it. One second, James was standing right in front of me, staring wide-eyed like a rabbit. The next he vanished into the ground. I would have thought he just blinked out of existence if it weren’t for the mound of turned dirt where he had been standing; and the fact that I could still hear him. 

Muffled and choked with dirt, but I could hear his voice screaming from under my feet. 

I started screaming then. I tried to dig for him but Grandma and Grandpa had come outside to check on the commotion and dragged me away. They held me between them, begging me to be still, until the sound of James’ screaming ran thin and died. In the ringing silence that followed the three little apple trees seemed to sigh. A breeze I couldn’t feel rustled their leaves like they were shaking themselves after doing an unpleasant task. 

Grandma reported James missing the next day. The police asked her all the right questions and she told just enough of the truth to keep suspicions away from her and Grandpa. 

They asked me questions too and I repeated what Grandma told me to say. The truth, but not all of it. 

We had an argument after we got home from school and James stomped off and I hadn’t seen him since. 

The police searched for months. Grandma carefully searched through the photo album for just the right picture of James to put on the missing poster. Neighbors brought over casseroles and pies, and told us that the police were sure to find him. 

Next year the apple trees produced so many apples that Grandpa sold them by the bucketful. He and Grandma mused about the quality of the soil being better that year over their morning coffee. 

If you’ve read this far, I’m sure you’re wondering where I’m going with all of this, and to tell you the truth, I’m not sure. I guess you can view it as a warning to be careful. 

Did you ever read the book ‘The Giving Tree’ as a kid? The one where the tree gives everything to a boy as he grows to an adult until the tree is just a stump that he sits on. I guess I’m telling you that the trees are only willing to give so much. Looking back I can see that the tree offered James an apple, just not the one that he wanted. It wasn’t until James refused that the tree…did what it did. 

I don’t know if all trees are like that. I’ve tried looking into disappearances that happened while people were apple picking and I didn’t really find anything conclusive. Maybe the domestic trees in orchards are more generous than the wild apple trees in our yard. 

If I’m being honest, for a while I actually started to believe the lie that Grandma and I told. It was a lot easier than believing that my brother was dragged underground by an angry tree. But yesterday I was forced to come face to face with the reality of what happened, literally.

Grandma and Grandpa passed away a few weeks ago and I was informed that they left their property to me. I spent the first few days checking out the house and boxing up things to donate or throw away until I had nothing left to look at but the apple trees that were still standing in the front yard. 

They looked exactly the same as they had all those years ago; scabby and twisted with black flaking bark covered in bright green moss. The only difference was the piles of apples gathering around their trunks with no one to collect them, way more than I ever remembered seeing at one time. 

I was standing right where I had been all those years ago when I saw it. I tried so hard not to see it, tried to convince myself that it was just very vivid pareidolia. But it was too consistent. Too obvious. Too *much*. 

In the apple tree farthest from the driveway, in the black, flaky bark covered with bright green moss, was James’ face. As clearly as if he had pressed his face into a plaster mold. Mouth open in a scream, eyes bulging in terror, like a ghoulish Halloween mask. I turned away from the awful sight just to be confronted by the second apple tree. Within its bark I saw two faces that I only remembered from photographs. And on the first tree, a weathered pair that I had seen every day of my childhood. 

I never asked Grandma and Grandpa how my parents died. Family told me the my grandparents passed peacefully in their sleep, but the funeral was closed casket.

I don’t know if this is a warning or a cry for help, but if you're ever in northern Michigan and happen upon an abandoned house with three scabby apple trees in the front yard, please, *please* be careful.

Because they’ll be hungry. 

And their roots run deep.
