The taxi came at nine.

The driver was young, twenty-five maybe, with a phone clipped to the dashboard and a paper pine tree that had stopped smelling of anything years ago. He watched me all the way down the front steps. He looked at my mouth. Then he looked up at the house and kept looking while I put my bag in the trunk. He didn’t offer to help.

"Gare d’Autun?"

I nodded. I hadn’t said a word since I woke up in the tower, and I wasn’t going to start on that gravel.

The avenue is four hundred meters long. We drove for six minutes. The limes went past in their pairs, grey trunks and yellow leaves, the gravel and its little dents sliding underneath us, and somewhere around the second minute I started counting the trees. I reached sixty, which is twenty more than there are, and made myself stop. The driver switched off the radio. He leaned forward over the wheel, squinting through the windshield like a man driving in fog, though the morning was bright and clear. Then the trees thinned, and the château rose up ahead of us, honey stone and slate, the east wing shuttered.

We had never turned around.

He said something under his breath, a prayer or a curse, and swung the car around on the gravel and drove away from the house. I watched it shrink in the rearview mirror. Then I looked through the windshield and watched it grow.

He tried twice more. The second time he drove fast, gravel spraying off the tires and rattling against the trunks, and the house came up ahead of us anyway, patient and enormous. The third time, he stopped at the foot of the steps and sat with both hands on the wheel. In the mirror, his eyes went from the house to me, and back to the house, and back to me.

"C’est vous." he said. *It’s* ***you****.*

He got out, lifted my bag from the trunk, and set it down on the gravel. Then he opened my door and stood holding it, looking at the ground. I got out. He didn’t say goodbye. He drove off down the avenue at a crawl, and I stood on the steps and watched the taxi shrink between the limes until it rolled through the gates and turned onto the road. I heard it shift gears and speed up, the sound thinning away into the hills. Then nothing but leaves.

I picked up my bag and walked after him.

I kept my eyes on the gates the whole way. They stayed exactly as far ahead of me as they were when I started, a little iron shape at the end of a green tunnel, and the trees went by, and the trees went by, and after ten minutes I looked down and saw the front steps under my feet.

I tried the fields. Beside the avenue there’s a pasture gone to thistle, and beyond it a hedge, and beyond the hedge a stone wall and the road. I waded through the thistles with my bag over my shoulder, pushed through the hedge until it tore my sleeves, and climbed out the other side into the kitchen garden, behind the house, between the bean rows. Madame Bataillard’s little white Renault was parked by the garden gate. Her keys were in the ignition. A baguette and a carton of eggs sat on the passenger seat, and a folded newspaper, and a pair of brown leather gloves, laid out neatly side by side.

I didn’t try the car. I already knew where it would take me.

I sat down on the front steps instead and stayed there most of the morning, my bag between my feet, watching the avenue. A blackbird worked through the leaves under the limes. The sun climbed. Once I heard a car pass out on the road, far off, and I stood up so fast my knees cracked, but it never turned in.

The heralds came at noon.

I’d gone inside for water and was standing at the kitchen window, over the sink, because from there you can see the whole avenue, and I had decided I would rather see. The light went first, flattening. The sun hung white over the hills, but the shadows drained out from under the limes until there were none, and the gravel turned pale and depthless as paper. The blackbird had stopped. Everything had stopped. It was the light from the road in my dream.

Then they were standing between the trees.

A figure beside every lime, facing one another across the avenue in a double row that ran from the front steps all the way to the bend. Thirty pairs.

Les hérauts. The heralds.

They were three meters tall and so thin their tabards hung off them in long flat folds like wet laundry. Every tabard bore the arms cut into my ring, a white hand on a red field, the fingers too long, and beneath it in faded gold thread: *Devant le Roi*. Their legs bent forward at the knee and back again at a second knee halfway down the shin, so they stood in a delicate, wading stoop, like herons in a shallow pond. Their arms hung well past their knees. When the wind lifted a tabard, I could see ribs running all the way down to the hip, far too many of them, close-set as the rungs of a ladder.

Their eyes were sewn shut. One gold thread ran through the eyelids of the first herald, across the avenue through the eyelids of the second, back to the third, zigzagging down the whole double row, every face stitched to the next. It glittered at head height like a web strung across the avenue. When the wind pushed one of them, the thread pulled, and every head in the row twitched toward it at once. One long, blind animal, held together by its stitches.

Each held a straight brass trumpet two meters long, hung with a red banner. The banners were soaked through, heavy with whatever they’d been dipped in, and they dripped crimson steadily onto the gravel, a slow patter I could hear through the glass.

Their lips had grown over the mouthpieces. The lower half of every face had been drawn forward and stretched, the jaw pulled long and narrow and fused around the brass, so the trumpet came out of the mouth like a stiff, gleaming tongue. Where skin met metal, the flesh was puckered, shiny and pink. In places it had split, and something yellow had dried in crusts down the brass.

The nearest one, at the foot of the front steps, wore Madame Bataillard’s coat.

The grey wool one I’d watched her button the evening before, stretched now over a chest a meter longer than the coat, so the hem rode up around her ribs like a child’s jacket. Her handbag hung from the crook of her elbow. Her reading glasses dangled from the gold thread by one arm, swinging a little. A few grey curls were still pinned to a skull drawn out long and smooth as a river stone, and her front teeth had been pushed flat and sideways by the brass, like headstones in sinking ground.

I was sick in the sink. I didn’t stop watching.

Then the whole row turned its head. Sixty sewn faces swung toward the house together, the thread drawing taut between them with a faint, high hum, and stopped. They were facing the kitchen window. Facing me.

I stepped back from the glass. The thread tightened, and sixty heads tilted a few degrees to follow.

They lifted their trumpets and breathed in, and their ribs swung open, each long rib pivoting out from the breastbone on a hinge no body has, until their chests stood wide as birdcages with the doors flung back. Behind the stretched tabards, their lungs filled, grey and mottled and enormous, like wet sails. Inside Madame Bataillard’s coat, something cracked, over and over, all the way down her side, a sound like knuckles being pulled one by one, and her handbag slid down to her wrist and hung there.

Then they blew.

The window bowed inward and held, bellying like a sail. The sound went through my fillings and into the bones behind my eyes, into the hinges of my jaw. My nose ran blood over my lips, over the blisters, and the little crown printed into my lower lip throbbed in time with it. My tongue moved on its own. It curled against the roof of my mouth, shaping something, and I felt a wet click forming in the back of my throat, an answer rising to meet the call, and I bit down on my tongue until my mouth filled with blood, and kept biting, and kept biting.

*Ne dis rien.*

When the sound stopped, the heralds were gone, and the thread with them. The shadows crept back under the limes. Every paired dent in the gravel had filled from underneath with something dark. And down the very center of the avenue, from the foot of the front steps to the gates, ran a single line of hoofprints.

I went out to look at them. I had to. Each one was as wide as a dinner plate and sunk deep into the gravel, deep enough to show the clay beneath. I crouched beside the nearest and held my hand over it, and warmth came up off it, like the heat that rises from a bed someone has just left. Every one of them pointed away from the house.

By evening, the house was no longer... A house.

I noticed it slowly. A hallway that ran three rooms farther than it had that morning. A staircase with one step too many, then two, then seven. The front hall used to end at the foot of the main stairs. Now the stairs stand off to one side, and the hall runs on east between pairs of open doors, room beyond room, so far that the last doorway is only a pale speck.

The parquet is warm and gives a little underfoot, damp along the seams. If I stand still, I can feel something beneath it, slower than any heartbeat. Breathing, two floors down. The electric sconces have become candles, thick and yellow, smelling of scorched pork fat, and their wax runs upward, in long greasy fingers that climb the walls and spread across a ceiling that has become a painted sky. Pink and gold clouds, soft and overblown. A pale road crosses them from one end of the hall to the other, crowded with tiny smiling figures. At the far end, the painted horizon swells upward, lifting the clouds with it, and along its crest someone has picked out a row of small gold points.

I stood under it for a long time, looking up. When I lowered my head, my neck ached as if I’d been looking for hours, and the candles had burned down by a finger’s width.

Tall gilt mirrors hang between the doors now. In every one of them, I’m on horseback.

I’m standing when I pass them. I checked my feet every time. But in the glass I sit high on something long and pale, a red tabard on my chest, reins wound through my fists. I stopped in front of one and raised my right hand, slowly. In the mirror, the rider lifted the reins.

Behind him, filling the whole depth of the glass, stand the heralds with their trumpets raised. Behind them walks the procession from my dream, powdered wigs and lace collars, a little boy in a sailor suit, Thibault with pine needles in his hair, all smiling their wide white burned smiles. Behind them, the soaked red train drags back toward a horizon that is beginning to rise. Every face in the glass is turned toward that horizon. Mine is the only one turned away from it. Looking out, past the frame, at whatever stands on this side.

The gallery had changed too. Thirty-one sitters have turned in their frames and knelt with their backs to the room, facing the pale shape behind them. Thirty-one bowed heads. Thirty-one pairs of heels. The last canvas, the one with my name on its brass plate, is no longer blank. Faint as a stain soaking through from the other side, it shows a rider on a pale horse, coming straight out of the picture. His face has no features at all, except the eyes. Those are open, and painted in perfect, loving detail, and they’re **mine**. Even the little fleck of brown in the left iris.

I took my shirt off in the upstairs bathroom to rinse the blood from the collar, and that’s when I saw the marks.

Between my shoulder blades there’s a hand. It’s grey, a dull, even grey, the color of the gallery canvas before it changed. The palm lies flat over my spine. The fingers spread up and over both shoulders, far too long, five knuckles to each, their tips resting on my collarbones. I can feel it. A weight, faint and steady. When I lean forward, it eases. When I stand still, it presses. Urging me on.

Across both palms run two deep red grooves, raw at the edges and weeping a little, the marks reins leave on a rider who has gripped them far too tight for far too long. I have never held reins in my life. The insides of my thighs are bruised nearly black in two long ovals, the skin rubbed shiny and hairless. Saddle sores, deep and old, the kind that come from days of riding without rest. I pressed one with my thumb, and the pain came dull and distant, as if it belonged to someone else.

When I bent to rinse my face, something shifted in my mouth. A back molar, loose in a socket that suddenly felt too big for it. My tongue worked at it without asking me, and it came free, and I spat it into the sink. There was no blood. The roots were long, curved and fine and pale as a crab’s legs, and they were still slowly flexing, feeling around the porcelain for something to hold.

I ran the tap until it went down the drain. I heard it ticking inside the pipe for a long time afterward. Going down. Then, a while later, coming back up.

I spent the evening in the kitchen with every lamp on, my back against the wall, listening to the house settle into its new size. It made sounds I’d never heard a building make. A long creak from somewhere overhead, something stretching. A soft repeated tapping from inside the walls, too regular for pipes. Once, from very far down the east hall, the sound of a great many people drawing breath at the same time, and then holding it.

Around eleven, the kitchen door opened on its own.

Down the east hall, the doors were moving. The nearest one swung wide, then the one after it, then the next, each opening just as the last one settled, a long row of doors opening one after another into the distance. Something was walking ahead of me, holding every door.

I was walking. I don’t remember deciding to. My footsteps on the warm parquet were slow and perfectly even, the footsteps from the recording, and the candle flames leaned toward me as I passed, every one of them, as if I were pulling a wind behind me. In the mirrors, the rider kept pace.

The doors brought me to the end of the east wing, to a pair of tall white-and-gold doors that hadn’t been there the day I arrived, and through them into a bedchamber so high its ceiling was lost in the dark. A gilded rail crossed the room at waist height. Beyond it hung curtains of heavy red damask, drawn closed all the way around something enormous.

Something behind them was breathing. The whole curtain swelled outward in one slow bulge, the gold fringe lifting off the floor, then settled with a long rush of air that smelled of something sweet, fruit gone soft in a shut room. It breathed out, and the candles bent away from the bed. It breathed in, and they bent toward it, and my hair moved toward it, and the tears in my eyes ran sideways toward it.

The tall ones from the road stood along the walls between the windows, stooped under a ceiling nowhere near their heads. I couldn’t see their faces. I didn’t try.

My feet took a step toward the rail. Then another.

Then a hand came over my eyes.

A palm, cool and dry, laid flat across them from behind. I knew it the instant it touched me. I woke under that hand every morning until I was nine. She stood close at my back, and I heard her breathing in tiny, careful sips, holding it in, holding all of it in. Then her voice came, thick and broken, right against my ear, the words shaped around the pieces of her tongue.

"**Ne regarde pas**."

*Don’t look.*

I said her name. I couldn’t help it. It came out of me before I could bite it back, one word, and her hand trembled against my face, and behind the curtains the breathing paused.

The whole room waited. The candles stood straight up. The tall shapes along the walls didn’t move, but I felt their attention swing toward us like a lamp.

Then the breathing resumed, slow and even, and her hand steadied.

She held it there until I stopped shaking. Then she turned me, gently, away from the bed, toward a narrow door hidden in the paneling beside the fireplace, and her hand lifted away. I felt how badly she didn’t want to let go. I felt her do it anyway. When I turned around, there was no one behind me, only the smell of her hand cream, faint and floral, the cheap kind from the supermarket she bought for thirty years.

The door opened onto a stone stair spiraling down. I went down a long way, past where the ground floor should have been, past where the cellars should have been, until the walls ran with water and the stone under my bare feet gave way to gravel. Somewhere above me, through the earth, I could hear the slow crunch of something heavy kneeling, and rising, and kneeling, moving along the avenue overhead.

At the bottom was a long vaulted stable lit by a single candle. Stalls ran down both sides into the dark, empty, their straw pressed into pairs of shallow hollows. At the near end stood an oak armoire, the white hand carved into each of its doors.

Inside hung a tabard. La livrée. The livery of the one who rides first.

It’s the most beautiful thing I have ever touched. Deep red on the front, the white hand spread across the chest, soft as the finest kid glove. It’s made of panels, each a different shade, old ivory, pinkish beige, a grey like weak tea, joined with gold thread in seams so fine I could barely find them. On every panel, faint and grey, there’s a long open hand.

They’re backs. Tanned and stretched and cut to shape. Along each seam, a name is stitched in tiny gold letters. Jean-Baptiste. Marguerite. Philippe-Auguste. My grandfather is the left side, and when I lifted it to my face, it still smelled faintly of pond water. Héloïse is the right shoulder, her hand the clearest of all. The neckline is edged with lips, dozens of pairs pressed flat and stitched in overlapping rows, each lower lip stamped with a tiny crown. When my breath touched them, they parted, all together, very slightly, and from somewhere deep in the folds came a single soft click.

Thirty-one of them knelt so that this could be sewn. Four hundred years of my family, cut and cured and stitched together to dress one rider.

On a hook beside it hung a bridle braided from long grey hair, the same grey as Héloïse’s in her portrait. Folded beneath the hook was a sheet of parchment sealed with a row of tiny wax points. I broke the seal. It was a list of places, written in a hand that changed every few lines down the page, as if each of my ancestors had added to it in turn. Autun. Chalon. Lyon. Paris. Then cities in languages I could read, then cities in languages I couldn’t, and then names I don’t think are anywhere at all. It filled both sides of the page and ran onto the margins.

It was a route.

Something moved at the far end of the stable.

It came into the candlelight slowly, lowering its head. A horse, or the shape a horse would be if someone had described one to whatever made the heralds. Its legs bent twice. Its pale hide was hairless and damp, hung slack between the bones, and through it I could see its ribs, dozens of them, rising and falling. Its eyes were sewn shut with the gold thread, and the thread ran from its eyelids up into the vaulted ceiling and through the stone, toward the avenue above.

The head of the blind animal. Every herald in that double row, stitched to this.

Its lips drew back. Behind them were human teeth, rows and rows of them, molars and canines with small white milk teeth set crookedly among them. Near the front, a back molar with long, pale, curling roots sat in a fresh red socket.

It pressed its muzzle into my palm, into the raw groove the reins had worn, and breathed out, warm, wet and sweet. Then it lowered its head further and rested its long skull against my chest, very gently, over my heart, and stayed there.

It knew me. It had been waiting for me since before I was born.

I always thought the motto meant on our knees, facing him. For thirty-one of us, it did.

**Devant le Roi.** Before the King. *Ahead of him.*

*The one who goes first.*

I don’t remember climbing back up the stair, but I remember the inside of the armoire door, because I saw it as I swung it shut. The oak was scored with fingernail grooves, hundreds of them, gouged so deep the wood had splintered. They made letters. Packed into the grooves were tiny flakes of pale pink polish, the same pink as a bottle that sat on our bathroom shelf for most of my childhood.

Il ne peut pas venir sans toi.

*He can’t come without you.*

My mother knew. That’s why she kept her hand over my eyes every morning until the sun was fully up. That’s why she tore the curtains down and bit her tongue to pieces. She was keeping the rider from the horse. She held it in for both of us for as long as she could, and she’s still holding it, wherever she is now.

I’m in the kitchen. The livery is on the floor by the range, where I threw it. An hour ago I lit the old iron stove, fed it kindling until the plates glowed, and shoved the tabard in with the poker. Every pair of lips along the neckline opened at once, and every flame in the firebox bent toward them and went in, and the stove went cold under my hand. When I dragged the livery back out, it was warm and soft, untouched by the flames.

The bridle won’t cut. I sawed at it with the bread knife until the blade folded over on itself, and the braid has no mark on it at all. It’s lying on the table now. I keep finding it in my lap.

Under the floor, the horse has started pacing. I can hear its hooves on the stone of the stable, slow at first, then faster, back and forth, back and forth, and every time it turns, the hand on my back presses harder, and my fingers ache to close around the reins.

The gates at the end of the avenue are standing open. I’ve never once seen them open before. The hoofprints run straight between them and out onto the road. For the first time since I came here, I think I could walk out of this place. I think that’s exactly what it wants.

So I’m not going anywhere. Not through those gates. Not on that horse.

My mother held on to the wall with her fingers until they came off. I’ve still got all ten of mine.
