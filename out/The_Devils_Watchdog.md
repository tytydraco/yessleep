I'm the son of a pastor. I was raised with the unshakeable belief that God was always watching me, always judging my every move. But by the time I reached my teenage years, after years of abuse and disappointment, I rebelled hard.

I started drinking. I treated women like disposable objects and saw every other man as a rival. By eighteen, I was addicted to illegal drugs, battling deep insomnia, getting into fights constantly. I began to enjoy hurting others—and hurting myself. I stopped caring about anyone. I stopped caring about myself.

My parents loved me, but I ran away from home anyway.

I moved to the countryside to live with my grandmother. She was the only person who still looked at me with love instead of disappointment. She was a small woman with calloused hands, a practitioner of Umbanda. Every night, she would try to reason with me, to put some sense into my head. I didn't listen. Every single day, I sank deeper into the abyss I was digging for myself.

Then things started getting strange.

It began with insects. First, ants crawling up from the floor of my room. No matter how much I cleaned, they kept appearing. Then came the flies, swarming around my mirror. The worst was the morning I woke up to find a massive spider perched on my pillow, less than a meter from my face. It was black, hairy, and completely motionless. I jumped out of bed, and when I looked again, it was gone.

After that, the phenomena intensified.

When I stumbled home drunk in the early hours of the weekend, the streetlights would flicker and die exactly as I passed beneath them. My laptop, which worked perfectly fine during the day, would freeze and refuse to turn on after sunset. The television in my room would switch on and off at random hours—usually at 3:00 AM. I blamed faulty wiring, old appliances, bad luck. But my grandmother never had a single problem with any of these things.

It was only me. Only my room. Only my presence.

I started isolating myself without even noticing. I stopped talking to everyone. I hated daylight, as if it was exposing something I didn't want to see anymore. I couldn't look anyone in the eyes—not even my grandmother. I couldn't love anyone, and I definitely hated myself.

Then came the night everything fell apart completely.

I was sitting on the sidewalk around 2:00 AM, smoking one cigarette after another, when I noticed something in the middle of the street. At first, I thought it was just a stray dog. But as my eyes adjusted, it seemed like I was observing another reality.

It was a massive black animal, too large to be any breed I recognized. It was sitting perfectly upright, like a guard dog at attention, its silhouette sharp against the dim glow of a distant streetlight. Its eyes were yellow—like a cat's eyes caught in a car's headlights. It didn't move. It didn't blink. It just sat there, staring directly at me.

I told myself I was hallucinating. I kept smoking. I finished the cigarette, stubbed it out, and went back inside. I closed the door. I took a deep breath. But curiosity—or stupidity—pulled me back outside.

I looked out.

It hadn't moved an inch. It was exactly where I'd left it, in the exact same posture, as if it had been carved from stone.

I couldn't sleep that night. I lay in bed, listening to a howl that never came, feeling those invisible eyes piercing my soul.

A week later, my grandmother passed away.

She was the only person who had never given up on me—and I hadn't returned her love. That night, as I lay crying in my room, I heard her voice—clear, unmistakable, and absolutely terrifying—whisper directly into my ear: "You brought the devil into my house."

I plunged deeper into my abyss. I threw myself violently into drugs and alcohol. At 1.80 meters tall, my weight dropped to 55 kilos. The bones of my spine showed through my skin. My eyes were sunken and bloodshot. I looked like a dead man in the mirror.

Then, one early morning, I woke to the howl.

A long, mournful sound. Something between a wolf and a scream, echoing from somewhere just outside my window. I staggered to the bathroom, gripping the sink for support. My mouth instantly turned bitter, as if I'd bitten into rusted iron. My left arm went limp and useless. And my heart—my heart began to beat so violently, so relentlessly, that I could feel it trying to claw its way out through my throat, pulsing beneath my jugular.

I was certain I was going to die right there.

The feeling didn't pass. It stretched through the entire night, through the next day, through the following night. This wasn't fear—I'd had panic attacks before, and this was something entirely different. This was the primitive, visceral certainty that a train was about to hit me from every direction at once. No matter where I went in that house, I wasn't safe. I couldn't hide. I couldn't breathe.

I spent hours curled in a corner, trembling, waiting for the end.

The end never came. Somehow, after what felt like an eternity, my heart finally slowed. I survived—though I still don't know how.

Since that day, I've done everything I can to stay sober. I've had relapses, yes—with alcohol, and once with cocaine, I won't lie—but they're getting rarer. The urge is weaker now. But deep down, I know one thing with absolute certainty: if I ever see that enormous black dog again, or if I hear that howl one more time, I will die.

Later, I learned that my parents—the same ones I abandoned—never stopped praying for me. And that my grandmother had done many things to protect me as well. I've never been a religious man, but I can't shake the feeling that, somehow, against all odds, someone answered my grandmother's prayers and my parents' pleas. Because of them, I'm here. Breathing. Typing this. Trying to warn others.

I'm not a good man. I still have insomnia. I still carry guilt and regret that I can't escape. I still cry when I think about my grandmother. But I'm alive. And I know—that voice I heard speaking of demons? That wasn't her.

So here's my advice to any young person reading this—especially those who think rebellion is a game, that drugs are fun, that treating people like garbage makes you more of a man: that lifestyle extracts a price. It can take your health, your sanity, and invite things you don't believe in—until they're staring you in the face with unblinking yellow eyes. Hold on to love, for others and for yourself.

But sometimes, I think it's already too late for me. I will die soon. And the last thing I will see, before everything goes dark, will be the Black Dog with Yellow Eyes.
