I don’t really know where to start with this, and honestly, I’m not sure I should even be posting it. I’ve read stories on here for years. I’ve always enjoyed the ones that make you question whether something is actually possible or whether someone is experiencing something they can’t explain. I never thought I’d have one of those stories myself.

My husband, Chase, was involved in a serious car accident a few months ago. It happened on a rainy evening while he was driving home from work. Another vehicle lost control, crossed into his lane, and hit him almost head-on. He survived, but his injuries were severe enough that doctors had to put him into a medically induced coma.  
For 47 days, I practically lived at the hospital. I learned which vending machines actually worked, which nurses brought the good coffee, and which chair in his room was slightly less uncomfortable than the others. I talked to him every day. I’d tell him about things happening at home, what I’d made for dinner, what was on television, and all the stupid little things that normally wouldn’t matter. Sometimes I’d hold his hand and tell him he needed to hurry up and come home because I was getting tired of sleeping alone. I don’t know if he heard any of it. I just needed to believe he did.

On the morning of the forty-seventh day, I fell asleep in the chair beside his bed. I woke up because I heard a strange sound. It was almost like someone quietly laughing. Then I noticed Chase’s fingers moving. At first, I thought I was imagining it. I’d spent so many nights staring at that bed that I was beginning to see things that weren’t there. But then his eyes opened.  
I immediately jumped up and grabbed his hand. “Chase?” He blinked several times, looking around the room. I started crying. “Oh my God. Baby, can you hear me?” He looked confused, understandably. His throat was dry, and he struggled to speak. I called for the nurse, who immediately came in and started checking his vitals. But before anyone could really explain what was happening, Chase looked directly at me and asked a question.

“Where’s Claire?”

I remember smiling because I genuinely thought he was confused. “Who, sweetheart?” He repeated her name and tried lifting his head. “Where’s Claire? Where are the kids?” I looked at the nurse, then back at him. “What kids, Chase?” That’s when he started panicking. He kept asking for a little boy named Noah and a little girl named Lily. He wanted to know why they weren’t there. He wanted to know where his wife was.

I told him I was his wife. He stared at me for a long time. Then he said something I’ll never forget. “No. My wife is Claire.”

I thought it was the medication. Maybe a side effect of the accident. Maybe some kind of confusion caused by the trauma. The doctors told me not to panic. They explained that patients recovering from serious brain injuries can experience memory disturbances, vivid dreams, and confusion. I tried to accept that explanation, but Chase wasn’t describing a dream.

Over the next several days, he started telling me about another life. He said he had been married to Claire for years. They had two children together. Noah was eight, and Lily was five. He remembered their house, their neighborhood, their friends, and even the names of their neighbors. He remembered teaching Noah to ride a bicycle. He remembered Lily crawling into their bed every morning because she was afraid of being alone. He remembered Christmas mornings, birthdays, family vacations, and arguments with Claire about things that married couples argue about. He remembered falling in love with her. He remembered their wedding. He remembered bringing both children home from the hospital. And according to him, he had lived with them for five years.

I asked him how that was possible. He said he didn’t know. He remembered being in the house with Claire and the children. He remembered ordinary days, not just significant events. He remembered what Claire made for dinner on Tuesdays. He remembered that Noah hated thunderstorms. He remembered Lily calling dinosaurs “din-din-saws” when she was younger. He remembered everything. And he was devastated.

One afternoon, I asked him whether he understood that none of this had happened. He looked at me and started crying. “I know what you’re saying. But I was there.” I reminded him that he’d been unconscious for 47 days. He shook his head and asked, “Then why do I remember five years?” I didn’t have an answer.  
The part that hurt the most was that he wasn’t simply confused about who I was. He missed them. He missed Claire. He missed his children. He would wake up asking about them, and sometimes he’d become so upset that the nurses had to help calm him down. I started feeling guilty for being relieved that my husband was alive. Because the man lying in that hospital bed was grieving a family I had never known existed.

Eventually, Chase started giving me more details about the house. He described the layout, the bedrooms, the backyard, and even a small storage space he said he’d built himself. He told me the street name, the town, and the color of the front door. At first, I ignored it. Then, one evening, after I got home from the hospital, I decided to look it up. I don’t know why. Maybe I wanted to prove to myself that none of it was real. Maybe I needed something concrete to hold onto.

I searched the street name and town he’d given me, and I found a house. The description was almost identical to what he’d told me. I stared at the pictures for a long time. The front door was the same color. The windows were in the same places. Even the little tree beside the driveway matched. I convinced myself it was a coincidence. There are thousands of houses in the world. People have similar memories. Brain injuries can do strange things. That’s what I kept telling myself.

Until a few weeks later.

Chase had been home for a while by then. His physical recovery was going well, although emotionally, he was still struggling. One evening, his phone rang. It was an unknown number. He answered, and I watched his expression change almost immediately. He didn’t say anything for several seconds. Then he whispered, “Claire?”  
I felt my stomach drop. He listened, and his eyes filled with tears. Then he said something that made me walk out of the room. “I thought I’d never see you again.” When he hung up, I asked who had called. He looked at me and said, “She found me.”  
I asked him who. He didn’t answer immediately. Then he said, “Claire.”

I told him that wasn’t funny. He started crying and turned his phone toward me. There was a message on the screen. It read, “I miss you. The children keep asking when you’re coming home.”

I couldn’t move. I asked him how someone could possibly know about the things he’d been describing. He said he didn’t know. I asked him to call the number back. He refused. I asked him to block it. He refused that, too. And then he told me something I haven’t been able to stop thinking about.

He said that during the last few days of his other life, Claire had started acting strangely. She would tell him she felt like something was wrong. She would wake up in the middle of the night and check whether he was still beside her. On the night before he woke up in the hospital, he remembered her holding his face and telling him not to leave. He said she kept repeating the same thing: “Please don’t disappear.”  
I asked him what he thought that meant. He looked at me and said, “I think she knew I was going somewhere.”

I haven’t told anyone else about this. I know how it sounds. I know there are explanations for vivid dreams, memory disturbances, and the effects of serious brain injuries. But I can’t explain the house. I can’t explain the phone call. And I definitely can’t explain the message.

Last night, I woke up around 3 a.m. and noticed Chase wasn’t in bed. I found him sitting in the living room, staring at his phone. I asked him what he was doing. He said he was looking at pictures. I asked what pictures, and he turned the screen toward me. It was a photograph of a little boy and a little girl standing in front of a Christmas tree. Noah and Lily.  
I asked him where he got it. He looked at me with tears in his eyes and said, “Claire sent it.”  
I took the phone from him. The photograph was attached to a message. There was no explanation, just one sentence: “Tell Daddy we miss him.”  
And underneath it was another message that had arrived less than a minute earlier.

“Why won’t you tell him what happened to us?”  
I haven’t slept since. Because I think I’ve finally realized something. I don’t think Chase was dreaming about another family. I think somewhere, there really is a woman named Claire. And I think she believes my husband abandoned her and two children.

I’m terrified of what happens if she finds out where we live.
