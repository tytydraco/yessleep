I think the man I’ve been driving around isn’t really dead.

 

I know how that sounds, but just hear me out.

 

I work for a certain transportation agency that caters to a lot of different people’s needs. A lot of times we transport people who can’t drive themselves, including the physically disabled and people with developmental disabilities who need assistance getting from one place to another.

 

That’s what the public sees at least, but it’s not all we do though.

 

I’ve personally been on more than a dozen cross-country trips, express-delivering everything from large, unmarked crates that take up the entire bed of a flatbed truck to brown paper bags with nothing but a number scribbled across one side, sitting in the passenger seat beside me.

 

I don’t ask what’s inside.

 

That’s one of the first things they teach you when you start working here.

 

Don’t ask questions.

 

That’s all to say, this is where my line of employment comes in.

 

Most people spend their entire lives in one particular place. It’s what they know. It’s where their friends and family are. It’s home.

 

But sometimes life doesn’t work out that way.

 

People move away. They relocate to be closer to their children or grandchildren. Sometimes they simply can’t take care of themselves anymore and have to move somewhere that can.

 

And sometimes, unfortunately, people die far away from what they once called home.

 

You see, every once in a while, someone will leave behind a final request in their will stating that they don’t want to be buried where they died. They want to be taken back home.

 

Back to the little slice of land they’ve lived on for most of their lives. 

 

Back to their family.

 

Back to the people they left behind.

 

That’s where I come in.

 

In cases like these, funeral homes don’t always handle the transportation themselves. Apparently, there are all kinds of licensing and insurance requirements involved with transporting human remains across state lines, especially over long distances.

 

So instead, the funeral home sends out a private bid to transportation companies willing to take the job.

 

The lowest bidder usually gets it, and most of the time that’s us.

 

We don’t win every contract, but I’d say we’ve gotten our fair share over the years. Usually, the job is simple enough. Pick up the deceased, drive them to their destination, and get them there within whatever timeframe the funeral home specified.

 

Normally it’s a few hundred miles, sometimes it’s even been a few thousand.

 

I’ve even done a couple that took me across multiple states.

 

I can’t speak for the rest of my coworkers. We don’t really know each other outside of talking on the radio. But I’m probably one of the few who has never missed a deadline.

 

Doesn’t matter what I’m hauling.

 

I’ve always gotten it where it needed to go.

 

I try not to think about the things I transport.

 

That’s another rule I’ve learned. 

 

Don’t get curious.

 

I’ll admit there have been times when I’ve wondered what’s inside the bags sitting beside me. I’ve wondered what’s inside those massive wooden crates that sometimes get loaded onto the back of my truck.

 

I’ve never looked.

 

I’ve never even tried.

 

Whatever is inside them isn’t my business.

 

And until tonight, I was perfectly happy keeping it that way.

 

Because about an hour ago, about three in the morning, I heard something that sounded like coughing coming from inside the casket in the back of my truck.

 

I pulled into an empty parking lot a few minutes ago and I don’t know what to do.
