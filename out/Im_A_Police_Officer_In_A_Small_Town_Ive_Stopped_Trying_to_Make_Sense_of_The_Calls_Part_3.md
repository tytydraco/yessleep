So like I said, I did try to contact the higher ups. I wanted to let them know what was happening here. I reached out to Sergeants, Lieutenants, anyone who would listen. I wanted someone above me to tell me what this place was.

Every single time I was met with the same type of response. A refusal to elaborate on this place. A careful practiced unwillingness to talk about it.

These are people I genuinely trusted so when I initially called, I thought I would get some answers. First person I called was my old Lieutenant. We had a great working relationship. He knew I was a hard worker and produced for the precinct so essentially I always made him look good.

“Chris! Long time no talk, how have you been?”

“Hey Lou, well if I am being honest, not great. Things have been happening here. Really weird situations that I can’t really explain.”

“Where did you transfer to again?”

“The 3rd precinct, you know the small town.”

He was quiet for longer than he should have been.

“Lou?”

“I’m not familiar.”

“Huh? You’ve been all over the job, I’m sure you know it.”

“No. I don’t. Listen I have to go.”

“Ah cmon, you joking? Give me something here, I feel like I’m losing my mind!”

“Chris. Don’t call me again.”

And just like that, our conversation was over. I tried calling him back once but I was blocked.

That is how pretty much every conversation about this town went. Nobody wanted to talk about it. Hell, they flat out refused to continue the conversation after a mere mention of this town.

With no other place to get answers I turned to the one person who I knew had gone through the wringer in this town.

Salty Steve.

Was it going to be awkward?

Yes.

Was he going to give me actual answers?

Probably not.

But guess what, I didn’t have anywhere else to go. My imaginary Sergeant with impeccable handwritten notes was certainly not going to help. And the crew of “no longer real” ghosts from the locker room weren’t chomping at the bit to break bread. So Steve was literally my only option.

So, the next time I saw Steve I walked right up to him and started asking questions.

“Steve I know you don’t like me. I know you don’t care what I have to say. I’m asking anyway, what the fuck is wrong with this town.”

Steve looked at me but didn’t say anything.

“Of course. No one has anything to say about this place. I’m just supposed to keep on trucking along like everything is great. I’m just supposed to pretend that cars crashing by themselves and fucking car keys magically appearing around the precinct is just run of the mill? Unbelievable.”

Angry and once again without answers I turned to walk away. I got about 3 feet before I heard the gruff voice behind me.

“First few are the hardest.”

“What?”

“First few jobs are the hardest. You think you’re being a good cop but your definition of that has to change. There is no over arching reason these things happen. I have seen things that a man is never prepared for. Things that will never leave the inner workings of my mind.”

“What do you mean my definition has to change?”

“I mean being a good cop is no longer about taking charge, getting answers, and stopping crime. It’s about listening. It’s about following what you are told. ‘Dispatch’. They aren’t preventing you from doing your job. They’re protecting you.”

“Protecting me? From what? What the hell is this place?”

“I know your type. I was your type. You’re good at your job and you’re hard headed. You tend not to listen. I didn’t listen either. Asked my questions. Made my calls. That was thirty one years ago. You think I’m still here because I love the work?”

After that we both just stood there. Unsure of what to say, I didn’t say anything. Neither did Steve, he walked past me without another word.

“Unit 1 on the air?”

Without hesitation I keyed up the radio.

“Go for unit 1.”

“Respond to the abandoned apartment building 3 blocks from the gas station. A priest from a nearby church will be waiting there for you. Help him with whatever he needs.”

I pulled up to the address and before I even had the door closed behind me, he was there. Tall, older, dressed exactly how you’d picture. He spoke like he was addressing a congregation of one.

“Hello, Christopher. Thank you for accompanying me.”

“You’re welcome? Accompanying you to what, exactly?”

“To put it simply, we are here to keep something at bay.”

“To keep something at bay. Okay. What exactly are we dealing with here?”

He didn’t answer. He just turned toward the building.

“Follow me, Christopher.”

The building was derelict. Boarded windows on the first floor. My flashlight was the only thing cutting through the dark, and the beam felt smaller in there than it should have. The elevators were dead, obviously, so we took the stairs. That’s where I started clocking things. Dark stains on the steps that I’ve seen enough times on the job to not need a lab report. Chunks of the railing snapped off. Gouges in the wall. I’ve walked into a lot of buildings that told me a story on the way in. This one was telling me to leave.

“Hey Father, don’t want to question you but where are we going?”

“There is an apartment on the 3rd floor. There is a ‘resident’ we must make sure cannot leave the building.”

“Wait, what? Someone living here? And we have to make sure they stay?”

“Christopher, to put it nicely this is above your pay grade. I need a witness for the ceremony and you are the only one available tonight.”

That seems to be the running theme. I am the only one ever available. And don’t think I brushed past the whole “ceremony” part. I heard him but what was I going to do. Steve just gave me his version of a Good Will Hunting speech and I’ll be damned if I was going to be stuck in this town for as long as he was. So I did what I was told, I followed the priest to the apartment on the 3rd floor. The series of events that took place there are something I will never be comfortable with.

We stopped at the last door at the end of the hall. Apartment 3C.

I could hear voices inside. Multiple. Overlapping, like a crowded room with the TV on. And then, cutting through all of it, a woman screamed.

Twelve years of training kicked in and I stepped toward that door without thinking. The priest’s arm shot out across my chest.

“Christopher. Whatever you hear in there, you will follow my instructions exactly. Do not be fooled.”

“Fooled by what?”

He didn’t answer.

He reached out and opened the door, and the second it cracked, the apartment erupted. A chorus of screams. Men, women, high, low, all of them at once, coming from everywhere and nowhere. My flashlight was up and my other hand was on my gun, for whatever good either was going to do.

The apartment itself was empty. Dust and bare walls. All of it, every voice, was coming from behind a single bedroom door.

The priest stopped a few feet from it and spoke through the door. Calm. Almost polite.

“It is time. You know why I am here.”

The voices stopped. All of them. Then one voice, low, from just on the other side.

“You brought someone new.”

A pause. I could hear it breathing.

“I can smell the fear on him.”

The priest pulled a small bottle from his coat and began marking the door frame. He was speaking, but not in any language I’ve ever heard. The words had a rhythm to them, like he’d said them a thousand times.

Then the thing behind the door changed its approach.

“Please.” A little girl’s voice. Crying. “Please, mister, I’m not supposed to be in here. He locked me in here. Please open the door.”

I didn’t move. I want you to understand how hard that was. Every instinct I have, everything I’ve ever trained for was pulling me toward that door but I didn’t go. I stood there and I let it beg. The priest never stopped his prayer.

And then it gave up on the girl.

“I’m sorry.”

I froze. Because it was my voice. Exactly my voice, the way it sounds on body cam footage, the way it sounds when I leave a voicemail.

“I’m sorry. I’m so sorry. I tried to save them. I tried.”

Over and over. My own voice apologizing through the door.

The priest’s prayer got louder, building toward something, and the thing behind the door lost whatever composure it had. It hit the door. Then again, harder. The knob rattled like someone was trying to tear it off. The walls shook. Dust drifted down from the ceiling like snow. I took a step back with my hand tight on my gun and the priest didn’t so much as flinch.

Then he said one final word, made a mark on the door, and everything stopped.

Silence. Total.

He turned to me like a man locking up a shop for the night. “It is done. We must leave.”

We walked out of that building at a pace just under a run. Outside, the night was quiet, crickets going like nothing was wrong with the world. The priest straightened his coat.

“Good night, Christopher. Best of luck in your future endeavors.”

And he walked off into the dark.

I’ve been back to that building five times since. It happens every six months, like clockwork. Same ceremony, same door. The voices are different every time. It’s tried the girl again. An old man. A cop yelling officer down. Once it was my mother.

Anyway. I’d tell you more but the note on the Sergeant’s door says my keys are in the freezer of the break room tonight, so I have to go dig them out.

More soon. Probably.
