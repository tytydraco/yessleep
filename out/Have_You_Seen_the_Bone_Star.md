For years, it was my mom and I against the world. She had a bad heart, but she was the kind of person that never let that stop her. You’d find her in the gym three times a week, and we were planning to do the NYC marathon together for her 65^(th) birthday. I think she planned to do it mostly because she wanted me to get out more, as I’m stuck at a desk job. She wanted me to get a bit more *appetite* for life. Run. Jump. Swim. Meet someone. *Want* something.

But life has a funny way of reminding you to sit down, shut up, and count your blessings.

It was a late August afternoon a couple of years ago. I was getting off work when I got a call. My mom was in the hospital. It looked bad. They were telling me to hurry – it was a matter of time.

I freaked out, and I didn’t think straight. I had to get to a cab, and I had to see her. I crossed a busy road not realizing it was open to oncoming traffic, and before I realized my mistake I was facing a land rover slamming on the brakes at 40mph. If it hadn’t been for me wearing a thick backpack, I would’ve slammed my head against the pavement and died. Instead, the entire weight came down on my legs.

I passed out from the pain.

 

When I woke up, I didn’t know where I was. I was covered in a paper-thin sheet, in a white bed. Just moments ago, it’d been around dinner time; now it looked like midnight. I could see the stars through the window. Ursa Major. For a perfect moment, I didn’t remember anything. Not the pain. Not the panic. It was just me, looking at an eternal night sky, remembering the stars.

“Excuse me, are you awake?”

There was a nurse in the room. I could pick out the apologetic tinge in her voice, and I didn’t want to hear it. Whatever she was going to say next was going to be bad. I knew it. I closed my eyes and tried shaking my head, but I had some kind of strap around my neck.

“Just… a minute,” I said. “Gimme a minute.”

But it was already too late. I remembered the call I got earlier. I remembered the land rover. And as more of my damaged nerves reached past the morphine and into my conscious mind, reality caught up to me. I was seconds away from pain. Physical and emotional pain on a scale I’d never felt before.

My eyes teared up, and the stars looked strange. One in particular; a bright one below the pole star, with a dark smudge in the middle.

 

The news was about as bad as bad news can get. My mom had passed from sudden heart failure, and I hadn’t even been there to comfort her. I’d broken both my legs, and I had a small crack in my pelvis. My left leg was, by far, in the worst condition. A severe compound fracture. They were about to put me under to see if it could be saved, but they put the odds at about 70/30 in favor of amputation. An above-the-knee amputation, at that. But what could I do? There was no time, and there were no options. I rolled the dice.

And yeah, I lost. The second time I opened my eyes, I was back in that same room, looking out the same window. I was on my back, with my head tilted outward. I didn’t know it at the time, but my left leg was gone. But for a moment, I was just looking at the night sky. It’s as if no time had passed, but it was almost a full day later.

This time, there was no nurse waiting for me to wake up. Instead, there was an old man.

 

I wasn’t expecting to see another patient. This man was *ancient*. Easily 90 years old, dressed in little more than a hospital gown. He slowly turned his head to me and smiled.

“You see it?” he asked.

“See what?”

I barely made a sound; my voice was so hoarse that it burned my throat. He seemed to catch the drift though.

“There,” he said, pointing out the window with a bony finger. “See that star?”

I didn’t have to look to know which one he was pointing at. It was the strange one, right under the pole star. The bright one with the black spot.

“That’s the Bone Star,” he continued. “You see it, yes?”

“Yeah,” I said. “I see it.”

“Beautiful, isn’t it?”

“Sure.”

We sat there for a while. After a couple of minutes, he got up and wandered over to my window. He pulled the blinds up a little further, allowing a little more of the night sky to peek in. He patted me on the shoulder, but I couldn’t feel a thing.

 

By morning, I got the full report. My left leg was gone, and my right leg was broken. I’d be spending the foreseeable future in a wheelchair, but with physical therapy I’d probably walk again. But we were talking about long goals. For now, my life would look very different.

I’ll be honest, most of what they told me sounded like the adults in the Peanuts cartoons. It was barely even a noise. I completely zoned out, only paying attention when they had some consent form for me to sign. I wanted to be angry at myself for being so careless, but it’s like all the emotion had run out of me. I wasn’t even grieving; it’s like I was barely cognizant.

I had some folks visit me in the hospital. A cousin, a couple of coworkers. One of them brought me a bouquet a blue sunflowers from the hospital gift shop. One of them got me a teddy bear with a red bow. I remember finally being able to sit up in my hospital bed and looking at it. Most of the time I was in a daze. I browsed a bit on my phone, checked missed calls and e-mails, but I couldn’t respond to any of them.

Then, at night, the old man returned. Old, brittle, and barely mobile. His arms so thin you could break them like a twig. He sat down in a chair by my window, leaning back with a juice box.

“You still see it?”

I turned my head, feeling an ache in my neck. The star was still there. Bright, pale, and with a black smudge.

“Yeah.”

“Remember what it’s called?”

“The Bone Star.”

“That’s the one.”

He sipped his apple juice, looking out the window with me. It was something about the noise of the juice box that frustrated me. I asked him a question just to shut him up.

“Why’s it called that?”

“It’s an old name,” he said. “Older than me, but just by a little bit.”

“You’re not *that* old.”

“Lying’s bad. It’ll cost you a leg and a half.”

I snorted at that. He leaned back in his chair, smiling from ear to ear. He had three teeth left, but he wasn’t afraid to show them.

 

His name was Joseph. He was 92 years old and had come in for a bacterial infection. By his own words, chances were, he wouldn’t be leaving. He didn’t mind though; Joseph was at peace with himself and where in life he was. As long as he could watch his favorite shows and drink his juice, he didn’t care.

Joseph stopped by my room every night. He said he used to stay in that room last time he was in, and he liked the view from that chair. They didn’t let him go up to the roof anymore, so this had to do. Every night, he shared a little something about his life, or his family, and sometimes the stars. In particular, the Bone Star.

“It looks strange,” I said once, pointing out the peculiar light. “I’ve never seen it before.”

“It’s rare,” Joseph explained. “They say *most* people can’t see it.”

“How come?”

“Getting hurt changes things,” he continued. “Getting old does, too. There was that thing my pappy used to say, how’d it go…”

He tapped his fingers a little and ran his tongue over his fading teeth. He frowned and looked out the window, nodding to himself.

“Spill of health, straying far. Blessed by palest bone of star.”

“What’s that mean?”

“Those who hurt can see it. If you *don’t* see the Bone Star, you’re not that hurt! Pappy used to drink until he *swore* he could see it, that’s when he knew he’d had too much.”

“I haven’t had a drop.”

“No,” Joseph agreed. “But you’re very, very, hurt.”

With that, we turned back to the window, looking out at the night sky together. He sipped his apple juice. It didn’t quite irk me as much this time around.

 

After a couple more surgeries to fix up my pelvis and right leg, we were going to start looking at recovery. Personally, I wasn’t there yet. What the hell does recovery even mean when your entire life has taken a sudden left turn? Every single day, I was coming to terms with things I’d never had to consider before. Like, what if I ever got married? Who would I have sitting in the front row? Who’d sit in my mom’s spot?

Joseph didn’t have these worries. He was of the opinion that life just kinda sucks, and then you die. He’d say this while munching on some kind of sauce pack. Sometimes he’d ask me to look something up on my phone, or show him a clip of something he’d suddenly remember. We spent hours looking at Monty Python clips; something he hadn’t seen since the 80’s. I wasn’t all that excited about it, but Joseph’s enthusiasm was infectious. He laughed so hard I thought he might call it quits then and there, and when he didn’t, I laughed right along with him.

All the while, I’d see the Bone Star in the corner of my eye, peering through the window. That one strange star with a dark smudge. If I really thought about it, it didn’t make sense. It was a real phenomenon. I knew exactly where to look, and it’d be there. It’s impossible that I wouldn’t have seen it before my accident. It was so clear, bright, and strangely shaped.

I asked a nurse about it one time. I pointed at it, asked if she could see it. She humored me, gave it an honest shot, but couldn’t see it. Maybe she thought the painkillers were getting to me, I dunno, but she was at least nice about it. But yeah, she had no idea what I was talking about.

Joseph knew though. He could see it, clear as day.

 

There was this one day that I remember quite clearly. They’d added an external fixation to my right leg, and the screws were killing me. Whenever I wasn’t on serious painkillers, it felt like dragging around an itchy metal bush stuck to my leg. It just kinda sucked. We were only a couple of days away from trying out a wheelchair, and I was practicing sitting up and doing some minor movements on my own. Nothing major, just getting used to new range of movements without any direct pressure.

Later that evening, I ended up sitting upright by myself, looking out the window. I tracked the stars, beginning with Ursa Major, and then looking over at the Bone Star. As my eyes drifted from it, I could see the lights of the city in the distance. Rooftops, the occasional plane, radio masts. All little temporary lights competing with the real lights above.

But there was something else there. Right across the street, on the rooftop. There was this blinking light going on and off every ten seconds or so, and as it blinked I could see a silhouette. It looked like a person.

I looked a little longer. If it *was* a person, they’d have to be standing right at the edge of the roof. No one in their right mind would do that, so… could it really be a person? I leaned in, squinted, and tried to get a better look.

“Don’t do that.”

I turned around to see Joseph wandering in. Today he was sporting some kind of tube going up his nose, and something taped to his face. He also had a portion of chocolate pudding in a cup.

“Don’t do what?” I asked.

“Don’t look at them.”

I couldn’t help but sneak another peek out the window. The person was still there, on the rooftop across the street. Then I turned my attention back to Joseph.

“They don’t like people looking,” he continued, slurping up another scoop of pudding. “So don’t do that.”

“Why not?

“Bone Star don’t like being seen.”

“You’ve been coming in here every night to look at it. Now you’re telling me it’s bad?”

“It’s bad either way, might as well enjoy it.”

He offered me his second backup pudding cup with a little plastic spoon. I turned him down, but I didn’t look out the window anymore. Not because I didn’t enjoy stargazing, but because I couldn’t keep myself from looking at whatever was waiting across the street.

 

It was a strange time. By day, I’d be sent this way and that, going through x-rays and check-ups. Signing papers, answering phone calls, trying to convince my landlord that we’d have to come up with some way for me to get up the stairs with a wheelchair, seeing as how the damn elevator had been broken for close to *eight months*.

But by night, it was just me and Joseph, sitting by the window. According to what he knew, the Bone Star was the only star in the sky that gazed back. He’d heard about it throughout his long life, but he hadn’t seen it until this last hospital visit. Now that he’d seen it, he knew there wasn’t much time left. That didn’t seem to bother him all that much. He’d just ask me to bring something up for him on my phone, and we’d chat the night away.

I tried to consider things logically. Maybe it was some kind of stress hormone thing. I’d gone through an intense physical trauma; there had to be some serious repercussions. You can’t overstate what kind of damage that does to a body and mind. Who says the Bone Star isn’t just some expression of physical trauma? Then again, how can it be consistent between two people?

And then there were the others.

 

The one on the roof was just the first. Joseph didn’t have a name for them. He just knew that once you’re far enough to see the Bone Star, you can start seeing *other* things that others can’t. Things you shouldn’t look too closely at. I noticed it a couple more times that week, but what bothered me *more* is that I started to see other things. Things that no one else seemed to pay attention to.

For example, one night I saw a man in the parking lot behind the hospital. He stuck to the shadows, but he was walking from one car to the next, looking at them one by one as if searching for something. At one point, he turned his head up toward me. I only saw his face briefly, but there was something strange about it. I couldn’t put my finger on what.

I think he saw me looking. Seconds later, he reached up toward the nearest streetlight and plucked the bulb from it. He must have been able to reach at least 12 feet to touch it.

 

As nights became increasingly uncomfortable, I started looking forward to my daytime routine. My hip was better, so I could sit up straight as long as I didn’t wave my leg around too much.

I remember sitting in the waiting room, counting down the minutes until another check-up. There was supposed to be a nurse coming to get me any minute. But as I sat there, I heard this noise coming from the end of the hall. I thought it might be the nurse bringing something, but no one walked around the corner. Instead, the noise continued.

It was like a whistle, but rhythmic. It roughly matched the rhythm of someone breathing, as if exhaling through a series of small straws. I sat there listening to it for a full minute, until I heard what sounded like naked skin hit the floor. Footsteps. Then a sudden clattering pang as something walked into a hospital chair. There was this frustrated grunt, and I watched the chair being thrown across the hall by something just around the corner.

Then a nurse poked me on the shoulder. I turned my head a little too quickly, feeling an ache in my neck.

“Sorry about the wait,” she said. “We’re almost ready for you.”

“Wait, wait, wait. You see that?”

I pointed at the chair further down the hall. The one that was just thrown.

“Yeah,” she said. “Weird. Why’d they put that there?”

“You didn’t see them throw it?”

“See who throw it?”

“There was someone just-“

I quieted down, not knowing how to explain it. I couldn’t hear the whistling anymore, but the chair was still there. Something *had* thrown it. Something had been standing right around the corner, listening.

 

The next night, as Joseph came by to visit, I didn’t dare to look out the window. Instead I lay awake, staring up at the ceiling. I was trying to hear that whistling. Maybe I heard it. I wasn’t sure. I’d had a lot of painkillers, and everything felt a bit dull. Meanwhile, Joseph was having chocolate milk.

“Is there really something out there?” I asked. “Other than the star?”

“Sure, yeah,” he said, nodding. “They don’t like being seen. Just like the star.”

“So what is it? Aliens?”

“I don’t recall any flying saucers.”

“I’m serious, Joseph.”

I sat up, looking him in the eye. He put down his chocolate milk and cleared his throat, looking right back at me.

“Would it make you feel better if I said it was a demon?”

“Is it?”

He didn’t answer. Then again, I hadn’t answered him either. He waited for me to say something. I rolled my eyes, giving up.

“No,” I sighed. “It wouldn’t make me feel better if you told me it’s a demon.”

“Then how about we shelf that question for a while and just enjoy the quiet, hm?”

He was right – it was quiet. No whistling, no anything. But I couldn’t stop trying to hear something. Maybe with a few more painkillers, I could actually relax for real.

 

Those painkillers ended up being a bit of a lifesaver. I’d get through day and night with them. They were effective enough that, for a while, I couldn’t even see the Bone Star. Not even during those calm nights where the sky was clear of planes. Joseph was a bit grumpy about it, but he didn’t go all high and mighty on me. He shrugged it off and asked me to google some obscure show he used to see.

I began using my wheelchair a bit more. Well, not *my* wheelchair, but a loaner. I wasn’t having any problem with the insurance company, but then again, I wasn’t really trying. They’d given me some numbers, I signed off on it, and that’s that. I didn’t really care. I just took my pills, did my check-ups, and waited for the next day. We were going on two weeks at that point.

As long as I kept up that regimen, I was fine. But even then, I’d spot little things I couldn’t explain. Strange markings on my bed, as if someone had been sitting in it. Taps against the window, as if someone threw pebbles at it. And sometimes, when I was sitting on the toilet, I could almost hear something whistling from the other side of the door.

 

After having been in the hospital for close to three weeks, and slowly starting physiotherapy, things were looking better. I slept better, most corrective surgery was over with, and I could start looking forward to recovery. I just had to take my pills and not look too closely at the night sky. But there was that one night where that didn’t work as intended.

Joseph came into my room with a nasty cough. He didn’t have a snack that time, and he was leaning on a walker. I couldn’t help but look concerned, but he scoffed it off.

“Get in your chair,” he coughed. “We’re going.”

“Going where?”

“We’re going to the roof.”

“They allow you back up there?”

“No.”

I looked at him for a while as he wiped his mouth. Nodding, I started the painstaking routine of getting in my loaner chair. The nerves spiked immediately, and I reached for the painkillers.

“Not tonight,” said Joseph. “Come on.”

“Not tonight what?”

“You’re not taking those tonight.”

“Why not?”

“Because I asked nicely, and you’re a polite young man.”

“Not *that* young.”

“And not *that* polite either. Come on, now.”

 

I did as Joseph asked and got in the chair, pain and all. We made our way to the hallway. Joseph was a bit giddy about it, like he was sneaking out of his dorm room. He checked around the corner for nurses and doctors, then waved me along. We made it all the way to the elevator, and I was getting into it. It was kinda fun. He pushed a couple of buttons, like a code, to access the rooftop.

“They just said I couldn’t go,” he grinned. “They didn’t change the codes.”

“What’s up there anyway?”

“It’s *all* up there. That’s the whole point.”

The elevator made a beeping noise as the door closed, pulling us higher and higher up, past the numbers. When the doors finally opened, we were looking at the wide-open roof.

It was one of the most beautiful things I’d ever seen. Every star twinkled in their own rhythm. I could sense their colors, and size. I’d never seen them so clearly before in my life. Even the Bone Star, with its unusual smudge, burned with an unreal intensity. From where I was standing, they weren’t stars – they were individuals, no different from a crowd of people.

“See?” Joseph said. “You don’t wanna dull that.”

“It’s gorgeous.”

“It is, isn’t it?”

With that, Joseph brought something out of his pocket. A Newport cigarette. I frowned at him, but he didn’t pay me no mind. He lit the cigarette and stepped away from his walker. His legs were a bit shaky, but as long as he was slow and steady, he was fine. For a 90-year-old.

 

I rolled into the middle of the roof and gazed at the stars with the strange old man. He had his cigarette, and I enjoyed the view. The smudge in front of the Bone Star wasn’t just a black cloud, it looked like something geometric. Like an enormous black rock, or a cube, blocking the light from an unknown sun. It just *looked* like smudge from this distance.

“What’re you gonna do when you get out of here?” Joseph asked.

“What?”

“When you get out,” he continued. “What’re you gonna do?”

“Work, I suppose.”

“That it? You just wanna work?”

“Want and want, it’s what I gotta do.”

“Then what?”

I didn’t have an answer. It’s the same question my mom had asked me a hundred times. Once work was over, what else was there? When you’ve been living month by month, bill by bill, you don’t really think about what else there’s to do. See a movie? Check out some restaurant? It all seemed shallow in comparison to what was looking down at me from that rooftop.

“Just think it over,” Joseph continued. “You got time. Do something.”

That was easier said than done. I hadn’t had any plans before all this. Once I thought about what the next few months would be like, the world felt a whole lot smaller; despite standing under the most open sky of my life.

Joseph flicked the ash from his cigarette and leaned on the railing. I rolled up next to him, barely seeing the edge of the parking lot. The streetlights were far less impressive than the night sky, but he didn’t seem to mind.

Then I looked a little closer. There was a small ledge you could roll up on, allowing you to see over the railing. As I did, I realized we weren’t alone.

 

The parking lot was full of people. I say ‘people’, but that was only their rough outline. If you looked a little closer, you could tell they were more like pale flesh-colored silhouettes; a cheap mimicry. I counted at least 30 before a realization nearly stopped my heart. They were looking up at me.

I rolled back, nearly falling out of my chair. I panted and wheezed as I heard a smattering of naked hands, slapping at the wall far below. Were they climbing?

“You alright?” Joseph asked.

“Don’t you see them?!”

“Sure I do.”

“They’re coming up the goddamn walls!”

“I suppose they are.”

I rolled down the ledge and up next to Joseph. He’d finished his Newport. I could hear more skin slapping against the wall. Louder. Rhythmic. Closer.

“We gotta go,” I said, reaching for his arm.

“Nah,” he said, pulling it back.

“The fuck you mean ‘nah’? We’re *leaving!*”

“Thought you said you didn’t have plans.”

“I said I didn’t have plans, not that I wanted to get *hunted!*”

“Haven’t you figured it out yet?”

He leaned forward, looking me in the eyes. I expected to smell the cigarette off his breath, but there was nothing there. I couldn’t smell a thing. I reached for his arm again, and watched my fingers pass through his hospital gown.

“The Bone Star lets you see things that you ain’t supposed to see,” Joseph reiterated. “Didn’t I tell you that?”

My words got stuck in my throat as a tall dark arm reached over the railing. Then another, and another, and another. Joseph looked away, and disappeared among them like a white mouse in a snake pit.

 

I pulled on my chair as fast and hard as I could. I pulled until my arms burned and the wheels touched the edge of the elevator. Something pulled the chair back, but my momentum shot me forward, making me tumble into the elevator haphazardly, sending another spike of pain up my spine. Like a fire trickling from my missing leg all the way up to the tension in my neck. I fumbled for whatever button I could reach, accidentally sending me down to the third floor. As the doors closed, I saw a pale flesh-like face look at me with an eyeless grin. Something akin to a tangle of kelp where a mouth ought to be, drooling with a gelatin-like texture.

The door closed, and my heart was beating out of my chest. I was lying on the floor of the elevator, my mind pulling between pain and fear. There was an audible thump coming from above, and as I held my breath, I heard a rhythmic whistle.

The lights flickered, and the elevator gave off this terrible metallic screech. I pressed buttons at random, and I think I got the open-door button. The doors opened as the screeching got louder. I crawled out as something collapsed out of the roof of the elevator, crashing through with at least 500 pounds of weight.

I was lying in the middle of the hall, screaming for help. It was the middle of the night, but I could hear something stirring. I couldn’t stop and wait; I had to keep moving with whatever strength I had. I flopped onto my stomach and crawled toward the nearest door; a bathroom.

 

I slammed the door shut behind me and locked it. Something crashed against it, letting out a high-pitched moan. The door shook more than it ought to. The hinges rattled, and I could see the screws in the handle move. I crawled back, leaning against the far wall.

There were at least three of them outside. One more on the other side of the leftmost wall, scratching against the tiles. Something was shaking the pipes in the toilet, and I could hear this humming noise coming from the light bulb.

I closed my eyes, blocked my ears with my hands, and screamed. With my eyes closed, I could see the Bone Star. The pale white light, obscured by something impossible. Even here, even now, I could see it. And hence, it could see me. *They* could see me.

And they didn’t like being watched.

 

The top hinge of the door came loose, shooting across the room like a bullet. The water pressure in the sink made the tap pop off as steaming hot water came shooting out. The light bulb cracked, soaking the room in darkness, as something increasingly desperate reached toward me.

In that moment, I couldn’t feel any pain. I couldn’t think about my job, or my apartment, or even my goddamn leg. I thought about my mom, and losing out on us running the NYC marathon together. The one we’d trained for. The one we’d been *owed*.

The other hinge came loose, and with a final push, the door fell off. A whistling breath escaped from a silhouette, glistening in the moonlight. Something that wasn’t even *trying* to look like people anymore.

I didn’t know what to think. I didn’t care about pain. I didn’t care about anything that’d happened. In that one moment, all I could think about was that there had to be another day, and I had to get another chance. It couldn’t end like this.

I opened my eyes and stared at something inhuman. Something without eyes or soul. Something that didn’t belong here.

 

I had a hundred thoughts at once, and none of them were about the Bone Star. Even if I wanted to, I couldn’t picture it in my head. I was thinking of all the things I wouldn’t get to do. Swimming. Fishing. Climbing. Making out with a girl outside a punk concert and getting frustrated that her damn piercings tickle my nose.

My mind flashed as the possibilities of life came crashing down on me, and at the very end of a long line of thoughts, there was nothing left. Just me, face to face with whatever wanted me dead.

But a slow realization dawned on me. I couldn’t picture the Bone Star anymore. I couldn’t even *imagine* it. And as that thought washed over me like a soothing balm, the thing in front of me stopped making sense too. I couldn’t picture it either. I couldn’t imagine *that* either. I couldn’t think of it. I couldn’t perceive it. I couldn’t look at it, and it couldn’t look back.

As the haze cleared from my mind, I watched a terrified nurse sprinting into my empty bathroom.

 

The damage was real. The damage to the bathroom, the elevator… it was all there. Don’t ask me how it can be, I have no idea. But I know I didn’t imagine it. You can ask the janitors and the technician who had to double-check the elevator. Funny how the elevator at the hospital got busted on the same day that the elevator in my apartment complex got fixed.

A couple of days after that incident, I got to go home. I was rolled out of there with a chocolate pudding cup in hand. I had a plan for my physical rehab, and I wasn’t going to be bound to a chair forever. It was a temporary solution. My doctor suggested I keep a goal in mind; that’s the best way to motivate yourself for steady progress. I came up with a goal and stuck to it.

It took months of work. Little over a year and a half, actually. But I ran the NYC marathon with a prosthetic leg.

I’m still hesitant to look up at the night sky sometimes. There’s a part of me that believes there’ll come a day when I see the Bone Star again. And I suppose, if what Joseph told me was true, I will. Maybe we all will.

But when I finally do see something I’m not supposed to, maybe it’s not all bad. Maybe it’ll be the kind of thing I can take in stride, stepping away from a walker for a final time.

And maybe in a crowd of impossible things, there’ll be some familiar faces.
