I moved back in with my mom a few months ago. She had a nasty fall on the basement stairs and needed someone to help out while she healed. I had recently finalized my second divorce and needed a place to stay. With Dad ten years gone and no siblings to speak of, I was her only option. With my bank account drained and the apartment signed over to my ex, she was mine as well. My mother reminded me of this several times in the ensuing negotiations, her voice sickly sweet and thin with age. So, reluctantly, I agreed to come home.

I didn’t notice anything was wrong at first. Yes, her arm was broken and still in a cast, and she moved slower than the last time I had visited two years previously. Whether that was the fall or age closing in, I couldn’t say. But she still got around well enough. She puttered and tidied and meandered through the house, though she avoided the stairs. She stayed up late watching Jeopardy reruns and made herself bowls of macaroni. Once, I watched her use a pair of salad tongs to snag a bag of chips from the top of the refrigerator. With her good arm, of course. I watched and wondered if she needed me home at all. Every sign of self-sufficiency was a tally mark in my mind, proof of her wellness and ability to live alone. I hoarded them greedily and let them soothe me. Only for a little while, I would think.

But that all changed a few weeks after I moved in.

I made spaghetti for dinner that night, a meal she usually loves. But now, I watched as she picked out chunks of tomato and scraped aside noodle, occasionally popping a piece of ground beef in her mouth. She didn’t touch her salad.

“Something wrong?” I asked lightly, wondering if she’d been snacking all afternoon again.

“With what? No, of course not,” she said, and immediately picked up a big forkful of noodles. She swallowed them down and hummed appreciatively.

She kept doing it over the next few days. She would pick at something until I questioned her about it, then eat heartily to convince me nothing was wrong. I was starting to wonder if she’d been ordering food while I was out, or, again, eating too many snacks. But I never found anything suspicious in the trash, and the bag of her favorite chips remained half-full.

She started to speak less. Gradually, I realized she hadn’t complained about the neighbors in days, and was barely responding in full sentences to me anymore. That was when I started to suspect she was angry at me about something. But what? She wasn’t one to be angry in silence; my mom has always had a short temper, and she’s always been happy to make it everyone else’s problem. I hadn’t done anything she had been clearly pissed off about in a couple weeks, which, now that I thought about it, was also strange.

Then, she wouldn’t leave the house anymore. This wasn’t so strange in isolation; she had always been a homebody. But I could normally get her to come with me to the store every once in a while. “It’s too hot out,” she would claim and wave me off. I would only ever catch her outside at night, head tilted up to the stars and eyes closed.

When I really got worried is when I noticed she’d grown a couple inches taller. She passed me in the hall on her way to the bathroom, glancing over as she gave me a wide berth. I stood there for a moment, shrouded in darkness as the bathroom door closed behind her, trying to place what had made me so uneasy. My stomach did a strange little flip when I realized what was off: her eyes were level with my own. She had been shorter than me for years now.

At first, I did wonder if it was me. Had I started shrinking in my old age? I mean, I’m not that old. But it made more sense to me than anything else.

I stood with my back flat to a wall in my bedroom and made a light pencil mark at the top of my head. Then, I turned and measured the wall. 5’7”, right where I had been since I was sixteen years old.

I checked and rechecked my height many more times that night. It did not change.

“Mom, can you stand against the wall for me? I want to measure you,” I explained the next morning as she came into the kitchen for coffee.

She grimaced. “Really? Why?”

“Please? Just do it for me,” I coaxed, using a soft tone so I didn’t anger her. Tone had always been everything to my mother. If I was too sharp, or short, her temper would bubble up and she would refuse any request I made. This time, she acquiesced, and pressed herself flat against the wall.

I measured her. 5’5”, her normal height. She started to move away towards the coffee pot. “Wait, please,” I said, a little too firmly. But to my surprise, she waited in silence.

I measured again. This time, as I moved away from the wall, I noticed her body shift. I blinked in surprise.

“Mom are you— were you crouching?”

“Don’t be silly,” she said, rolling her eyes. She made her way over to the coffee pot and poured a cup. The tape measure dangled limply from my hands. Something roiled in my stomach.

“I’m worried about you,” I choked out. It was hard to say out loud. “You’ve been acting weird.”

She turned around to face me, eyes level with mine again. “There is nothing wrong with me,” she said slowly, her tone reassuring, the sentence the longest one she had managed in a week.  She clasped the hot cup of coffee between her palms. She did not take a sip from it.

A few days later, I found her tooth on the bathroom floor. It was yellowed and chipped with an old silver filling in the center. A molar.

I begged her to go to the hospital. “This isn’t normal,” I said, clutching her tooth in my palm so hard it could have broken skin. “Please, Mom, something is wrong!”

She waved her hand in a shooing motion, absorbed in something on the television. “I’m fine.”

“Mom!” I yelled, grabbing her face in my hands, forcing her to meet my eyes. “Your tooth fell out! You are not well!” I forgot to check my tone. I did inherit some things from her, after all.

She wrenched her head away, eyes flashing. That familiar anger rippled across her face. I was almost glad to see it. But she stayed silent, storming out of the room as quickly as she could manage. The slam of her bedroom door echoed through the house.

I stood there in the middle of the spinning room, standing in the eye of a hurricane, close to panic but unable to move. When I had grabbed her face, her mouth parted involuntarily, and I saw the truth.

This was not the first tooth she had lost. She must have hidden them from me, this last one left on the floor an oversight. Her mouth was riddled with holes, some still bloody, more teeth missing than there were left in her head. And in the gaping, gummy spaces, something sharp was growing to fill them.

I called an ambulance. I begged at her locked bedroom door, two EMTs beside me with a portable medical kit. Trying to keep it together, I explained her missing teeth, how she’d been acting strange, withdrawn and not eating much. I did not mention her height. The EMTs were sympathetic, but told me there was nothing they could do. They couldn’t force my mother to get medical care, stuff her into the back of the ambulance kicking and screaming.

“If she changes her mind, please give us another call,” one said, nodding to her partner. With that, they packed up and left.

Mom didn’t leave her room again until late that night. I was already in bed, scrolling on my phone, when I heard her door creak open across the hall from my own.

I almost called out to tell her there were leftovers in the fridge, but something stopped me. I sat up and looked towards my door. I could see light spilling out from her room under the crack. In the center, two twin trunks of shadow stood, unmoving. For one minute, then two, then five. I watched the minutes pass by on my phone. Dread crept up my spine.

I don’t know what made me do it. It was just my mother, standing out in the hallway. But when the sixth minute ticked past, I jumped up and tiptoed over to my door, locking it in one swift motion.

Immediately, something hit my door with a loud thud.

I leapt backwards. My heart thumped in my ears. “Mom?”

She didn’t respond. She didn’t hit the door again, but twisted the doorknob, gentle turns becoming frantic rattling when she found it locked.

I didn’t say anything else. My throat felt solid, like I might never speak again. I stepped backwards until my butt met the bed, and I sat down, watching her pull at the doorknob every few seconds.

She tried to get into my room for hours, until the black of night lightened to gray, her attempts getting slower and farther apart as the long night ended. Finally, when the sun started to peek over the horizon, I heard her shuffle away, back into her own room. She closed her door with a quiet click.

I laid down where I was, pulling my feet up onto the bed and curling into a ball facing the door. I tried to get some sleep. I did not.

Still, I waited several hours before leaving my room. I heard my mother get up and use the bathroom, move around in the kitchen, and finally settle in front of the TV before I dared to unlock my door and leave my sanctuary.

“Morning,” she said as I entered the room, speaking carefully so her lips covered her teeth. She must have been doing that for some time now. I nodded. I didn’t bother to ask her about last night. It was clear she didn’t remember. Either that, or she didn’t think there was anything wrong with it. I wasn’t sure which option was worse.

When she held out the remote to change the channel, I noticed her good arm was a sickly, pale gray. I could see this now, because her wrist stretched a couple inches past the long sleeve of her nightgown. The fingers that wrapped around the remote control seemed to be a little longer than I remembered. She was sitting, but I knew she had grown taller too. It was something about where her shoulders sat relative to the back of her armchair. I could see the change, feel it in the pit of my stomach where every new evidence of wrongness congealed together.

I didn’t say anything. I settled back onto the couch and watched TV with her. My body felt like I was running a race sitting down. I let the sound of my heartbeat pounding in my ears hypnotize me.

You can’t panic forever. Eventually, you will calm down, even when you think it’s impossible.

Another week or two passed, somehow. We moved around each other during the day, pretending everything was normal and looking past the things that were not, like the way my mother had almost stopped speaking entirely, or the way she seemed to grow a little longer every day. She wouldn’t eat anymore. Well, she wouldn’t eat in front of me. I caught her one night, standing in the soft glow of the basement freezer, picking raw strips of frozen chicken out of the package and swallowing them whole. I stood silent on the dark staircase, but her eyes shot to me anyway as she slurped a piece of fat from her chin and licked the juice from her fingers. Neither of us spoke. I just backed away and left her to it. After that encounter, whole packages of meat would disappear in the night. She stopped trying so hard to hide it.

Every night, I would lock my door and wait. Some nights, she didn’t come. Others, we would be in a stalemate from sundown to sunup, a few feet away from each other on either side of a locked door while she relentlessly tried to get inside. To get to me.

I knew I should do something, but I had no idea what. Call the police? She hadn’t done anything illegal, and I was so afraid of what would happen. What if they took her away? What if they hurt her? Or what if… I didn’t know. She had already refused medical treatment. If I kept calling the ambulance out, I might get in trouble.

It came to a head one night when I stayed out of my room too late. Mom and I were watching some old movie we both loved, and for a little while I didn’t look at her too closely, and let myself forget. She laughed some, and even managed a few words.

The room darkened by degrees as the sun sank outside, but I didn’t notice at first. I only realized something had changed when I glanced at my mother and saw her staring slack-jawed at the screen. She didn’t blink. A thin strand of drool trickled down her chin and puddled in her lap.

“Mom?”

She blinked once and turned her head to me, no light of recognition in her eyes. She didn’t wipe away the drool. She just stared at me, and as she did, her pupils expanded so wide that they seemed to pass the confines of the iris entirely and consume her sclera. A soft moan fell from her open mouth.

She stood, rising from the armchair to her full height, except she seemed to go on and on forever, and keep rising when it shouldn’t have been possible, until the wispy gray hairs on her head brushed the ceiling. She hunched over, because she had to, because she had grown too tall to stand up straight. Finally, her ascent stopped.

I, who had been frozen in terror, thawed and shot up from the couch.

She lunged at me, and her thick, sharp fingernails caught my robe as I scrambled away. Her jaw unhinged, and her new teeth gleamed blue in the television glow, and saliva dripped onto my sleeve as she lowered her head to my arm.

I tore myself from my robe, slipping it off just as her teeth clamped down on empty fabric. I don’t know what she did next because I was gone, running to my room and shoving the dresser up against the door and then my back against that. My arm was bleeding, I noticed from somewhere far away, four jagged scratch marks trailing down its length and oozing bright, fresh blood. Mom threw her weight against the door and rattled the dresser so hard that several drawers dislodged and fell to the ground. I grabbed a pair of socks that had landed nearby, mopped at my wound mechanically and waited for the night to end.

When the sun had been up for at least an hour, I stood by the door and listened. After clawing at my door for half the night, Mom gave up and wandered away. I wasn’t sure where she had gone. I couldn’t hear her moving anywhere.  I considered trying to escape through a window or even making a break for the front door. But the windows in my room were old and swollen shut, and I wasn’t sure I could unlock the front door before she got to me.

I opened my door a crack and peered out. No sign of her. The house was dim and silent. I tiptoed down the hall, passing my mother’s empty room, the bathroom, the hall closet—

A sound. Barely perceptible, coming from the hall closet directly to my left. I hesitated, torn between running or checking on her.

“Mom?” I whispered, and pulled the door open.

She was crouched on the floor, nesting in a pile of sheets that had fallen from the shelves above. Her eyes were wild and still full of her pupils, only a tiny hint of white at their edges. The sheets beneath her looked like a crime scene, and for a moment I worried she was hurt. But no, it was just hunks of uneaten steak spoiling and oozing red, and— I gagged at the sight of a picked-over rat carcass. I hadn’t even known we had rats. Then, I caught a glimpse of something half hidden under the sheets, something much larger than a rat. A dead raccoon. We definitely didn’t have those. She had been outside.

Her head tilted and her eyes fixed on my arm, on the deep scratches still half-caked in blood that I never bothered to clean. Her brow furrowed. Something passed behind her eyes and lingered.

“S-sorry,” she said through a mouthful of sharp teeth. “S-so sorry.” The moment passed. Her eyes squinted at the soft morning light intruding into her den. She reached up with long gray fingers and pulled the closet door gently closed again.

I stood there, sick with worry and fear and grief. I didn’t move for several minutes, just lingered in the hall in silence only broken by the sound of soft movement against sheets, and wet, frenzied chewing.

I don’t think I’m going to be leaving the house again. I’ve holed up in my room for now, typing this all up on my laptop while I listen to her stalk up and down the halls. I locked and barricaded the doors and windows to the best of my ability so she can’t get out again, but I know it’s only a matter of time.

I just want someone to know what happened to me, I guess. To know that I existed beyond this place.

And my mother was never a saint, but she didn’t deserve this. My hope is that once I’m gone, and there is nothing of me left to sustain her, she will pass in her own time. I know it’s cruel to leave her to starve. But I can’t let her hurt anyone else. And I won’t kill her.

So when I am finished writing, and the sun comes up tomorrow, I will take her down in the basement with me, lock the door and throw away the key. I am afraid, but I offer myself freely.

What else can I do? She’s my mother, after all.
