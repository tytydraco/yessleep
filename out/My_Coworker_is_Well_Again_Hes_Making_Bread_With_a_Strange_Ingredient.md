Ken’s cancer disappeared in a matter of four days without any chemo or medication.

I didn’t want to question a miracle, especially not for a man I’d worked alongside for five years. He was happy, our coworkers were happy. The world had magically become all right again. The bakery was full of gratitude and joy, and patrons declared it a miracle to see him beaming as he brought out doughnuts and lemon meringue pie.

But he smelled off.

My sense of smell has always been sensitive. It’s served me well in food service, though I wish it didn’t come with the responsibility of being the chief of detecting spoiled milk and cheese a whole day before other people.

It was faint. But Ken let off this smell of iron. Slight as a knife after cutting red meat. Slightly sweet on the edge of metallic sour. It was definitely him, as the moment he left the prep area, the smell disappeared.

I didn’t say anything. Whatever med or miracle had worked, smelling a little weird was a fine trade off.

The odor didn’t seem to be from poor health or hygiene. After a week, Ken’s complexion - normally ruddy from rosacea and patches of broken blood vessels - became hale and smooth. People asked his secret and he just laughed. “I think my body is glad to be back to normal!”

It was three days before he started making the bread.

“It’s bad. We’re not selling it.”

Carla was a timid girl, eighteen and fresh out of high school. If a worldly woman like me told her to pitch the lot, she did it. But for once, she paused. “Miss Emma, this is Ken’s batch. Are you sure it’s bad? I just asked because the color was a little different…”

I was fifteen years too old for “Miss,” but Carla was too sweet to age anyone by calling them “ma’am.” I tapped my nose. She nodded. “Gotcha.”

I never minded when Carla asked if something smelled right. She’d had COVID last year, and her sense of smell wasn’t as strong as it had been. Sweet and hard-working, Carla was thoughtful and absorbed everything we taught her. The owner Linda loved her, and it had absolutely nothing to do with the fact that she was her niece. It was a rare instance in which nepotism was no trouble.

The bread did have a strange color, I thought. A slight pink tinge to the pale crumb was barely noticeable, but the smell was enough to make me gag. Carla dutifully headed toward the back of the bakery, but Ken appeared from the door to the front. She jumped.

“Hey Carla! That the bread I finished?” he asked, eyes bright. The girl glanced at me. I approached.

“Sorry Ken. I’m getting a whiff of something. Might be the eggs were too close to date,” I said. “Better safe than sorry.”

He gave me a strange look, eyes skimming along the top of my head like a dragonfly off a pond. No eye contact. “I don’t smell anything bad. Do you, Carla?”

“Um, the color was a little different. So I asked Miss Emma.”

Ken’s brows rose. “It does have a couple new ingredients. Different spices. That’s why the color is different.”

“You changed the recipe without asking Linda?” I asked. He blinked, no concern or guilt reflecting back. “I mean, she’s lenient, but you should probably run it by her, yeah?”

“Aw, better to ask forgiveness than permission. Give it a try,” he urged. “It’s delicious, really. This new recipe will be a popular one!”

“I’m not comfortable with it,” I said, “I know pungent and I know bad. Something in it is bad. Throw this out, clear it with Linda, and we’ll try the recipe again.” Ken stared at me, finally meeting my gaze. My shoulders tightened and I tried to understand the dark eyes fixing on me, contemplating me.

Then Ken sighed, shrugging. “If that’s your decision, I respect it.”

Relieved - and not sure why I was - I accompanied Carla outside after dumping the loaves into a bag. The dumpster lid was heavy and I hauled it up so she could tip the fresh bag in. It thumped softly against the bags already clustered on the bottom. Satisfied, we headed inside to clean up and start the rest of our work.

A good day at the bakery for me is one where I never have to go up front. Never been great with customers. I prefer being in the back, kneading dough and baking. I’d worked out a deal with Ken; if he would be the one to go up front and help with overflow at the register, I would take out the garbage. Even if we had a failed batch of sourdough. I could hold my breath that long.

Hauling out another full bag, I stopped outside the door. The back of the building was old brick and we kept it neat, and it was only a few steps to the dumpster. Everything was like when I’d come out with Carla. But something was missing.

I looked around, realizing what was off. No animals. Normally when I hauled garbage out, raccoons and squirrels and pigeons would be sequestered nearby, lurking in trees, ready to pounce on the new meal if they could work their way into the dumpster or grab anything dropped. But the only animal I saw was a squirrel, absently nibbling on a walnut shell.

Swinging the dumpster open, I tossed in the new bag. It was getting full, the smell of fermenting bread and crusty buttercream rolling over me sickly sweet. And somehow I could still make out the iron odor of the off bread through plastic.

A screech rose behind me. I turned to see the squirrel, walnut abandoned.

I’d never seen a squirrel angry before that. Scared, sure. But never angry. Its fur puffed out and it screeched, backing up fast, tiny ears pulled back as it bared its flat little teeth. Then it bolted.

I slammed the dumpster closed and hurried inside to scrub my hands raw.

Call it paranoia. But there was nothing in that bin that hadn’t been there before except that bread. I was like an animal, I thought ruefully. Afraid of a weird smell, set on the tips of my toes and teeth, hair bristling.

At the end of the day, I sank into one of the break room seats. Carla and I were closing, and she was handling the front of the shop. She came in with several white cake boxes stacked in her arms. “The front’s all wrapped up. I can run what didn’t sell down to the soup kitchen.”

“Thanks Carla,” I said. “I’ll take next run. Ready to head out?” No one walked out to the lot alone at night.

She hesitated before setting down the boxes. “I need to talk to you.”

I crossed my legs with effort. They ached after standing all day. “Pop a squat kid.”

Carla sat down in the chair across from me. I got the impression she was picking over words in her head like a bird over seed. Her green eyes were sweet, darting. “Um. I don’t…want to get Ken in trouble. But I think he’s not be in his right mind.”

“…What’s up, hon?”

“He…ate the bread. Out of the dumpster.”

Her hands were clenched. I glanced toward the back door. “You’re sure?”

“I went out back because I heard something rustling and I though maybe an animal had gotten in. But it was Ken, digging through the garbage. I asked him what he was doing, and he…he said it was a crime to waste it. That he’d eat it if no one else could. He was just scarfing it down.” Carla had wrung her apron into a sweaty ball between her fingers. “He tried to get me to eat some. I told him he shouldn’t be eating stuff that was dirty. He…stopped. But he scooped it all up and took it to his car.”

Ken had left an hour ago. I almost asked why she hadn’t said something earlier, but I remembered there was a security camera up front. She didn’t want anyone else to hear. “We need to tell Linda. He had a serious diagnosis…who knows how that can mess up your head?”

She seemed unsure. I definitely was, but it was the only thing I could believe at the moment. Unless he’d dosed the bread with drugs. Which opened up a whole other host of problems I didn’t even want to consider.

“Aunt Linda wouldn’t just fire him, right? She’ll talk to him.”

“Of course. He might need time off. We can’t trust him with food right now.”

I’ll always regret not texting Linda. It seemed better to discuss it in person than to fire off a message saying, “Hey, Ken’s eating out of the trash.” I should have called, but I hated making calls.

I’m making excuses. I just didn’t want to deal that evening, because I knew even if Ken wasn’t dismissed, he’d be up for hardship if he couldn’t work while recovering. Maybe I should have asked him about it, maybe I should have just told Linda.

It’s all moot. Because I entered the bakery the next morning to see Linda chatting with Ken. Her brown ponytail bounced enthusiastically as she spoke, and Ken smiled wide and with brighter, shinier teeth than he’d ever had. “Hey Emma!”

Linda turned, brushing crumbs from her smart yellow sweater. “Emma, the new recipe is amazing! You have to try it.” Linda gestured toward the pan on the counter and I stared at the fresh, lovely rolls letting off the smell of new death.

“You don’t smell that?” I asked, already knowing the answer.

“I smell fresh bread. It’s sweet and chewy…reminds me of my Mom’s rolls. Ken’s got the ratio down!”

I faltered. These had come out of the oven minutes ago. It wasn’t the bread from the garbage. But the same odor clung to them and to Ken, more intense than ever. “I need to talk to you, Linda.”

“I know what you’re about to say. Yes, he ran the recipe by me,” Linda said, sighing as she took another bite. “I approved!”

“Ah…it’s about something else…”

“The dumpster, right?” Ken’s face was bright with mirth. “I think Carla got the wrong idea. I was getting the bread to feed to some animals. I wanted to take it home to leave it out for the local critters.”

The glint in his eye sparkled with a lie. Carla wouldn’t have come to if there had been any other way to interpret the situation. But I just nodded. “Ahhh, gotcha.” He seemed satisfied and I waited until Linda had trailed off to her office before slipping in behind her and closing the door in a hurry.

“Linda, Carla was sure of what she saw. Ken was eating out of the garbage. She wouldn’t say that without being sure.” I glanced back at the door as I talked, wondering if Ken might be listening.

Linda nodded faintly. “Hm. Well, he says he wasn’t. So…”

I waited for more. She offered nothing. I could smell iron on her breath, wrapped in the sweetness of carb and crumb. “Are you going to talk to him?” I pressed.

“Already did. It’s a he-said-she-said situation. So if there’s nothing else…”

I didn’t know what to do. If she wasn’t seeing the problem - if she wasn’t believing her own niece - what was the solution? Spy on Ken and watch for more weird behavior?

The squirrel’s reaction sat in the back of my head all day. Ken was chipper and cheerful, and people took a whiff of the rolls and immediately wanted to try them. I felt sick watching them munch and enjoy, exclaiming about the wonderful taste.

I knew something was wrong. But what was I supposed to say? That I smelled the reek of death and meat in the bread that literally no one else detected? Linda hadn’t gotten a hint.

Could I just be nuts? Could it be that Carla had really been mistaken? No one got sick over the course of the day.

“What’s your secret?” I asked casually over lunch. Ken was eating a steak sandwich - with the new bread making up the slices  of course - and he licked the sauce off his fingers as he thought.

“Hm…it’s probably the proteins in it. I have something special that makes the bread extra chewy and dense.”

“What is it?”

He smiled and took another bite. I watched him. The silence went on. I tried again. “Have you cleared this new ingredient specifically with Linda?”

Ken licked his fingers again and I watch the steak drip pink onto his plate. “Do you not trust me, Emma?”

The Ken I knew could never keep a secret, always double and triple checked ingredients, refused to compromise on quality. He didn’t believe in secret recipes; he wanted everyone to know what was in their food so people could be safe.

Plus he always ate his steak well done. A crime against beef, sure, but he hated the taste of blood.

When I left that day, there was a pan of the rolls in the oven. I cranked the oven up as high as it could go and hurried out the door.

One batch wasn’t enough to make a difference, but it was better than nothing. Should I call the health inspector?

As it turned out, they were behind on requests. It would be a week before they could make it out to a tiny bakery that had never had issues before. A week of this. I clamped down on my unease, hovering in a haze of uncertainty. What if I really was just smelling things?

People were clamoring for the rolls. Delight gleamed in their eyes as Ken handed them out, beaming, assuring them there would be more coming out of the oven soon when the trays emptied.

Linda didn’t seem to hear me when I asked about the “secret ingredient.” Her eyes were distant as she chew on the bread, savoring its sweetness. “Like manna from heaven,” she said once.

Carla didn’t eat it. During a breakfast rush, she stepped close to me and whispered, “This is weird, right? It’s like…people are obsessed. Like when Labubus were in.”

I didn’t know what that was. I didn’t care. But obsessed was the right word. Women who counted every calorie were devouring the rolls, cut men on keto diets and protein shakes consuming four at a time. Children gnawed on them, dunking them like cookies in milk and cocoa.

And over the course of a few days, I was seeing differences in our regulars. People with acne had clear complexions. No one used canes anymore. Wrinkles were filling, age was falling away like dead skin off a snake.

And everyone reeked of iron.

I turned to Carla. “Don’t eat one, no matter what. I’ve got a health inspector coming in.”

But what if they didn’t smell anything off? Would it be enough? Could I get them to test it in a lab?

The best thing I could think to do was follow Ken after he got off work. Whatever he was getting to put in the bread, he kept it hidden, dancing around the question every time. I’d seen a bucket in the cooler once, but it had already been emptied and cleaned. So I had to see the source of this could ingredient if I wanted to figure out how to stop this.

It’s hard to explain the paranoia and terror that overcame me when I saw people eating the bread. Certainly people becoming obsessed, even addicted, was bad. But as far as I could see, it was making people healthy and happy. But not even once did I consider that it might just be a miracle. I knew it was wrong the way my nose knew the sour sweetness of rot in beef.

I felt like that squirrel, almost rabid with anger.

It was nearly eight when Ken left the lot that night. Parked across the street at a deli, I pulled out after him, trying to keep some distance between us. This was crazy, I told myself, memorizing his license plate.

We drove for miles, and the sleepy town evolved into trees. I recognized the area; we had a state park not far, and most of us had walked it before. Ken navigated the winding road leisurely, and at this hour, there weren’t many other cars up here.

When we finally reached the lot, I parked toward the playground, relieved to see a few families still present, one of them grilling out in the last of the summer evening sun. I could be heading over to them for all he knew.

Ken didn’t even look my way as he climbed out of his car. He held a clean bucket in his right hand and immediately took the path nearest to him. I climbed out of the car and hurried after him.

Following him on the trail was a little easier, though the dark was descending quickly. He had a flashlight and I had one on my phone, though it took me a minute to figure out how to work the thing. I followed the bob of his light, keeping my own trained on the ground to follow the path. My hands thrummed with my heartbeat, and when I covered the beam with my palm - just in case he turned around, just in case he was watching - I could swear I saw my veins pulsing pink in the flesh.

We left the path and I caught a new odor when the wind blew in my face. I tilted my head, trying to gauge what it was. Tinged with the odor of offal, there was sweetness to it, and something that made my nose tingle.

The smell grew strong as I followed Ken. He made no sudden movements, a dark figure against a wavering sliver of light, heading deeper and deeper into the trees. The ground leveled out and Ken disappeared between two trees ahead of me. I listened as I reached the trees and stopped.

His shoes shuffled against the dirt and I peered between boughs and branches, inhaling the scent of green and bark to help offset the foul odor. Ken was on his hands and knees and the light fell over a dark hole I couldn’t see into. He lowered his arm into it.

Wet, squelching noises hit me and I tried to think of what to do. Ken was here, and he was scooping something out of the ground. I saw pink, spongy something in his hand as he hefted it into the bucket. I couldn’t even imagine what it was supposed to be. Scoop after scoop, he filled the bucket.

Another handful, and this time, he shoveled it into his mouth. I gasped in disgust.

He lifted his head. Caked on his chin was pink, clotted mush, waxy like pork fat. Chunks of it stuck in his teeth as he chewed with a dreamy expression. “Come to see the source, Emma? The miracle.”

The slip in the bucket reeked and my nose was on fire. Standing up, Ken turned to face me. I backed up. He examined me, taking in my skittishness. “I was in a bad way. I was so sick. I came out here to enjoy the trees, the breeze…didn’t think I’d get many more.”

One hand extended toward the pit. “It called me. I could smell it so far off. Like…life given a smell. Blood and bone and heartbeat, al going and going. Eternal. I came here and knew I had to dig, knew. You’ve never known anything until it tells it to you.”

His eyes rolled as if he were drunk on the memory. I didn’t understand and I didn’t want to. “And now…everything is different. And I couldn’t keep that gift to myself. It keeps giving, you see?”

I didn’t. He held out his hand, fingers covered in the dense, fatty substance. “It’ll make everyone better. Want a taste, Emma?”

I ran. I ran and ran, terror pouring speed into my feet and lifting me over grass and boughs. I couldn’t hear him following but I was afraid of this place, terrified of Ken, and I had to get someone out there to help him.

He heard a melody of life, but it was a dirge. Ken wasn’t Ken anymore.

I didn’t throw up until I’d reached my home, called the police, and sat still, ruminating on the substance sticking to his face. Poor Ken. I couldn’t get his distant, delighted expression  out of my mind as I emptied my stomach into the toilet bowl.

It had called him. Maybe because he was sick, maybe because he was desperate. And it worked on other people, gauging by their reactions. What was going to happen if people kept eating it?

Could I convince him? Could I get him to see reason? Was this some kind of parasite or something more? Would the police deal with it?

The next day I went into work, unsure of whether Ken would be there. The second I entered, I heard his laugh from the back and tensed.

He was bringing up more bread, and when he spotted me, he offered a smile. “Hey Emma. Let’s talk after I get these set up.”

Patrons were already waiting. I grabbed the pan and glared at him. “This can wait.”

He sighed. “Okay. Folks, wait just a few minutes.” Several patrons gave me plaintive looks, but I ignored them as we headed into the break room.

“The police didn’t find it? Your…source?” I asked.

“They did. I explained to them. The ingredients are special and secret. They respect that.” Ken smiled. “They love a roll with coffee in the morning. Better than donuts, they say. They trust me.”

The cops were compromised. It affected people that ate it. The addiction wasn’t in my head, none of this was just in my head.

“I wish you felt the everyone else does, Emma.

He looked different. I had started look at him with fear, with revulsion, so his appearance had stopped really registering.

He was taller. A few inches at least. And his cheekbones were more pronounced. So was his jawline. I scanned him and realized that, under his clothes, he looked…lumpy. His skin pushed and strained at his joints, taut over bone, but he wasn’t gaunt. It looked like his bones were swelling.

“You’re sick, Ken. Something is wrong with you.” I let my shoulders drop, staring at him and willing him to understand. “Let’s get you to a doctor. This isn’t right. Please trust me, please.”

His expression was blank, lips parted slightly. Had his teeth always been so long? His gums looked ready to pop. “I’m better than I’ve ever been, Emma.”

Should I grab a bread knife? No, it was on the counter. I set my hands in my lap, hoping he couldn’t see them shaking. “You’re on cash register right now, right?”

“That’s right. I’ll handle things.” He gave me another toothy grin before slipping out of the room. Fixing my eyes down, I exhaled shakily. So it was up to me. Nuclear option. After work? No, now. While he was busy. Maybe Carla would help me? Stall him?

I headed out back. Carla sat on the bench outside. “I need your help kid. I’ve figured out-“

She turned her head to look at me and smiled. She seemed dreamy and content and far away. “This sandwich is extra good today.” My eyes fell onto it, and I saw pink crumb on the bottom slice. Peanut butter and jelly mingled with rot.

“Did you…eat that on purpose?” I asked.

“Hm? Oh. I guess someone swapped the bread out.” Carla sighed. “That’s not good. But I guess it’s not a big deal. It tastes really great…you want a bite?”

My blood was on fire. I excused myself to the restroom. Instead, I grabbed my keys and took off for the nearest gas station. But not before grabbing one of the metal thermometers and wedging the point under Ken’s truck tires.

I knew where the source of the substance was. If I could get a head start, I could beat him there.

A five gallon jerrycan of gasoline and a new lighter accompanied me back into the woods. My eyes scoured the rearview mirror for vehicles charging up behind me, for police lights. I didn’t know how much influence Ken had or if he’d noticed I was gone yet. None of the cars in the park lot were familiar, and I dared to hope I’d beaten him.

I sprinted down the trail, letting the smell tell me where I needed to deviate. The odor that called to other people curdled in my nostrils, and no matter the reason, I was the only one who could stop this.

The ground sank under my feet and the wet odor rose strong enough to make me dry heave. I approached the hole, peering over the edge, until I saw the opening.

A gaping crack in a white stone revealed pink, dense, organic material. I covered my nose with my sleeve. It was like a dog’s breath, moist and hot, and I had to breathe through my mouth. Sliding down the side of the hole, I inspected the gap.

The white stone wasn’t stone at all, I realized. The exterior was smooth and pale, but the interior was spongy, riddled with delicate holes. And between these holes the fatty substance formed.

It was a bone. Thick around as a young tree with a hole as big as my head. I shivered uncontrollably - how long had it been here, waiting for someone sick enough to be lured? What would happen when this was destroyed? Could it help Ken and the others?

I unscrewed the cap from the jerrycan and poured gasoline liberally over the opening, soaking the bone and its insides and the soil around it. The odor almost swallowed the iron and I crawled back up the side of the hole before pulling out the lighter.

Foliage rasped and grass rustled. “Emma. Don’t.” Ken was breathing hard, as if he’d sprinted from the lot. I didn’t give him a second to protest again.

The gasoline lit up like a dream. The pink substance began to bubble and pull from the bone, and the smell of fat and gelatin cooking mingled with gasoline burning.

Ken started to scream. I turned back to look at him and screamed in tandem.

He was a foot taller than he’d been, skin stretching along his back and neck. His fingers strained, his spinal column sticking out like knobs. His mouth filled with teeth, too long to close it any longer, breath whistling between them. Tears streamed down his face but his eyes were dull, and he shambled closer on bones growing too big and too fast.

He didn’t do anything to me, just started digging. “It’ll live. I’ll give myself to it. It will live here in me. Like the last,” he mumbled through his teeth.  His hands were like spades, clawing up fistfuls of soil. I stared down at him as he panted for breath, trying to crawl into the dirt.

I didn’t know where the bone had come from. But I thought I knew what would happen if I left him.

So I seized Ken around the middle and started dragging him away. He howled and flailed, kicking as he tried to drag himself back toward the earth. I lost my grip, gasping at his strength. The substance - the marrow - had made him healthy and strong in body but his mind was all but gone.

But if his bones had grown so quickly…

“Sorry about this Ken,” I said, before bringing my foot down on his forearm as hard as I could. The bone inside snapped like a candy cane, crisp and sharp. Ken bawled, pulling his limb close to his chest.

I dashed around to his back as he writhed, kicking at me and trying to get away. Tears welled up as I grabbed his ankle. “I’m sorry, I’m sorry! I can’t let you stay here!” He kicked me in the stomach in reply, the treads of his shoes scraping my skin. I coughed and stumbled.

I had to get a leg. I had to keep him from running. The lower leg would be easier to break. Ken started climbing to his feet, and I ran at him, grabbing him around the waist and driving my knee into his ankle. The rubbery sensation of his Achilles tendon twisted aside and I rammed my full weight into his leg.

This snap was harder, meatier, and Ken stopped fighting. He fell to the grass, sobbing, and I sobbed too as I hooked him under his arms, dragging him away from the clearing. The gasoline was nearly gone but the marrow was cooking away, and Ken wailed, “I have to go! I’m part of it! Let me be part of it!”

I snarled, letting the anger lend me strength as I hauled him toward the trail. “You’re not part of it. Never gonna be.”

That was a week ago.

The people who ate the bread seem to be returning to normal. Their aches and pains have returned. They’re not growing or bulging like Ken. Most of them seem confused about how they were so obsessed. “It was good, but it was just bread.” Carla was fretting after a fresh zit yesterday, so I think they’ll be all right.

Seems like it takes a lot of the stuff for it to start changing. He ate from the source, so it took hold fast. I’ve dug around in the woods near the bone. I haven’t found anything else out there, so either the rest of it is buried too deep for me to reach or something, somehow left a piece of itself there.

I don’t know, and I don’t care. As long as no one else gets caught up in the thrall, let it rot in the earth.

Ken is in my basement. He still sobs to be taken back to the forest, to be in the dirt, to be part of it. I’ve rigged the door to lock from the outside. I set his limbs as best I can and bound him in place with padlocks and towing chains I got from the hardware store. I hope the police are still a little out of it, or else the missing persons’ report Linda filed might lead them to my house. They found his truck in the park lot with three tires. Looked like he drove there missing a wheel, tore the truck all to shreds. But no one saw my car there. No security camera.

I also hope Ken starts reverting if he has more time. Even if that means he has to start the fight with cancer again. God forgive me, I’m sorry. I’m sorry for taking his choice. But he’s not in his right mind. The Ken I know would never put anyone at risk, not even for his own life. I hope that Ken comes back. I miss him.

Please Ken. Turn back to normal. I don’t want to hurt you anymore. But I can’t let it take hold. And I’ve noticed people outside the house sniffing the air, like they smell something enticing.
