My dad lived half a state away from me. I moved out back in 2020 right before the pandemic hit, and it’s been hard to visit him in person ever since. We still kept in touch, since I only moved out to find a job that fit my degree. He ended up selling our old house after I moved, and he got himself a smaller place further away from the town we lived in. I couldn’t blame him. After we lost mom, he got more closed off. He was still a good dad during my closing years in college.

It was earlier this year when he told me he decided to buy some security cameras, said he needed them after he caught someone trying to break in one morning. I was worried, but he assured me that he was safe and alright. He could still take care of himself in spite of his older age. He always said as much and I had every reason to believe him. 

“I had to raise myself when I was just out of middle school,” he used to say, pretending his shitty parents made him some sort of self-sufficient badass. Despite it all, his confidence was rarely misplaced. He took care of me and mom, raised me by himself in the last years before my departure. He was good at it. Everything seemed okay after I moved. Life carried on as normal.

We continued to talk every few days and made plans for meeting for lunch around halfway between each other. Some burger place, local to where we met up. We had just finished telling the waitress our order when he pulled out his phone. 

“Karl,” he said. “You know this kind of stuff better than I do.” He passed his phone to me with an app opened. I looked up at him, waiting for him to explain more. He continued, “They don’t let me actually look at any of the stuff my cameras record without using this thing.”

“Yeah, a lot of stuff runs like that now, dad,” I answered, looking down at the screen. The text size on his screen was large. Normal for a guy his age. I looked back up at him after inputting a little information. “Says it needs a code from the packaging. Did you save the box?” He nodded and gestured to his truck outside. 

“I left it in the truck for you,” he said with a dry smile. “You think I came out here just to eat lunch with you?”

We laughed a bit. I gave him his phone back as the waitress came back with the appetizers. We talked about other small stuff for the next hour. Lunch was good. He ordered extra bacon on his burger; It was a miracle heart disease didn’t take him out before then. When it was all done, we went back out to his truck. I climbed into the passenger seat and grabbed the box. Seems like he got one of the fancier security cameras. After waiting for him to finish climbing into the driver’s seat, I took his phone back and signed him into the app. Just like that, we could see his house in real time. The front yard camera feed showed a dirt road a fair distance from his property. The back yard feed showed the woods behind his house. I looked at it for a minute, wondering if it was one I knew. Seeing that my mind was wandering, dad pointed at the screen. 

“That’s the edge of Moore’s Creek. Just outside of Lexington,” he explained. “It’s nice. Would do you some good to come visit sometime. Maybe go on a hike again.”

I nodded. We used to hike on weekends, leaving after I got home from classes on Friday night and coming home in time for dinner on Sunday night. I didn’t have the heart to tell him I didn’t miss the hikes all that much since I moved out. 

“Yeah, maybe,” I answered noncommittally. I started messing with the camera app, showing him the functions. “On the side here, you can select hours to rewind to and watch the footage. I think it’s supposed to delete after a week if you don’t tap the save button.”

Catching my tone, his eyes narrowed before he shifted his focus back to the phone. He quietly observed as I showed more features. Finally, I got to the motion notifications. I paused to take in the number. Well over two hundred notifications, dating back to only four weeks ago, when he first set up the camera. Opening a few of them, I relaxed when I realized that it was notifying him from the smallest things. Bugs flying into the line of sight at night, small animals coming in and out of the forest edge. The two of us scrolled through the notifications, the times random. I almost closed the tab before I finally noticed a pattern. Motion detected every night at 3:32 and 3:40 AM. 

“Huh,” I said out loud. 

Dad looked at me and back at the phone. “Are you gonna open one?” he asked after a long pause between us. I hesitated. Something felt wrong. I felt like I was about to see something I shouldn’t. In retrospect, I really should have just closed it. 

“Yeah,” I stuttered out, feeling my nerves on edge. I tapped the 3:32 notification for that morning. The footage overtook the whole screen. We watched, seeing a black and white view of his back yard. The footage played for a minute without anything out of the ordinary. Then, a figure stepped out of the treeline. Far enough away that the camera couldn’t pick up any defining details. I was so distracted that I didn’t notice that two other figures were emerging nearby until dad pointed them out.

“What the hell are they doing?” he asked, his finger jabbing the screen where the third person stepped into view. The three people just stood there. Staring at his house. Unmoving. I felt a chill run down my spine. They were just silhouettes. Distinct in shape but vague in every other way that matters. We sat in silence, waiting to see what else would happen. For eight minutes, we watched. By the time 3:40 hit, the time of the second repeat notification, the figures simply turned and retreated back into the trees. Disappeared into the night. We were both dumbfounded. 

I turned to him after closing the video. “Are those your neighbors?” I asked, my voice quiet. He shook his head. 

“No,” he replied. “No, Joe and Edith don’t look like that. He’s taller and she’s a little heavier.” His voice lowered to a whisper, a touch of fear I never heard from him before. “I don’t know these people.”

I looked back down at the phone, my hand trembling a little bit. I scrolled to the day before. 3:32 AM. For a minute, nothing. Then, the three people stepped into view again. Just stared at his house before turning around and disappearing into the trees at 3:40. I kept scrolling further back. Wednesday. Tuesday. Monday. Sunday. Saturday. On the Friday from the week before, the same timestamps for motion detected were recorded, but the footage was deleted. On all seven days we could access, those people showed up. The same time every night. 

“Should we call the cops?” I asked, my throat starting to feel dry. 

“What the fuck… Uh, yeah,” my dad nodded, reaching to take his phone back. I sat next to him, idly looking down at my hands as I heard him dial the police from his area. He explained our situation to the officer on the line. At most, he could only really report as trespassing. That was enough for him, and he eventually hung up. Our last in-person conversation wasn’t what I wished it could’ve been.

“I don’t get it,” he said, rubbing his temple with his thumb and forefinger. “They just… stare…”

“I don’t understand it either, dad. Maybe they’re homeless and checking if they can - I don’t know - squat in your house if it’s empty?” It was a flimsy rationalization, one that didn’t make any sense with how consistently they appeared. He looked at me like he knew that, too. 

“Whatever they’re there for, I don’t like it,” he grumbled. He reopened the video and just watched them standing there. A medium sized person with short hair, a taller figure with long hair, and a short bald one. Distinct enough shapes to recognize if they were someone familiar. He narrowed his eyes more.

“It could be a prank or something, but-” I began to suggest.

“It’s too consistent to be something that small,” he interrupted. “A prank would be knocking on the door and running or throwing eggs on the front porch. Not this.” He looked back over at me and put the phone back in his pocket. His now-free hand landed on my shoulder. “They should stop once the cops talk to them. If that don’t work, I can just show them the old Winchester. That oughta change their minds.”

I let out a small, amused exhale as I leaned back and stretched. “You’d shoot them for standing and staring at your house?”

“If they start doing more than that, sure,” he confidently replied. He let go of my shoulder and rubbed his knee for a moment, thinking. “I really would like you to come over sometime soon, son. We don't have to hike if you’d rather just stay inside and… what is it you college boys do now, drink tea?”

“It’s not that I don’t like hiking anymore, I just don’t have the time to commit a whole weekend to it anymore. I was lucky to fit this in today,” I confessed. “Maybe when I take my vacation in December, we can fly out somewhere.” I shyly met his gaze again, feeling the slightest weight of guilt for turning him down now. “Besides, I don’t wanna go to that place near your house if it’s got freaky hobos stalking you.”

“You don’t have to spare your old man’s feelings, Karl. I get it, you got your own life now. No time for pops anymore,” he said wryly. He patted my back, the impact hard but loving. “Just promise you can still take me out to eat sometimes, alright boy?”

“Of course, dad,” I reassured. With that, we hugged before I climbed out of his truck. I walked back over to my car parked a few spots away from him. I looked back at him and waved as he turned his engine on. As he pulled out of the parking lot, I saw his face in the rearview mirror. I didn’t know that it would be the last time I saw him in person. 

Three days later, he called me. I sat up in bed and blinked the sleep out of my eyes. Seven in the morning. I picked up, yawning before speaking. “Morning, dad. Did the cops come?”

“They did. They saw the videos and waited for those peepers to come back,” he answered, his voice gruff and frustrated.

“Did they catch them?” I asked. 

“No. They didn’t show up. Waited all night, and nothing,” he explained. He sounded angry. An emotion I rarely if ever heard from him. He continued, “that’s not the worst part. They came back again last night. Same exact time. Just saw the video.”

I felt something drop in the pit of my stomach. I swallowed before finding my voice again. “So now what? Should we call the cops again?”

“What are they gonna do? Those people don’t show up if anyone else is here. I’m going to wait for them tonight. I don’t know what kind of messed up game they’re playing, but I’m ending it.”

There was a determination to his voice that was both terribly in-character for him and woefully underprepared. Clutching my phone tighter, my voice shook a bit, my anxiety gripping my chest with more force every passing moment. “Dad, I don't think you should do that.”

“What else is there to do here, Karl? The cops are going to think I’m staging this to waste their time or tell me it’s just a harmless prank and stop coming. I’m just going to politely tell them to stay the hell off my property,” he rebuffed. 

“With your gun?”

“No, of course not,” he answered sarcastically. “Who do you think you're talking to, Karl?”

I heard the smile in his voice. I could see it in my mind. That tired, confident smile. I felt the corners of my lips turn upwards as I continued trying to talk him out of it. “Dad, I have a really bad feeling about this. I’ve been feeling it since the other day.”

“I have a bad feeling, too, son. But I have to do something about it,” he insisted. I heard him open a drawer through the speaker. His voice was further away as he continued his heroic speech. “I can handle this. You know that, Karl.” After he finished, I heard the distinct clicking and shuffling of his Winchester. He was loading it. His voice became loud and clear again. “I’ll keep you updated, alright?”

I nodded. A stupid habit of mine he was well aware of. I heard a soft breath leave him, the smallest of chuckles. “Stop nodding, son. Use your words,” he chided. 

“Yeah, uh, alright. Yeah. Got it. Just… Please be careful, dad,” I squeaked out. I should have pushed harder. I should have done a lot of things. I should have told him I loved him so much. Told him how grateful I was for him being my dad. How grateful mom was for him, even when she was sick. Instead, just a meek “Bye, dad. Talk to me tomorrow, okay?” 

He hung up. That was seven months ago. I stopped hearing from him after that. His neighbors couldn’t find him. Cops, extended family, nothing. People eventually figured he got lost in the woods and probably died. Nobody knew about those people from the woods. The cops didn’t try to find them, figuring that they had little to no connection to his disappearance. It’s not a surprise, dad had told me before that the police in that corner of the town were next to useless. Seven months. It still hurts to think about it. This morning, I finally went to his house. I couldn’t stop thinking about how he invited me there so often. Not just for hikes. He just wanted to spend some more time with his son. I had to blink back tears when my thoughts lingered on it for too long. Aunts and uncles told me that since he’s legally considered dead I should go through his house and see if there’s anything for me there before the arranged estate sale. 

I felt a surge of emotions when I found that he collected some of my old drawings from middle school. Back when I thought I wanted to be an artist. They were terrible. He even said it himself with that big smile on his face. Mom would be mad at him for it. He apologized by hanging it in his study at the old house. I felt warm tears streaming down my face as I smiled sadly at the box in my hands, the sketches of characters and objects still in pristine condition. He kept them that way. I looked around the room to try to distract myself and stop crying. That’s when I noticed the cords from the cameras outside. I remember that the police said they didn’t see anything suspicious the night he vanished. He simply walked into the trees at 3:30 in the morning and never came back out. I decided to install the app out of curiosity. I figured they would have selected that night’s video to be spared from the automatic deletion. I wanted to see him again, even in such a small way. 

After entering all the credentials, I opened up the saved footage tab. Sure enough, that night from over half a year ago was waiting for me. That cold grip of anxiety began to form around my spine as I pressed play. It was just as described. Dad walking out of his house, gun in tow, and entering the treeline. Fast forwarding for the rest of the night’s saved footage, all that remained was a still forest edge and the occasional bug in frame. Closing the video, my curiosity pulled my attention to the motion detection notifications. I didn’t want to look. I didn’t want to see those three things again. I stopped believing they were people long ago. My desire for more closure won out in the end. I tapped the screen and was greeted with hundreds of old notifications. The most recent being only an hour ago, when I had entered the house. I shouldn’t have been surprised to see that the two consistent notifications were still there. 3:32 and 3:40 AM. Yet my blood still ran cold all the same. Scrolling as far back as I could, the alerts were there every night in at least the last three months, even without footage past a week ago. The backlog stopped at that point. 

My fingers trembled and smudged nervous sweat on the screen as I tapped one. Last night. 3:32 in the morning. A minute of stillness. Then, the first figure emerged. Medium height with short hair. The second figure stepped into view a few moments after. Taller and with long hair. The third came out only slightly delayed from the second. Short and bald. My heart sank when a fourth figure stepped out. The four stared at the house for eight minutes. At 3:40 AM, they retreated back into the woods. They disappeared in the same order they appeared. I watched helplessly as my father once again vanished into the trees.
