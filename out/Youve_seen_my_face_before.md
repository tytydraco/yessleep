You've seen my face before. You don't remember it, but you have.

If you were anywhere near a television in this state during the last week of May, you saw it twice an hour. A still frame from a security camera: a man in a gray jacket, looking up into the lens. The banner under him said WANTED. The anchors said his name, which is my name, and showed his face, which is my face. That was my face. But that was not me.

I know how that reads. Every guilty man says it. The one defense I need is the one liars have already used up. So I'm not going to spend this whole account insisting. I'm not writing my name here. Not to hide it,  you could find it in ten minutes if you wanted. I'm not writing it because I'm no longer sure which of us it refers to.

Some things about me, for the record. I'm thirty four. I process insurance claims remotely for a company whose name you'd recognize. If I quit tomorrow, my queue would redistribute across eleven other adjusters by lunch, and the company would barely notice my absence. I rent apartment 1B in a three story brick complex built cheap in the seventies. Behind the building, past the parking lot, there's a treeline, forty acres of undeveloped woods the county has been meaning to do something with for as long as I've lived here. My window faces it. The view knocked eighty dollars a month off my rent. Nobody wants to look at trees, apparently.

And I'll tell you one more thing, I have felt, for most of my adult life, like the kind of person the world keeps spares of. Not despised. Not even disliked. Just interchangeable. The guy whose name the landlord gets right on the second try. The tenant. One more registered voter in a district that hasn't flipped in forty years. If you'd asked me, I'd have said everyone feels that way, and maybe everyone does. But I want to be honest with you: when this started, some small sick part of me was almost flattered.

Somebody was paying attention.

It started with a sitcom. An old one, the kind you put on to fall asleep. I'd seen this particular episode maybe forty times. The lead delivers a line about his mother's meatloaf, deadpan, and the laugh track swells. And above me, through the ceiling, a man laughed.

Not after the laugh track. On the punchline. With it.  
I muted the TV and sat there. At the next joke, timed off nothing but the silent picture, he laughed again. I'll spare you the two days I spent explaining it to myself, same episode, same streaming service, some freak of synchronization. I killed that theory the way you'd expect. I put the show on my phone, earbuds in, screen tilted away from every surface in the apartment, volume so low it was mostly imagination. He laughed at every punchline. His laugh and mine landed so close together they braided into one sound. But his was underneath mine, a hair behind, the way a shadow is behind a hand.

So I started testing. I changed shows. He followed. I paused before punchlines, thumb on the screen, letting the setup hang for ten seconds, twenty. The ceiling waited with me, an attentive silence, and laughed when I let the line land. I found a paperback of collected jokes at the thrift store, brought it home, and read them silently, lips still, in the armchair by the window. The ceiling laughed at the fourth one. The fourth one was the only one I'd thought was funny.

I woke myself at 3 a.m with a timer under my pillow, and his bed creaked above mine within the minute. I stayed motionless in bed until almost noon, to see who'd break first, and the ceiling lay still with me, patient. I dropped a paperback flat on the hardwood at a random moment, a thud above, half a beat late. I coughed; he coughed. I paced, he paced. Once, feeling like an idiot, I jumped straight up in the middle of the kitchen, no warning, no reason, and I heard him land.

By the end of that week I'd stopped thinking of the word spying. Spying means watching. This was closer to rehearsal. I did the things you're supposed to do. I took the smoke detector apart. I unscrewed the outlet covers and the vent grilles and taped wax paper over the ducts. I checked the lamps, the router logs, the undersides of shelves, the little dark seam where the cabinets meet the ceiling. I found dust, a dead wasp, a penny from the nineties.  
I already knew I would. A camera can copy what you do. Nothing that runs on electricity copies what you almost do.

I went up to 2B. The stairwell to the fourth floor smells like the rest of the building, which surprised me somehow. His door is identical to mine, same brown paint, same brass 2 where my 1 is, and scuffed, I noticed with a cold little drop in my stomach, in the same places mine is scuffed. Low on the left, where I catch it with the laundry basket. I knocked. I knocked harder. I put my ear to the wood and heard the nothingness of someone holding their breath six inches away.

The landlord, Mr. Jones, keeps office hours in a converted unit on the first floor, an enormous man with watery eyes who has never once looked glad to see anyone. I got two sentences into my complaint before he sighed and said "We've been through this"  
I told him we hadn't. He turned his monitor around. Three noise complaints, filed from 1B against 2B. The first was dated in March. Each one signed.  
The signature was mine. I had never filed a complaint. Before that afternoon, I had been in that office exactly once, two years ago, to sign the lease.  
"He's never once complained about you" Jones said, as if that settled something.

Sometime in the weeks after that, the delay reversed. I can't give you the date it happened. But by mid April, the man upstairs wasn't following me anymore. He got out of bed one second before I opened my eyes. I understand the objection, if my eyes were closed, how do I know the order? Because the creak of his floor is the thing my eyes opened into. The sound was already in the room when I arrived in it. Morning after morning. He wasn't keeping my schedule. He was cueing it.  
I'd hear him cough, dry, twice, and a few seconds later my own throat would itch, and I would cough, dry, twice, and stand there hating myself for it. One night I heard glass break above me. A specific sound, a tumbler, tile. Three days later my hand fumbled a tumbler at the sink and it hit my kitchen tile, and I stood in the wreckage with my heart slamming, because I recognized the sound. Mine had premiered upstairs. My life was arriving to me secondhand. 

In early May I woke in the middle of the night to footsteps outside. Not above. Outside, on the strip of grass outside of my window, back and forth, unhurried. I lay still and listened to the stride, and here's a thing I learned that night: you know your own walk. You'd think you wouldn't, nobody hears themselves the way others do, but you know its rhythm the way you know your own knock on a door. It was my walk. Out there in the dark, pacing. Through the curtain, backlit by the light over the parking lot, I could make out the shape. My height. My build. The shoulders sloped my particular way, left one low. No face. Just the outline of me, standing on the grass, turned toward the treeline.  
I got up. And the moment I crossed my room, the ceiling crossed it with me, footsteps above tracing my exact path, bed to window, stride for stride, while outside the shape walked off toward the trees. I ripped the curtain back. Grass. Lot. Treeline, black against blacker. Nothing.

Above me, one last footstep completed my journey and stopped where I stopped. I'd grabbed my phone on the way to the window, one of those useless reflexes, and gotten a single photo through the glass. Blur, orange light, a smear of figure halfway to the trees. Useless, like I said. I mention it because of what my phone did with it later.

After that, an envelope appeared in my mailbox from my bank. My full name, middle name included, which nothing and no one uses, printed above the address of apartment 2B. I stood in the lobby holding proof that somewhere, in some system that prints addresses, I lived upstairs.

My phone finished sorting my photos one night, and when I opened the album it keeps of me, the face album, the one it builds itself, the newest photo in it was the blur. The orange smear at the treeline. My phone is very good at faces. In six years it has never been wrong about mine.

Mrs. Martinez from 1A stopped me by the mailboxes, her little dog straining at nothing, and said "You're always whistling that. What is it? It's been stuck in my head all week." And she hummed four notes. I don't whistle. I have never whistled. I told her she must be thinking of someone else, and she laughed like I was being modest.

That night, the ceiling whistled it. Ten days later I caught myself humming it at the kitchen sink and had to put both hands on the counter. It had gotten a song into me. Whatever direction this thing flowed, it flowed both ways.

My email locked me out on a Sunday. Security question: what street did you grow up on? I typed the true answer. Incorrect. I sat there for a long moment, and then, out of some instinct I still don't understand, I typed Delmore, which is the next street over from the one I grew up on. One block north. The houses there were a little nicer.  
It worked. Someone had changed my childhood by one block.

Later that week, I went to the grocery store because I wanted fluorescent lights and scanner beeps and strangers. I wanted to stand somewhere the world still worked, somewhere with prices on things, proof of a system that knew what belonged to whom.  
I saw him in the pasta aisle.

From behind. Just the back of a man twenty feet ahead of me. My haircut, grown out the way mine was grown out. My build. My green plaid shirt, not one like it, the one I was wearing at that exact moment, so that for one unglued second I looked down at my own chest to check. You know the back of your own head from photos, from mirrors angled at other mirrors. You know your own standing shape. It was ahead of me, occupying space, reading a label. He took down the pasta sauce I have bought for six years. Considered it. Put it back.  
I stood there watching myself decline my own taste.

I followed him. He rounded the endcap by the canned tomatoes, three seconds ahead of me, and when I cleared it the aisle was empty in both directions. Long, bright, empty. A woman with a cart came around the far end and gave me a look, and I realized I was standing in the middle of the aisle with my mouth open, breathing like I'd been running.

At the register, the cashier, a kid, nineteen maybe, no reason in the world to lie to me, glanced up and said "Back again?"  
I hadn't been in for a week. I said "Ha, yeah."   
After the store, the public version of me came loose from the private one entirely, and the world made its preference clear.  
On the Tuesday team call, Renata from compliance held me back after to say, a little hurt, that I'd walked right past her at the farmers market on Saturday, that she'd waved, said my name, everything. I was home Saturday. My phone's health app remembers differently: eleven thousand steps. When I found the route trace, it was a straight line, repeated. From our parking lot to the treeline and back. Forty one times.

My boots by the door were wet on a morning it hadn't rained, laced differently than I lace them.  
On the last Tuesday of May, my phone lit up with a county alert, and under it, a news notification with a still photograph, and the photograph was me.  
A man had been beaten to death two nights earlier behind a gas station on the county road, a stranger, a man I have never met, closing up alone. There was a warrant. There was footage. The footage autoplayed before I could decide whether I wanted it to.

I'm not going to describe what the man in the video does. It's findable, and I won't be your path to it. I'll tell you what everyone else sees in it: me. My gray jacket. And I'll tell you what I saw that no one else could have: the wrongness of him. Nothing wasted, nothing hesitant, no flinch when it lands. And the smile. He smiles through the middle of it. It arrives and then it stays, unattended.

At the end, he straightens, and he looks up into the camera. Not the way people check for cameras, a dart, a duck. He looks into the lens the way you look into your phone when you're recording a message for one specific person. He holds it. Three full seconds. Long enough for a clean still. He was making sure there'd be a good one. That look was addressed. He knew, while he was doing it, exactly who would be watching it later, on a phone, in apartment 1B, with the sound off. I sat very still under the spot where his footsteps had taught me my mornings, and above me, for the first time in days, there was no sound at all. It was the silence of someone who has finished.

They came Thursday morning. Two cruisers, no lights. I want to tell you I considered walking out with my hands visible and explaining. I did consider it. Instead, I went out the back stairwell. The front of the building was cruisers and radio chatter, the back was the lot, and past the lot, the only direction left. The treeline I'd been paid eighty dollars a month to look at. I knew, crossing that grass, that I was doing exactly what the trace on my phone had done forty one times. I went in anyway. There was nowhere else that was mine.

A hundred yards in, past where the light from the lot dies, I found the first one. He was kneeling in a clearing no bigger than a bedroom, hunched over a deer that was no longer alive. His hands were in it to the wrists. His head was down, and the sound he was making was chewing. Steady, uninterested, efficient.

He was wearing what I was wearing. Not something similar. The green plaid. The jeans with the bleach mark. The boots that had come back wet on a dry morning. Me, to the thread, crouched in the leaf rot with my arms in an animal. He lifted his head and looked at me, and his face was my face at rest, the exact face I've hated in every candid photo ever taken of me, with a dark smear from lip to ear. No surprise in it. No hunger, even. Just a mild, patient attention, the way you'd look at a delivery arriving on schedule.

Then he came up out of the deer like a man remembering himself. I ran. I have nothing organized to tell you about the next few minutes, a creek I didn't know was there taking me to the knee, branches snapping beneath my boots, my breath very loud and very high. He was behind me. I never heard him hit a branch. I never heard the creek touch him.

And then, between two pines ahead of me, there was another one, and I went down trying to stop.  
This one stood. Calm. Clean shaven the way I am on important days, hands loose at his sides, and he was wearing my funeral suit, the black one I bought for my father's funeral eight years ago and never wore again, the one that is, as far as I knew, hanging in the back of my closet in a dry cleaner's bag. Clean shoes. In the middle of the woods, clean shoes.

The crashing behind me stopped. I didn't see a signal. I only registered, from the ground, that this one had raised his hand slightly, not a wave, the flat, low gesture you make to a dog, and that the woods behind me had gone orderly.  
He looked down at me for a moment. Then he said "Go home."  
In my voice. Not an impression of it, my voice, the one on recordings, two words, mild as a memo.  
"Go home."  
I went sideways, then downhill, then along the treeline at its margin, where I could see the lot through the trunks, and I lay in the brush like an animal while the light went long and orange. The cruisers left a little after seven. No handcuffed silhouette in the back window, they just left, unhurried. I lay there another hour anyway.  
Then I went home. Partly because I could think of no other place on earth where some version of me wasn't already. And partly, because home is where I'd been told to go, and I no longer had the standing to disobey myself.

The apartment looked right. That first breath inside the door, it looked so right that my legs nearly went. Lamp, chair, blanket where I leave it. Then the second breath, and the wrongness came in on it, quiet and total.

It smelled like wet leaves. Faint. Under everything.  
My toothbrush was on the wrong side of the sink. The dishes were done, I had left a pan, I always leave the pan, and the mail on the table had been opened, each envelope slit clean like a fish, contents squared in a tidy stack. The electric bill was paid. I checked, standing right there with my boots still on. Paid three days early. I have never once in my life paid a bill early. Someone had been living my life at a higher standard.

And then the photograph. The one framed thing I own, my mother, my father, my sister and me, the summer at the lake when I was eleven, the photo that has stood on that shelf for two years and traveled in a box for a decade before that. I was past it before something dragged me back. My mother's face is too narrow. Barely. The way a face is narrow in a mirror that's a half degree curved. My father's scar, dock cleat, 1979, he told the story at every cookout, sits over his left eye. It was his right. My sister is smiling with a smile that has never been on her face, she smiled with her mouth closed, always, our whole childhood, it drove our mother crazy.  
And I am smiling too wide.

I have never smiled like that in my life. But I had seen that smile before, and my whole skin knew it before my brain caught up: it's his. It's the smile from the footage. It's on my eleven year old face at the lake, and it fits.

I did the phone thing. I have a scan of that photo backed up in three places, and I stood in my kitchen at midnight pulling up all three, and all three match the frame. Narrow face, wrong eye, closed smile open. So either the photograph was always like this, or everything was edited at once, or I was. And here is the thing I understood standing there, past a certain point, there stops being a difference. Proof is just whatever the copies agree on. All my copies agree, and they don't agree with me.

I thought about calling my sister. Six years since we've spoken, my fault, mostly, in ways that don't matter now. I sat with the phone in my hand and understood that whatever she said, whichever smile she claimed, I would have to decide whether the voice saying it was hers. That's where this leaves you. I put the phone down.

The arrest was the top story by morning. TAKEN INTO CUSTODY, the banner said, and there was a mugshot, and the mugshot was me, height lines, my face hanging there with the smile finally switched off. Apprehended without incident at a location the reporter called "near the suspect's residence."  
For one full breath, I felt saved. I mean that physically, a breath that went all the way down, the first since March. They got it. It was in a room now, a room with cameras and ink pads. Then the breath came back up, and it brought the rest with it.  
There is now an official me. Booked, printed, photographed. He has my name, my full name, the middle nobody uses. My birthday. My address. My face, from every angle, in the state's own files, and somewhere in a county database there are ten fingerprints filed under that name. The system had exactly one slot with my name on it, and the slot is filled, and the world does not care which of us is real. It never did. It cares which of us answers to the name, pays the rent, unlocks the phone, sits still for the photograph. It has one of those in custody.  
My innocence exists. It just doesn't belong to me anymore.

And even so, lying on the couch that night with the TV light going, the arithmetic wouldn't rest. The upstairs one. The window one. The store. The deer. The suit. Custody holds one.

The ceiling stayed quiet for nine days. And I know because I spent them in a silence I can only describe as amputated. I had wanted it gone for three months. Then it was gone, and I sat under the quiet where it used to be, and never in my life felt less accompanied.

Last night, it started again. And then I did something I'm not going to defend. It crossed the room, and I got up, and I crossed with it. It stopped; I stopped. It shifted its weight; I found my weight already shifting. We stood there, the two of us, stacked, and through the ceiling I could feel the attention the way you feel sun through a curtain, and for the first time since March, the attention felt less like theft and more like the answer to it.

I told myself I was studying it. Learning its patterns the way it learned mine, mapping, preparing, gathering…but this is the third night now, matching it is the only time I feel like a person. When I lift my foot the moment after its foot lifts, when my breath lies down on top of its breath, I am for those few seconds, definite. Somebody's copy is at least somebody's.

I used to be afraid I was replaceable. That was vanity, it turns out. Replaceable at least implies a you to replace. But I'll leave you with this, if you live somewhere with thin floors, an old building, cheap ceilings, and some night the apartment below yours starts keeping your rhythm, it's probably nothing.   
But it might be me.  
I have to be someone.
