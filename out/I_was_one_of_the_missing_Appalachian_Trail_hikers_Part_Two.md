The rest of the hike down Mt. Katahdin that day was relatively uneventful. The tree line abruptly began as we drew closer to the bottom, and the sun continued its journey across the sky. I could feel my skin burning from the rays that were only slightly shrouded by the trees. My mind still lingered on the mysterious circle from the peak, but I did my best to push them out of my mind as I made conversation with Ilanna. 

The climb down was much less strenuous than the climb up, for which I was thanked heavily by my straining back carrying the weight of my overpacked bag. It was, however, more awkward. I had to plant my feet with each step, ensuring I didn’t throw myself down the path. There was one close call towards the bottom when my foot caught a loose rock, launching me forward. Thankfully yet unfortunately, Danny was there to catch me. He followed his heroics up with a joke about how I was better off carrying cinder blocks on my back than whatever was in my pack. 

For most of the trip down though, I caught up more with Ilanna. She told me about her parents' conversion to Christianity after Nico passed and how helpful my mother had been, about how she goes home every weekend for dinner just like we used to do, and about her life in Boston. She told me all of her favorite food and coffee spots in the area, and how one reminded her of the subpar pizza place from college. She explained more about her job to me, or at least, she sort-of did. I still don’t quite know exactly what she does at her soil-testing company, but I could tell that she enjoyed the people she worked with. She told me about all of her other friends from work who weren’t on this trip, and of the many unsuccessful dates she had gone on. She cited snobiness and “lack-of-general-kindness” as the two main qualities in her bad streak of guys.

“Not even Danny, huh?” I had said quietly to her, half-joking and half-prodding for information. Her mouth opened in shock and she gave me a mock slap on the shoulder.

“What is wrong with you?” she whispered demonstratively, “Never in a million years!”

“Okay, okay,” I replied, smiling, “I’m sorry for even floating the idea,”

She rolled her eyes and shook her head, but she beamed at me with a smile that had come to be her signature trait. It cut through my regret for a brief moment. It filled me with warmth. Even though it was nice to see it again and it felt good to be with the closest person I had to a friend, I still could feel the cloud of my guilt hanging over me, like an invisible wall that prevented me from truly enjoying this time I had with her. 

By the time we had reached the bottom, the sun was setting, and I thought Ava was going to blow a gasket. She stomped ahead of the entire group, walking as fast as she could. I wondered how she was even able to move that fast, given how beat we all were. We followed behind, like a group of fresh recruits at boot camp being led by an angry little drill sergeant. There were a few moments where she got so far ahead that we lost her. We only eventually caught up because of the trail markers.

Our trek to the campground was silent, partially from our exhaustion from the climb but also out of fear of angering Ava any more. Once we reached the campground, the golden glow of the sunset had disappeared in its entirety. It left a dark blue haze as the last traces of light were pulled under the veil of the black mountain ranges. Ava was already waiting at the booth to check our passes, which were on Ilanna’s phone. 

As we trudged up to her, I quickly whispered to Ilanna while the two of us were still behind the rest of the group, “I feel like I should apologize. I’m the reason we’re here so late.”

Ilanna shook her head at me. “She’ll get over it,” she said, “She just has a weird thing with time. And her and Kayode haven’t been on the greatest terms.”

I raised an eyebrow. “Really?” I asked. I knew that Ava and Ilanna had become pretty good friends, so I knew she had all the details. She nodded.

“I’ll tell you later,” she said, pulling out her phone. We had just gotten within ear-shot of the rest of the group, and Ilanna walked over to the window, swiping on it to pull up the passes.

Our campsite for the night was in a quiet little corner of the campground on a pond. There was no sound except for the chirping of crickets and the soft thuds of our boots on the beach. Any residual light in the sky had been completely eliminated, leaving us as Ava predicted: setting up our tents at night. She was the furthest from me, so I thankfully was not subject to her angered face in the dark. Shockingly, I was the first to finish setting up out of the whole group, which meant I was volunteered to make a run to the campground store for some firewood and s’mores stuff. It was just about the last thing I wanted to do. I was so tired that I could barely speak and the flurry of the day’s emotions still had my head spinning. As I was about to make my way, flashlight in hand, Danny walked up to me.

“Do me a favor,” he said, slapping a twenty dollar bill in my hand, “get as many cans of Coke as this can buy.” 

“Danny,” Ilanna spoke up, still struggling with her tent, “How do you suppose he’s gonna carry all that?”

“I’ll go with him,” Brent said. I was surprised to hear him speak up as he walked towards me. I only just realized then that he kind of talked like he was from California. Like he was about to hit the sickest surfboard trick you’ve ever seen or try to sell you weed. I nodded my thanks and we walked off into the dark.

It was pitch black outside. The tall dark pines that lined our dirt path to the store cast shimmering waves of moonlight that peeked through the needles. The occasional haunting cry of a loon or rouge cricket broke up the silence. The air was moist and the humidity stuck to my skin, especially after a long day of hiking. I almost wanted to jump into the lake next to our site. Maybe in the morning. 

The campground was also surprisingly barren. We passed numerous vacant lots that I expected to be full of people by this time. It reminded me of the old abandoned boy scout camps that I used to explore with Ilanna. No tents, no RVs, no people passing by us, and no campfires, even though I could smell the smoke in the distance. Our flashlights did little to dispel the darkness cast by the emptiness of the park. The only other source of light came from the blood orange moon that had crept up into the sky. It looked much closer to the earth than normal.

“You wanna know something crazy?” Brent asked. He must have noticed me admiring it. “That’s an optical illusion. Your brain is tricking you by comparing it to other objects on the horizon. It’s always the same size.”

I didn’t much care one way or the other, but he seemed eager to nerd out, so I obliged. “Huh,” I said, “Never knew that.”

He grinned. “If you don’t believe me, hold your thumb up to it at arm’s length. You’ll see it’s not nearly as big as you think.”

I did what he said, holding my arm up as we walked. I guess it looked a little smaller, but if my brain was actually playing tricks on me, this certainly didn’t stop the ruse. I nodded at Brent anyway, feigning surprise. The fatigue fogged my brain, but I supposed I should try to make conversation with him. 

“Are you into that kind of stuff?” I asked him. “Space and all that?”

He shook his head. “Nah, man. I just love nature. I’m always most at peace when I’m out in the wild. Just feels right, like we’re meant to be here, you know what I mean?”

I shrugged, knowing I held the exact opposite sentiment.

“Humans were never meant to live in houses, dude,” he said, “It’s in our instincts, in our own DNA to be just… in the woods, hunting our food. Surviving, man. And it’s our own technological advancements that are taking that away, stripping us of what it really means to exist, what we were born to do.” He turned to me, his voice almost stern, “We’re our own worst enemy.”

I chuckled at how philosophical he had gotten so suddenly, but I think he might’ve been serious. I quickly recovered.

“Well,” I said, “I guess the way I see it is that we’ve made these advancements because of our instincts. We survive because of our brains and our ability to problem-solve.”

“Maybe,” Brent said, scratching his chin with his free hand, “but doesn’t life outside of nature just feel wrong? Like you’re not meant to be anywhere else but here?”

“I guess,” I replied. This guy was something else.

“That’s just how it feels to me, anyway,” he concluded, and our hike fell silent again for a few minutes, except for the crickets.

“So,” he asked as we were further up the road, “you’ve been friends with Ilanna since you guys were kids, huh?” 

“Since before we were born, I’ve been told,” I chuckled, “But yeah, for a long time. We went to college together.”

“Right on,” he replied, “I’ve only seen her a few times before the trip, but every time, your name would come up. She’s always talking about you.”

I laughed awkwardly. Hearing how much Ilanna thought about me only deepened my guilt. A small part of me hoped that she’d pushed me to the back of her mind, that I was nothing more to her than a fond childhood memory, but that was increasingly seeming to be untrue. 

“Nice to finally put a face to the name,” he said, holding his hand out for a fist bump. I returned it.

After about ten more minutes of walking and a quick conversation with Brent about how he couldn’t wait to smoke his first joint on the A.T., (called it,) we reached the campstore. It was a small, lonesome little building that had been beaten down by the Maine weather. The pine siding showed its age, but a newer, more modern looking patio area was attached to the back, lined with picnic tables. The orange light from inside sent a welcoming glow into the night with its promises of AC and sustenance. I reached the door, opening it for Brent and following him in.

The bell jingled as the door slammed behind me, and I took a look around. An assortment of metal and wooden shelves were lined with candy, clothes, baking items and canned goods, as well as first aid supplies. Brent had already made his way to the coolers in the back of the store in search of Danny’s Coke. The store was not very well stocked. Every shelf had at least one empty spot on it, and there were countless labels for items that were nowhere to be found. The skeleton staff didn’t seem interested in remedying this, as I watched the singular old man behind the counter lean back in his chair, reading a book and sipping from a thermos. He put his items down and cleared his throat.

“Finding everything okay?” he grumbled as he adjusted his trousers and stroked his white beard. It wasn’t every day you found someone who still retained the dying Maine accent, so it was a pleasant surprise to see one still kicking around. 

“I’m all set, thanks,” I said as I grabbed marshmallows off a shelf. No graham crackers or chocolate were in sight, however. They’d have to deal with it. I brought a few bags and plopped them on the counter, and the man strained as he reached for his scanner. 

“Can I also grab a few bundles of firewood, too?” I asked.

“Sure thing, they’re right outside,” the man nodded, scanning the first bag, “Y’all A.T. hikers?”

“Yep,” I replied, “First day.”

“Ah. SOBO hike then.” he mused, “You two from around here?”

“We’re here with a group,” I answered, “I’m from Ashland, but him and the rest are all from the Boston area.”

“Oh, flatlanders, huh? Well, be safe out there in the hundred miles. Long way from the city.”

“We’re planning on it,” I said. The man nodded, as he punched in the price for the firewood into the register. “Hey, um… are there usually so few people here this time of year?”

The man turned to me and shook his head. “Nope, very slow year for us, barely seen any hikers like yourself neither, ‘specially southbound.”

“Any idea why?” Brent piped up, now standing behind me in line holding two cases of Coke.

“Might have something to do with all those hikers gettin’ sick this past year,” he answered. 

I shot a confused look back at Brent, who mirrored mine. “Sick?” he asked.

“Yep,” the man said matter-of-factly, “Bunch of people after finishing the trail fell ill. Dehydrated, losin’ weight, throwin’ up. Somethin’ in the water people figured.”

“Jesus,” Brent said.

“Did any of them die?” I asked.

“Not sure,” the man said.

I was more than ready to get out of the store already, but then the man leaned in.

“You wanna hear what I think, though? The real reason nobody been out here recently?” he asked. Neither of us responded, but he continued, “I think there’s somethin’ out there.”

Brent and I turned to each other again. 

“‘Something’?” Brent asked, “As in…”

“I don’t know,” the man responded, leaning back in his chair and folding his arms, “I don’t know. But sometimes here at night, when it gets real quiet, you can hear things. Howlin’, but… all demented-like.”

“So, like a coyote?” I asked, feeling uneasy.

“Nah, not like that. Different. Ain’t like no animal I’ve ever heard before. It don’t happen too often neither, but if it catches the wind just right, you can barely hear it on a quiet night. I’m sure y’all will hear it too.” He said it so matter-of-factly, not like an elder weaving an old folklore legend, but with a certitude and a certain uneasy familiarity. “Or maybe it is a coyote. I don’t know, I don’t know,” he repeated, “35.98.”

It took me a moment to realize he was talking about my price. I was snapped out of my apprehensive trance. “Oh, yeah. Sorry,” I said, pulling my wallet from my pocket.

“Take it easy, now,” the man said after he rang up Brent and the two of us headed for the door. Brent waved, but I didn’t even look back. Once we were outside, I turned to him.

“You believe him?” I asked, swallowing the pit in my stomach, “About the water and the howling?”

 “People getting sick?” he replied, “I believe that.”

“Yeah,” I said quietly, “but what about the noises?”

He shrugged.

“Guy’s out here, working the night shifts at a lonely little shop,” he said, “he hears about people getting sick… I don’t know, maybe the mind tends to wander.”

We didn’t say much after that.

I felt like throwing up. I wasn’t sure how much I bought into the shopkeeper’s vague tale, but the nebulous notion that something was potentially out in the hundred mile wilderness was not helping my growing apprehension. The unsettling tree-less circle from the mountain peak was also coming to mind again. Combined with my uneasy feelings about Ilanna, it was pushing my regret to its breaking point. The more I followed Brent into the darkness back to our site, the more I seriously considered turning around the next morning and hiking back up Katahdin. I’d call my mother, and even though her opinion of me would reach an all time low, she’d come get me, even if she says she won’t. Ilanna would be disappointed too, sure, but I was honestly doing her a favor. I knew facing me again was as uncomfortable for her as it was for me, even if she didn’t show it. 

By the time we had walked up to the site, and I heard Ilanna’s familiar laugh and Danny’s raucous voice, I had made my decision. The idea of going home to my apartment and leaving all of my guilt behind was becoming too appealing to deny it. But I’d wait. I’d call my mom tonight, rip the bandaid and tell Ilanna tomorrow, right before I set off up the mountain.

Upon reaching the campsite, I saw that everyone had finished setting up, and Ilanna was already getting a fire going with Kayode. Ava watched, sitting on a log next to the pit. I saw Ilanna’s eyes light up when she saw me, and my shame carved up my back like a hot knife. I set down the firewood, and she grinned at me.

“How’d it go? You find everything?” she asked.

I palmed the marshmallows in my hand. “No s’mores stuff. Just marshmallows. We got Danny’s Coke, though.”

Danny emerged from the darkness as Ilanna struck a match, sticking into a patch of dry leaves under a teepee of sticks in the middle of the pit.

“Hell yeah,” he said as Brent procured the drinks from his bag, “Gonna be a good night tonight.”

Ilanna noticed my confused countenance. “He wants to make Jack and Coke for everybody,” she said, rolling her eyes.

“Uh, uh, uh. My famous Jack and Coke,” he bragged as he snatched the cases from Brent’s hands, “and there’s more than enough here for you all, don’t worry.”

“I still don’t get what makes it so famous,” Ava piped up, “It’s two ingredients.”

“That’s right. But it’s all about the ratio!” Danny replied as he walked to his tent. As Ilanna rolled her eyes, I rubbed my sore, weary ones.

“You guys are gonna drink?” I asked, incredulously, “Aren’t you all tired from the hike?”

“Of course I’m tired,” Ilanna said with a grin, “But you gotta live a little, Eli.”

Brent walked over and took a seat opposite Ava, sighing. “I need a drink after that,” he said, “You all should’ve heard this guy at the store. Scared the shit out of Elijah over here.”

Kayode looked up from the fire for the first time, and Ilanna raised an eyebrow. “What do you mean?” she asked.

Brent explained the man’s story to Ilanna, Ava and a very interested Kayode. 

“Yeah, I heard about that too, the hikers getting sick. I should’ve mentioned it sooner,” Ilanna said as she reached in her bag beside her. She pulled out a tiny bottle that looked like eyedrops. “Water purifier. If we end up having to drink from the ponds or streams, use this. There’s enough for everyone.” She put it back in her bag. “Apparently some of it in this area hasn’t tested well recently.”

I shot a look at her. “You don’t think that’s a little alarming?”

She shrugged. “There should be water spigots along the route anyway. This is just an extra precaution”

I looked around. “None of you are worried?” I asked, flabbergasted.

“Can’t think about that stuff, dude,” Brent said, “There’s a million things out there that we could worry about, but you just gotta roll with it. Take it as it comes.”

Ilanna nodded in agreement, and that was that. Kayode mindlessly stoked the fire, but I could tell he was turning this information over in his head. He kept silent, though. I was shocked at their recklessness, especially Ilanna’s.

As I watched the fire grow from a whimper to a roar, my nightmares replayed in my head. The crackling of the dry firewood splinters reminded me of the way my bones shifted and shattered, displaced by my melting muscles and skin. I instinctively looked down at myself to make sure I was still intact. I fell nauseous again, and my heart pounded as I realized I’d be experiencing them outside of my apartment for the first time. I was not looking forward to it. 

I excused myself to the outhouse, deciding that now was as good a time as ever to make that call to my mother. Once I was out of earshot, I pulled my phone out, dialing her number and praying I had service. As I held it up to my ear, I sighed in relief as I heard a ring. I saw Ilanna glance at me along with Danny, who was pouring the Coke cans into little tin cups. They both gave me a questioning stare, but I turned away, waiting for her to pick up.

She didn’t. I squinted in frustration as her familiar voicemail played in my ear.

“Hello, this is May Simmons, and I can't come to the phone right now. Please leave your full name, your phone number, the date and time of your call, and I will return it at a later juncture. Good day. And remember, Jesus loves you.”

After the dial tone, I began my message. “Hi mother, it’s Elijah. It’s uh… I don’t know the time, I’m sorry, but it’s the same day you dropped me off at the trail. Listen, I um…” I paused. Was it really worth it? Was it worth it to disappoint her beyond belief and feel the shame of her scorn? I stood there, in the dark, as I shifted back and forth on my sore calves, letting the seconds tick by on the voicemail. I felt the dust and pebbles in my shoe scrape up against my toes, and the humidity left droplets of dew on my brow. 

As I watched Ilanna by the fire with all of her friends, I saw how much she fit in. I saw her laugh and smile and hug these people that I couldn’t be any more different from. I knew that I could never be a part of her life in the way that they were, in the way she needed me too. I would never fit in, and I didn’t deserve to. 

“Look, we can talk about it on the ride home, but I want out of here. I’m honestly miserable. And I know that you’ll… that you’ll probably be disappointed. Maybe even a little mad. But I just can’t handle this. I’m not ready… I’m not ready. Tomorrow morning, I’m gonna hike back up the mountain, so if you could just meet me at the lot by late afternoon, that would be great. I’m sorry. Bye.”

I hung up, feeling that rising pit in my stomach as I trudged back to the firepit. I hoped with every fiber of my being that she’d be waiting for me in that parking lot tomorrow. I couldn’t decide if her concern for my safety outweighed her desire to have me complete this hike. But I had made my decision. If she wasn’t there, it’d be Ubers and hitchhiking all the way home.

As I sat down next to Ilanna on her log, she remained silent. I took a few discreet deep breaths to calm my nerves as Danny held a tin cup out to me filled with his esteemed cheap whiskey and lukewarm Coke combination. I held my hand up.

“None for me, thanks,” I chuckled as I looked at Ilanna, who stared straight ahead, drink in hand. “It’s a little early in the trip for me to start hitting the bottle.”

“Aww, come on, man,” Danny scoffed. “It’s a social lube! Would you rather be playing ‘Two Truths and a Lie’ or ‘Would You Rather’ or some shit like that? No better icebreaker!" 

I forced a laugh. “I appreciate it, but I wouldn’t wanna deprive anyone else.”

“Nah, nah, nah, nonsense. It’s like I said, there’s more than plenty to go around.”

“You look like a homeless guy, Danny,” Brent said, mocking his holding of the tin cup, “waving your little can around for spare change.”

Danny flipped Brent the bird and quickly turned back to me. “Come on,” he repeated, “You gonna make me stand here like an idiot for much longer?”

I thought it over. I had only had a few drinks in my lifetime, which mostly consisted of a glass of wine and the occasional IPA. However, I suppose if I was going to be leaving these people the next day, then sharing a drink with them tonight wouldn’t hurt. I nodded and grabbed the cup from his hand. 

“That’s damn right!” Danny said, clapping his hands together and sitting down on his seat. Brent, sitting next to him, had pulled out a blunt from his pocket and was already taking a lighter to it. He took a huge puff, then passed it to Danny. It went all the way around the circle, until it reached Ilanna as Kayode held it out to her.

“I’m all set,” she said, waving the smoke out of her face. Danny snickered. Kayode nodded in my direction, holding the smoking stub out.

“Oh, I’m good, thank you,” I said, “Don’t really smoke.”

Brent reached across me and Ilanna to grab it. He took a puff as he sat back down.

“You two definitely grew up together,” said Danny with a grin.

Ilanna rolled her eyes.

“Yeah, you’re probably the only two people left on the planet who don’t use weed in some way,” Brent added, “It’s basically got nothing but benefits, health-wise.”

“Oh really?” Ilanna snorted.

Brent nodded. “I got a million studies I could show you if we had service.”

“Wait,” Kayode said to me, sipping his drink, “I was not aware that you and Ilanna grew up together.”

“I’ve told you that a million times,” said Ava.

Kayode ignored her as I nodded at him.

“Yep. Our parents knew each other before we were even born,” Ilanna said.

“Her mom and dad helped out my mother a lot,” I added, “She fell on some pretty hard times before me and they were always there for her.”

Ilanna smiled at me, a little flash of light in my pitch-black pit of regret. I pitifully hoped my praise of her parents would at least somewhat make up for everything.

“We were also born only two days apart,” she said.

“Same star signs and everything,” Ava added. I had no idea what she was talking about. Danny and Brent held back their laughter.

“Ah,” Kayode said, “So you have been close for quite some time now, then?”

I watched Ilanna shift in her seat slightly, and I hoped the heat from the fire excused my bright-red face.

“We drifted a little bit towards once we graduated,” Ilanna said after a brief pause, “But he’s here now, and I couldn’t be happier.”

I briefly locked eyes with her as she beamed at me again. I attempted to return it.

“There was something else too though, right?” Ava asked, “Didn’t his mom help your parents or something?”

Ilanna looked at Ava, saying nothing.

“Well she converted them to Christianity, for what it’s worth,” I said, “My mother’s pretty devout.”

“No…” Ava responded, “There was something else, right? I’m drawing a blank.”

Ilanna’s expression got even blanker. She remained frozen in that state for a few seconds before forcing a laugh.

“Nope. Must be thinking of someone else,” she said quickly.

“Hmm,” was all Ava said.

In all honesty, the suspiciousness of that interaction went right over my head at the time.

The night went on fast. We ate some of our provisions for dinner, and toasted our marshmallows. The drinks kept flowing. Danny kept everyone’s cup filled, including mine, which I sipped haphazardly, trying to drown my guilt with his disappointing concoction. 

At some point, Ilanna turned to me. “So… who were you calling?” she asked with the slightest air of suspicion. I could smell the whiskey in her breath as she leaned in close to me.

“Just my mom,” I replied, my own head buzzing from the liquor. “Telling her how my first day went.”

“Oh really?” she asked, as she leaned in even closer to me, “You’re not… leaving us?” She snickered in my face. 

“What? No way,” I replied. The alcohol had made me a worse liar than I already was. “What makes you- why do you ask?”

She turned to the rest of the group. “Guys,” she slurred, a huge grin on her face, “Elijah’s leaving us!”

The rest of the group groaned and tittered at the same time in mock disappointment. Danny, the most demonstrative of the bunch, threw his hands up.

“C’mon, Elijah,” he roared, “First my dad leaves me, now you? I don’t know how much more of this I can take!”

The rest of the group burst out into fits of laughter. 

“No, no,” said Ilanna, “His dad left him too. He gets it!”

I don’t know if it was the drinks, my guilt, or a combination of both, but I actually laughed at that one. It didn’t seem like Ilanna’s kind of joke, though.

“Wait, wait, wait,” she said, leaning her head on my shoulder. She was practically spitting out her words now. “You guys should start a club. The ‘My Dad Left Me’ club!” More drunken laughter ensued, but I was slightly less amused now. I had never seen her like this.

“This guy’s mom left him, does that count?” asked Ava, gesturing to Kayode. 

Kayode shot her a subtle, yet icy glare. He held his drink to his mouth, taking a sip.

“That is not quite what happened.”

Ava raised an eyebrow at him. She seemed pretty plastered herself.

“I was just making a joke!” she said.

Kayode just shook his head, downing the last of his beverage. He held the cup out in Danny’s direction.

“Another, please,” he said sternly.

Ava laughed in shock.

“Well fuck me!” she said incredulously, “I didn’t mean to offend you or anything. My bad.”

Kayode just ignored her, staring at the flames in front of him as Danny refilled his cup. Ava waited for a response and upon not getting one, turned her head to do the same. 

The group fell silent for a minute after that. Ilanna finally picked her head up off my shoulder. She gazed up at the clear night sky, the blood red moon filling her blue pupils with its orange hue. She sighed, touching my arm.

“Nico would’ve loved it up here, don’t you think?” she asked me.

I think I physically flinched at hearing his name again. I looked at Ilanna, analyzing her face as my heart pounded out my chest. She was smiling, but her eyes darted from star to star, like she was hoping to find her brother among them. 

“Yeah…” I said quietly, “Yeah, definitely.”

“Nico…” Kayode asked solemnly, “That was your brother, correct?” 

“Yep,” she said, finally looking down, “He was a good kid. Couldn’t get enough of the outdoors.”

I nodded. The rest of the group was silent. I didn’t realize until after, but I was physically clenching my teeth, as I anxiously hung on every word out of her mouth. Ilanna drunkenly swung her neck around to look at me.

“Are you sure you’re not leaving us?” she asked, “Kinda seems like you are, honestly”

She seemed more ernest now, and I tried to match it.

“Ilanna,” I said, “I’m not.”

“You swear?”

“Yeah. Yes,” I said, rather unconvincingly. 

“Because… it wouldn’t be the first time,” she said, staring me dead in the eyes, her words blending together in her inebriated speech.

“What do you mean?” I asked nervously. I knew exactly what she meant.

“After the funeral,” she answered, “I mean you just kinda… *fwsh*, skipped town right after.”

I stared her dead in the eyes. I had no words, no explanation or apology to offer this woman who pleaded to me for answers. 

“Didn’t say goodbye or anything,” she continued.

I swallowed. My dry mouth was sore from the smoke and I was sweating profusely from the heat, but it was nothing compared to the way I was being grilled by her.

I began to say, “Ilanna I-”

“And I really don’t think that was cool. Like you shouldn’t have done that,” she said. The entirety of the group was silent, staring down at their feet like mourners at a funeral. I was more than at a loss for words. I scrambled for a response that would have any modicum of believability. 

“I had already found an apartment,” I croaked, wincing as the lameness of my excuse settled in.

“Yeah but you could’ve stayed,” she retorted, “You could’ve stayed. And I needed you, man. I needed someone. I was in a hole.” 

I rubbed the back of my neck with a clammy hand, spitting out a variety of anxious “ums” and “uhs.”

“I mean, Mom and Dad were useless,” she continued, the words shooting from her mouth like venom, “Couldn’t handle their grief for a second to check in on their own daughter. I was really just so alone. So alone.”

Every word out of her mouth ripped through me like a bullet, piercing my lungs and sucking the air out of me. I stared straight ahead, frozen in time. I saw tears forming in her eyes.

“And that just really sucked.” She whispered, her silky sweet voice reduced to a blubbering, spittle-ridden mess.

I was beside myself. I subconsciously bounced my leg up and down, my eyes glued to the flames that resembled the ones plaguing my dreams. I felt the searing gaze of everyone sitting around me, and Ilanna’s warm body next to me was like a hot iron, burning me for my sins.

“But whatever, it’s stupid,” she said, getting up from the log with a start. She placed her tin cup where she sat, and it wobbled on the rounded edge. She wiped her eyes and turned to the group. “I’m going to bed.”

She stumbled off into the darkness behind the fire. I heard her tent unzip and the shuffling of her sleeping bag as she climbed in.

The rest of the group retired pretty soon after that. Brent wordlessly spread out the ashes of the fire around the pit, while Danny collected everyone's cups and gulped down the unfinished ones. I sat with my head in my palms, trying to force myself to get up. But I couldn’t. I was paralyzed by Ilanna’s words. They confirmed my worst fear about the feelings she harbored deep down, and they confirmed that I needed to get away from her as soon as possible. I don’t know if I’d be able to keep the full truth from her any longer.

“Boy, I am going to regret this tomorrow,” said Danny, polishing off the last of his drink, “We packed Advil, right Brent?”

“Motrin, I think,” Brent replied.

“Goddamn it,” Danny said, walking off to his tent, “I hate Motrin!”

“It’s the same thing,” Brent said under his breath, “It’s the same damn thing.” He followed behind Danny.

Ava got up next, and briskly walked by me without saying a word. Kayode slowly stumbled behind her, but not before giving me a brief pat on the shoulder.

“I recommend being well-rested for tomorrow, Elijah,” he said as he passed me.

I weighed his advice in my head, eventually deciding to heed it. I pushed myself up off of my log, tripping a bit as my inner ear was miles off. My feet shuffled past everyone's tents before I reached my own. 

I climbed in, fighting off the gurgling nausea from the alcohol. As I slid into my sleeping bag, I winced as I rubbed the sweat, dirt and grime from the day’s hike into it. I rested my head on the pillow and shut my eyes. 

As I dozed off, I heard two voices from outside in hushed, angered whispers. I couldn’t make out what they were saying, but I could tell it was Kayode and Ava’s. Their tent must have been the one closest to mine.

I covered my head with my pillow, drowning out any noise from outside and partially slowing my spinning head. I felt my face press against the cool, vinyl tent floor, and my consciousness slowly slipped. My intoxicated mind finally gave way to sleep, and I watched the flames creep into my peripheral as I drifted.
