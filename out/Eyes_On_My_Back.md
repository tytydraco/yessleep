I liked the countryside. Ever since I was a kid, I remember hearing whip-birds calling in the forest, and the sound would make me feel happy. But I also recall the feeling of something lurking in the back of my mind. When you grow up in a suburban, city environment, you grow used to the urban noise - when it’s taken away, your mind starts to fill in the gaps - although out here, brown snakes and cane toads are the only real dangers you face.

Lately though, I’m inclined to wonder.

I could never really put my finger down on what it was about those places. There are scattered dreams from over the years - seeing something moving in the darkness behind the fence, or a man watching from a distance along the winding hilly road. It was always something far away, just close enough to be perceived.

When the lockdown arrived, our family home in the city was on lease, and so we needed a place to stay. My aunt Alice and uncle Jack, ever the welcoming type, let us stay at their countryside house with them for a while. Their house was quite big, so they had no trouble fitting all five of us. I look back on it all quite fondly now. Their house was always animated with sounds of their two children playing and back then, when they were younger, we used to play Mario Kart together on their TV, go for bushwalks and explore the surrounding area. Alice and Jack also had two dachshunds -  Honey and Shelby - yapping little things that have a barking conniption the very moment a car emerges down the  driveway to their property. Shelby was the youngest and most energised of the two, always jumping up and down against the window, while Honey would watch intently before scampering off to meet us at the front door. 

Come a few years later though, It had been quite a while since we had gone to stay at their place. Their kids had grown older and the 2022 floods had left them in no great position to accommodate us - Not to mention, their house is a 10 hour drive north of Sydney, and I can't exactly blame my parents for not wanting to endure such a trip with 3 children in the backseat. So when I drove up there myself for a holiday, it felt like a homecoming. Driving down the narrow road leading to their gated entrance, I swore that at points I could see something on the horizon that had changed. What looked to be a radio tower of sorts on the distant slopes of the surrounding valley. I can’t remember there being a tower in that place before - I would have noticed it, given the way it stood out amongst the forested countryside hills. 

Something about the radio tower made me feel upset. Like that idyllic landscape had been tainted by this looming, meaningless structure - but a sudden bump in the road had quickly gotten rid of those thoughts. Potholes. A lot of them. My car was a Honda, so it could take them, but she’s an old girl, pushing 22 now. I needed to take it easy if I didn’t want to break down in the middle of the Australian countryside. Pulling into the driveway, I looked out for Honey and Shelby jumping up and down at the front window. I kept my ear out for their incessant barking - But I couldn’t hear a thing. They must have been asleep. I parked my car and greeted Alice and Jack with open arms. It was nice to see them again, after all this time. But then again - there were no Honey and Shelby - until I  was greeted by another dog - Maggie. Alice explained to me that Honey and Shelby had died about a year prior. I was devastated - I had never known. 

“These things happen when you’re out here.” Alice explained. Alice and Jack were both country nurses and had developed a certain attitude of calmness towards death, given that it was part of their job. They had been buried in their backyard - which was less a backyard and more of a fenced-off section of a forest clearing - where they led me to the post of a planter box - tied around it was Honey’s collar. Shelby’s wasn’t there though. That stuck with me a bit. If both had died, then where was Shelby’s collar? Not wanting to be rude, I kept it to myself, though the thought of it lingered in my mind for a while. 

They both seemed to have a slight disquiet about them, in spite of their usual happy demeanour. They had been through rough times as of late. Besides the floods and the passing of their dogs, Jack had been facing employment troubles with his hours, and their son had contracted meningitis about a year prior. And yet through it all they maintained a strong patience about them. I always admired that about them both, and I was certainly not alone. In this part of the countryside, the communities are surprisingly closely-knit, often gathering around a local church and regular get-togethers. If anything, the floods had only proven the community’s resolve. It was quite a touching tale to hear from Alice about how all the community pooled their resources together to help with the aid effort. 

“Okay driving here? Champion?” Jack patted me on the shoulder after giving me a tight hug. 

“Ehhh, mostly. The road leading here is full of potholes, though.” 

“Ahh. Yeah, we’re still waiting on that to get fixed. It’s taken the local council a while to get out here.”

Alice made way for the front door and motioned for me and Jack to come inside. “You two, it’s going to be dark soon!... Would you like some tea, sweetie?” 

Alice made a really nice tea that she would drizzle with maple syrup. I remember having it frequently when I last came to stay, so the thought of it made me finally relax my muscles. 10 hours on the road can really wear you down, believe you me. Despite the lovely hospitality from both Alice and Jack, I still couldn’t shake the small feeling that there was something off about things - not just my aunt and uncle - but in the environment around us - and it was familiar too. 

that night was restless and fitful - as is the case when you’re trying to sleep on a new mattress. I must have been tossing and turning for hours - because by the time I finally decided to get up and get something to drink from the kitchen, the stars were fully out in the sky. I slept up in a granny flat, separate from the rest of the house, so to get to the kitchen I needed to walk out onto a balcony and down a short flight of stairs outside. The balcony is quite elevated, letting you see over the roof of the house and onto the wider property. As I stepped out onto the balcony, the motion lights turned on - and for just a split second - I saw something small metal catch the light in the corner of my eye. Normally I wouldn’t have paid this any mind, but in the near pitch black night, I could have sworn that when I turned to face it, a patch of darkness, just slightly darker than the surrounding environment seemed to move, behind the fence around the backyard. 

When I went down this morning to see what I had seen, I did not find any footprints or anything. I was almost relieved. Once again, the mind was playing tricks on me. I could never really have gotten used to the countryside darkness. As I turned around though, I noticed something that had changed. Turning my gaze back, it took me a while to notice what was off with the picture I was looking at. But when I noticed it, it hit me like a punch in the gut - and my back started to burn with the feeling of distant eyes watching me. Tied around the garden post, just as tight as Honey’s, was another collar.

“Shelby”
