I work nights at a gas station off Highway 231 in LA (Lower Alabama).

I'm not naming the store because I still work there, at least technically. I'm supposed to go in tomorrow night. My manager has already texted me twice asking if I can cover Ms. Teague's shift too, because apparently "personal emergency" means "scheduling opportunity" when you're in retail.

And before anybody asks why I don't just quit: rent is due on the tenth, and being scared does not pause the light bill.

I'm writing this because I don't know what else to do.

The first time I saw the total, I thought it was funny.

That was my mistake.

It was around 2:40 in the morning. The dead part of the shift. Not peaceful dead. Gas station dead. Coolers humming, floor sticky no matter how many times you mop it, hot dogs turning under those little orange bulbs like evidence from a crime scene nobody wants to process.

A trucker came in wearing flip-flops and a CAT hat. He bought black coffee, a honey bun, and prepaid diesel on pump four.

His total came to $6.66.

He looked at the screen and laughed.

"Well, hell," he said. "Guess I'm buying breakfast for the devil."

I laughed too, because what else do you do at 2:40 a.m. when a stranger makes the exact joke everybody would make?

The receipt printer clicked three times before it printed.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

Then it spit out the longest receipt I've ever seen. Not coupon long. Not CVS long. Longer. It curled over the counter, hit the floor, and kept feeding until I had to grab it with both hands to keep it from rolling under the cigarette rack.

The first part looked normal.

BLACK COFFEE HONEY BUN DIESEL PREPAY PUMP 4 TOTAL: $6.66

Then there was a blank space.

Then one line printed at the bottom.

CUSTOMER ACKNOWLEDGED.

The trucker stopped smiling when I showed him. He didn't ask for his coffee. Didn't take the honey bun. Didn't fuel his truck. He just walked out.

I watched him climb into his rig and pull back onto 231 with his headlights off.

That bothered me more than the receipt. People forget coffee. People get creeped out by numbers. Nobody drives a semi onto a dark highway with no headlights unless something in them has gone sideways.

Here's the other thing, the thing I didn't chew on until later. Coffee and a honey bun don't leave enough diesel on a $6.66 ticket to be worth prepaying. And I never rang up pump four. I checked the journal tape twice. The register added it on its own.

I threw the receipt in the trash.

Pump four stayed authorized.

At 3:16 a.m., it turned on by itself.

I heard the handle click from inside the store. That sound is normal when somebody picks it up. It is not normal when the island is empty and the security monitor shows nothing but bugs throwing themselves at the canopy lights.

The pump ran to $6.66.

Then stopped.

I went outside because I am apparently the type of man who investigates haunted gasoline for $14.75 an hour.

There was no truck. No person. No hose lying on the ground. Just gas spilled on the concrete in three little streaks. They looked like claw marks.

I mopped it up, bagged the trash, and told myself the pump glitched. We had old equipment. Register two froze if you scanned beef jerky too fast. The ice machine made a noise like a dying goat every time it dumped a batch. A pump glitch barely made the list.

Then I restocked the roller grill, because the hot dogs have a par level, and the par level does not care what I saw.

Morning shift came in at six.

That was Ms. Teague.

She's worked there forever. Sixty-something. Smokes Misty cigarettes behind the dumpster and knows everybody's mama, warrants, preferred scratch-off, and whether their debit card is about to decline before they swipe it.

She took one look at me and said, "You seen it."

I hadn't told her anything.

I said, "Seen what?"

She pointed at the trash can behind the counter.

The receipt I threw away was sitting on top. Not crumpled. Folded. Neat as church clothes.

I said, "That's not funny."

She said, "No. It ain't."

Then she opened the register drawer, took out a penny, and slid it across the counter to me.

It was black. Not dirty. Black. Like it had been burned and polished.

"Don't take change from it," she said.

"From what?"

She stared at me for a long second.

Then the receipt printer clicked.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

It printed one blank strip of paper.

At the bottom:

CLERK ACKNOWLEDGED.

Ms. Teague closed her eyes like somebody had just told her bad news she'd expected for thirty years.

"You laughed, didn't you?" she said.

I didn't answer.

She didn't need me to.

For the next week, I saw 666 everywhere.

I know how that sounds. I know about pattern recognition. I know once your brain notices something, it starts dragging it out of the world like a kid finding pennies in a couch.

But this wasn't that.

This was my phone showing 6:66 for one second before flipping to 7:07. This was the freezer inventory count printing 666 units when we had maybe two hundred items total.

This was a woman buying gas on pump six, six lottery tickets, and six cans of cat food, then looking me dead in the face and asking, "Did that make it easier for you to see me?"

I asked her what she meant.

She smiled too wide and too long, and her gums were the gray of old receipt paper.

Then she walked out without paying.

The register still approved the transaction.

CUSTOMER ACKNOWLEDGED.

That Thursday, somebody on the county Facebook page posted pictures of a semi sitting abandoned on the shoulder of 231 south of town. Doors open, no driver, cab packed ankle-deep with receipt paper.

The comments all said meth.

I recognized the CAT hat on the dash.

I started taking pictures.

That was another mistake.

At first, it felt like proof. I'm a practical person. I like evidence. You can tell me you feel haunted all day, and I'll still want a timestamp, a camera angle, and the name of who else saw it.

So I made a folder on my phone.

I called it "666 bullshit."

That was probably disrespectful.

By day three, the folder had sixty-six pictures.

I did not take all of them.

Some were screenshots of my lock screen at impossible times. Some were pictures of receipts from angles I couldn't have reached unless I was standing behind the register while also being in my bed.

One was a photo of me asleep on my couch.

Flash on.

Mouth open.

The black penny sitting on my tongue.

I woke up choking.

The penny was on the coffee table.

Dry.

Warm.

I called Ms. Teague after that.

She answered like she'd been waiting.

"You took pictures," she said.

I didn't even get hello.

"How do I stop this?"

"Stop feeding it."

"What is it?"

"You don't ask that."

"Ms. Teague, I woke up with a penny in my mouth."

"You took it from the drawer?"

"You handed it to me."

"I showed it to you," she said. "You picked it up."

I remembered her sliding it across the counter.

She swears her hand never left it.

I remembered telling myself not to touch it. Then I remembered it in my hand.

That scared me worse than the photo.

Memory is supposed to be yours. It can be wrong, sure. It can be fuzzy. It can be edited by pride, sleep, shame, or whatever liquor your uncle brought to Thanksgiving. But it should still feel like your property.

This didn't.

This felt like somebody else had signed my name on a form and filed it before I got to read the requirement.

Ms. Teague told me to delete the pictures.

I did.

The folder emptied.

Then my phone buzzed.

Sixty-six files restored.

I deleted it again.

Restored.

I factory reset the phone.

When it came back on, the wallpaper had changed to a picture of pump four.

The photo was taken from inside my apartment. Through my bedroom window.

I live twelve miles from the store.

I drove to Ms. Teague's house that afternoon.

I know showing up at an older woman's house uninvited sounds bad. I get it. But when your phone starts taking pictures of gas pumps through impossible windows, etiquette gets shoved into the glovebox.

She lived in a little brick house outside town, with plastic geese on the porch dressed like it was still Easter. The geese bothered me. I don't know why. Maybe because everything else felt dead serious and there they were in tiny bonnets, committed to the bit.

Ms. Teague met me on the porch before I knocked.

"You shouldn't have come here."

"I need help."

"You brought it with you."

Behind her, something inside the house knocked. Three times. Not at the door. Inside the wall.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

Ms. Teague looked tired enough to fall through the porch boards.

She reached into her apron pocket and handed me an old receipt.

It was dated June 6, 1996.

Same store. Same pump. Same total.

$6.66.

At the bottom:

CASHIER ACKNOWLEDGED.

Her name was printed under it.

M. TEAGUE.

"I was young," she said. "Dumb enough to think evil would announce itself bigger."

"What does that mean?"

"It means people expect horns. Latin. Floating beds. Something useful enough to believe in." She nodded toward my pocket, where my phone felt heavy as a brick. "Most times it starts smaller. A number. A smell. A thought that don't feel like yours until it says something smart."

"Then what does it want?"

"To get paid."

The knocking came again.

Three taps.

Ms. Teague looked toward the wall, like she could see exactly where the sound was standing.

"It can scare you all night long," she said. "It can turn the house mean. It can spoil your sleep, borrow your mouth, make everybody around you think you're crazy. But it can't take, not the part that matters. It has to be paid."

"What am I supposed to pay it?"

"You're thinking money because you're still lucky."

My phone buzzed.

UNKNOWN NUMBER: SHE CANNOT VOID YOU.

I dropped it.

Ms. Teague saw the screen.

Her mouth tightened.

"It's using your words yet?"

I said no.

Then I thought:

Don't lie to her.

That thought was not mine. It was calm. Helpful. Almost friendly.

I grabbed the porch rail so hard my fingers hurt.

Ms. Teague said, "There it is."

I asked her what happened after that.

She told me it doesn't happen all at once.

"It invoices you in pieces," she said. "First signs. Then missing time. Then words. Then hands."

"What comes after hands?"

She looked over my shoulder toward the road.

"The person becomes the receipt."

I wanted to ask what that meant.

I didn't.

Some questions feel like handles.

That night, I tried to stay awake.

I turned on every light in the apartment. Living room, kitchen, bathroom, bedroom, even the closet light, which felt stupid right up until I thought about turning it back off.

I put the black penny in a glass of salt water because the internet told me salt helps. The internet also told me rubbing toothpaste on headlights works, so I was not exactly dealing with peer-reviewed demon control.

At 3:16 a.m., every light went out.

Not the power. Just the lights. The TV stayed on. My phone stayed charging. The microwave clock stayed lit.

6:66.

I sat on the couch and stared at it.

Something tapped inside the microwave. Three times.

I did not move.

My phone buzzed.

UNKNOWN NUMBER: YOU ARE DOING WELL.

Then:

COUNT THEM.

I turned the phone face down.

It buzzed again.

COUNT THEM.

Then the microwave tapped. Then the window. Then the bathroom mirror. Then under the couch.

I put my hands over my ears like a child.

That's when I heard myself whisper, "Six."

I bit my tongue so hard I tasted blood.

My mouth whispered, "Six."

I clamped both hands over it.

My throat moved anyway.

"Six."

Everything stopped.

The lights came back on.

The glass of salt water on the coffee table was empty. The penny was gone. On the inside of the glass, something had scratched one word:

ACKNOWLEDGED.

Then my phone buzzed one more time.

BALANCE DUE: $0.01

I sat there shaking, and one cold part of my brain did the math anyway. It had pulled words out of my throat like they were on a string, and the balance hadn't moved. Whatever it took from my mouth didn't count, because it took it. Ms. Teague's rule held. It can't take. It has to be given.

And the glass still said ACKNOWLEDGED.

Acknowledged. Not paid. It keeps separate books.

That should have made me feel better.

It didn't. It meant the thing had rules, and it was following them.

I called 911.

I told them somebody was in my apartment. That was the only version I could say without getting transferred to the kind of help that does not bring weapons.

Two officers came out. They checked every room. One of them was young and polite. The other looked like he'd rather be wrestling a methhead behind Dollar General than listening to me explain scratches in a drinking glass.

They found no one. No forced entry. No footprints. No fingerprints on the glass except mine.

The older cop asked if I had been sleeping. I said not much. He asked if I'd taken anything. I said no.

He looked at the empty energy drink cans on my counter, the vape by the sink, the store uniform hanging over a chair, and me standing there barefoot at four in the morning with blood on my tongue.

He did not say what he thought. He was professional enough to let silence do it.

When they left, the young officer came back to the door.

He held out a penny.

"You dropped this by the couch," he said.

I told him it wasn't mine.

He glanced at it.

Then his expression changed.

"What the hell?"

The penny was black.

The year stamped on it was 0666.

I told him not to take it.

He laughed. Nervous, not mean.

Then he flipped it toward me.

I didn't catch it.

It hit the floor between us and spun for too long. When it finally stopped, it landed standing on edge.

The officer stopped laughing.

His radio crackled.

A voice came through.

Not dispatch.

Mine.

"Customer acknowledged."

He backed away from my door.

I don't blame him.

I would have backed away from me too.

Yesterday morning, Ms. Teague didn't show up for her shift.

My manager called me because I'm apparently the emergency contact for everybody else's emergency now. I drove to the store even though I wasn't scheduled.

Pump four was bagged. Out of Order. Somebody had written on the bag in black marker:

NOT OUT.

Just those two words.

Inside, register two was printing receipts nonstop. Blank paper all over the floor. Hundreds of strips curled around the counter, the hot food case, the cigarette rack, the lottery machine. It looked like the store had tried to toilet-paper itself and given up halfway through.

Every receipt said the same thing.

BALANCE DUE: $0.01 CLERK ACKNOWLEDGED.

I went into the back office to shut off the printer.

The security monitor was on. It showed live camera feeds. Front counter. Beer cooler. Pump island. Dumpster. Office.

I saw myself on the office camera.

Standing behind me.

I turned around so fast I knocked over a stack of vendor invoices.

No one there.

On the monitor, camera-me leaned close to my ear.

I felt breath. Warm. Real.

My own voice whispered, "One cent is consent."

I ran, past the chips and the motor oil and the jerky display, and I want to tell you I did something smart on the way, hit the emergency stop for the pumps, grabbed a fire extinguisher, anything, but all I did was run, and I made it exactly as far as the sales floor before the front doors clicked and held shut.

The receipt printer stopped. All the lights flickered once.

Then pump four turned on.

The bag over the nozzle moved like something underneath it was breathing.

Gas began spilling onto the concrete outside. It didn't spray. It poured, slow and even, like somebody tipping a can they didn't want to waste. It ran in a thin line across the pump island, under the locked doors, and into the store.

Gasoline should not move uphill.

This did.

It ran straight to register two and circled my shoes.

Then the register display lit up.

ADD $0.01 TO COMPLETE TRANSACTION? YES / NO

I hit NO.

The screen blinked.

YES / NO

I hit NO again.

The gasoline around my shoes warmed. Not caught fire. Warmed. Like bathwater.

The screen changed.

CARD DECLINED.

Then:

TRY ANOTHER FORM OF PAYMENT.

The drawer popped open.

Inside was one black penny.

I'd left it at home on my kitchen counter. I knew that the way you know your own name.

It was in the drawer anyway.

I did not touch it.

I picked up the whole cash drawer and threw it through the front door. The glass cracked but didn't break. The penny stayed in the drawer, sitting upright in its little coin slot like it had manners.

Behind me, something laughed.

It wasn't loud, and it wasn't demonic. Demonic would have helped.

It was my laugh. The one I used the first night. The one I gave away for free.

The doors opened.

I ran to my truck, drove home, and haven't been back.

It let me go. That's the part I keep chewing on. Then I remember what Ms. Teague said on her porch. It couldn't take me. It could only run up the pressure until I paid. Locked doors, warm gasoline, my own laugh; that wasn't an attack.

That was a salesman closing.

My manager has called six times. His last voicemail said the floor was clean, the glass wasn't cracked, and the cameras show me walking out calm as any Tuesday, so would I kindly explain why the cash drawer was sitting on the sidewalk.

The floor was clean.

Sure it was.

The store number has called sixty-six times.

Somebody called from a restricted number too. Nobody spoke. I listened to a receipt printer clicking somewhere on the other end until the line went dead.

Ms. Teague's daughter called last. She said her mother was in the hospital, dehydrated and confused, asking for me. She said the nurses keep stripping the bed because they find paper in it. Blank strips. Thermal paper, slick and curled, the kind that comes off a register roll. Housekeeping has changed the sheets three times today, and nobody will say where it's coming from.

She said Mom keeps telling the nurses she tried to void it. The nurses think that's the dehydration talking.

Then she asked if I had "the penny."

I said no.

She said, "She says not to lie where it can hear you."

I looked at my kitchen counter.

The black penny was sitting beside my keys.

I hung up.

That was two hours ago.

Since then, I've been typing this. I can still smell gasoline in my shoelaces. My tongue still hurts where I bit it. My mama texted me a scripture Sunday, like she does. I didn't answer it. I've gotten real careful about what I answer.

My manager just texted again about tomorrow's schedule, like the world is still a place where schedules matter.

I don't think posting this will help me. I need to be honest about that. This is not a clever plan. This is not me tricking a demon with Reddit, which is a sentence so stupid it almost makes me miss being a normal idiot with normal problems.

But Ms. Teague told me something else on her porch.

She said attention feeds it, but agreement feeds it better.

There's a difference.

Seeing the number is not agreement. Laughing at it might be. Counting it might be. Taking pictures definitely is. Picking up the penny is.

Saying yes is.

And I know what every one of you reading this counts as.

You're attention. Thousands of little taps on the glass.

I can't help that now.

So I'm asking you plain: don't be agreement. Reading is attention. Answering it is agreement. If it says something to you, and I think it might, do not answer it.

The trucker lasted four days. Ms. Teague has lasted thirty years.

I think the difference is he answered it.

I haven't said yes.

Not yet.

That's why I'm posting here instead of checking myself into a hospital or walking into a church or driving until my truck runs out of gas. I need people who understand strange things to tell me what to do before I make the next mistake.

Because the total is still short.

One cent.

That is what it wants from me. Not money, not really.

Permission.

I can feel it now. That's the part I hate admitting. I can feel it arranging thoughts in my head before I think them. Practical thoughts. Helpful thoughts. Thoughts in my own voice.

Go back to the store. Finish the transaction. Void the fear. Balance the drawer. Make it clean.

I keep looking at the penny.

It is closer than it was when I started typing. I know that sounds impossible. I also know I haven't moved it. It was beside my keys. Now it is beside my laptop.

The microwave just changed to 6:66.

It's 11:42 p.m.

Something knocked under the table. Three times.

I am not going to count them. I am not going to pick up the penny. I am not going to say yes.

But I need to go to the bathroom, and the hallway light just went out.

My phone buzzed while I was writing that sentence.

UNKNOWN NUMBER: TAKE THE PENNY WITH YOU.

Then another message came through.

This one was from Ms. Teague's daughter.

All it said was:

Mom woke up. She says don't read the comments.
