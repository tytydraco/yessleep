I don’t have long now.

Maeve’s waters broke around midnight, and we spent thirty sleepless hours in a birthing suite. Intravenous oxytocin was needed when the contractions slowed down. I didn’t complain about any of this, given my wife was the one enduring the gruelling labour, but my point is: we weren’t in our right minds.

That’s why I didn’t believe what I saw at first.

The senior midwife, Lucy, expressed no concerns as our baby boy’s head started to crown. I had been focusing on Maeve’s face throughout the labour, talking to her and comforting her, but I chanced a glance down below when I became aware of the midwife heaving out something substantial.

I expected to see a newborn boy entering the world, but instead found Lucy heaving something inordinately large out of my wife’s birth canal. And I quickly became aware that the texture was all wrong.

It was something wooden. A dark walnut box-like shape, with intricate detailing of trefoils and arches. It was a hand-carved antique. The midwife was pulling a hand-carved antique out of my wife, in lieu of the baby boy I expected. At the bottom of this unexpected delivery swung a pendulum, and above was a clock face, its black hands caked in blood and vernix; and at the very top of this clock was a little door, from which I expected a cuckoo bird would emerge at the striking of each hour.

“What the hell are you doing?” I screamed at the midwife. “What is that?”

The several women in the room scowled my way, but Maeve stole their attention back as she screamed through her final push. Of course, I wasn’t looking at her now. I was looking at the wooden clock, far too large to have worked its way through my wife’s cervix, and certainly too large to have sat within her uterus for the past nine months.

“A beautiful baby boy, born at six minutes past six in the morning,” said Lucy.

The midwife brandished this large cuckoo clock with strange ease, as if she were holding something far lighter. And none of the midwives nor Maeve, who took our son from Lucy with a gleeful sob, batted an eyelid at the absurdity of treating a walnut clock as if it were a human child.

Maeve sobbed and giggled at me. “We did it, Reece. Baby Evan is here.”

This wasn’t a joke. My wife wouldn’t joke about such a thing, and I’d witnessed something that had no practical physical explanation. I decided I had to either be dreaming or so sleep-deprived that I may as well have been dreaming.

“I… think I need some sleep…” I said.

Maeve laughed. “*You* need some sleep? I suppose you do, after all that hard work.”

“Francesca’s going to stitch you up, and I’ll help you feed Evan,” said Lucy.

I hardly felt present as I watched Lucy help Maeve breastfeed our ‘baby boy’. My wife brought the door of the cuckoo clock to her teat, and the walnut patterns seemed to swirl as the thing fed on my wife. The wood groaned, and my wife and the midwife giggled, and I sank deeper into my chair, hoping it might swallow me in a swirling vortex of its own.

I spent the next hour stealing moments of sleep on that bedside chair, only to open my eyes each time with a pang of terror as I saw Maeve still cradling not a baby boy named Evan, but a bulky cuckoo clock. What had happened to our son? I’d seen him on the ultrasound scans. There had been a boy in there. Where had he gone?

I bit my lip to stifle the cries or screams that my body was desperate to let out. Sleep might’ve helped, but it was rendered impossible by the clock’s persistent ticking, as its pendulum tongue swung to and fro. I watched Maeve caress the clock’s inflexible ridges with a tenderness as Lucy helped clean the waxy gunk off its wooden flesh, and then I forced my eyes shut again for another few minutes. I went to wash my face in the bathroom a couple of times, hoping I might clean some of the gunk out of my own eyes and finally see a healthy baby in Maeve’s arms.

I woke at one point to Lucy having a heated discussion with another member of staff. “Dr Weston should be here. It’s been forty minutes. Maeve needs to at least speak to the doctor on the ward. Where is he?”

The cuckoo clock made a noise not at the striking of the hour, but at six minutes past seven, precisely an hour after its ‘birth’, if we must call it that. There was a clunk as its little door opened to reveal a black void that stung my eyes; so I turned to face my wife instead, and my skin turned cold as she began whistling two notes repeatedly, producing something of a cuckoo sound.

“What are you doing?” I asked.

Maeve stopped whistling. “Talking to the baby.”

In a sleep-deprived and terrified haze, I lost all sense of reason and decided to confront the situation head-on. “You were whistling like a cuckoo clock.”

Maeve laughed. “What? No, I wasn’t. Anyway, do you want to hold him now? I’ve been hogging him for long enough.”

I’d known the moment would come. I’d known I couldn’t avoid the ‘baby’ forever. But after Maeve’s eerie little cuckoo tune, I was less inclined to hold him than I would have been five minutes prior. As my wife held out that clunky abomination with ease, much as the midwife had, the cuckoo clock’s face seemed to stare into mine, its Roman numerals not quite fixed in place. They seemed to move.

They saw me.

I shook my head rapidly from side to side. “I need to find Dr Weston.”

Maeve frowned, finally sensing that something was up with me. “No, you don’t. Lucy’s sorting it.”

“It should be me,” I said, shooting to wobbling legs. I skirted around the baby clock my wife was offering me in outstretched arms. “After the IV drip and the, erm, the dropping heart rate of the baby during labour, Dr Weston needs to see us, to make sure we’re okay… I mean, to make sure *you’re* okay.”

Lucy and the other midwives didn’t look impressed as I excused myself from the room, but it was the best lie I could conjure. I would’ve sounded insane if I’d told them what was actually wrong.

I went up and down the ward asking after Dr Weston, and it was a healthcare assistant who pointed me in the right direction in the end. She’d seen him skulking around in a ward at the other side of the hospital level, where he wasn’t supposed to be.

And when I went to the ward in question, I recognised Dr Weston immediately. I’d seen him when we first came to the birthing suite, and I’d seen him just after ‘Evan’ was born. I was sure of it. The doctor had been out in the corridor, staring through the slats of the window blinds, separating our room from the rest of the ward.

Why hadn’t he come into the room to check on us? That question played on my mind as the doctor clocked me and started to stride away.

“Hey,” I called after him. “Dr Weston, where are you going?”

“I have patients,” he said as I followed.

“Yeah, my wife is one of them.”

“Please go back to your room, sir. I’ll be with you in a—”

I grabbed the man by the shoulder and twisted him to face me. “Why are you running?” I asked. “What’s going on?”

Dr Weston’s eyes were fraught with the same glassiness I’d seen in my own, when washing my face earlier. He wasn’t right. He looked as if he’d seen a ghost.

He looked as if he’d seen *something*.

“You were at the window when Evan was born,” I said. “You saw… him.”

Dr Weston tried to worm out of my grip. “I’m going to call security if you don’t go back to your room.”

“What did you see? *Tell me what you saw!*” I finally unhanded him and put my face in my hands. “I think I’m having a… psychotic break or something, doctor. I need to… I don’t know. Maybe I need to be sectioned. Maybe I need—”

“Then we both do.”

I opened my eyes, and the man’s lips were trembling. He was staring over my shoulder, as if frightened that my wife might come walking down the corridor, from the other end of the hospital, with Baby Evan in her arms.

“You saw the clock too?” I asked the doctor in a cracked voice.

“Clock? I don’t know what you mean. I saw a newborn baby with skin that was… wrong. It looked wooden.”

I clammed up, horrified that this doctor had witnessed the same thing. I needed to be crazy. I needed Dr Weston to section me. I needed everything with Evan to be okay.

It was real. I hadn’t wanted it to be real.

“The baby looked at me,” Dr Weston continued, “and I heard a ticking in my head. An incessant ticking that just wouldn’t stop, so I ran. I left you and your wife there, and I’ve been ignoring the calls from the nurses and the healthcare assistants, and…”

It was the crazed doctor’s turn to seize my shoulders, and I felt a pang of dread as he did. “Please,” Dr Weston said. “*Please* don’t make me go back to that room and that… thing.”

The man let me go, and I lowered my head, and turned to trudge back across the hospital level. Alone.

I returned to the birth room where Maeve was still cuddling Evan the Cuckoo Clock. My wife didn’t ask me to hold the ‘baby’, and the midwives didn’t meet my gaze. I understood that. From their perspectives, I was completely in the wrong. But now I had confirmation that I wasn’t insane. Dr Weston had seen something too. Something slightly different from me, but still, whatever ‘it’ might be, he had seen it.

After a few minutes of silence, a doctor finally came to check up on us. Not Dr Weston. I don’t recall her name. Everything had faded into noise at that point. All I remember is she reacted to the baby the same way as the midwives and Maeve. She said that both Mother and Baby were healthy, and that we were free to go home later that day, but we were welcome to have a little rest in the room first. And then the doctor and the midwives left our family alone.

“Rest,” said Maeve. “Maybe that’s what you need.”

“I’m so sorry,” I said.

My wife turned in bed to face away from me in the bedside chair, taking her baby cuckoo clock with her.

And then I summoned all of my strength to save the situation. “I’m ready to hold him now.”

My wife turned around with a tear-stricken face and begrudgingly passed the bulky cuckoo clock to me. I was terrified, but decided that this terrible spell or curse might be broken as soon as I held our child. Perhaps some skin-to-skin would create a bond, and I’d see the beautiful baby boy Maeve saw.

But the curse held.

I was cradling a sizeable cuckoo clock, seemingly too lofty and broad and weighty to be held comfortably, yet it was as light as a feather and fit snugly in my arms. More impossibilities. I looked down into Evan’s clock face, and saw my own face reflected back in its polished enamel surface. The texture of the walnut finish in my hands felt wrong, not that I knew what would have made it feel ‘right’; I wasn’t accustomed to cradling walnut clocks in my arms as if they were babies.

And then the wooden ridges of the hand-carved detailing began to tickle at my hands and arms, as if the baby were wriggling around in my hold. I shuddered at this but steeled myself, determined to maintain my composure for my wife’s sake; *any second now, I’ll see a normal baby boy in my arms*, I told myself. *And then Maeve will forgive you for your little hiccup earlier. We’ll all go home and put these two days of sleep deprivation behind us*.

But I knew that wasn’t so. I hadn’t imagined that conversation with Dr Weston.

*He saw it too.*

The tickling sensation grew painful, as if each grain of wood were working into my skin, and perhaps puppeteering my body, gluing me to the chair and constricting my vocal cords. I stared down at the cuckoo clock with horror as the wood malformed to create a slit, and then it clamped down on my palm, taking a piece of flesh.

I screamed and reflexively yanked my wounded, blood-stained hand away, dropping the cuckoo clock to the floor; and my wife screamed in turn, pulling her weary body out of bed to pick the clock off the floor. As I got to my feet and pressed myself up against the wall, putting as much distance between myself and the clock as possible, Maeve got back into bed and cuddled it close to her chest. “You dropped him!”

“It... He *bit* me, Maeve! Did you not see?”

“Bit you? You were rubbing your palm against his little nails with an awful smile on your face.”

“What? That isn’t… That isn’t what happened.” I showed her the flap of bloody skin on my hand. “Does that look like a little scratch to you?”

Maeve winced. “How did you do that to yourself? You’re not seriously suggesting a newborn baby could do that, right? Something’s wrong with you, Reece. Maybe it’s the sleep deprivation, but I’ve not slept either and I’m not behaving like this.” She shook her head and clutched Evan tightly. “You’re not holding our baby again until you’ve spoken to… someone.”

I pointed my blood-stained hand at the clock in my wife’s arms. “That’s not our baby, Maeve. You need to put it down and—”

*“HE’S NOT AN ‘IT’! HE’S OUR SON!”*

I ran my hands through my hair, quaking at the very sight of that thing in my wife’s arms. “I need a moment. Just… give me a moment.”

My wife sobbed as I sealed myself in the bathroom and stared into the mirror. My eyes looked different. Not tired so much as changed. It looked as if the pupils had formed into trefoils, and I rubbed at them repeatedly, but the pattern wouldn’t go away. I stumbled back from my reflection, shaking my head. This wasn’t happening. None of this was happening.

None of this *is* happening.

My wife had stopped crying.

All I could hear was Evan’s hands ticking away out there.

I gingerly eased open the bathroom door and crept around the corner to find my wife gone. All that remained in bed was that infernal cuckoo clock.

Impossible. Impossible. Impossible.

I hadn’t heard Maeve get up and leave the room. I hadn’t heard the door to the room. Nobody had entered or exited. The crying had stopped abruptly, leaving only the ticking of the pendulum which was somehow moving with ease even as the cuckoo clock lay on its side, crumpled up in the duvet.

I went over to Evan and watched as his minute hand struck six past the hour. The little wooden door clunked open again, but this time revealed more than a void; out jutted a titchy platform on which there spun a miniature wax figurine of a woman. I recognised her blonde plaited hair and flowing hospital gown.

Maeve.

Her open mouth was moulded into a fixed, eternal scream.

And I would have screamed too, had I not been whistling that cuckoo [tune](https://www.reddit.com/r/dominiceagle).
