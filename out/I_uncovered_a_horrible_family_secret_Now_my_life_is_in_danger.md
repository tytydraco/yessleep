There is no safe harbor for me anymore. 



There is no alleyway, no basement, no home, no zip code, and no country where I can hide, because every place I know of is certainly known by them too. And soon, they will come for me. I can only hope that when they arrive, they’ll afford me the mercy of a quick death. The same death all the others before me experienced. I can only hope that, in their eyes, I’m owed that as family. But there are certainly no guarantees. Not anymore. Not after my cousin Daniel. 



At first, babysitting my four year old cousin was just an easy way to earn spare cash, but with time, it evolved into something more. Aunt Agatha and Uncle Marc worked odd hours to keep their family afloat, placing my 11 year old self in their stead. Even at that age, I found their constant absence a bit strange, but my discomfort was quickly replaced by something I never anticipated. Daniel taught me the value of family, and every marathon run of Super Smash Bros or Pokemon VHS tapes reinforced it. He was my little brother; the sibling I never had. Of course, I never voiced this to him (it’s an open secret in the family that his mother had a miscarriage before he came around), but I felt it in my heart nonetheless. Only now do I know how deeply he felt it too. 



He was only seven when doctors diagnosed him with cancer. Daniel wrapped himself against me and sobbed into my shoulder that night. He even called me his big sister; I was the sibling he never had, and his only source of comfort in his empty home. His days were soon submerged by sleep. Always sleeping. Always leaning against me on the couch. Those hours of silence nudged me into knitting for him. It didn’t turn out great, but that slice of bright orange fabric quickly became his favorite. We shared it on the couch for years, a constant source of warmth for Daniel’s worst days. But his worst days soon came every day. 



Bit by bit, he was withering away. Daniel hardly moved on our last days together. He couldn’t muster the strength to say goodbye when I left for college. Just a faint smile. But I left knowing that only a single semester separated us from watching the snow twirl around Christmas lights together. But instead, I returned to find his house empty. 



“They moved out of town for better care,” Mom said. The dark, second floor windows of Daniel’s home loomed over the cul-de-sac. A realtor sign swayed silently in the freezing wind. All of Daniel and I’s years together had been reduced to eight simple words, delivered the moment I walked through the front door, like nothing more than a checked box on a list. My parents refused to elaborate further on the matter. “Hard times fall on everyone, Hannah,” my Mom stated over breakfast, “And it’s never good to pry.” But what about Daniel? He was so skinny when I left. So tired. So empty. Was he better now? The same? Or was he worse? To that, absolutely no answer was given, and one wouldn’t be given for another five years. 



**Message from Mom: Daniel passed away last night. There was no pain, and Aunt Agatha and Uncle Marc never left his side.** 



My phone slid from my hand to the floor. Nothing can prepare you for a moment like that. Nothing can prepare you for the deluge of memories and tears. Nothing can prepare you for understanding that the pain and the past are all that remain. Nothing can prepare you for never beholding their face again. 



It was a closed casket funeral. No answer was given for that, either. Only a mounted portrait of his weak, tired smile watched us from above the casket. It was all too much to bear. But I pushed myself forward just enough to survive my walk to the lectern to deliver Daniel’s eulogy. My love for him - his smile, his warmth, his humor, his sweet, gentle nature - everything I never had the chance to express flowed through the funeral parlor that afternoon. 

  
I took a deep breath, dried my eyes, and inched back to my pew. As I looked out across the chapel, and the hundreds of eyes greeting my own, I noticed something. Something that felt wrong. 



There was no sadness in the audience that afternoon. No crying, no sniffling, no identifiable expression of grief. Just the cold, distant eyes of my entire family staring through me. Not even Uncle Marc spared a tear for Daniel. Aunt Agatha simply stared at the floor, her lowered head revealing dark roots against platinum blonde (had she always dyed her hair?) as I returned to my seat. As the services proceeded with clerical pace, I still felt eyes on me. A cold, constant drilling into the back of my neck. I knew immediately that it was her. We had locked eyes the moment I arrived that day. 



Aunt Sophia shadowed over the entire family. Rarely seen, but deeply known. Rarely heard, but quoted often. Rarely felt, but always present. Her existence clung to the family tree in ways defying her reclusive nature. Family gatherings, holidays, vacations - none of them came to pass without a sampling of her wisdom. But for all I knew, that day at Daniel’s funeral was my first time ever seeing her. A black buckle on the shoulder of her dress caught the light, illuminating its white flower emblem. Her austere silence and cold, pale eyes were unmissable. You remember someone like that. You remember how their gaze feels. 



That feeling remained with me as services ended, and the family rose and funneled out into the parking lot in silence. A quick gaze over my shoulder reminded me of just how empty Aunt Sophia’s eyes were. Even from behind rows of moving heads. I quickened my pace through the foyer just in time for Aunt Agatha to corner me into a hallway. Her eyes were drained and wide. She was trembling. “He wanted you to have this,” she quickly said, and shoved a bag into my arms. Uncle Marc immediately approached and guided her out by the arm. Tears streamed from Agatha’s eyes as she was whisked out of view.   


I gazed into the bag and was greeted by Daniel’s orange blanket.



I clinched the bag shut, not wishing to break down in front of my family, and made my way to the car. Aunt Sophia’s eyes met me in the rearview mirror, her presence unmistakable from the far end of the parking lot. They stayed with me as I entered a frontage road, then disappeared onto the highway. I shivered the memory of her out of me when I arrived home and stuffed Daniel’s blanket in a closet. His death was too recent to endure its sight. A week passed before a new text message roused me out of sleep. 



**Message from Mom: Aunt Agatha is dead. She hanged herself.**



The room froze around me. There, suspended in the darkness, strangled by shock, my mind leapt to Uncle Marc. Did he find her? Feet dangling from the ceiling?   
  
I lurched up and turned on the lights. I felt sick. I rushed to the bathroom and curled against the toilet, heaving through the horror. The floor twirled and danced beneath my knees. Limbs of sweat stretched across my face. I gripped the seat and prayed for the waves of nausea to release me. Breathe in. Breathe out. Soon the world steadied around me, just enough to stand. I shattered into tears and stumbled for the closet and grabbed the only thing I knew to hold: Daniel’s blanket. 



An envelope fluttered out from it and silently drifted to the carpet. 



***To Hannah***



I reached down and plucked it from the floor, my thumb trembling as it parted the flap. Inside, a letter: 



***Sweetheart,*** 



***I want you to know it wasn’t me.*** 

***Go to Rock Mountain.*** 

***Don’t let her stop you. Please forgive me.*** 



***-Aunt Agatha*** 



Her wide, frightened eyes flooded my mind. She left this for me. She needed me to read this. But as I stood there beneath the flickering closet light, struggling to make sense of Aunt Agatha’s final words to me, a sickly pang of dread gnawed through me. Her letter stirred something buried deep inside of me. Something I couldn’t know, had never known, but knew instinctively. I knew where Rock Mountain was. 



***She hanged herself.*** 



My mother’s text message echoed in my head as I drove west that morning. West. Rock Mountain was west. A place I had no recollection of was to my west. But I continued driving all the same. For Daniel. For Aunt Agatha. As the flat bluestem grass and farmland boiled into jagged hills and pine trees, my mind began to turn again. Twisting. Uncoiling. Dredging something up. 



A girl. A young girl, no older than seven. A girl with short blonde hair, a toothy smile, and a polkadot dress. A girl with a twisted leg. Gnarled and thin, dangling short. Each passing mile marker revealed more of her. She was there in every passing car, every tree, and around every bend. A girl I knew, just as I knew that traveling west on Internet 70 would bring me to Rock Mountain. But I couldn’t remember her name. I pulled into a gas station as Dad’s voice told me. 



*“Mia, your sister will go in with you. Hannah, help her.”* 



Everything plummeted like I had missed a step on a staircase. I had a sister. Mia was my sister. Then Mom added *“Let me fix your dress first, sweetie. We need to keep your leg covered.”* Her leg always needed to be covered. Mom said it scared people. My vision tumbled, but I clenched the steering wheel and shuddered for control because *I helped Mia out of the car, and stood by her side as she dragged her foot forward to the bathrooms.* I wiped the sweat from my brow and pulled back onto the interstate, a sign beckoning to me in the side mirror: ROCK MOUNTAIN - 100 MILES. 



Mia’s ghost followed me through the mountains. Her spirit coursed through me in freezing waves, each rush injecting me with the inescapable truth that I had been lied to my entire life. 



**INCOMING CALL: MOM**



I couldn’t speak to her then, and I wasn’t sure if I ever could again. Regardless, there was no time to speak, because I was finally nearing my destination. 

  
I knew this because *“Okay girls, it’s just up ahead. When we get there, your Mommy and I need to move some bushes around to let the car through. But when we stop, we need you to stay down until we tell you two, okay?”* I pulled to the side of the road just beyond a bend and followed their footsteps, doing as they did and *“Remember girls: you always have to put the bushes back exactly as you found them!”* I rolled the car forward, drawing the bushes back into place, then turned to find myself in a dense, evergreen thrush. The dull roar of cars racing down the interstate barely cut through the foliage. As I returned to my car, I stepped on something. A strange flower, its white petals fibrous and velvety. 



*“This is an Edelweiss flower. They’re very important to our family, and they’re your Aunt Sophia’s favorite.”* A few more dotted the dirt road that ascended the hillside, beckoning me forward. I crept my car up the road, my eyes discovering more and more flowers as I drove. A few scattered about, then a cluster, and another one, and *“Okay girls, let’s see how many you can count!”* 



Edelweiss flowers punctured the emerald vegetation. 



*“1……2……3……”* 



I gulped as the hilltop came into view. The road, and the flowers, were leading me somewhere. I had no choice but to proceed. Daniel and Agatha demanded it. Mia, too. 



*“4……5……6……”* 



I rounded the final corner. 



*“7……8……9……”* 



And pulled to the top. 



*“10!”* 



The roof came into view first, creeping over the ridgeline. Then stone, shouldering itself up into great walls, rising among the trees. *“You’re right, it does look like a castle!”* The house was in immaculate shape, every conceivable inch meticulously maintained. *“Aunt Sophia likes to keep her house tidy.”* Indeed. I backed my car down the hill a bit, keeping it just out of view. But there was no one around. No cars. No lights in windows. No curious eyes. I was alone. 



**INCOMING CALL: MOM** 



*There were cars dotting the driveway and road that day. Their voices carried all the way outside. There were so many of them. Were they all family? I looked up to Mom and Dad. They looked sad all of a sudden, like they were both about to cry. He forced a smile as his eyes met mine. “Come on, we don’t want to keep them waiting.”* 



But I couldn’t just walk inside. I needed to be deliberate and silent. I finally understood that I was never meant to see any of this. After a quick scan of the empty expanse surrounding the property, I hurried to the side of the house. A window, partially ajar, opened into a kitchen. No lights were on inside. I wrapped my fingers around the window frame and -



*Dad opened the door. Everyone turned to face us. There were dozens of them, maybe a hundred. Mom started to cry. Aunt Agatha hurried forward and wrapped her in a hug.* 



\- I stepped into the kitchen. Aunt Agatha had been here after all. She was another forlorn face at the mere sight of my family that day. I pushed deeper into the house, and as I rounded a corner, the walls peeled away into a massive living room. A menagerie of portraits lined the walls, all spanning decades, if not centuries, of family history. Some were recent and filled with recognizable faces. Others predated even my oldest relatives. There were - 



*So many people I had never seen before. Mia and I exchanged glances. We were bewildered. The same hands that embraced my Mom soon took my Dad and I, too. Everyone was sad. Tears stained cheeks. I glanced back to Mia, to discover that she was alone on the other side of the room.* 



\- rows of filled, mahogany bookshelves framing the living room, but one book in particular caught my eye. A photo album, frilled with floral accents, sitting in the middle of a coffee table. I stole another look over my shoulder, and to every window in sight, then approached. My heart raced as my fingers froze at the cover, then - 



*Mia began to cry. Alone at the edge of the room. She instinctively pulled her dress down. “Mom?” she cried. “Dad?” Neither turned to face her. She limped forward. I reached for her, but a hand (I don’t know whose hand) restrained me. She stared at me with wide eyes, tears lining the edges of her open mouth, ready to scream, when Aunt Sophia rested her hands on her shoulders. Everyone turned to face her.* 



\- I flicked the album open. A thousand eyes peered back at me from behind a cellophane sleeve. A family portrait, worn and muddied in sepia tone. A few hundred people arranged in orderly rows before a forest treeline. All top hats, silk dresses, and fine jewelry. A written description buttressed the photo in a language I couldn’t understand, except for the year and location: BAVARIA, 1858. I turned the page only to discover another portrait. And another. And another. An entire bloodline captured in over a dozen, large print photographs: 1861, 1866, 1874, 1881, 1885, 1894, and so on. 



**INCOMING CALL: MOM** 



Every portrait, however, bore a difference. A difference I didn’t notice at first glance but, once caught, it became impossible to ignore. The family - 



*Fell silent as Aunt Sophia gracefully descended to her knees before Mia. Her warm, motherly smile seemed to dry the tears from Mia’s face. She brushed her pale hair back in place and said, “Mia, we have a very important task to complete. Will you come help me?” Mia turned to Mom and Dad. Dad nodded. Mom wouldn’t look at her. Aunt Sophia stood up with Mia’s hand in her own, her black buckle catching the light as she did. There was an Edelweiss flower on it. I tried to follow them out, but Dad blocked me with his arm and said “No.” Everyone went back to talking among themselves. Aunt Agatha guided Mom out of the room, an arm draped over her shoulder. I looked to the windows for a sign of Mia. None. I needed to wait a few minutes. Bid my time until no one was looking, then go find her.* 



\- was becoming smaller every year. Not dramatically so, maybe five or six missing from one year to the next, but over time, a few hundred became half that size. All those who vanished from sight were children. I pulled a portrait out of its sleeve for a closer look. My fingers became smudged with black and red ink. I turned it over and froze. Then I pulled out the others. All of them had one, too. Diagrams. I turned - 



*The handle to the back door and stepped out into the humid expanse of green and mud. No one was looking, and no one had heard me leave. I closed the door silently. A small trail snaked through the trees just beyond the house. Mia’s laugh echoed from somewhere just out of view, just down the trail. I ran in hopes of catching up to her.* 



\- them all over, arranged them in chronological order from the top down, and beheld a handwritten family tree. Rigid boxes of blank ink framing names and birth years, all connected by angular branches that descended all the way to present day. The stream of names stretched back beyond the dates labeled on the portraits. 1691 was the oldest I saw. All the way down too, however, were - 



*Edelweiss flowers. Dozens. Hundreds. Everywhere, all leading me further down the trail. Soft impressions of Mia’s giggles whispered through the trees. Another sound rose to meet them. The low hum of a melody carried on Aunt Sophia’s voice. There were words to her song, but none in a language I understood. Mia didn’t seem to mind, because every step forward brought me closer to her laughter. The soft, white flowers crackled beneath my feet.* 



**INCOMING CALL: MOM** 



\- bright red “x’s.” Names crossed out from the family tree. Dozens of them. Almost every family had at least one. Some had more. A few branches were severed completely. I shuddered as I focused on the ages hidden beneath the red ink. Many of those crossed out from the record never learned to speak. Or walk - 



*Closer to them. I was closer to them now. The flowers clustered and merged into a seamless pathway leading to a small clearing. I quickly hid behind a tree, because in the middle of the clearing were Mia and Aunt Sophia. They were facing away from each other on opposite ends, picking bundles of Edelweiss in their hands. Small mounds dotted the rim of the field, stretching all the way back into the trees. The flowers seemed to spool out from them. It felt like watching a dream. Aunt Sophia’s song and Mia’s soft laughter illuminated the floral expanse. As Aunt Sophia continued to sing, she slowly turned to Mia.* 



\- or laugh or cry or live. There were so many of them. I flinched and pushed the portraits away from me, scattering them across the floor. I wanted to leave. I needed to leave. But Mia forced me to stay. So did Aunt Agatha. So did Daniel. 



**INCOMING CALL: MOM** 



**MESSAGE FROM MOM: Please answer.** 



My eyes returned to the photo album, now partially stripped of its contents, and locked on something small. Something small and pale behind the empty cellophane sleeves. I turned them aside and gagged. I whimpered in disbelief. Looking up at me on a page of its very own was the death portrait of a young boy. 



*Aunt Sophia reached for the buckle on her shoulder as Mia continued collecting flowers. She pulled the top away, unsheathing a small knife. It shimmered in a sun beam. She inched closer to Mia’s back. Her singing never stopped.* 



He was no more than seven, his face now sealed in black and white. His tiny hands held an Edelweiss flower against his chest. The year ‘1923’ was stenclied just beneath it. A birth certificate, a collection of baby photos, and a tiny sock adorned the rest of the page. ‘Adam. Hemophilia.’ 



*Aunt Sophia crept forward. Closer. And closer. And closer. Mia never noticed her sneaking up. I was confused. I didn’t understand what I was watching. Mia chuckled some more.* 



I groaned in disgust as I turned the page. Another child. A girl. ‘1924. Emily. Spina Bifida.’ 



*Aunt Sophia steadied herself on one knee. She was directly behind Mia now. She raised the knife to shoulder height, angling it directly at the middle of Mia’s back.* 



Another child. Another girl. ‘1927. Mary. Cerebral palsy.’ 



*The singing continued. The laughter continued.* 



‘1931. Noah. Scoliosis.’ 



*My breath caught. My arms and legs froze over.* 



‘1938. Lina. Mental impairment.’ 



*I needed to call out. I needed to scream.* 



‘1941. 1943. 1944. 1948.’ 



*But I couldn’t. I couldn’t say anything. I watched as Aunt Sophia remained in place. The blade was perfectly steady in her hand.* 



‘1950. 1951. 1958. 1962.’ 



*Aunt Sophia pulled her arm back in time with her song.* 



‘1966. 1969. 1972. 1977.’ 



*Mia dropped a flower. She giggled and plucked it between her fingers.*



‘1981. 1984. 1985. 1989.’ 



*The blade bobbed slightly in Aunt Sophia’s hands as she angled it just right.* 



‘1990. 1991. 1992. 1993.’ 



*Aunt Sophia took a deep breath.* 



‘1994. 1995. 1996. 1997.’ 



*And slid the knife into the middle of Mia’s back. Mia flinched and stiffened. Her face slackened. She slumped forward into the grass, whined, then fell silent.* 



**INCOMING CALL: MOM.** 



I stared down at Mia’s picture in silence. ‘1998. Mia. Blount’s disease.’ My parents brought my sister here to die. They needed me to make sure she would come. The weight of my family’s conspiracy, their violence, suffocated me. As this new understanding of my mother, father, cousins, aunts, uncles, and grandparents tremored through my hands in a million needles, instinct took over, and forced me to turn to the last page of Aunt Sophia’s book. 



‘2026. Daniel. Leukemia.’ 



**INCOMING CALL: MOM** 



I slammed the book shut, shot to my feet, and ran for the door. No thoughts, no feeling, just the sheer panic of escape. 



*Aunt Sophia gently pulled the knife from Mia’s back and wiped it clean on her black dress. She slid the knife back into the buckle, still humming.* 



***She hanged herself.*** 



Aunt Sophia would be back soon. 



**MESSAGE FROM MOM: Please, Hannah. Please pick up.** 



*Aunt Sophia’s head snapped to face me. Neither of us spoke. We simply stared. After a moment, Aunt Sophia rose to her feet and walked towards me and I couldn’t speak because I was so scared and then her hand covered my mouth-* 



I sprinted out the front door and down the hill to my car. As I turned the ignition, I looked out to see Aunt Sophia watching me from the hilltop. The blade glimmered in her hand. 



I don’t remember much of my drive out of there. I see frames and fragments of it, but not the full picture. Panic. Sobbing. Crashing through bushes. Fish tailing onto the interstate. Red lining the engine. Exiting onto another highway. Going south. Keep going south. Faster. Faster. Hours south. Trees and summit peaks gave way to beige expanses of sand. Screaming adrenaline waned to a dull roar. Eventually I pulled over into the sand, opened the door, and vomited into the sand. As I wiped my mouth clean with my sleeve, I saw it. It had been slipped under my windshield wiper, pinned perfectly in place, and entirely ignored in my frantic escape. 



**INCOMING CALL: MOM** 



A plain, white envelope with an edelweiss flower glued to the corner. I yanked the envelope out and opened it, revealing a note: 



She shouldn’t have told you.



A Polaroid slipped out from beneath the five word letter. The white film of its back begged to be turned over. I did so. I only viewed the top corner of the picture before recoiling from it. Clumps of dyed, blonde hair stuck to chunks of shattered scalp. Dark roots. I saw enough to know it was Aunt Agatha. 



***She hanged herself.*** 



I fled further into the desert until my gas tank ran empty in the arid landscape. A small motel was attached to the parking lot of a Chevron. I needed gas, and I needed sleep. I stared through the bedroom ceiling well into the small hours of the night. The burden of centuries of systematic murder rested on my shoulders. I knew it could only be relieved by confessing the sins of my family. 



But as the picture of Aunt Agatha told me, my window for doing so was shutting. I was next to die. 



**12 MISSED CALLS FROM MOM** 



Dawn arrived quietly through the motel room shades and insisted that I continue driving. After a splash of cold water on my face, I opened the door to find another envelope tumbling down from the door frame to the concrete walkway. Its edelweiss flower was carried off on a breeze. I had no choice but to open it. Another letter: 



***Hannah,*** 



***I was eight years old when I watched my brother die.*** 



***My mother called me into the bathroom a few nights after she had given birth to him. She held him in her arms. He was a small, pathetic thing. Withered, shriveled, struggling to move and breathe.***



***She lowered him into the bathtub. He struggled and spasmed in the water, but my mother pushed her deeper. She taught me the song that night. The song that all mothers in our family are expected to know. It was written for moments like these.***



***Eventually, he stopped moving. Mom pulled her hands from the water, and his tiny body floated to the top. He drifted lifeless for a moment before Mom turned to me and said, “I know it’s hard, but remember that thinning the herd is a moral obligation.”***



***It took me years to appreciate her words, but when I was a little older, I understood. He would’ve never survived in this world. The humiliation, indignity, and ridicule that the world planned to drown him would destroy him. The family would’ve been destroyed along with him. I grew to learn that Mom saved him that night. He was saved from the cruelty of this world. The same cruelty Mia and Daniel both endured.***



***Now they rest with the flowers.***



***Thinning the herd is a moral obligation.***



***We will see you soon.***



There was no photograph attached to the letter this time, only a perfectly cut square of Daniel’s blanket. As it tumbled from my hands, another piece caught my eye. Just within the corner of my vision. Inside of my car. 



There, wrapped in the blanket and arranged in the front seats of my car, were the remains of Daniel, Aunt Agatha, and Mom.
