The sounds of mourning doves over grass bathed in an orange hue. The Christmas lights that used to shine brighter. The ambient, lonely tracks of Minecraft playing in a dark room.

Moments in time that bring along a cooling breeze, but also a heavy, dangerous internal warmth.

It’s all fake.

Don’t remember.

But memories have a way of finding you when you’re quiet. No matter how hard you try to bury them, they wait somewhere in the back of your mind, untouched by time, waiting for you to get weak enough to let them back in.

Mine started with a rainy morning.

It was finally move in day, and the high of independence made the downpour feel invisible.

"Honey, do you really need to take all these rocks and hippie mumbo jumbo? I don’t think that’s something college boys like," my dear mother said, holding a fistful of crystals and my tarot cards.

Usually, that would trigger a whole routine: me correcting her, then a fight, then hours of silence. But that day, nothing could touch me.

"I’ll take them, but I’ll keep them in a drawer," I said with a big smile.

She looked oddly satisfied, almost relieved, at the response.

"I knew you would come around. Plus, some things shine a little too much light."

God, I hated when she said that. It was always her preamble to that Ouija board story she’d repeated a thousand times without ever revealing real details, just vague warnings about opening doors you can’t close.

Thankfully, I was spared. While moving boxes, relics of the past kept emerging. Behind my dresser was a folded cutout of a paper ruler. The memory forced its way into my mind like a static jolt, starting with a cold whisper, a quick recap looking for flaws, and then that sudden, narcotic warmth in the pit of my stomach rushing up to my heart.

It had been taped next to my door so Mom and Dad could keep track of my height. We never wrote directly on the drywall because they never expected to own the place.

In the memory, I was only knee high to my dad.

"Wow, you’ve gotten so big," he said, his voice giving a little shake at the end.

Mom joined in from the hall. "See? That’s why you have to keep eating the nasty trees."

For a split second, I swore I saw Dad look at Mom with something far worse than disgust, a hollow, exhausted look. Then, like an old tube TV being unplugged, the memory clicked off. The warmth vanished.

"Are you sure you don’t want to take this?" Dad asked, holding up a bright red book.

It was the dieting book Mom had gotten me for Christmas when I was twelve. I remembered the message written on the inside cover before I even saw it: *If you’re going to indulge!*

For a moment, the room slipped away. The Christmas lights were brighter than they had any right to be, spilling gold across the floorboards and making everything look soft and safe. They wrapped around the windows and reflected off every glass ornament until the whole room glowed. Dad was holding the book, laughing at the inscription. I laughed too.

Then I looked past him.

The wall behind him should have been glowing gold, too, but a dense patch of darkness cut right through the light. It wasn't caused by furniture. It wasn't shaped like anything in the room. It was just there, impossibly dark against all that warmth, as if the light itself refused to touch it.

Dad was still smiling, totally unbothered, as if the shadow was just part of the wallpaper.

For some reason, I knew better than to look behind him again.

The memory flickered out.

"So I take that as a no," Dad said.

"Yeah, no thank you."

He pointed at the message in the book and glanced at Mom. "Like that stopped us," he muttered, lowering his voice. "We’ve still got the cookies hidden in the garage."

I forced a smile. Across the room, Mom rolled her eyes, her face tight and guarded.

"The body may want it," she said quietly, staring right at me before disappearing into the doorway, "but the mind pays the price."

By the time everything was packed into the car, the rain had set into a heavy downpour. Leaving home didn't feel real until the highway stretched out behind us.

My new apartment didn’t have the same feel as my old room, but it was mine. A kitchen that could barely fit two people and a bathroom two steps from the living room.

What people don’t tell you about independence is how loud the silence is.

I tried to occupy my mind with cleaning and unpacking, but there were only so many boxes. Eventually, I was just sitting on my beanbag in the middle of the bare floor. Tap water tasted faintly of copper. I left the TV on, but the audio sounded thin and paper like, as if coming through a cardboard box.

I tried calling Maya, but she didn’t answer. Her last text, a curt "Have fun in college," sat at the top of my screen. She was still bitter about me moving two states away. I stared at my phone, typing out texts, deleting them, scrolling through old group chats just to feel the temperature of an afternoon that didn't exist anymore.

I looked around the room. This was what I’d wanted: my own place, my own rules.

So why did it feel like a vacuum?

I sat there doing nothing, staring at the ceiling. I don't remember when I stopped thinking about Maya. I just remember letting go.

Suddenly, I was back home. Mom was calling from the kitchen. Dad was laughing at something on TV. Everything was solid and warm.

Then I blinked.

The apartment was pitch black. Hours had vanished without a single thought to show for them. The room was cold, my legs were stiff, and that heavy, sweet warmth was lingering in my chest like smoke.

Days turned into weeks. The apartment stopped feeling new; it just felt hollow.

I’d be sitting in a lecture and suddenly remember the exact way hallway light used to creep under my bedroom door at night. I’d walk back from the bodega, smell wet asphalt, and for five seconds I wasn't in college, I was back on the porch at sixteen.

At first, I craved it. It made the gray days tolerable. But then it started taking longer to pull out of it. A song would play and I’d lose twenty minutes. I’d stand in front of an open fridge, staring into the cold light until my knees ached. My mind had found somewhere better to be, and it was getting harder to convince it to come back to the present.

That was when I dug through the drawer beside my mattress until I found it: the smoky quartz.

It was one of the first stones I’d ever bought. The brown faded in and out depending on the light, with tiny fractures trapped beneath the surface like frozen smoke.

Mom would’ve called it hippie mumbo jumbo. But holding it now, I realized why she kept her fistful of rocks. She wasn't playing with magic; she was trying to keep herself tethered.

I sat on the linoleum, lit a tall white candle, and placed the stone between my knees.

Grounding.

Keeping your feet on the floor when your mind starts wandering into places that want to keep you.

I breathed slowly. The carpet. The hum of the fridge. The rain against the glass.

Here.

I repeated it until the coldness of the stone seeped into my palms. When I opened my eyes, nothing had magically changed. It was just a rock. But I wrapped a rubber band around my wrist, tucked the crystal underneath it, and pulled the band taut.

If I started slipping again, I’d snap it. Something real. Something sharp to sever the tether.

Almost immediately, as if taking the ritual as an invitation, the room began to soften. The TV static started sparkling with gold light. A distant smell of cinnamon rolled in.

*Snap.*

The rubber band slammed hard against the stone. The gold light vanished instantly. No cold whispers, no cozy memories. Just the dreary, flat reality of my room. But the warm comfort was gone, too.

For a while, the pain kept me present. I mentioned the band to Maya over text; she just told me to get healthier distractions: chewing ice, cooking, doomscrolling. I knew those were just milder ways to numb out, but at least they belonged to the real world.

*Snap.*

Time began to drag with a brutal, agonizing weight. Seeing every step to my car, the 25 minute drive, every single letter of my lecture notes in painful, high definition clarity. Everything carried a chilling, uncomfortable edge.

*Snap.*

The bell rang.

I looked up, my wrist throbbing under the stone. For a second, I couldn't remember which building I was in. The classroom looked standard: desks, whiteboard, harsh fluorescent tubes overhead.

Then I looked at the window.

The reflection in the glass showed the classroom, but there were no desks in it. Just a vast, empty room.

I blinked. *Snap.*

I looked again. The desks were back. Just my imagination.

I packed my bag, my heart hammering against my ribs, and stood up to leave. But as I reached the exit, I caught my reflection in the dark glass of the classroom door.

In the reflection, the room behind me was completely stripped bare again. No desks. No whiteboard.

And standing right in the far corner of the dark, empty space was the patch of darkness from the Christmas memory, taller now, silent, waiting in the background.

I froze. My breath hitched in my throat.

I turned around fast.

The classroom was normal. Rows of empty chairs. A professor erasing the board.

I turned back to the glass door. The reflection had reset to normal, too.

I hooked my thumb under the rubber band, pulled it back as far as it would stretch, and let go.

*Snap.*

I kept walking into the hall, but the pain didn't bring the warmth back, and for the first time, it didn't feel like enough to keep me here.

And I was right, it just kept going.

The misremembering started with Maya. I texted her to ask why she brought up the summer of 2018 during our phone call on Tuesday. She replied hours later: "We didn't talk on Tuesday. I haven't called you in a month." I checked my phone history. An outgoing nine minute call to her number sitting right there. But when I tried to recall her voice, all I could hear was my mother's flat tone telling me to keep things hidden in a drawer.

The sensory bleed escalated. I was sitting in a crowded lecture hall when the smell of damp pine needles and burnt toast filled my lungs so thick I gagged. The harsh fluorescent lighting softened into that heavy, golden amber of a December afternoon. For ten full seconds, the linoleum floor felt like dusty carpet under my palms. I reached for my wrist to snap the band, but the rubber was long gone. My skin was just red and raw, bruised in the shape of a habit.

I began slipping into the script. A guy in my hallway asked if I wanted to share a pizza order. I looked at him, my head heavy with that sweet, suffocating heat, and heard myself say, "The body may want it, but the mind pays the price." He stepped back, looking at me like I was insane. I didn't even realize the words had left my mouth until the silence settled. I couldn't remember his name. I couldn't remember my own room number.

The smoky quartz lost its cold edge. Holding it felt like holding warm butter. I looked in the bathroom mirror while washing my face and realized my reflection was lagging by a split second. I stopped moving, but my reflection kept blinking. Its eyes weren't looking at my face. They were looking past my left shoulder, tracking something standing in the doorway behind me.

When I turned around, my apartment hallway wasn't there. It was just that dark, light absorbing patch from my childhood home, stretched wide across the drywall, pulsing gently like a breath.

I ran to my room and, like a little kid, went under the covers for protection and called Dad.

I pressed the phone to my ear, my hand shaking so hard the glass screen clicked against my teeth. The dial tone sounded heavy, like it was pulling through mud.

He picked up on the third ring. "Hey, sweetie. How’s college treating you?"

His voice was warm and familiar, but my chest felt so tight I could barely draw air.

"Dad," I choked out, clutching the phone with both hands. "I think... I don't know what's wrong with me. I keep losing track of time. I can't focus. Everything looks wrong."

"Oh, honey," he said, smooth and completely untroubled. "First semester jitters. You're just spiraling. Run a hot bath, put an old movie on, and just tune out for a bit."

"No, I don't want to tune out," I gasped. "It's too quiet here. Dad, please, just put Mom on. I need to talk to Mom."

The background noise on the line froze. No TV static, no hum of the house. Just dead air.

"Your mother is resting," he said. His voice didn't drop or change pitch; it stayed that exact same light, pleasant cadence. "She's tired, sweetie. Go put something comfortable on in the background. You'll feel fine once you stop trying so hard."

"Dad, please, just let me speak to her for one second!"

"I'm going to let you go now," he said cheerfully. "Go take that bath."

The line clicked off. He hadn't sounded worried at all. He sounded like a recording left on repeat: smooth, safe, and completely empty.

The screen went black, leaving only my own wide, bloodshot eyes reflected in the glass.

Beneath the blanket, the air was becoming stifling, but I couldn't pull it down. The silence in the apartment didn't feel empty anymore. It felt heavy, like standing at the bottom of a deep pool while amber light bled straight through the fabric of the quilt.

It wasn't a creature climbing into bed. It was the space around me shifting.

The smell of damp pine needles and furnace dust filled the air under the blanket, so thick it coated the back of my throat. The temperature didn't drop; it rose into that heavy, suffocating heat of an old radiator. My limbs felt leaden, anchored down by that intoxicating promise of rest.

*You don't have to keep fighting it.*

The thoughts weren't even mine anymore. They were layered echoes: my dad's cheerful chuckle, the hum of the old television, the crackle of holiday lights. It was a golden tide washing over my mind, eroding the edges of who I was, inviting me to just lie back and stop trying.

*The mind pays the price.*

My mom's voice cut through the noise, sharp and cold, like a splash of freezing water against a fever. It wasn't sweet. It didn't offer comfort. It was the only memory that carried teeth.

I gasped, ripping the blanket off my face.

There was no creature at the foot of the bed. There was only the wall. The dark, light absorbing patch from my childhood home had spread across the entire far corner of my room, silent, dense, and pulsing gently. It wasn't moving toward me. It didn't need to. It was just standing there, swallowing the lamplight, waiting for the room to finish emptying out.

My chest heaved as I scrambled for my phone in the sheets. My hands were shaking so hard I could barely unlock the screen.

I didn't call my dad. I didn't text Maya. I dialed the one number I had spent my whole life trying to escape.

The knock on the door at three in the morning wasn't gentle. It was three sharp, impatient raps against the thin wood.

When I opened it, my mother didn't throw her arms around me. She didn't look at my swollen eyes or my bruised wrist. She just stepped inside, set her tote bag on the floor, and looked around the dim, cluttered living room with that familiar, tight line across her mouth.

"Open the blinds," she said, her voice flat and clipped.

Old resentment flared instantly in my chest, hot and bitter. I had called her terrified, practically begging for help, and within ten seconds of walking into my life, she was already picking it apart.

"I don't want the blinds open," I muttered, my voice raspy. "It's three in the morning. I'm exhausted."

She ignored me, walking straight past me to yank the cord. The amber streetlights poured through the glass, cutting through the shadow that had been pooling by the window.

"Get the trash bag from under the sink," she said, turning to face me. "There are four empty takeout containers on the coffee table. Put them away."

"Are you serious?" I snapped, my hands shaking as I gestured to the dark patch still clinging to the corner near the hallway. "I called you because I'm losing my mind. I'm losing time, I'm seeing things, and you're telling me to clean my apartment?"

"Yes," she said. She didn't raise her voice, but her eyes were cold, hard, and fixed entirely on me. "I am."

I hated her. In that moment, I hated her with every fiber of my being. She was the same rigid, unfeeling woman she had always been, incapable of giving a single drop of real comfort when I was drowning.

Defiant, I turned away from her and walked to the kitchen. I opened the freezer, pulled out a tub of ice cream, and grabbed a spoon. I just wanted to sit on the floor, eat something sweet, and tune everything out.

Before the spoon even touched the tub, her hand came down firmly on my wrist.

She didn't yank me, but her grip was like iron. She snatched the tub from my fingers, put it right back on the freezer shelf, and slammed the door shut.

"Don't do that," she said quietly.

"It's just ice cream!" I yelled, tears finally spilling over my cheeks. "Why are you doing this to me? Why can't you just let me rest?"

"Because rest is how it gets you," she said, her voice dropping to a razor thin whisper. "You sit in the dark, you numb yourself, and let your mind float somewhere else. By the time you wake up, you're not all the way here anymore."

She let go of my wrist and pointed at the sink.

"Hot water. Soap. Wash the pan from yesterday."

I stared at her, breathing heavily, every instinct screaming at me to throw something or tell her to leave. But when I looked past her shoulder toward the hallway, the dark patch was pulsing, waiting for me to keep arguing, waiting for me to give up and drift.

Grumbling under my breath, I turned on the tap.

The water steamed instantly, almost painfully hot against my knuckles. I poured the green soap onto the sponge and began scrubbing the caked grease off the bottom of the frying pan. The metal scraped loudly. The steam filled my nostrils with the sharp, artificial smell of citrus.

My mind tried to wander toward the quiet warmth of a snowy Christmas morning, but the stinging heat of the water dragged me right back.

Scrub. Rinse. Wipe.

I did it three times, my muscles aching, my skin red from the heat.

When I finally turned off the faucet, the apartment was completely quiet. Not the heavy, suffocating silence from earlier, but a simple, plain silence.

I looked at the corner of the room. The dark patch was still there, but it wasn't pulsing anymore. It had shrunk, receded into a dull, flat shadow tucked behind the bookshelf, dormant.

My mother was sitting at the small kitchen table. She had poured two mugs of black tea, steam rising slowly into the air between them. On the table lay a paperback book, open to the middle pages.

She didn't smile at me. She didn't apologize for being harsh. She just pushed one of the mugs toward my side of the table.

"Sit down," she said, her voice quiet. "Read a chapter. Then you can sleep."

I looked at the tea, then at the worn cover of the book, and finally at my mother's tired, guarded face. The bitterness in my chest didn't vanish instantly, but for the first time in my life, the sharp, cold distance between us didn't feel like cruelty.

It felt like a fortress.

I pulled out a chair, sat down, and picked up the mug.

The steam from the black tea bit at the back of my throat. It didn't taste cozy or sweet; it just tasted bitter and hot. Real.

I turned to page forty two. Beside me, my mother took a slow sip from her own mug, her posture rigid as a guard on duty, her eyes locked onto her own pages. Outside, the rain continued to hit the window glass, but for the first time in months, I didn't try to remember what driveway it was falling on.

In the corner behind the bookshelf, the dark patch stayed flat and quiet. It was going to be there tomorrow, and the day after that, waiting for me to get tired, waiting for me to look for an easy escape.

But as long as the tea was hot, the pages were turning, and my mother was sitting across from me, it was going to have to wait.

I turned the page and took another sip of the bitter tea.

Don’t remember.
