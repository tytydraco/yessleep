The night we asked, I was trying to get three dollars and twelve cents back from the phone company. They'd charged us for a paper bill after I switched us to email. I left the envelope beside the phone, and every time I passed the counter with June I thought about how good it was going to feel to get those three dollars back.

I need you to know what we were like with our kids. Hannah used to warm the wipes in her palm while she got Micah's diaper off. I'd walk June around the downstairs until I couldn't feel my feet. When the feeling came back it hurt so much I'd do another lap before trying to stand still. Neither of us had ever been rough with either of them. There were nights one of us had to say, “Take her for a second,” and the other would come over with both hands out.

I got laid off in August. They let me leave through the loading bay so I could skip walking past everybody with my lunch bag. By the time the red-bordered envelopes started coming, I'd learned to open them with my thumb without letting Micah see. He'd been looking for a letter from his grandma. He knew she'd died, but a card from her had come a few days afterward and he figured she'd sent more.

Hannah lost her mom in the second trimester. We lost our place in town and moved to her late grandmother's farmhouse nine miles out. The only neighbors were a mile down the road. The first afternoon there, Hannah opened the kitchen drawer where her grandma used to keep the rubber bands from the newspaper. She stood looking at one that had dried up until I took the moving box out of her hands.

June was born six weeks early by emergency C-section and spent three weeks in the NICU. Micah and I went to the vending machines while Hannah sat by the incubator. Once he picked pretzels because he thought they'd be easier for June to eat than chips. I said she couldn't have them yet, and he asked me to save them for her. They were still in the glove box when the first snow came.

When June came home, Hannah started keeping a notebook by the rocker. At two in the morning she'd record each feeding down to the ounce, what June had actually taken instead of what we'd put in the bottle. I can tell which week a page is from by the size of her writing. By January she could fit two feedings on the same line.

Hannah's incision kept opening at one end. If I saw her wince getting up, she'd send me for something that was already within reach. That was her way of getting a minute to look at the dressing by herself. Once I came back too fast with a clean shirt and she had the old one rolled up in her fist. She said, “I wish my mom could see how long June's eyelashes got.” I sat next to her with the shirt on my lap, grateful she'd said something I could agree with.

June liked the sound the bread bag made. She'd turn her head toward Micah when he said her name, and he'd move a little farther away each time to see how far she'd turn. At eleven every night she'd start screaming. She kept it up until three or four, and if you held her you could feel the effort through your shirt. Sometimes I'd get her coat out to take her in again, and by the time I got a sleeve on she'd gone soft with exhaustion. They hadn't found what hurt her the last time, either.

Hannah brought the notebook and a video to the doctor's because June could go a whole appointment making little satisfied noises with her fist in her mouth. The doctor asked if either of us got any sleep. Hannah said, “He does the first part of the night,” and I said, “She sleeps when the baby sleeps.” In the car she asked when I thought the baby slept. I drove most of the way home before I said I didn't know.

Micah started wetting the bed around then. We put a folded towel under his sheet so I could get him into a dry bed quicker. The first night I did it he held the corner while I tucked the towel in and said he was sorry I had to go to work in the morning. I hadn't had a job in months. I said I was right where I wanted to be, and when I asked him to scoot over he made me about six inches of space.

Some nights Micah would sit straight up in the dark with his eyes open, crying so hard he couldn't hear me. In the morning he remembered enough to ask whether he'd woken June. He always wanted to know which of us had come in. If I said it had been his mother, he'd ask how she'd managed with June in her arms.

We knew a storm was coming. I brought in enough wood for a day and left the rest in a heap under a tarp. When I went out to drag the bins back up the drive, the neighbor's dog barked. He did it every night. You'd hear six or seven barks, then a pause while he waited to find out if anybody cared.

By two-thirty I was on the kitchen floor with June against my chest. I'd sat down because my sock had twisted under my toes and I couldn't get it right without using a hand. Hannah had gone upstairs to change her dressing. I heard the bathroom pipes knock when she turned on the faucet. I'd been meaning to get up there with a wrench.

June got enough air to make a fresh start, and I felt the back of her head go hard against my palm. I looked at the black window over the sink. I could see the kitchen reflected in it, and my own knees sticking up on either side of her. I was thirty-six. I hadn't prayed since I was a boy. I said it out loud anyway. “Please. Anybody. Please just make it stop.”

Upstairs on the bathroom tile, with the water running so I couldn't hear, Hannah was saying nearly the same thing. She told me later she'd said “anybody” too. I remember the first snow against the glass because for a second I thought something from the stove had drifted up past it. About a minute after I spoke, June stopped in the middle of a scream. Her mouth was open a little longer than there was sound. Then I felt her cheek settle against me.

June took small breaths that moved the collar of my shirt. I carried her upstairs and met Hannah on the landing. She put her hand on June's back and smiled at me when she felt it move. I hadn't seen her face let go like that in weeks.

We slept six hours. Micah came in after nine and told us he'd tried to get two bowls down but one was stuck inside the other. Hannah handed me my glasses from the nightstand. There's a version of me who feels something wrong as soon as June's mouth goes quiet, and I know how easy it would be to write that version. I was grateful. I sat on the floor with my face in my daughter's hair and thought somebody had finally done us a favor.

The next night I was downstairs when I heard the long shush. June had been crying on the monitor while I scraped dinner off a plate. There was the little pop the speaker made when she got too loud, and then underneath it, at a steady volume, “Shhhh.” I stopped scraping. The sound kept going until I'd taken the time to put the plate down and listen properly, and it went on after June had gone quiet.

I went up expecting to find Hannah trying something the nurse had shown her. The nursery was empty. The crib sheet still had a shallow crease from where June had been lying, and the rocker was facing the wrong way because I'd shoved it aside to plug the monitor in that afternoon. There was frost on the inside of the window. When I rubbed it with my thumb it came off in wet grains.

A long way back from the house, where the yard runs into the trees, somebody was standing with their hands folded in front of them. Our light only reached that far because of the snow. I remember thinking it was like seeing an usher at a funeral who'd come out of the building to wait for you. It took me a while to work out what bothered me. The old fence posts each had a little white cap, even the thin one I'd broken with the mower. Every flake I could see near that person went past.

Hannah called from our room. She was sitting up with June against her. She'd brought her in when I went downstairs, and June had kept crying right up until Hannah heard the shush. I asked if she'd been shushing her. Hannah said she'd been trying to get her burp cloth out from between the bed and the wall. She thought I'd been trying to settle Micah.

I went down to the back door in case someone had got stuck on the road and come up through the field. I flashed the outside light twice and opened the door enough to see the porch steps. The snow was level with the second one. Upstairs I told Hannah I'd seen somebody at the trees. She asked if I'd seen them leave.

In the morning the wood was stacked on the porch, with kindling lined up on the sill so it wouldn't roll. I thanked Hannah while she was buttering Micah's toast. She told me she'd heard me go out the night before. It took another minute to figure out what we were thanking each other for. I went back out and saw three of the pieces I'd left under the tarp because they'd hurt my wrist to lift.

The bottles were washed and standing in order of height, with every nipple turned the same way. I'd always put the short ones nearest the sink so I could reach them one-handed. Hannah thought I'd got mad about a mess and cleaned the whole room. I thought she'd done it while I was at the porch. Then we found the sleepers.

They were in the top drawer, folded into squares about the size of matchbooks. I tried to shake one loose and it stayed folded. Hannah worked a thumbnail into the middle of it and pulled, carefully at first because she'd liked that sleeper since June finally got long enough to wear it. She got one of the feet out and the cloth around it was white at the creases. There were six more in the drawer. We both stood there looking at how much work it would have taken.

Before dark I brought the receiver down to see what it did with June in the living room. I put my hand over the speaker to see if it ran hot. As soon as I lifted my palm, a voice said, “I heard you. I came as quick as I could. It's a long way.”

It spoke so softly I reached for the volume wheel. There was a space between each thought, enough time for somebody on the other end to say they'd been worried. When I didn't speak, it went on. “You asked so nicely. Nobody has asked in such a long time.” Hannah came in holding June and asked who was calling. I moved my hand so she could see what I was touching.

By dark it was on the porch, a foot or so off the mat. Up close, the fold of one hand over the other was too low. I tried standing that way afterward, and no matter how much I dropped my shoulders I couldn't get my fingers that far down my legs. Through the glass I got a pale stretch of something where I thought a cheek ought to be. It was looking over my shoulder at Hannah.

“You're so tired. I can hear how tired you are,” it said. I put my thumb on the dead bolt to make sure it was turned all the way. It watched me do that without moving closer. “Let me have a turn with her. I'm very good at quiet.” The part I can still hear best is the tiny, almost pleased lift on the word “turn,” as if it thought that was the thing we'd both been waiting for someone to offer.

The landline was dead. I'd tried the truck that afternoon and got far enough to see where the snow had drifted over the cut between the banks. It had taken twenty minutes to back it up to the house. Now I was trying to work out how far Hannah could walk and what I'd do with both kids when she couldn't go any farther.

I told Hannah I'd try the neighbors. They had a tractor and might have a way to reach somebody. I went for my socks and found Micah on the bottom step with his boots on the wrong feet. He'd heard that I was going. While I took them off I told him his mom needed him to show her how to open the applesauce without spilling it. He thought about it and said I was the one who usually spilled it. I kissed the heel of his foot before I put him back down.

I checked the back porch before I left by the front. It was empty. I got as far as the mailbox before I realized I'd been waiting to hear Roy. He should've heard me breaking through the crust, even at a mile. I had to follow the fence to tell where the ditch started. I remember how much better I felt once I picked out their porch light.

Their front door was wide open and heat came out past me into the snow. I yelled for Ellen, then went in with my boots on because I thought somebody might have fallen. An oxygen tank stood at the kitchen table with a week's worth of pill organizers, all the lids closed. Ellen had looked after her dying husband for two years. The last time I saw her she'd bought ice cream and told me she'd almost put it back because it was the first thing she'd put in the cart for herself.

I found them in the bedrooms. Ellen and her husband were in the big one, on their backs with the blankets up to their chins. Each of them had their hands folded on top. Ellen's nails were clean all the way around. I remember it because we sometimes talked while she pulled weeds out of her drive and her nails always had dirt at the corners. Their grown daughter was in the little room across from them, tucked in the same way. Roy was at the foot of her bed, wrapped in a bath towel. The tip of one ear had been folded into the towel and when I lifted it away, the ear stayed bent.

I touched Ellen first because I knew her best. I managed to say her name normally, twice. When I couldn't feel anything under her jaw I reached for the bedside phone. I had it up to my ear before I noticed the line was coiled beneath it. The plug at the end had been tucked into the middle of the coil to keep it from coming undone. I got up and went to look for the phone in the kitchen. I don't know what I thought was different about that one.

I saw the visitor when I got back out onto the road. It had stopped a little beyond the end of the drive, pointed toward my house. I went past it at a walk and made it as far as the first fence post before I heard it follow. Every time I stopped to get my foot out of a drift, it stopped too. I couldn't hear it breathing. I could hear both of its feet come down in the snow as plainly as I could hear my own.

“She asked too,” it said. I thought at first it was going to tell me what she'd asked for, and I waited even though I'd already started walking faster. “She was so tired. They're resting now. Did you see how quiet it is there?” It let several steps go by. “I can make it that quiet for you.”

I thought about Hannah's feet in those socks with the grips she'd brought home from the hospital. How many times would she have to set June down to get her boots on? It let me go up the steps first, and I slammed the door so hard the latch bounced. Hannah was at the stairs. I told her what I'd found and had to say it again because I kept saying “their hands” in the places that needed names.

The next time I saw it was after a bad night. I went down to the mudroom because I could smell cold air with the stove still going. It stood between my boots and Micah's, its head lowered to fit under the shelf with the hats on it. I got as far as the light switch before I saw its hands.

That day I nailed boards across the back door. I left the front one usable because I still meant to get my family out of there. Hannah took some salt out of the cupboard and ran a line across the mudroom sill, and when she went to look for her grandmother's Bible I held June and stayed where I could watch the salt. I was looking right at it late that afternoon, hearing Hannah read upstairs in the voice she used to read to Micah, when the visitor said, from beside the refrigerator, “You don't have to be frightened.” There wasn't a speck of salt on the kitchen floor. “I didn't come for anything. I came because you asked.”

I got Hannah's grandfather's shotgun out of the closet while she kept Micah upstairs. In the kitchen I waited for the visitor to do something that would make pulling the trigger feel like the next thing to do. It said, “You don't have to watch. You can lie down, and when you wake up it will be so still.” I kept thinking I could shoot it and it would stand right there, waiting politely for me to put the gun down.

The next day I walked up to the rise where I sometimes got a cell signal. I tried till I couldn't feel the glass under my thumb. When I got back, Hannah had packed a bag, with clean underpants in the outside pocket where Micah could get them himself. I watched her zip it after I told her about the signal. We left it beside the front door.

The smoke alarm had been chirping on and off. One night I was getting Micah a drink and I muttered that I wished the damn thing would quit. It was the kind of thing you say into a cupboard while you look for a cup. In the morning there was a white circle on the ceiling where it had been, and Hannah found it wrapped in a dish towel in the drawer under the counter. I put it back up. It started chirping again almost as soon as my hand came away, and that morning I was glad to hear it.

After that, it stood at the foot of the stairs. Sometimes it spoke while I was walking June, and I would lose track of which room I'd meant to go into and have to stop and look down at my own feet. “I don't sleep,” it told me once. “I can hold her all night. I can hold her for always.” On the word “always,” it sounded like somebody who'd just remembered they didn't have work in the morning and could stay a little longer than they'd planned.

Hannah said she'd give anything for Micah's night terrors to end. That night I found him lying on his back, asleep with his eyes open. From the hall light I could see the pink mark over his eyebrow where he'd bumped the nightstand. Usually some part of him moved in his sleep, a finger working at his pajamas or a heel sliding on the sheet. I had to touch his stomach to feel anything move at all.

At breakfast he asked if we could take the towel out from under his sheet. I asked if he'd had his bad dream and he said he'd started to. “The Shh sat on my chest so it couldn't get out,” he said. He put the bottoms of both hands flat against his own ribs to show me. Then he told me I could go ask the Shh to help me sleep too. He said it in the same voice he'd once used to tell me there was another cookie in his pocket if I wanted it.

I got both kids into our room that evening. Micah had fallen asleep across the foot of the bed and June was working up to crying in my arms. I had one hand under each of them, trying to get Micah far enough up the mattress to pull the covers over him. From the hall the visitor said, “Which one is the heavy one? I can take the heavy one. Or I can take them both, and then your hands would be free.” I slid my hand out from under Micah and shut the door. Micah slept with his toes sticking out until Hannah came in and covered them.

We tried to stop saying things around the house. Instead of asking Hannah for something I'd stand there waiting for her to see what I was looking at. If I caught myself thinking about June going quiet, I'd start naming things I could see. Plate, chair, window. Then I'd be looking straight at the chair where I wanted somebody else to sit with my daughter for one damn minute. I tried closing my eyes. It was worse with my eyes closed.

I told Hannah about my prayer in the kitchen. I wanted her to hear it from me, because I'd started to believe the visitor might say the words back to her in my voice, and I couldn't bear to let it go first. I got as far as “anybody” before Hannah asked me what time I'd done it. I said I didn't know exactly, but she'd been in the bathroom. She put her hands flat on the counter and said, “That's what I said. I said anybody too.”

I had the idea I could claim it as mine and she could get up and walk away from it. Instead I asked her to sit with me. We got down against the cabinet where I'd prayed and I took her weight for the last few inches. She put her forehead against my cheek. I hadn't held her that close in months. There was usually a baby between us, or one of us had to get up, or I was too busy remembering the last kind thing she'd said to me to say anything kind myself.

We made an agreement down there. Somebody would be awake all the time. We'd wake each other before we thought we needed to, and if one of us couldn't speak without asking for help, we'd put a hand on the other's shoulder. We told Micah that if he wanted something he could come to us, even if we'd been cross with him that day, even if his bed was wet. I remember him waiting to see if there was going to be one where he couldn't come. Hannah told him he could wake us for a glass of water even if he already had one and just didn't want to drink it by himself.

For two nights the visitor stopped shushing June. She screamed six hours both nights, and it stood at the top of the stairs with its hands folded. During the first night I carried her past it to take her downstairs and felt it lean back enough to give me room. “I'll wait,” it said, so close that I stopped for a second with one foot on the step below me. “I'm good at waiting. You'll ask again. They always ask again.”

The second night I went to settle Micah. He'd heard June and come out, and afterward he lay with his knees pulled up and his hands together between them. I stayed till he let one leg slide down. When I went back, Hannah set June in my arms and bent until her forehead rested on the changing table. We got her to morning. It mattered to us that we'd done it twice.

On the third night I went down in the upstairs hall on my way to get Hannah. I remember putting my hand on the wall because I was having trouble getting one foot in front of the other. The next thing I remember about that stretch of hall is how the seam in the carpet felt against my face. I heard Hannah call to me from the nursery. I meant to say that I'd be there in a minute. I don't know if I said it.

Hannah told me what happened in that room. She told me twice, a little differently each time. The first time she said June was turning purple, she'd been screaming so hard, and the second time she said the color was mostly around her mouth. I think she'd spent the time in between trying to be fair to what she'd actually seen. It was the fifth hour. When she shifted June to the other arm she felt the wet part of her shirt stick to her incision.

She'd been up with that baby since they cut her out. She'd let nurses do things she had to let nurses do, and she'd let me take June when I could stand up longer than she could. Even then she watched the handoff until she could see where I'd put my hands. In ten weeks I'd never seen her put June down and take her eyes off her at the same time.

When Hannah looked up, it was in the doorway with its arms out. “Let me. Just for a minute,” it said. “You can sit down. I'll give her right back. I've helped so many tired mothers.” Hannah said it tipped its head toward the rocker, and that was when she remembered she could sit. She'd been standing long enough that she'd forgotten the chair was there. She told me she'd thought, very plainly, that somebody had come because she'd needed somebody to come.

She showed me afterward how it took June. She held her forearm out in front of her and put her other hand up behind the place where June's head would have been. She said it brought that long hand up under June's neck before she had to move a single finger. “There now. Shhhh,” it said. Hannah sat down in the rocker. The last thing she remembered was having one foot still planted farther out than the other, ready to push herself back up.

I woke and lay still for a while, trying to understand what had changed. I could hear air coming out of the register at the end of the hall. That sound had been there all winter and I hadn't heard it upstairs once at night. From the nursery door I could see Hannah in the rocker, mouth slack and chin down. It was the first time in a year I'd seen her sleep without some part of her face drawn up.

June was in the crib. She'd been swaddled so tightly I could see the shape of her heels through the cloth. The two corners at the top were even, with a crease straight down the middle. Her hands were out, folded together on top of the blanket. I could see the tip of her thumb. Usually she tucked it inside her fist, and I'd let her get hold of my finger because I worried she was digging her nails into herself. Now I could see the whole nail, clear as anything in that light.

There were three steps between me and the crib. I knew I should put my hand on her chest. I stood there long enough for the spot where the carpet had rubbed my cheek to start pulsing. I could have touched her as lightly as Hannah had on the landing, the night we prayed. I told myself she'd wake and it would all start again. Then I went to bed. I slept nine hours.

I told the sheriff I checked on June. I told Hannah I'd put my hand on her and she'd been breathing. I said it the first time when she was still holding June and looking at me for something she could believe. I've had the chance to tell the truth since then. Here it is. I stood in the doorway and I chose the quiet.

Hannah's scream was what woke me. I heard it twice. The first time I thought she was calling me to take a turn, and I got mad because it was light and I thought I'd already done mine. Upstairs she had the blanket open and was trying to bring June around, saying her name with every breath. When I took June from her I kept telling myself to remember how small her chest was. I'd learned what to do beside a plastic baby in a class at the hospital, and the woman who taught us had said we could ask her to show us as often as we needed. I kept thinking of that plastic chest giving the same amount every time.

I kept talking. I said, “I checked her, I was right here. She's just cold. Get the other blanket, the warm one.” I said it so fast that the last part came out all in one sound and Hannah asked me to say it again. By then she had both blankets in her hands, and for a few seconds I wanted her to pick which one I'd asked for. My throat hurt afterward like I'd swallowed something too big. I remember that better than I remember sitting down.

The plow took two more days to get through. I went back out to try my phone, and by the time I got a bar the calls still wouldn't go through. The first person up the drive was a county man in a jacket with his name sewn on it. I stopped him before he'd got his door open all the way. I got out the part about Ellen's house before I could tell him what was wrong at ours.

A doctor used the words “sudden” and “unexplained.” He said it was nobody's fault. He had a blue pen in his hand and at some point he stopped writing and turned it over, so the tip was pointed back at him. He did that while he waited for me to answer whether I'd noticed any change in her breathing. I gave him the answer I'd given the sheriff. Later they put down what happened to Ellen and her family to a faulty furnace. I thought about the heat coming out of that open door and Roy's ear folded into the towel. I didn't tell anybody about his ear.

Women from a church we'd never been to brought casseroles. One had put a piece of tape on the lid with her name and another with the oven time. At the sink I caught myself trying to peel the label off without tearing it. Somebody was going to want that dish back. I knew how to deal with that.

The house was so quiet I heard the woman who brought it drive all the way down to the turn. There used to be a certain hour when I could stand at the kitchen sink and close my eyes and know I'd have three or four more hours of June crying before I got to close them again. I stood there at that hour the first night after the plow came through. I could have heard the smoke alarm chirp from the far end of the house. This was exactly what we'd asked for.

It was still there. It stood just inside the hall while I put the clean casserole dish on the counter. I heard a little breath before it spoke, the sort of breath somebody takes before asking a question they've been trying hard to work out for themselves. “She isn't crying anymore. I did it just the way you asked. Why are you still so tired?”

Hannah was sitting on the stairs when it said that. The next time it spoke to her directly, I was upstairs trying to get her to take a cup of water. It bent toward her until I could see the top of its head beyond her shoulder. “Is it you now? Are you the one that hurts? I can help with that too.” I got between them and spilled some of the water on her knee. She looked down and pinched the wet fabric away from her skin as though that was the only thing either of us had done.

Hannah stopped speaking after she told me the second time how she'd handed June over. Then she stopped eating. I could get her to drink a little if I put the mug where she could reach it without getting up. I had a place lined up for us in town and told her I'd take whatever I could fit in the truck and come back for the rest. She got up and brought me the feeding notebook. I put it in the bag with our papers because I thought if she was choosing what to take, she was coming.

That night I heard her through the wall. It was one word, so faint I had to sit up to make sure. “Please.” By the time I put my feet down I knew where she'd gone. I could see the strip of light under the nursery door the whole way across the hall.

She was in the rocking chair with June's blanket drawn up to her chin. Her hands lay folded on top of it. The blanket was too small to reach all the way to her feet. One of her socks had slipped down around her heel, and I pulled it up before I touched her face. I wanted to do something for her while there was still a chance she might feel it. She looked rested. I stayed there until I heard Micah get up to pee, and then I went out and pulled the door shut behind me so he could get to the bathroom without seeing.

It's me and Micah now. He asks me things during the day and I answer the ones I can. I've learned to get through the rest with my teeth together. At night I cry on the bathroom floor where Hannah sat, with the faucet running. Yesterday he asked why I go in there to be sad when he can still hear me from his bed.

We're supposed to go back to town in the morning. One of the church women gave us a room. I brought him back for clothes before dark and he fell asleep while I was packing, right there by the bag. I told myself I'd let him sleep a little and we'd leave when he woke up. I lifted him into bed and put his hand on the stuffed rabbit so it would be where he expected when he woke up. I very nearly sat down beside him with my coat on. I came downstairs because I wanted to get this written somewhere he wouldn't be able to hear me.

It's three in the morning. I'm at the kitchen table. Whatever you are carrying, carry it. Do not say “anybody.” It can be the middle of the night and you can be certain you've kept your voice down. I was talking to a window.

The monitor is still plugged in on the counter. I put my hand on the plug once and heard a little bit of ordinary room noise upstairs, just the sound of air moving, and I couldn't pull it. I can hear something moving now, a soft drag, like cloth going over the carpet. The other half of the monitor is still in June's room. Micah must have got out of bed. I heard him take a breath the way he does before he says something he's practiced. “Please make Daddy stop crying.”

There are footsteps on the stairs now. He's four. He loves me and he's been lying awake listening to me through the wall, and it was nice to him when he was scared. I can hear what he heard in it. The steps have reached the kitchen doorway behind me and stopped. I'm still typing. From right there, in the same voice you would use to tell a child that everything's been taken care of, it says, “Shhhh. He asked so nicely. Don't you worry about the boy. I'll stay with him. I like the small ones.”
