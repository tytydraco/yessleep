I didn’t want to post this anywhere public, and if my office IT guy finds out I made an account on a work computer, I’m going to hear about it for the rest of my natural life. But I need to put this somewhere before I start gaslighting myself into thinking I’m just tired, or lonely, or too new to rural pathology and therefore gullible in a uniquely embarrassing way.  
  
For context: I’m 29, recently board-certified, and very much the youngest person in a county office that still keeps a microwave labeled “FOR STAFF USE ONLY” because somebody once defrosted a raccoon in it. Stuartsville is a small Appalachian town with one traffic light, four churches, two funeral homes, and enough rumors to qualify as an ecosystem.  
  
I took the job because student loans are predatory, because I wanted to get out of the city, and because the medical examiner here retired suddenly after “health reasons,” which everyone in town says with the kind of tone that means don’t ask. I am now two and a half weeks into the position, and I have officially seen things I cannot explain in a way that satisfies me.  
  
The first oddity was small enough to ignore.  
  
On my third day, I autopsied an elderly man found in a shallow creek bed behind a ridge road. Drowning was the presumptive cause, though he had a "bad heart" and the water was only shin-deep where they found him. Standard stuff. Except when I opened the body bag, there was mud under his fingernails in neat half-moons, like he’d been digging his way to something.  
  
That, by itself, could have been defensive injury, or simple human instinct, maybe even digging for nightcrawlers along the bank. But the mud was dry. Not dried out, exactly. Dry as if it had never been wet. Almost like flakey clay.  
I documented it, took some photos, and mentioned it in the report.   
  
My deputy, Ruth, looked at my notes and said, very calmly, “Don’t write that in the final copy.”  
  
I asked why.  
  
She gave me a look I cannot adequately describe except to say it was the look my mother used to give me when I tried to tell her that the noise under my bed was “probably a raccoon.” Ruth is in her sixties, wears cat-eye glasses on a chain, and has been in this office longer than I’ve been alive. She said, “Because folks get superstitious when you say a body had to dig itself out.” I laughed, because that is objectively ridiculous. She did not laugh back. I dismissed it as some folk legend that comes from years of isolation and endless gossip.  
  
I never found a reason he would be in that water. Just the bloody froth in the airways of a drowning victim. No MI, no stroke. Whatever. Stranger deaths have happened before. I wrote down drowning and accident on the form.  
  
The second oddity was that same evening, when the funeral home called and asked if we could “please, for the love of God, stop sending them cases with their mouths open.” Figured they were joking, but their tone said otherwise. They hung up in the three seconds of silence before I could form a response.  
  
Ruth explained, in the tone people use to explain why you never answer a ringing phone at 3:13 a.m, that sometimes the dead here arrive “unsettled.” Not a lot of them, sure. But enough that the undertakers notice. Lips pulled back and eyes not fully closed. Hands with their fingers curled like they’re holding on to something invisible.  
  
“Do you mean rigor?” I asked.  
  
“Honey, I know what rigor is.”  
  
That should’ve been the end of it. Rural town, stressed undertakers, old people dying alone out in the hills. A few weird visual details and a staff full of folklore. I was ready to file it under local color and move on.  
  
  
  
Then last Friday, we got the case of Daniel Pike.  
  
Thirty-two year old, male, found dead in his truck at the edge of a logging road. Single gunshot wound to the chest, no weapon in the vehicle, no blood pattern consistent with a struggle. If I sound clinical, that’s because I was trying very hard not to think about the fact that I knew his sister from the grocery store.  
  
Her name is Marla. She has the kind of face that makes you trust her immediately. She brought me a mason jar of blackberries the day after I moved in and said, “We’re glad to have a young doctor.” I remember that because I had just spent an hour crying in my car over a dead possum and a parking ticket, so the kindness almost wrecked me.  
  
Marla identified the body before I made the incision. She was quiet until I told her we would need to wait on toxicology. Then she put both hands on the counter and said, “He told me he was hearing somebody outside the house.” I asked when.  
  
She said, “Since Mama died.”  
  
That’s not especially unusual in grief, so I asked a few standard questions. Sleep? Substance use? Hallucinations? Depression? She shook her head every time.  
  
Then she said, “Not hearing voices. Just... knocking. Always three times.”  
  
I wrote that down. Everything in this job gets written down.  
  
The next part is where I started feeling deeply, profoundly stupid.  
  
When I examined Daniel, I found three circular bruises on the inside of his left wrist. They were spaced evenly, about the size of coins, and each one had a faint pale center, as if something had pressed there from the inside.  
  
I asked Ruth to look.  
  
She went still.  
  
“What?” I said.  
  
She didn’t answer right away. Then she reached out and turned Daniel’s wrist over, very gently, like she expected the skin to bruise under her finger. “Did Marla say anything else?”  
  
“No.”  
  
Ruth swallowed. “Did she say the knocking stopped?”  
  
I checked my notes. No.  
  
I asked if she knew something she hadn’t told me. She said only, “Don’t let anybody bury him before sunrise.”  
  
That got my attention.  
  
I told her she was being ridiculous. She told me I was being young, which is a worse insult than it sounds when said by someone who genuinely likes you.  
  
We argued until the sheriff came in, at which point Ruth went quiet and the sheriff—who is built like a feed store doorframe and has the emotional range of a laminate countertop—asked if Daniel had family arrangements yet. I said no. He nodded once. “Good."  
  
The autopsy itself was unremarkable in almost every way that matters. Gunshot wound, hemorrhage, no exit. The bullet had passed through the second right intercostal space at the sternal border. Partially severed the bronchus and ascending aorta. At least he didn't suffer long. There was soot in the wound track, close-range discharge. Suicide was possible, though messy. Homicide was possible, though there was no weapon. The only thing that kept me from settling into the nice, familiar logic of death was what I found when I opened the stomach.  
  
There was a mouthful of dirt in it.  
  
It wasn't perimortem, or even premortem for that matter. It was packed, deliberate, pushed down the esophagus without being swallowed or aspirated. The grit had embedded plant fibers I didn’t recognize. I sent a sample to the lab and stared at it long enough that my eyes started to ache.  
  
Ruth came in while I was still standing over the table.  
  
“Don’t tell me,” she said.  
  
“There’s dirt in his stomach.”  
  
She nodded like I had told her the weather. “Then he was trying to get back in.”  
  
“To where?”  
  
She looked at the body.  
  
Then at me.  
  
“Depends,” she said. “Did you ever hear what they say happens when somebody dies on a road cut before they’re baptized?”  
  
I told her no.  
  
She said, “Town lore says the mountain doesn’t always let go.”  
  
I wasn't sure what to make of that. I shook it off and wrote down my preliminary report.  
  
Immediate cause of death: Exsanguination due to perforating gunshot wound to the chest  
  
Manner of death: Unknown  
  
  
  
At 2:14 that morning, the office phone rang.  
  
I know because I checked the call log three times afterward, thinking maybe I’d misread it in the dark. No caller ID, just a recorded line from the county phone tree, except the voice on the other end wasn’t the automated message.  
  
It was Daniel Pike.  
  
I am aware how that sounds. I also know what voice recordings sound like when they’re damaged, when a phone echos down a truck cab, when someone is crying and speaking through mucus and shock. This was not that.  
  
He said, very quietly, “Please don’t let her cover my face.”  
  
Then the line went dead.  
  
I did not sleep after that.  
  
The next afternoon, Marla came back to the office asking if she could see her brother one more time. The funeral home had already received the body, but she said something felt wrong. I asked what she meant. She stood there for a long second and said, “I keep hearing him at the back door.”  
  
I probably should not have asked this, but I did: “What does he say?”  
  
She stared at me with bloodshot eyes and said, “He says he can’t come in until somebody names him right.”  
  
That sentence sat between us like a dropped scalpel.  
  
I don’t think I believed in anything supernatural before Stuartsville. I’m an evidence person. I like test results. I like reproducible findings. I like things that don’t rearrange themselves the second you turn your head.  
  
But the following morning, when I signed Daniel’s paperwork, I noticed something I had not seen the night before.  
  
His full name on the intake form was Daniel Pike.  
  
But on the lower corner of the page, in graphite, as if written by the pressure of a fingertip on the next sheet underneath, there were four words:  
  
DON’T BURY MY MOUTH  
  
I checked every paper in the stack. The message didn’t repeat. It was only on that one form. I showed Ruth.  
  
She looked at it for a very long time.  
  
Then she said, “Well. That’s newer than usual.”  
  
I asked her what that meant, and she finally told me the thing nobody in this town seems eager to say out loud:  
  
There are people in Stuartsville who believe the mountain keeps what it takes unless you send it away properly. That bodies can be “caught” if they die wrong. That names matter. That if you don’t do the burial right, if you cover the face too soon, or forget a prayer, or put the dead in the ground without “giving back what was borrowed”, the person doesn’t always stay buried.  
  
She said all of this in the same tone a sane person might use to explain local flood patterns.  
  
Then she looked at me and added, “And before you ask: no, I do not know whether it’s true. I only know the old cases that were handled right don’t make people nervous.”  
  
I had a decision to make.  
  
As an ME, I’m not supposed to take instructions from folklore. As a person who had just received a phone call from a dead man about his face, I was also not in a position to be brave in a clean, procedural way. So I did the only thing I could think of.  
  
I called Marla back.  
  
I told her to come to the funeral home before dusk, and to bring a clean handkerchief, her bible (everyone has at least one around here), and any photo of Daniel where his face was fully visible. She didn’t ask why. She just said, “I knew you’d understand.” I did not understand.  
  
But at 7:06 p.m., in the back prep room of Mercer & Sons Funeral, Marla stood on one side of the table and I stood on the other while the always cheerful undertaker waited with his hands folded.  
  
We uncovered Daniel’s face.  
  
His eyes were still closed.  
  
His mouth was open by maybe a centimeter, just enough to show the line of his teeth.  
  
Marla made a sound I can only call a gasp if you’ve never heard someone realize their brother is not entirely where he should be.  
  
Because something was in his mouth.  
  
Something dark and folded neatly behind his teeth, like a little strip of wet bark or cloth.  
  
The undertaker stepped back so hard he hit the cabinets.  
  
I wanted to run. Instead I leaned in, because apparently curiosity is a pathology all its own.  
  
The thing in Daniel’s mouth shifted.  
  
It looked, impossibly, like a thumbprint in mud.  
  
And then *knock, knock, knock*.  
  
Three times.  
  
Not on the door.  
  
On the inside of the body bag.  
  
Marla screamed. The undertaker swore. I have never been so aware of my own heartbeat.  
  
The bag jerked once under Daniel’s body, as if something had exhaled.  
  
Then the dark shape in his mouth withdrew, smooth as an animal sliding into water, and the room went still.  
  
I do not know what happened there. I know what I saw. I know I am a rational adult with a medical degree and a board certification in forensic pathology, and I know I am also a person standing in a funeral home at dusk while a corpse sounds like it’s trying to answer a door.  
  
We buried Daniel at sunrise.  
  
Ruth insisted on it, though nobody could really tell me why that mattered except that everybody in the room seemed to agree it did. Marla put the handkerchief over his mouth herself. She cried the whole time, but when the casket lowered, she said something under her breath I couldn’t catch.

 

  
  
I haven’t had another strange case since then. Not officially, anyway. Just the usual amount of town weirdness: a church lady who says the creek is speaking again, a boy with mud on his boots who swears he met his grandfather in the fog, a family that won’t cross the south bridge after dark.  
  
Ruth has started leaving me coffee with “STAY AWAY FROM THE HILLS” written on the cup in permanent marker.  
  
Maybe it’s all nonsense. Maybe Stuartsville is just one of those places where grief grows extra teeth, where local belief gets mistaken for pattern, where old stories stick to new people because they’re lonely and tired and listening too hard.  
  
Or maybe there really are places where the dead don’t leave cleanly.  
  
I don’t know.  
  
What I do know is this: if you ever hear knocking from the inside of a body bag, do not, under any circumstances, answer it.  
  
And if someone tells you not to bury the face before sunrise, consider taking them seriously.  
  
I’d really prefer to be wrong about all of this.  
  
But tonight, when I lock up the office, I’m going to check the bulletin board first.  
  
Because this afternoon, pinned under a stack of old missing-person notices, I found a note in handwriting I don’t recognize:  
  
YOU NAMED HIM WRONG
