The day I turned eighteen was the day I left home. It was the day I cut everyone out of my life and me out of theirs. I had a good life, an easy one. My mom was good to me, and I spent time regularly with my friends. I had everything I was supposed to want, but I didn’t want it anymore. Not for my sake, for theirs.

My dad passed away when I was fourteen, and every day since then I’ve felt alone in this world. Even with all my friends and family around me, there was a gaping hole in my mind that none of them could ever heal. A certain hell, custom-made just for me. Sometimes it would stay buried for weeks, even months, and then something would bring it back to the surface. Arguments, fights, long stretches of silence, and late nights alone in a dark room with his revolver cocked in my hand. It was my hell, but the people around me could see it too.

So I decided to leave.

I’d been planning the move for nearly two years. I saved whatever I could from every job I worked, cashing out each check on payday and putting a little more away. I bought the supplies I needed piece by piece, making sure I had everything I could think of before the day came. I knew my mom would call the police when she realized I was gone, but I would be eighteen. A legal adult. There wasn’t anything she could do to make me come back.

I did all the maintenance I could on my dad’s old Ford, the same truck he drove to work and used to pick me up from school. Eventually, I paid my mom to put it in my name. I spent nights working on it, making sure it could carry me wherever I decided to go. And then there were the other things I had to do, the things I couldn’t tell my mom about. I’d tell her I was going out with friends, then drive two towns over and steal license plates from cars parked in quiet neighborhoods. It was just another way to make myself harder to find.

I’d spent two years preparing for that night. Every dollar, every tool, every gallon of gas, every little detail had been part of the plan. I wanted to make sure that when I finally left, I wouldn’t have to come back.

The night I turned eighteen, I waited until the house was quiet. I snuck out the front door and carried everything I owned out to the truck. I threw my bags into the bed and realized the engine would wake my mom.

So I put the truck in neutral and pushed it.

I pushed just far enough out of the driveway and into the road, listening to the tires turn and groan against the pavement as I struggled to get it far enough away from the house. Once I was clear, I climbed back inside, turned the key, and listened to the engine come to life.

Then I left.

I cried as I drove. I didn’t say goodbye, I didn’t leave a note, and I never got that last hug from my mom. Nobody knew where I was going. Nobody even knew I was leaving. They were all just going to wake up in the morning and realize I wasn’t there anymore, as if I’d been wiped off the face of the universe while they slept.

But underneath the sadness was something else. Relief, maybe. Because they wouldn’t have to deal with me anymore. They wouldn’t have to watch me fall apart, worry about where I was, or wonder whether I was going to be okay. I could finally disappear from their lives and let them have the peace they deserved.

Maybe I was doing them a favor.

That was three months ago. By then, I’d gotten pretty used to life on the road. It had become a routine, almost without me realizing it. Wake up, drive, stop, eat, drive some more, stop again, eat again, and eventually find somewhere to sleep. The days all started to blend together.

Most of my savings were still tucked away in the glovebox. I barely spent any of it. Gas was really the only thing I consistently needed to pay for. For food, I’d mostly hunt or scavenge whatever I could find. Water was a little harder. I’d find a river or stream when I could, and on a good day, I’d get lucky enough to catch some rain. I bathed once or twice a week. I mean, I didn’t plan on anyone else smelling me anyway.

Every now and then, something on the truck needed fixing, but nothing major. I kept it running, and that was all that mattered.

I didn’t have a destination. I wasn’t trying to get anywhere in particular. I just drove. I’d stop whenever something caught my attention, stay for a while, then keep going when I got tired of looking at it. I knew I was far away from home by then. I’d crossed at least two state lines, though I couldn’t have told you exactly where I was most of the time. I never really cared enough to check.

But where I found myself now wasn’t anything like the places I’d been before.

It was a desert.

Well, it wasn’t all desert. Not at first, anyway. Every now and then I’d pass through a small town or some other little pocket of civilization, but the farther I drove, the more spread out they became. One town would turn into another, then another, until eventually there was nothing but miles of empty road between them.

Then even the towns disappeared.

The only sign of civilization for miles was a gas station sitting alone beside the highway. Hanging over the building was an old letter-board sign. The letters were faded and crooked, with a few of them hanging loosely from the board.

“LAST GAS STATION FOR 200 MILES. MAKE SURE YOU FILL UP!”

A big curved arrow flashed underneath it, pointing toward the building.

I looked down at the gauges on my dashboard. I was sitting just above a quarter tank.

I pulled into the empty lot and stopped beside one of the pumps. I turned the truck off and opened the door. It groaned as I climbed out, the old hinges complaining in the same tired way they always did. For a second, it sounded almost like the truck was talking to me.

*Let’s stop here for today.*

I ignored its cries and shut the door.

I looked around. It looked like nobody had stopped here in decades. The sun hung low in the sky, and I knew I had a little while longer before nightfall, but I’d need to hurry if I wanted to make at least a little progress across the desert.

I walked around to the passenger side and opened the glovebox to get some of my savings. As I reached inside, I looked up and something caught my eye.

A white flower.

It had trumpet-shaped petals, and there were several of them growing from a single stalk about two feet tall. Its leaves stretched almost as long as my forearm.

I wouldn’t have expected to see something like that out here.

I stared at it for another second before shaking my attention away and heading inside the gas station.

A small bell rang when I opened the door.

The inside was definitely in need of a renovation. Everything looked as if it had been sitting in the same spot for thirty years without being moved or touched once. Dust covered most of the shelves, and the fluorescent lights above me hummed faintly.

But I mean, if you own a gas station in the middle of nowhere, where you might get one customer a year, I guess it doesn’t really matter.

An older woman sat behind the counter, reading a newspaper that looked older than me. She barely looked up when I came in, as if I hadn’t come in at all.

I walked up to the counter.

“Excuse me,” I said, placing my hands on the counter. “I’m gonna need to fill up my truck.”

“The pump’s on, doll,” she said. Her voice alone told me she smoked. “Just come in when you’re ready to pay.”

“Thank you.”

She nodded and went back to her newspaper without another word.

I made my way back outside and started filling up the truck. I set the pump on automatic and leaned against the side of my one companion. It was slow. Extremely slow.

Eventually, my attention drifted back toward the flower. Its spiny leaves seemed almost inviting, beckoning me closer. I glanced back at the gas pump and then at my truck before walking over to it.

I knelt down and reached out, touching one of its soft, white petals with my thumb and forefinger. It was delicate, almost impossibly so. The petal brushed gently against my skin, soft enough that I barely felt it.

I sat there for another minute, just looking at it, before finally snapping out of whatever had come over me.

I turned my head toward the truck.

Gas was overflowing from the nozzle.

“Shit.”

I jumped up and ran back, pulling the nozzle from the tank and shutting off the pump. The side of my truck was soaked in gasoline.

With a frustrated sigh, I put the nozzle back and looked up at the building.

The woman was standing in the window.

She was perfectly still, her posture straight as a board. She didn’t move. She was just watching me.

Uneasy, I returned the nozzle to the side of the pump. When I turned back around, the woman was gone.

I walked slowly toward the door and opened it. The little bell above it chimed as I stepped inside.

“Ma’am?” I called out.

No response.

I heard something from the back of the store. Loud clattering, like someone tearing a room apart in a desperate search for something they couldn’t find.

“Ma’am?”

The clattering stopped.

I stood there, listening.

It was so quiet I could hear my own heartbeat in my ears. I wiped my sweaty hands against my jeans, the dirty denim doing a poor job of drying them.

Then her voice came from the back.

“I’m gon’ need you to leave, doll.”

Her voice was different this time. Grim.

I didn’t ask any questions. I took the warning and ran back to my truck.

The engine roared when I cranked it up. I threw it into drive and slid out of the parking lot, kicking up dust behind me as I put as much distance between myself and that gas station as I could.

My nerves eventually calmed, but I couldn’t stop thinking about what had happened. I hadn’t even paid for the gas. Had I done something wrong? Why had she been staring at me through the window? What was she searching for?

It grew dark, and I grew weary. I hadn’t seen another person since leaving the gas station, so I pulled over and decided to call it a day.

I turned off the truck. It gave one last grunt, almost as if to say, *Finally,* before settling into silence.

I turned sideways in the bench seat, my back against the door and my legs stretched out in front of me. I reached into my bag and pulled out a bottle of water, a book, and my last stick of beef jerky. Hopefully the rest of the drive wouldn’t be too bad tomorrow. I could find somewhere to restock on food.

Before long, my eyes felt heavy, and I was out for the count.

When I woke up, it was almost noon.

Something was off.

I checked myself and realized I was soaked in sweat. I guess that’s what sleeping in the desert gets you.

I rubbed my eyes and took a swig of water before sitting properly in the driver’s seat. I looked out through the windshield.

Something was caught underneath the wiper.

A piece of paper.

I opened the door and stepped out. The moment my feet hit the dirt, I noticed something on the side of the truck.

A symbol had been painted across the driver’s door in a dark, rusty red. It was roughly circular, but not quite. Three crooked lines curved inward toward a small, blackened-looking center, while thin strokes branched outward like roots—or veins. The paint had been applied unevenly, some parts thick and dripping while others looked as though they’d been dragged across the metal with a trembling hand.

I looked around. No footprints. No tire tracks except my own. No other vehicles on the road. Just the desert stretching in every direction, silent beneath the morning sun.

I touched the edge of the symbol with one finger. The paint was dry. Whoever had put it there hadn’t done it recently. My stomach tightened. 

I looked toward the road, then toward the empty desert behind me. I pulled the paper from beneath the windshield wiper.

I unfolded it.

In neat, elegant handwriting, the note read:

*One of our garden’s flowers told us about you.*

Something darted across the edge of my vision.

Before I could turn to look, something forced its way into my mouth. I gagged, choking on the sudden intrusion, and tried to pull away, but a sharp pain shot through my jaw as whatever it was yanked backward.

I fell hard against the ground. Before I could even begin to struggle, hands seized my arms and legs, pinning me flat against the dirt. There were too many of them. I twisted and kicked, trying to break free, but the hands only tightened their grip. A coarse, bristly rope stretched from my mouth to the ground, holding my head in place and forcing me to stare up at the cloudless sky.

I couldn’t move.

A thin figure stepped into view, his movements slow and deliberate. He wore a white shawl stained with dirt and smeared with black handprints. His skin was so completely black that, at first, I couldn’t tell where his clothing ended and his body began. It was as though someone had covered him from head to toe in charcoal.

But it was his mask that caught my attention.

It was made of polished chrome and covered his entire head, leaving only his jaw and mouth exposed. The metal reflected the desert sunlight, bright enough to make me squint. I couldn’t see his eyes, only the warped reflection of the ground and the frightened face staring back at me.

My face.

He crouched beside me until his mask was only inches from mine. When he spoke, his voice was deep and soft, almost gentle.

“You will do wonderfully in our garden.”

I stared into the mask, searching for something human behind the metal. My own eyes stared back at me, wide with fear, but something was wrong. Beneath them, where my mouth should have been, was a smile. His smile

His gums were blackened, almost rotten-looking, but his teeth were the cleanest, straightest, most perfect set I had ever seen. They gleamed in the sunlight, bright against the darkness of his mouth.

And all the while, my terrified eyes stared back at me from the chrome, framed by a smile that wasn’t mine.
