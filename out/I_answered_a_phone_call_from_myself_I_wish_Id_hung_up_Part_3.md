For a second neither of us moved.

The guy I had just stabbed opened his mouth. No words came out. His eyes found mine. They weren't angry. They were confused.

He staggered backward. The blade slid free.

Blood spread across his shirt faster than I thought was possible.

One of his friends screamed. The other grabbed him before he hit the ground.

"You fucking stabbed him!"

I couldn't answer. Because they were right. I looked down. The knife was in my hand.

One guy wrapped an arm around his friend’s shoulders. The other pressed both hands against the wound. Blood spilled between his fingers.

They stared at me with a mixture of fear and disbelief.

The tall man let out a low groan.

Then, almost together, they started dragging him back toward the riverwalk.

"Call 911!"

"Keep pressure on it!"

Their voices grew quieter as they disappeared toward the lights. I stood alone on the riverbank. The whole thing couldn't have lasted more than thirty seconds. It felt like an hour.

I looked at the knife. Blood dripped from the tip onto the rocks. 

I remembered the voicemail. ‘Tie the knife to something that floats...but weigh it down a little.’

The branch was still lying where I'd dropped it.

I don't know why I kept listening to the messages. Maybe because every time I'd ignored one, things had gotten worse.

My hands moved before my brain caught up. I threaded the shoelace around the knife handle. Wrapped it around the branch and pulled it tight. Good enough.

I bent down and picked up the first rock I could find. It wasn't very big. I stared at it. I tucked it under the shoelace and hurried toward the water.

The current was stronger than I'd realized.

I threw the branch as hard as I could. It landed with a splash about twenty feet from shore.

For a second it disappeared beneath the surface. Relief. Then the branch bobbed back up.

It floated. The rock wasn't heavy enough.

I watched helplessly as the current carried it downstream. The knife dipped below the surface every few seconds before rising again. It wasn't sinking.

I took two steps into the river before the current nearly knocked me off my feet.

The branch was already too far away.

I stood there, ankle-deep in freezing water, watching the only piece of evidence disappear into the darkness.

I almost laughed. The voicemail hadn't been wrong. I'd been wrong.

I turned and walked back toward downtown.

I noticed the blood halfway back to the hotel. Not on my hands but my shirt. Dark stains stretched across the front where the man had fallen against me.

I zipped my jacket all the way to my neck.

Every person I passed seemed to glance in my direction. Hair a mess. Jacket zipped up despite it not being too cold. Dirt on my pants. One shoe missing. Maybe they weren't looking. Maybe they were. Probably they were.

By the time I reached the hotel lobby, I'd convinced myself everyone knew. I looked guilty. The elevator ride felt longer than it had that morning.

When I reached my room, I locked the door, flipped the deadbolt, and pulled the curtains shut before I finally took off the jacket. The blood looked darker under the room lights. Almost black.

I turned on the TV for a distraction. Local news. I stripped off my clothes and turned the shower on as hot as it would go.

I scrubbed my hands until they were red.

Then I washed them again.

I couldn't smell blood anymore. Only soap. I stood there long after I was clean. Trying to think. It was self-defense. Wasn't it? They shoved me. One of them rushed me.

Why had I thrown away the knife? Why hadn't I called the police? Because somewhere between the voicemail and the bathroom trash can and the river I'd stopped acting like an innocent man.

When I came out of the shower the local news was halfway through the broadcast.

"...breaking tonight..."

A helicopter shot showed flashing police lights along the riverwalk. My stomach dropped.

The anchor talked about how a man was stabbed that evening near the river. Police said the victim was transported to a nearby hospital with serious injuries but is expected to survive.

I closed my eyes. Alive. Thank God.

The anchor said that the suspect left shortly after the altercation and is described as an unhoused.

I turned off the TV. The room went silent. I sat on the edge of the bed.

He was alive. Maybe everything would be okay.

I don't remember falling asleep. But my phone woke me. 12:01. Unknown number.

The hotel voicemail service.

For several seconds I just let it ring. Then I answered. 

My own voice spoke immediately. Calm. Matter-of-fact.

"No more gambling."

Click.

I stared at the phone. That was it. No explanation. No warning about the stabbing. Just ‘no more gambling.’

"What about the knife?" I said aloud to the empty room. "What about the guy I stabbed?"

The phone rang again.

I jumped.

For one terrifying second I thought the voicemail service was calling back. It wasn't. A different number. Unknown.

I let it ring.

It stopped.

Then started again.

Again.

Again.

Five calls in less than a minute.

Finally I answered without saying anything.

The voice on the other end laughed softly.

"I knew you’re there."

It was the same man from the call in the bathroom.

"We want our money, Michael."

My mouth went dry.

"You have one hour."

Another pause.

"Fourth Street Bridge."

The line went dead.

I looked at the clock. 12:05.

The voicemail had told me to stop gambling. Whoever these people were they already expected me to have the money. I actually had the winnings from my earlier bet. Just a little over two grand. 

I didn't bother trying to sleep again. Even after the phone stopped ringing. Even after the room went quiet. I just sat on the edge of the bed staring at the carpet.

Who were these people? How did they know my name? And why were they so certain I owed them money?

If paying them made this stop. Fine. I'd pay them.

I threw yesterday's bloody shirt into a plastic laundry bag from the hotel closet and tied it shut. I wasn't carrying that thing through the airport tomorrow. I'd find somewhere to throw it away after I met, whoever, under the Fourth Street Bridge.

I got dressed, grabbed my wallet, and left.

The Fourth Street Bridge wasn't hard to find. The river cut through downtown, and half the streets seemed to cross it eventually. The bridge itself was quiet. A few cars. No pedestrians. I walked underneath.

Concrete columns disappeared into darkness on either side. The sound of traffic echoed overhead.

I checked my phone. 1:02. Maybe I was early. I waited. Five minutes. Ten. Still nobody.

The phone rang. Unknown number. I answered immediately.

"I'm here."

Silence.

Then the same calm voice.

"No, you're not."

"I'm under the Fourth Street Bridge."

"No."

"I am."

"We've been waiting."

I turned in a slow circle.

The bridge. The river. The graffiti-covered columns. There was nowhere else to be.

"I don't know what to tell you," I said.

"We know where you are."

The line went dead.

I started to run to the hotel. Pack and get the hell out of here was all I thought.

Then my phone rang again. The voicemail service.

I wanted to ignore it. But I didn't.

My own voice sounded exhausted. Not frightened. Defeated.

"Give them the money." Heavy breathing. "They're not bluffing."

Click.

“I tried,” I shouted. The street was empty. Luckily.

I reached the next intersection just as my phone rang again. Another voicemail. I frowned.

This was the third one today.

I answered.

"Don't hide the knife."

My own voice again.

"You'll need it."

Click.

I stopped walking. The city suddenly felt very quiet. I looked down at my empty hands. Too late.

"I already got rid of it." I laughed. A short, bitter sound.

People across the street glanced in my direction. I didn't care.

One message had told me to throw the knife away. Another was telling me to keep it.

For the first time the messages were wrong. Or one of them was.

I stood there trying to make sense of it.

I couldn't. There wasn't anything to understand. The knife was gone. Whether that had been a mistake or not it couldn't be undone.

I spent the next few hour walking aimlessly through downtown. I kept expecting someone to step out of a doorway. Or grab me from behind. Or call my name.

Nothing happened.

Eventually I found a public trash can outside a convenience store. I looked around. Nobody was watching. I dropped the plastic bag containing my shirt into the bin.

For a second I considered digging it back out. Instead I turned and walked away.

By the time I reached the hotel the sky was beginning to lighten.

I hadn't slept. I hadn't eaten. I felt hollow.

I packed my suitcase without really thinking about what I was putting into it. Laptop. Phone charger. Toothbrush. The little souvenir snow globe I got my wife. I'd almost forgotten to get her something.

At exactly 9:01 that morning my phone rang. The voicemail service.

I answered automatically.

"Don't forget to bring your wife something from Chicago."

Click.

I looked down at the snow globe sitting in my luggage.

"I already bought one."

Another message from the service came fifteen minutes later.

Something about work.

A meeting I'd almost forgotten.

I made my way to the airport. Another voicemail came reminding me to email a client before I left.

The messages were now normal things. Almost comforting. It was as if, for a few hours, the nightmare had simply paused.

Then the boarding announcement echoed through the airport terminal. Passengers began lining up. I picked up my suitcase.

My phone rang one last time. Unknown number. The voicemail service.

I answered. There wasn't any panic in my voice. No shouting. Just one quiet, deliberate sentence. 

"Don't board the plane."
