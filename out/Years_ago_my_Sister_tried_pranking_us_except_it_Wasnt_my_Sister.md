I was raised in Indiana in a strictly traditional Hindu household. Like most Indian homes, ours had a tiny mandir carved out in a quiet alcove - a sanctum decorated with brass idols, brass thalis of vermilion and turmeric, smoldering incense, oil lamps, and a heavy, ornate mirror hung flat against the drywall directly behind the deities. My parents treated that space with an almost suffocating degree of reverence. We, being dumb kids, did not.



I was eleven when my two cousins came over to spend the weekend. My parents were preparing for an overnight haul to a family wedding in Los Angeles, which left my older sister in charge of babysitting the three of us. The catalyst for the worst night of my life was painfully ordinary - an ancient, whining family laptop and dial-up internet so slow it felt prehistoric. Bored out of our minds, my older cousin dug through some obscure, early-2000s occult forum and found the translation of a supposedly old Japanese blood ritual. It was clearly edgy teenage fan-fiction, full of dramatic lore and broken phrasing but to eleven year old boys, anything pulled from the web carried the weight of forbidden gospel. The instructions said of standing before a polished reflective surface in total stillness, piercing your fingertip, and chanting phrases to pierce the veil between rooms



Naturally, the largest mirror in the house was the one sitting behind the idols in our prayer room.



My cousin and I nicked a thick sewing needle from my aunt's embroidery tin. Standing shoulder to shoulder on the velvet rug, stifling nervous, hysterical snickers, we jabbed the tips of our index fingers until bright beads of crimson welled to the surface. We pressed our bloody thumbs against the cool glass and began to mutter the translated phrases of memory. We were barely halfway through the chant when my mother appeared in the doorway. I have never seen a person's expression warp so violently, the residual warmth in her face instantly vanished, replaced by a pallid, wide-eyed fury so suffocating it sucked the oxygen from the room. She shrieked at us for desecrating sacred ground, slapping our hands down. But the clock was ticking and they were already late for their departure window. Swearing she would deal with us the moment they returned, she grabbed her purse, shoved us out into the hallway and slammed the front door shut on the way to the driveway.



The house fell quiet with an abruptness that made my skin crawl. It didn't feel haunted in the Hollywood sense - it just felt violently vacant, like the pressure had suddenly dropped right before a hurricane. My sister didn't say a single word - she just stared us with flat, irritated eyes, stepped through the kitchen door into the attached garage and clicked the latch shut. We figured she was just furious about getting dragged into our mess and wanted to ice us out for the rest of the evening.



Ten minutes dragged by before the power abruptly cut out. It wasn't a standard grid blackout - the lights didn't flicker or die softly. Every single wall switch in the hallway and living room audibly snapped downwards simultaneously, clicking sharply against their plastic housings. Heart pounding, my cousins and I flicked on our old flip-phone screens, casting weak, greenish cones of light against the walls as we checked the fixtures. That was when my cousin let out a choked, ragged sound from the hallway. We rushed over to find him pointing at one of the master switches near the foyer. A thick strip of clear packing tape had been wrapped tightly over the toggle, pinning it rigidly in the ON position. The adhesive was fresh - completely free of dust. None of us had touched a roll of tape that day.



Before we could process the sight, came a deafening crack of thunder that violently rattled the picture frames off the drywall. A sudden, vicious Midwestern squall had rolled over the neighborhood out of nowhere, battering the siding with sheets of rain so thick and heavy it sounded like the someone was hurling buckets of wet gravel against the glass. The sky split open with prolonged, blinding arcs of white lightning that turned the backyard into a strobing, silver nightmare. And standing dead-center on the grass was my sister.



She was completely barefoot, wearing the exact oversized yellow sweater she'd had on earlier, hovering roughly three inches above the soaking lawn. I know how insane that sounds - I've spent the better part of my adult life desperately trying to bargain with that memory, telling myself it was a play of light, an optical illusion caused by the torrential downpour, or some twisted prank pulled by an older kid down the street. But there were three of us huddled against that windowpane - our breath fogging the cold glass - and we all saw the same impossible geometry. Her clothes were drenched but her waist-length black hair hung perfectly bone-straight, entirely immune to the whipping gale around her. In the blinding flashes of lightning, she stood rigid as a marble effigy staring directly into our eyes.



My cousin whimpered under his breath, desperately trying to reassure himself that she was just messing with us. I wanted nothing more than to believe him. Then her lips began to peel back. It wasn't a grotesque, ear-to-ear movie grin - it was a slow, subtle, sickening smirk - the look of an animal watching something small corner itself. My older cousin's hand shot out and clamped onto my wrist with bruising force. "Laundry room," he hissed. "Right now."



We tore through the dark hallway like hunted prey, diving into the tiny laundry room at the back of the house - the only room without an exterior window - and slammed the deadbolt home. We crouched in the suffocating heat between the washer and dryer, listening to the tempest tear at the roof shingles. Then, rising above the roar of the wind, came the rhythmic, deliberate sound of wet footprints padding across the hardwood outside. They dragged slowly down the corridor, coming to a dead halt right on the other side of our hollow-core door.



My younger cousin curled into a ball, muffling his sobs into a pile of dirty towels while my trembling fingers dialed my mother's cell phone. The second the call connected, I shattered, screaming hysterically into the receiver to make my sister stop, begging her to call Didi off, crying that she was outside in the rain trying to scare us to death. A suffocating silence stretched across the line for two agonizing beats before my sister's sharp, annoyed voice cut through the static. "What the hell are you screaming about?"



My blood turned to pure ice. In the background of the call I could distinctly hear the hum of highway tires over wet asphalt, the dull drone of an FM radio station and my father barking at her to ask what was wrong with the boys. She snapped that they were already an hour out on the interstate, my mom had dragged her out to the minivan at the last second to help with navigation, furious that we were left unsupervised. None of us had seen her get into the car. I lowered the phone and looked up at my cousins in the dim light of the screen. We didn't need to speak. If my sister was sixty miles away on the highway, then whoever had walked into the garage, whoever had taped down the switch and whoever was currently standing inches away on the other side of the wood was something else entirely.



My parents pulled a reckless U-turn and race back across the state lines. By the time their headlights cut through the driveway, the storm had vanished as abruptly as it had arrived. The air outside was dead, muggy and still. The wall switches were back to normal, the packing tape was nowhere to be found and the garage door was bolted from the inside. The house appeared entirely untouched, pristine and safe - until my father walked into the prayer room.



A low, guttural gasp tore from his throat, and he called for my mother in a voice that didn't sound like him. We cautiously crept to the threshold of the mandir. The mirror behind the brass deities had completely shattered, but not from an impact. The fracture lines radiated out from the center in violent, jagged spokes and the thousands of shards were bowed distinctly outward into the room in swollen ridges, exactly as if something immense pressed against the glass from deep within the wall and tried to push its way through.



My father didn't ask questions. He stripped the shattered frame from the drywall that very night, wrapped it in thick burlap and drove it to a landfill miles away. He never bought a replacement. Decades have passed and I've sat down with both of my cousins as grown men, quietly pleading with them to admit we hallucinated the whole thing out of childhood panic. Neither of them ever has. They remember the taped switch. They remember the freezing terror in that windowless laundry room. And they remember the barefoot silhouette in the grass while my sister was miles down an empty highway. We don't bring up the folklore anymore, we don't dare play with blood and to this day, not a single person in our family will allow a mirror inside a house of prayer.
