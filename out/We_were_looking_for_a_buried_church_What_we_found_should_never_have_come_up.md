I dug for a co-op. That means I did the digging, I filled in the forms, and somebody else signed them.

Nine sites in six years. The contract ends when the site ends.

This one was the pieve of Santa Maria in Runco. Flat country, eleven kilometres off the provincial road, six hundred souls, a bar that shuts at seven.

The church was sitting down on its north side. A crack started at the third pillar, ran up at an angle and died on the cornice.

The diocese had got the money for the underpinning. Before you push concrete under a foundation you have to dig, and where you dig the law wants an archaeologist standing there. The diocese also had a hunch there was something older down under the church.

There isn't only bone under the ground. Sewage, carbon layers, pockets with no oxygen in them. In a confined space you find everything, so the four-gas meter goes on your belt in the morning and it stays there.

In six years I never heard one go off. By the second day I'd stopped looking at mine.

*What's going to happen?*

There was a crack gauge glued over the split in the nave, two plastic plates with a grid on them, one sliding across the other. Every evening before bed I read the number and wrote it in the register.

Zero point two. Zero point two. Zero point two.

I slept in one of the two caravans in the yard. Home's two hundred kilometres away and I don't have a car. I wasn't staying for love of the site. I was staying because after it I had fuck all, and in my line nobody calls you again once you've walked off a job halfway.

I wore my mother's wedding ring. It wasn't my size and it turned loose, and I never took it off. Not for gloves, not for the shower, not for sleep. There's a chip on the outer edge — she dropped it the day we buried my father.

The heritage officer for the area was called Vigorelli. He came in the second week, looked at the trench from the top without coming down, and said the timescales were long and the money was nearly gone.

There were two labourers. One was a kid, nineteen, too many years held back at school and a mother waiting for him at seven.

The geologist was Fabio. He did the site visits for nothing at the end, once the budget for him had run out. He had a thing for me and I never told him I knew.

It rained on the fourteenth of March.

The GPR man turned up with a sled antenna, walked the naves up and down and left before dinner. His report was going to decide that site, and my stomach for the months after it.

*Probable natural cavities.*

Everything and nothing. And everything and nothing is never enough for the people paying. On the plain, natural cavities are as common as a toilet in a bathroom. Then the second line.

*Drainage gradients counterintuitive.*

That's the sentence that blessed us. That's the sentence that finished us. Some of the tunnels dropped at an angle water doesn't make.

"Risks?"

"Manageable," I said. "Nothing out of the ordinary."

"We'll see."

The second tranche hit the account that evening. Peanuts, obviously.

Before bed I went out in the rain to read the gauge. Zero point two.

A karst cavity on the Po plain is an everyday thing. Water has its own way of moving, though. It digs with shaky hands.

That conduit had straight walls.

I went down on my own, measured rope on the karabiner, meter on my waistband. Eleven metres, fifteen percent.

*Those can't be paleochristian foundations.*

*A necropolis, maybe. A medieval store.*

Whatever it was, a hand had cut it. Right then that was the only thing that counted.

With my palm on a wall I couldn't see, every so often I felt hollows packed with sand. Channels blocked on purpose. I'd get them checked.

Then the slope went flat, and without seeing it I could hear how big the chamber was that I'd come into.

I came back up shaking, and not from the cold.

The paleochristian foundations came out the week after. The diocese's hunch and my hope. The site would carry on. My table would see meals a while longer.

In the spoil against the west wall a disc-shaped sherd came up, finely worked. A badly made christogram, I thought. I know that period and I let the excitement fool me.

I showed it to Vigorelli. He took a photo on his phone and it looked to me like he sent it to somebody.

An hour later he had me called.

"Carry on."

That week the swallows were gone from under the bell tower eaves. The priest said some years that happens.

*Some years that happens.*

There wasn't supposed to be an underneath and nobody had gone looking for one. I was working the paving at the middle of the chamber when the ground let go. Short drop. It hurt.

It was a cave with hand-smoothed walls, bare, made like it wasn't meant to hold anything. A space that separates.

The sign was down there too, cut better. Much better. The one upstairs looked like a copy done from memory.

The next morning the ditch behind the bell tower was full of dead animals. A coypu, three cats, a heron. Not a wound on any of them. They'd gone down in there and stayed.

The meter settled it. Methane. It sits in the low ground and it goes up with a spark.

Vigorelli gave us the go-ahead on one condition. First man down — or worse, first dizzy spell — and he'd close the site.

We kept working it, and in the end we found it.

Under the smoothed cave there was one last chamber.

Six metres of vault. We had to go down on ropes and nobody spoke on the way. I called it the cathedral and nobody asked me why.

The floor was covered in a coarse dust, grains not ready to be sand yet. I picked up a handful and under the torch it looked like silver.

Then they scratched my palm. I opened my hand and they dropped.

They bounced. Then they moved. All in the same direction.

I watched them run along parallel roads, reach the wall and go up it. Against gravity, without a sound, like something going home. Then they stopped, all together.

I put the torch on it and I didn't understand at first, because it was too big to sit inside one beam.

They'd settled into microscopic dimples and they made a disc. Figures around it. The same thing as the sherd, a thousand times older and a thousand times better made.

An image that doesn't exist unless you're holding the right stones.

Fabio came down the next day. He looked at it for ten minutes without saying anything, then he said two things.

That those pellets weren't iron. Zinc, maybe tin. And that a wall that pulls them like that isn't magnetite.

"So what is it?"

He shrugged. Then he looked up at the vault and stopped smiling.

"Six metres of rock over your head and not one concretion. Not a film of it. Nothing's ever dripped in here."

On the Sunday I went into the church. I went in every week to photograph the cracks in the plaster.

The christogram on the north wall had no arms. It was a full disc.

I stood in front of it with the camera in my hand. Then I thought I'd never really looked at it, that it was ruined plaster, that memory plays tricks.

I took the photo and went out. I didn't open the file.

I found the well in the last week of October, and I found it with my face.

I'd stayed late, the way I always did. My toe went into a gap and I went down flat on the floor of the cathedral.

A square hole, thirty centimetres a side. Not a hollow. A hole. Warm air coming out of it.

The torch didn't reach the bottom. I got on the edge and looked in.

I don't know how long I stayed like that. I know that at some point the ring went down.

It didn't slip. My hands weren't on the ground, I wasn't working. It came off my finger on its own, like somebody was pulling it, and it dropped straight in.

It didn't make a sound. I never heard it reach the bottom.

And there was something at the bottom.

I pulled back before I saw it. I remember that clearly. I pulled back *before*.

I went back to my caravan. The young lad's was next to mine. He didn't come back that evening.

He didn't come back the next day either. His caravan stayed there three weeks, then somebody moved it.

The other labourer carried on working. When I asked him about the kid he stared at me and said nothing. Then he went back to work.

I called the carabinieri myself.

They came, they looked at the site, they asked if I had his address. Then they left and I never saw them again.

I waited for somebody to call me. A brother, an uncle, the mayor, somebody.

Nobody called. A village of six hundred souls loses a nineteen-year-old and the week after the bar is full at the same time as always.

I started looking at the village.

I hadn't before. I went through it at seven in the morning and eight at night, and in between there was the hole.

The primary school is at the top of the street, past the monument. It's got the glass and the gates and the yard. It took me nine months to notice the grass in that yard was up to my knees and there's a notice on the door dated 2006.

There are no children in Runco. Not one.

And yet...

The houses are lived in. Lights go on, washing goes out, cars get moved. But at six in the evening people come out and stand along the site fence, and they don't talk, and they don't look at the dig. They look at the church.

At the bar in the next village along I told them about the animals in the ditch. The girl behind the counter nodded and turned round to rinse the cups.

The bar in Runco was different. I went in twice in nine months. The second time, when I opened the door, they all stopped talking at once, and it isn't that they turned to look at me. They stopped, and they waited.

I ordered a coffee and drank it standing up, and in those two minutes nobody started again.

The bell rang at night too. Not the hours. One strike, and not always at the same time. The first month it woke me, then I stopped waking up, and that's the one I still can't put down.

One night a woman turned up, about forty. She planted herself in the middle of the yard in front of the façade and swore for ten minutes.

"Give him back!"

There was nobody in front of her.

I walked her out through the gate with a hand on her arm. She didn't fight me, she was crying quietly. Then, once she was over the line, she turned round and spat in my face.

"You shouldn't have dug. You should have left it buried."

I didn't wipe it off straight away. I stood at the gate with that on my cheek and watched her go.

She'd come a long way. She was the kid's mother.

On the third of December I went into the church to take the photos.

The christogram wasn't there. Not changed. Not there.

I looked at the stoups, the Stations of the Cross, the marble plaques with the names of the fallen. I went round the nave three times.

There wasn't one Christian thing left in that church. Not a symbol, not a figure, not a word.

And the priest was down at the far end, doing the flowers.

I went out, crossed the yard and went down.

I didn't take the rope. I didn't take the meter.

I did the last ten metres in the dark, because the torch was shaking too much and I'd rather not see the walls.

I got to the cathedral, I went to the hole, I knelt on the edge.

My ring. Stupid thing to want. I wanted it back and I wasn't going without it.

Warm air was coming out, and the air had a smell to it. Not earth. A shut room with a fire lit in it years before.

This time I didn't pull back. I didn't, because something took hold of me — maybe really, maybe only in my head. It wasn't a hand and it wasn't a claw. It was something else.

I looked in, and at the bottom of the well there was the disc, and under the disc there was the thing the disc has always meant.

I screamed.

It wasn't my voice. It was a voice from long before me and my mouth was only the place it came out of.

It went on until I ran out of air, and when I got my breath back it started again, and the second time I wasn't the one deciding anything.

They heard it up top. I know because the next day nobody mentioned it to me.

I came round on the floor with my cheek in the dust and the torch still lit in my hand.

I was calm. I can't explain it, but I was calm.

I went to the east wall where the sand-packed channels are and I picked up the iron bar.

The gas was coming from there. Somebody had plugged them long before us. All we'd done was finish the job properly.

The sand came out dry, by the handful. The third conduit let out a long warm breath that tasted of garlic.

I unhooked the ventilation hose and laid it on the ground.

Then I took the lid off the junction box and left the terminals bare. The pumps start on their own off the float, and every time they start the contactor makes its spark. Every forty minutes.

I still had sand in my hand. All I had to do was put it back.

The climb out was hard. Halfway along the tunnel I stopped because no air was reaching me, and I thought I'd be staying there, and it didn't move me one way or the other.

I came out into the open at five to six.

The air was cold in my throat, the mud came over my boots, my mouth tasted of battery, and the bell was going.

The church lights were on and the door was wide open.

It had rained all afternoon. The mud in front of the door was smooth. Not a footprint in it.

They were all inside.

The pews were full, people standing down the aisles, more of them at the back. My labourer in his good jacket. The girl from the bar in the next village. The kid's mother, front row.

I went in to tell them to get out. I'll swear it on anything you like. That's what I went in for.

They were all on the north side. The pews on the south side were empty down to the last one.

All of them in the same position. Body turned three-quarters, head a bit lower than the shoulder, right arm down the side and the left one not.

I'd measured that position four times. At four different depths, on four stones, and every time it was made better.

Nobody looked at me. I was standing in the middle of the doorway with mud up my calves and not one of them turned round.

The church smelled like the hole. A shut room with a fire lit in it years before.

I went out walking backwards, crossed the road and sat down on the low wall opposite.

The floor lifted half a metre, all of it at once, like a breath.
