I wish I were not in this gravel driveway staring at the blue-paneled house, but when our services are requested, it’s up to us, the ones who drive through all hours of the day and night, to close the deal. As I stared at the front door, the deeply stained entry to the house on the wide porch with the wooden swing, I could feel the pain coming on. I reached inside the glove box and retrieved the only thing that could dull it.

Pills, and a flask filled with tequila this time. I had grown burnt out on bourbon and whiskey; it was time to switch it up. I twisted the pill bottle open. Three should do, I thought to myself as I poured the pills into my hand. Who was I to question fate when four of them fell out? A little extra oomph before I put my salesman face on.

I had also found that chewing them up, feeling that nasty, chalky taste spread across my gums and teeth, made them work faster. Or maybe I just told myself that, hoping to psych myself up enough to dull the ache.

Unscrewing the top of the flask, I could smell the tequila as I took a long gulp, washing the crunched-up pills down. “That’s the good stuff right there,” I said to myself.

As I stepped out of the car, I could feel the wind push my hair ever so slightly. They knew I was here, too. I tucked the flask into my suit pocket, walked behind my car, and popped the trunk. A painfully plain briefcase lay inside next to several empty bottles of tequila, which reminded me I needed to actually clean my car. I grabbed the handle, pulled out the case, and slammed the trunk before slowly shuffling toward the small staircase, my feet crunching the gravel lightly with each step.

The dark wooden door with three windows at the top creaked open as a woman in her mid-thirties stepped out. She had deep dark circles under her eyes, distracting from almond-shaped irises with a pretty shade of hazel. “You’re Mr. Relinski, right?”

“That would be me,” I replied. “You requested some of our services. Were you a referral, by chance?”

“No, I found you online.”

Referrals were becoming fewer and fewer these days; instead, somebody would stumble upon our website on some advice forum. Once they went to the link, they filled out a little form with their information, did a brief phone call with one of our reps, and then someone like me was sent out to make the sale.

She continued to stand in the doorway, looking over my drab blue suit and my crumpled tie. Let’s just say I had given up on the whole idea of dressing to impress these days. “Can I come in?” I asked, finally.

She nodded as she left the door cracked and walked back inside. I reached for another drink, prepping myself to close the deal as I followed her in.

Walls of a sky-blue shade were lined with pictures of the woman and a man in all sorts of different locations: beaches, restaurants, even one of them in front of the Eiffel Tower kissing. It was almost too cliché for a cynic like me, but judging by the heaviness in her eyes, those times had long since passed.

“I’m in the living room, Mr. Relinski,” she called out from the doorway at the end of the hallway. I made my way across the light hardwood floor and entered a room to see her sitting on a cream-colored couch in front of a glass table with a mug of hot coffee, steam billowing above the rim.

“So, as you may have read on our website, for any services rendered today, my company and I are not to be held liable,” I said, placing my briefcase on the table. “My company offers many services, such as referrals to lawyers, counseling, and even shell companies to hide your money in case such things should arise.”

She took a long sip of coffee, her fingernails bitten down to the nub against the ceramic, before placing the mug back on the table with a clink. “I am not interested in those services. I am interested in a particular one.”

“I see.”

I popped open the briefcase. It was always the same. I was never called if someone wanted the other services we provided. To be truthful, I wasn’t even sure how those other services worked, because no one ever picked them. They wanted the goddamn mirror.

“So how does it work?” she asked as I pulled out an old bag, the stitches fraying from age and from how long I’d kept it. Too long, I told myself weekly. I pulled out the mirror by its brass handle, which was carved with small words from places I had never been and in languages whose meanings I didn’t actually know.

I looked at the small hand mirror, catching my own reflection, a tired face with red, bloated cheeks from too much drinking and eyes glazed over from too many pain pills.

I handed it over to her, and she grasped the handle. “You just look into it for a moment, think about what you want, but I also want to tell you to think very hard before...”

“What the hell? That hurts!” she screamed at me as the mirror fell onto the glass table. But it didn’t break, crack, or even seem affected by the fall.

It was already too late. The small nails housed inside the handle had pierced her skin. The blood slowly started to work its way across the glass of the mirror, forming a pool in the center of it.

“What is happening?” she asked, a hint of panic now in her voice. Her blood from the nails continued to seep up and collect on the mirror. I pulled out a cigarette, lighting it without permission. She didn’t notice, as her mind was probably spinning, trying to figure out a way to rationalize what was happening.

I took a long drag, waiting for the next part. The mirror went dark, as though it were looking somewhere else that wasn’t the ceiling of the room, and the blood slowly started to drain while her eyes could do nothing more than watch.

“It’s going to want to see you,” I said, sliding the mirror toward her with a small scratch. She looked at me, and I knew what she saw: eyes already dim with a quiet despair, the kind that comes from watching something like this too frequently.

“What do you mean, ‘it,’ Mr. Relinski?” she asked as she grasped the brass handle of the mirror. I made my way to the couch, sitting beside her and grabbing her arm, adjusting it so she was looking right into the mirror.

On the other side was a dark room, dimly lit, almost like a pit. “It will be here any second,” I said as I felt her arms tremble against my hand.

She screamed as she saw its sunken wide eyes with the abyss-black pupils looking back at her, its wrinkly, discolored face splitting into a crooked-toothed grin that would chill the blood of anyone who had never witnessed such a thing.

Before she knew it, it had disappeared. The pit where it stayed slowly faded into something else as she tore her eyes from the mirror and threw it back onto the glass table. Once again, not so much as a crack or scratch on it.

“Tell me what the hell is going on!” she shouted, standing up and crossing her arms, still trying to make sense of what she had just seen.

“You are the one that asked for the service,” I replied. “The rep should have...”

“The person on the phone only said that once it starts I can’t take it back!”

I stubbed out my cigarette on the table and looked at the mirror, at the dim glow where the pit had been, before turning my gaze back to her. “And they would be correct on that,” I muttered.

“Then you should tell me what the hell it is!” she yelled at me, her arms crossed tighter now, her voice more scared than angry.

“I don’t know what it is, but I’ve been carrying that mirror with me for a very long time.”

“Where did you get it?”

“A very long time ago a man came to me offering a job,” I replied. “I was desperate at the time. Actually, everyone was back then. But I was just the unlucky son of a bitch who took him up on the offer.”

“And you just took the damn thing?”

“Yep, I’ve had it since nineteen-thir...” I tried to reply, before a sharp pain hit my ribs, like someone kicking me with a hard worker’s boot over and over again. It was listening. It didn’t like the finer details being spoken in front of clients.

I started to have a coughing fit, blood trickling from my nose. A punishment I had felt before, any time the booze had made my lips too loose for its liking.

Then I heard a man’s voice from the table, and her head snapped toward it. She grabbed the mirror, and I pushed myself up from the couch, gripping my side, and lumbered over to see the man from the pictures staring back at us.

“Debra, is that you?” he screamed.

She shouted into the mirror. “Yes, Stephen, it is me!”

“You can’t communicate with him,” I said. “You can only witness what is about to happen.”

“Then why did he call out my name?”

I started to reach for my flask. The worst part was about to come. “Because the creature looks just like you.”

“You mean it’s going to kill him looking like me?” she cried out.

I nodded, then unscrewed the flask and took a long gulp. “That’s how it works. The hurt lover gets to see all the mess without having to do it themselves.”

She stood frozen holding the mirror, her entire body shaking as she watched the creature get closer to the man. “Debra, what are you doing?” he asked.

The creature wrapped its hands around his face. Hands with a familiar skin tone, the same as the woman standing beside me. They even had the same nails, bitten down to the nub, and an identical wedding band.

“Make it stop! Stop it now,” she wailed. “I don’t want this, I just thought he was cheating on me!”

“I can’t stop it,” I replied, as the thumbs pressed into his eyes, blood erupting from the sockets.

“Debra, please don’t do this. I love you,” he pleaded. But the thing pressing its thumbs through his skull didn’t know the meaning of love. Before too long the man could no longer form words, only painful screams and groans, before finally becoming quiet.

The mirror dropped to the floor, fading to a deep black before revealing the room’s ceiling again. “What happens now?” she asked, her voice flat.

“One day you will look in a mirror, any mirror, and you will see it again,” I replied, picking the mirror up from the floor and walking back to the table to place it in the old frayed bag.

“What will it do?”

“Not a clue, I am never here for it,” I said. “Could just leave a body behind or drag you away.”

She watched as I lumbered slowly over to the briefcase, placing the mirror inside before latching the case closed. “Mr. Relinski, can I ask you one more thing before you go?”

“I can’t help you,” I replied.

My joints started to ache as I straightened up. The thing they don’t tell you about living this long is that yes, I may look like a thirty-eight-year-old on the outside, but my bones and organs feel every bit like someone born in 1894.

“What do I do now?” she sobbed, as I lifted the briefcase.

I didn’t say anything. I only walked away down the same sky-blue hallway, past all the pictures of the couple, and out the door. The sharp daily pains started to rise from my legs as I reached my car, and I pulled the door open and nearly fell inside. I opened the glove box, pulled out the bottle of pain pills, and dumped more in my mouth. I didn’t bother counting this time. I just crunched them up.

How could I answer such a question truthfully? It was the same thing I had been asked hundreds of times since this cursed mirror came into my life, and the answer never changed. You live with it. They all lived with the same aching feeling that I carried with me.

Regret.
