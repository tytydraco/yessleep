I need to set this down before it finishes going, because it is going, faster now that I've started writing, and I think the writing is part of why. Read it anyway. There's a thing I have to ask you at the end, and it's the only thing I've ever asked anyone that mattered.

There were four of us. Film students, out in the world on a trip we'd told the department was fieldwork: a documentary about places the guidebooks had given up on. *A Place No One Goes.* That was the pitch. Ben drove, because Ben always drove; he couldn't pass a human being without talking to them, and the astonishing thing was that they always answered. Miles planned everything to the hour, carried a laminated map he didn't need, and knocked on wood twice with two knuckles every time we crossed a border. Ellis filmed. That was Ellis. She shot everything and appeared in none of it: three years, hundreds of hours, not one frame of her own face. *Somebody has to stay behind the glass,* she'd say, and laugh, and we thought it was a joke.

We'd been arguing about fried bananas for two days when the town assembled itself out of the hillside ahead of us. We were so hungry that our first thought wasn't *how beautiful.* It was *please, God, let there be somewhere to eat.* And there was. Halfway down the main street, a window full of warm light, a wooden sign with a single word none of us could read, and a table in the window that seemed, I know how this sounds, set and waiting for exactly as many of us as there were.

The old woman came out from behind the counter drying her hands, and when she saw us her face *opened.* There is no better word. As though we were not four filthy strangers but something she had waited a long time for and was, at last, relieved to see arrive.

"Ah," she said. And then, softer, confirming it to herself: "Ah."

She sat with us. She told us about her town: the church older than the town's memory of itself, the spring above the valley where the water came out cold enough to hurt, *where the girls used to go,* and she did not say which girls, and we did not think to ask.

Ben, casting for more, asked if anyone had ever come to film them before.

She went still. Not troubled. Still, the way water goes still. "Not to film," she said. "But there was a group. Long ago, when I was young. Young people, like you. They came in off the road half-starved, like you, and sat just there," she nodded at our window, "where you are sitting now. One of them we kept. His picture, I mean." And she crossed to the wall behind Ben's head and turned a small framed photograph so the light fell on it. "That was our friend."

A young man stood in front of the diner, the same sign behind him, caught mid-laugh, wholly delighted. One hand held a slice of pizza lifted toward the camera. The other was raised in a thumbs-up, that most ordinary gesture, the one that means *everything's fine, don't worry about me.* His smile was wide and open and without complication.

"He looks happy," I said.

She looked at me a half-second too long. "He was," she said. "Very happy here. They all were."

The church bell rang the hour while we ate. I count things, a stupid habit, the kind you never think will matter. It rang five times. I was almost certain it was four o'clock. I told myself I'd started counting late and reached for the wine. That was the first one. A bell, miscounted, in the happiest hour of my life. Five, for the four of us. Everything that followed was only that same small wrongness, patient, growing, waiting for me to stop letting it go.

She watched us out the door with one hand half-raised, and called after us to come back before we left the town: she'd want to hear everything.

"We will," Ben called back. "We'll tell you everything."

We gave the town three days, or we meant to.

By the second day we had it by heart. The fountain, the dry bridge everyone still called the bridge, the flower shop, the war memorial, and, at the eastern edge, past the last honey-coloured house, in a field gone to weed, the grey building. Concrete where everything else was honey and ash, large past all reason, six storeys or seven; it resisted being counted. Its windows were empty. Its face was scrawled to a surprising height with graffiti in a script I couldn't read, and among it, again and again, in different hands, one shape recurred: a circle, roughly drawn, with marks around its edge. As though it were the one thing everyone who had ever come there agreed was worth setting down.

Miles said no. Ellis raised the camera and said, quietly, "We should film there. It's the only thing here that doesn't want us to." None of us knew what she meant. I'm not sure she did. But we went. Of course we went; a place no one goes had led us here, to the thing even this place had given up on.

Inside it was colder. We climbed. Empty rooms, then empty rooms whose windows framed the town into pictures, so that the building began to seem a thing built for no reason but to look at that beauty. On the third floor we met a man, ordinary, middle-aged, a flask beside him, a cigarette going, entirely at ease, as though he'd been expecting company and was mildly surprised it had taken so long. He asked if we liked the town. We said we did, very much. Something crossed his face, and he looked out at the dusk and said, "Yes. Everyone likes the town." I didn't like the way he said it, and I couldn't have told you why.

The fourth floor was empty rooms. The fifth floor was empty rooms too, all but the last. At the end of the fifth-floor hallway, where the corridor ran out into shadow, there was a door that was not like the others. Steel. Closed. Across it, bracket to bracket, ran a heavy chain, the links thick as my thumb, drawn tight and fastened with a combination lock, old, pitted with rust, but its four brass wheels wiped strangely clean, as though hands still touched it. Above the door, painted in the same red as the recurring circles, was a number. And the door said, as plainly as a chained steel door in a dead building can say anything, that what lay above was not for us.

Which is why we tried it.

The banging brought the man up from the third floor, and his face had changed, the tired ease gone out of it. "No," he said. "Leave it." He crossed the corridor faster than a tired man should have been able to and put his hand flat on the steel, the way you gentle an animal. "It does not open. People have tried. Everyone tries." He seemed to hear himself, and to decide to say less than he'd been about to. "There is a code," he said, more quietly. "Without it, nothing. And no one has the code." He looked at each of us, and when he came to me he held, a half-second, a weighing, and I had the sensation, I'll set it down though it sounds like the thing one adds afterward, that he was sorry for me in particular. Sorry in advance. The way you're sorry for a person who has an appointment they don't know they have.

Then he went back down. We should have followed him. We knew, even then, that we should have. But there was a broken window at the end of the corridor, and a concrete ledge ran along the building's face beneath the sixth-floor windows, and the nearest of *those* was also broken, also open, and a young person, a foolish one, might go out the one and along the ledge and in the other and be on the sixth floor without ever troubling the chain.

"No," said Miles. But he said it the way he'd said it to the building. Already knowing.

I went first. I have wondered since whether that mattered: whether, by going first, I chose something, or was chosen. The stone was cold and gritted under my hands. The town lay below and to my left, all its lights coming on, warm and small and impossibly far. I edged along with my cheek to the wall and my friends' voices behind me: Ben telling me to be careful in a voice gone high with fear and joy, Ellis's camera breathing, Miles saying my name once, quietly, and I reached the far window, and got my hands over the sill, and pulled myself up and in, and my foot came down on the floor of the sixth floor...

...and came down on the worn stone under the table of the diner, and Ben was reaching across me for the menu, and the old woman was coming out from behind the counter drying her hands, and it was the golden afternoon of the first day, and none of it had happened yet.

There was no seam.

That's the first thing I'd want you to feel, and it's the one I've never been able to make anyone feel. When you fall asleep and wake, there's a seam: a darkness crossed, the body's knowledge that time has passed in your absence. There was none. My foot came down on the sixth floor and my foot came down on the diner's stone in the same step. Two days of walking had been quietly removed from my body. My clothes were clean again. Ben said, "You all right?" in the first day's voice, the voice from before anything.

I couldn't answer him. I looked at Miles, and Miles was opening the map on his knees. I looked at Ellis, and Ellis was setting her camera on the table, its small red eye open, and I thought, wildly, *it's still running, it has the building on it, I can show them,* and knew, with a certainty that came from nowhere and has never once been wrong, that it would not. That whatever the camera had, it would not have that.

The old woman came out, and her face opened, and she said "Ah," and asked where we'd come from.

I understood two things at once, and they have shaped every hour of my life since. The first was that I was not going to wake, because I was not asleep. The second was that I was the only one at the table who knew.

I went back to the building that night, of course I did, and climbed the ledge and put my foot on the sixth floor, and Ben was reaching for the menu. That was the second return, and it taught me the only thing the sixth floor ever taught me plainly: it did not let you *out.* It let you back. I'd been trying to escape through the one door that led further *in.*

I don't know how many loops there were. Counting left me. I'll tell you what I learned, because learning was the only thing that ever moved in that place.

The bell rang five, always, for the four of us, and no one else ever heard the fifth stroke. I tested it against Miles's recorder: I counted five aloud, the recording gave four, and then, faint, my own voice saying *five.* There were no children anywhere in that town, not one, not crying, not playing. Miles noticed it once, on some loop. "It's the most peaceful place I've ever been," he said, "and I've just worked out why." Then he forgot he'd said it.

The photograph on the wall began to change. It only ever subtracted. First the pizza was gone. His hand hung empty. Then the thumb. Then the smile came down, degree by degree, into the flat worn pleasantness of a man posing for a picture he hasn't agreed to. It never changed while I watched. The subtractions happened in the dark behind my own eyes, between one looking and the next, so that I only ever met the aftermath. And I understood what he was. Not a keepsake. The visitor. The last traveller the town had kept, being worn away, detail by detail, until one day some *newer* traveller would sit at that table and be shown my picture, and say *he looks happy,* and the waitress would say *he was, he was very happy here.*

Because the waitress was growing younger.

Not while I watched, never while I watched, always in the intervals, so that I only ever met the results: a firmness come back to the cheek, the white gone from the hair, until the grandmother had become a handsome woman and the handsome woman a girl, lovely in the particular way the town's gold light seemed designed to fall on. And everything the growing-young cost her was quietly taken. It took Tomas first, the old regular whose seat she'd warned us about, until she'd never made such a friend. It took the stew, her mother's stew, and then it took the mother she'd learned it from. It took the word *my dear.* It left the laugh and took the friend the laugh had been for. It was not killing anything. It was doing the worse thing, the thing I had no word for until I'd watched it done to my mother's memory and my friends' faces and my own certainty: it was *erasing.* Unwriting. Taking not our lives but the evidence that anyone had ever had them, until there'd be no difference anyone could find between a person who had died and a person who had never been born.

I tried to leave a message. Nothing on paper crossed the loops, so I carved: found a nail and scratched into the concrete by the chained door, over a whole loop, three ugly, certain words. **YOU ARE REPEATING.** Next return the wall was smooth. Not scrubbed. Smooth, as though the loop hadn't undone the carving so much as declined to have ever contained it. Once, despairing, I cut the message into my own forearm with a shard of glass. I woke at the table with the skin clean. Not healed. *Unhurt.* Never hurt.

I tried to call home. Most calls rang into a long grey hiss. But once, and never again, though I've tried a thousand times since, it connected, and my mother's voice said *hello,* her real voice, and I said "Mum, it's me," and there was an ordinary bad-line pause, and then she said, kindly, patiently, the way you speak to a stranger who's dialled wrong: "I think you have the wrong number, love, there's no one here by that name." The way she couldn't finish the sentence, because she had no name to finish it with, told me everything. The erasure had a longer reach than the valley.

The one thing the town could not reach was the scar. Ellis's camera had been dropped on the first day of our first year, and the fall had put a hairline crack in the corner of the sensor, so that every frame she ever shot carried, in the top left, a faint pale thread of light that belonged to no scene. It was how you knew a thing was hers. When I finally made myself go through her footage, I found the town, unchanged, but the long shot she'd turned on us that first afternoon, the one time she ever broke her own rule and filmed *us,* held only three of us. She was behind it, of course. And every frame carried the scar. That thread of light was the one proof Ellis had ever stood anywhere at all, and it was the only proof, because she'd spent three years making certain she was in none of it. *Somebody has to stay behind the glass.* I sat in the dead building with her camera in my hands and understood: when the town came for her, there would be nothing to hold up and say *she was real.* No photograph. No frame on any wall. Only hundreds of hours of a world she had loved enough to keep, with a scar in the corner where she used to be.

I decided that if I couldn't save all of us, I might save one.

I chose Ben first, because Ben loved me most and would follow furthest. At the spring, on the first day, Ben had once begun to tell me something: about the noise in his head, and the reason he talked so much, *so there'd never be a gap for anyone to look into and see how,* and he'd stopped, casting for the word, and into that gap Miles had named a constellation wrong, and Ben had risen to it, and the sentence had closed over. On loop after loop I took him back to the spring and said *how what, Ben, tell me, how what,* and every time, at the exact moment before the belief landed, the woman in the doorway would call to him about the flowers, and the current would take him, and the gap would close. The town did not need to defeat my love for Ben. It only needed to interrupt it, forever, at the exact moment before it arrived. That is a crueller thing to do to two people than anything with blood in it.

I chose Miles next. Reason I could open and never get through; I built him proof on proof and he admired the proofs and forgot them. So I tried the shut door. "Tell me about the pick," I said, the blue guitar pick he carried in every jacket and never played. And Miles went still, and took it out, and turned it in his fingers, and said it had been his brother's. That was all. *It was my brother's.* I had not known Miles had a brother; in three years he'd never once said. And I understood that the rigor, the folding, the laminated maps, the whole architecture of Miles's need for a world that behaved, had been built over a hole where a brother had been, and that I was about to lose Miles too, and had only just, at the very end, met him. He opened his mouth to say more. And the day turned over. And he was in the passenger seat with the map on his knees, whole and sealed and unreachable, and I never learned the brother's name, and there was no one left in the world to ask.

And Ellis I could not reach at all, and the reason I could not reach her is the reason I loved her. She came nearer than any of them: she'd frown at the little screen and say *have we been here before* and my heart would leap, but when I begged her to do the one thing that might fix her in the world, to turn the camera around and film herself, just once, so that one frame would have her in it, she refused, gently, absolutely. "Somebody has to stay behind the glass," she said, and smiled, and it was the joke and it was not the joke, and I wanted to take her by the shoulders and say *the glass will not save you, the glass is where it will put you, please, for once, be in front of it,* and I did say it, some returns, I said all of it, and she listened with her whole grave attention, and the day turned over, and she was in the back beside me with the camera up, filming everyone, filming no one, leaving the world exactly as much proof of Ellis as she had always meant to leave, which was none.

I stopped trying to escape and started trying to understand.

The number above the chained door. I'd learned by then to interview the town: the museum man, the baker, the old man with the dog, and no two of them agreed on anything, the town's age, who built the grey house, and not one of them was lying. But the number I found everywhere once I began to look: cut into a step of the church, inked in the museum ledger, and once, on the stones of a little walled yard I'd taken for a graveyard until I saw that none of the stones had names, only that same number, over and over, a whole yard of one number where the names of the dead should have been. **Two hundred and forty-seven.**

And I went back, many times, to the man on the third floor, because a friendship the other person cannot remember is still a friendship to the one who can, and he was the only creature besides me who seemed to understand that something was wrong. You do not abandon the only other person awake in a burning house, even if he forgets your face each time the alarm resets. I asked him what was above the chained door. He looked out at the town and said, "The top." I asked why only the sixth floor was locked. "Because the rest is only a building." I asked, once, whether he'd ever gone up. He was quiet a long time. "I stood where you stand," he said. "I did not go along the ledge. And I have been trying to get back to that window every day of my life." He did not explain it. I did not ask him to.

Then, on a loop I didn't know would be the last, I lifted my head from a meal and the photograph had, for the first time, *added.* The man in it was restored: laughing, happy as the first day, and behind him, small, at the very front, three figures had come back, arranged in the gold light the way friends arrange themselves for a photograph they're glad to be in. Ben in the middle, enormous, beaming, his arm around the man who had glared at me for what felt like my whole life. Miles beside them with his careful pleased half-smile. And Ellis. Ellis, who had turned all the way round at last, out from behind the glass, facing the camera, facing *me,* with the expression I'd seen on her a thousand times, the one she wore when she'd caught a thing worth catching and wanted to be sure I'd caught it too: *look. Look at this. Are you getting this.*

And between them, held up in the middle of the group, turned out toward me the way a class holds up a banner, was a piece of paper, and on it, in Miles's small precise hand, I would have known it anywhere, were three numbers.

Two. Four. Seven.

I didn't think. Thinking was the wrong country and I'd learned to leave it. I ran: out of the diner, up the main street, across the weed-choked field, in through the cold door and up, past the third floor where the tired man looked up and did not try to stop me, only nodded, the way you nod to someone leaving on a journey you cannot take with them, up to the fifth floor, to the steel door and its four brass wheels.

Four wheels. Three numbers. And I understood the last of it the way you understand the last line of a poem, all at once, without working it out: the fourth number was the one that had rung out over the town again and again, the extra stroke, the one too many: the number of those who came. And the last wheel I turned to one. One, for the one of us still standing in the corridor with his hand on the lock.

Two. Four. Seven. One.

The lock opened in my hand with a small oiled click, as though it had only ever been waiting for someone to lose enough to be given the numbers. The chain ran off the door and pooled at my feet with a sound like something breathing out. I put my hand flat on the cold steel where the tired man had once put his, and I pushed the door open, and I was not on the sixth floor of a ruined grey building.

I was in a corridor, and everything in it was white.

White tiles, white walls, a light that came from nowhere and cast no shadow. The ruin was gone. The corridor was immaculate, more than immaculate, it was *kept,* dusted, made ready, the way a house is made ready for the return of someone long away. It ran further than the building could have held, and along both walls were doors, each with a small frosted window, and behind each window there was light and movement and the small far sound of voices.

I understood, without being told, that these were classrooms. The knowing was part of what made it terrible: I arrived already fluent in a grammar I had never learned.

I opened the first door. Inside was my grandmother's kitchen. Not a room like it. *It,* the yellow walls and the particular light of an afternoon in a decade I'd stopped believing had been real. My grandmother at the stove, back to me. A small boy at the table, four or five, legs swinging, not reaching the floor. She was telling him a story I'd entirely forgotten and that came back whole the instant I heard her voice. It was the last afternoon I ever spent with her, ordinary, unremarked, the kind that should have been lost the way all our afternoons are lost. And it had been kept. It was here.

I understood I was not being *shown* these rooms. I was being *walked through* them, the way you're walked through a house that is for sale: my whole life laid out in sequence, and a place that keeps every afternoon of a life, perfect, forever, behind glass is not a place where those afternoons are treasured. It is a place where they are stored.

I had misunderstood, from the first day, what kind of story I was inside of. I'd thought it was a story about a town: a haunted place, a trap in the hills. But the town was only the mouth of it. This was the stomach. It had never really been about a town at all. It was about *keeping.* About what it costs to hold a thing so still that it cannot be lost: a friend, an afternoon, a face, and about the fact that holding a thing still and losing it are, in the end, the same act, performed at different speeds.

There were rooms further down I'm ashamed of. A friendship I'd lost so completely I'd lost even the loss: a boy who'd been, for two years of my childhood, closer than anyone, whom I'd dropped without drama, simply by letting the phone go unanswered a few times too many when brighter friends came along, until he faded the way a photograph fades. He was in the room, at the age I'd left him, waiting by a window with the patience of a child who has been told someone is coming and is beginning, slowly, to understand that they are not. I had done that. Not the town. And I stood in that white doorway and understood, at last, what I'd been screaming at the waitress across all those loops. *There were four of us. There were always four of us.* And understood that I had once *been* the waitress, had once looked at someone who loved me and said, in effect, *what friend, my dear,* and gone on drying my hands.

And there was a door behind which I learned why I watch. I'd always told the story of myself as though the watching were a gift, a temperament. The room told it differently. Behind the glass was a stairwell in a house I'd lived in at nine, my parents on the landing doing the low relentless cutting a child hears through a wall and cannot stop and cannot leave, and there, halfway up the stairs, was the boy I'd been, a toy camera up to his eye, pointing it at them, pretending that if he was filming it, it wasn't happening to him. He was not a boy whose home was coming apart. He was a man behind a camera, safe, recording. That is where I was made. Not by a gift. By a plastic camera held up as a wall between me and a thing I could not bear, and I'd held a camera up as that wall every day since, done to the people I loved the gentlest version of what Ellis did with hers and the town did with its photograph: kept them at a distance where they could be seen and not touched. The town had not chosen me at random. It had chosen the one kind of person already halfway its creature: a watcher, who would notice everything and reach no one.

Near the end of the hallway the rooms became the years I'd spent with my friends. The first day of the programme: the enormous shy boy who'd made a joke to cover his shyness and made me laugh and made himself, in that instant, forever, my friend. A rooftop, all four of us, swearing we'd make things that mattered and never lose each other. The night of the banana fries: the actual night, the wrong town, the unreadable menu, the four of us laughing until the owner came to see what was wrong and then laughing harder because nothing was. I stood in that doorway a long time. It is the afternoon I would have chosen, if the town had told me to choose, and I think the town knew it, and I think that is why it left the door open.

Because there was a door, near the very end, and behind its frosted glass was light and movement and the small far sound of Ben's laugh, his real laugh, arriving before the joke was finished, and I put my hand on the handle, and I could not turn it. I understood, the way you understand everything in that place, below thought, that if I opened it and went in I would not come out; that the kept afternoons were not to be visited but *entered and joined;* and that this was the mercy the sixth floor held out at the very last to the ones it had emptied, not freedom, never freedom, but this: the chance to lie down inside the best day you ever had, with the people you loved, and stop, and be kept, perfect, unchanging, forever. It is the most tempting thing I have ever stood in front of. I will not tell you how close I came. A man is allowed to keep a few of his own doors shut.

I took my hand off the handle. And a little further on there was one more room, and it was Ellis's, and it undid me in a way none of the others had, because the sixth floor did there the one thing I had begged the whole town to do: it put her in front of the glass. The room was dark, and at the far end a screen was playing, and on the screen was Ellis. It was the ferry: the one photograph Ben had ever taken of her, the one she'd made him delete, restored, not a still but somehow moving, three seconds of her, caught laughing with her hand not quite up in time to stop it, the wind off the water in her hair, looking not at the camera but at Ben behind it, at all of us, open, unguarded, out from behind the glass for the length of a breath. And I crossed that dark room faster than I have ever crossed anything, because I understood this was the only place in all the worlds where Ellis could be *seen,* the one frame she'd spent her life refusing and the town had granted only here, only now, only to me, and I meant to keep it, to burn it into myself, to memorise every part of her so that when I was out, if I was ever out, I'd have one true image of my friend to set against the nothing she'd left.

The screen played the three seconds. And went dark. And did not play again. I stood there and said her name to the dark screen and put my hands on it and it stayed dark. The town had shown me Ellis, once, for three seconds, and then taken her back behind the glass where she'd always chosen to stand, and I understood that even here, even at the top of everything, she would not be kept the way the others were kept: that she was, in this one thing, uncatchable, ungiven, hers. And I did not know whether to curse the town for taking her or love her, one last time, for being the only one of us it could not entirely hold. I have carried the three seconds since. They are already going. I'll tell you, before the end, how much of them is left.

At the very end of the hallway, past the last kept afternoon of my life, past the door I could not open and the screen that would not play again, there was one more door, and it was not white.

The hallway changed as I came toward it, and the change was backward, out of my life and out of anything I could have a memory of. The doors grew further apart. Their frosted glass dimmed, and then they stopped being doors and were only shapes in the wall where doors had been, sealed, the way a wall remembers a window that has been bricked up. The white tiles gave way to older stone, and the stone to something older than stone, worn smooth and dark, and I understood that I was walking not only to the end of my own kept life but past it, off the edge of everything the sixth floor had gathered from me, into a part of the building that had been there *before there was a me to keep.* And I was afraid in a way I had not been afraid of any of the horrors below, because those had had something to do with me, and this had nothing to do with me, and never had.

The door was black, and it was open, and no light came out of it.

I'll set down what I have, and I will not do more than that. I will not explain it. The room was black, not dark, *black;* the walls and floor and ceiling a black that drank the little light I brought in and gave nothing back. There were candles, many of them, and their flames did not move. They stood perfectly upright and perfectly still, as though the air itself had stopped, as though even fire were held there, forbidden to flicker, because flickering is a kind of time. The candles stood at the points of a great figure drawn on the black floor: a circle, with marks around its edge. I knew it. I'd known it from the first hour in the building, scrawled low on every floor in every hand and every colour, the one shape everyone who had ever come there had felt compelled to copy without knowing why. This was the thing they'd been copying. This was the original. And the red it was drawn in was *wet,* and caught the still light, and moved when nothing else did.

And there were girls in the room.

I did not know them. I want to be exact, because it is nearly the last exact thing I have: I did not know them, and yet I felt, the moment I saw them, that I had heard of them once, in a sentence taken out of an old woman's mouth before she could finish it. *The spring above the town, where the girls used to go.* These girls. They stood around the circle among the unmoving candles, facing inward, and I could not hold any of their faces in my eye long enough to fix it. They wore no expression I could name. They were not afraid, and they were not cruel. They were only there, attendant, patient with a patience that made the town's patience look like haste, and I had the overwhelming and unprovable sense that they had been there longer than the waitress, longer than the man in the photograph, longer than the town's oldest record of itself: that they were older than the place that kept them, that the town had perhaps grown up around them the way a pearl grows around a grain, to hold them, to hide them, to feed whatever this was. Everything I thought I'd been uncovering, the loops, the hallway, the kept and stolen life, was only the outermost skin of a thing whose centre was here, in the black, with these girls I did not know, around this wet red figure that was older than everything.

One of them raised her head. I could not hold her face. But I felt her attention arrive on me, full and cool and complete, and I understood that I was expected, that I had been expected for longer than I had been alive. And I saw that the circle on the floor was not closed. There was a gap in it, a place, and the place was the size and shape of one more person. And I understood that I had climbed all this way, and lost all that I had lost, in order, at the very last, to be brought to the edge of it and shown where I fit.

I did not step into the circle. Whatever else is true of me, and you have seen, in the room of the friend by the window, that not everything is to my credit, I stood at the edge of that black room and did not take the last step. Or I did not take it *yet.* And in the held breath of that not-yet, in the exact instant between the arriving of her attention and whatever was meant to come next...

I woke.

I woke in a bed I knew, under a ceiling I knew, in the grey ordinary light of a morning in the country I came from, and for a long moment I did not know which of my lives was the dream. A tap running somewhere. A bird outside. The light grey and flat and beautiful in the way only the ordinary can be beautiful once you have been shown the alternative. I was home. It was over.

I have to tell you the rest, because the rest is why I've written any of this, and because you'll have to decide for yourself what it means. I gave up deciding a long time ago.

I have never found the town. I know how to research a place, and I've spent years looking for a honey-coloured town in a green valley under white mountains, and it is on no map, and no one I've asked has heard of it, and the road I remember arriving on does not, as far as I can determine, exist. A place that keeps the best day of your life forever was never going to be a place you could drive back to.

The friends are harder. I have their faces. I did, for a long time. But there is no record of them anywhere in the world outside my own head. No programme remembers enrolling them. No families remember raising them. The footage: I made myself watch all of it, and the town is there, exactly as we filmed it; but the four of us are never in a single frame together, and Ellis is in none of it at all, and the shots of the three of us are framed wrong, built around an emptiness. And there is a scar of pale light in the corner of all of it: the only proof any of us was ever there, a flaw in a lens, in the top left of a world none of us appear in.

The photograph came in the post, years ago, in an envelope with no stamp and no return address, found one morning on the mat as though walked up to the door and set down by a hand. It's a photograph of a young man in front of a small diner in golden light, laughing, wholly delighted, a slice of pizza lifted in one hand and a thumbs-up raised in the other, his smile wide and open and uncomplicated: the smile of a person who has just been told he can stay as long as he likes. The young man is me. I don't remember it being taken. By the time I understood where I was, I was long past that kind of happiness. And yet there I am.

I keep it on the wall where I'm writing this. I can't help it; it's the animal habit of a creature checking its enclosure. But I've learned never to look at it twice in the same day, because the photograph, I've come to understand, is not the thing that changes anymore. *I am.* And I'd rather not know how far along I am. I'd rather not be, like him, the last to understand my own decline.

I set out to write this because film had failed me and memory was failing me, and I thought words might be the one camera the town could not reach: that if I got them down, Ben and Miles and Ellis, the banana fries and the wrong constellations and the sentence Ben never finished, they'd be safe, there'd be at last one record that held.

I understand now what I've been doing. I've been building the sixth floor.

To keep a thing is to store it, and to store it is to take it out of the living world where it might change and grow and surprise you, and set it behind glass, perfect and lost. Every afternoon of theirs I have fixed on this page is an afternoon I have handed to the hallway. I have been, all this time, doing the town's work for it, in the town's own way, out of the town's own love, because it was love, I am nearly sure it was love, the way the old woman's face opened when we came in, the way she stood in the door with her hand half-raised and told us to come back before we left. She would want to hear everything.

*We'll tell you everything,* Ben called back. And we never did. And I am, at last, and here is what it costs.

Because as I've written them down they have begun to go. I didn't expect this and I don't know how to stop it and I'm no longer sure I'm meant to. Miles is thinnest now; I've set down the pick and the Tuesdays and the brother, and having set them down I find I reach for Miles and get the page instead of the man. And Ellis. I have to tell you about Ellis. When I began this I had three seconds of her, the ferry, the wind in her hair, her hand not quite up in time. I've written the three seconds down. And now, when I close my eyes, I cannot find her face. I have the wind and the hand and the laugh; I have the scar in the corner of every frame; I have *somebody has to stay behind the glass.* I have everything about Ellis except Ellis, everything except the one thing she spent her life making sure no camera would ever hold. I understand, finally, and too late, what she was protecting, and from what, and that she failed, and that I am the one who finished what the town began.

I wrote the dedication for this on the first day, before I understood any of it. *For the ones who were with me at the table.* When I wrote it I could still name all three. I'm writing the last page now, and I've gone back to look, and I can name two.

So I'm going to ask you the thing I've been building to since the first line, and it's the only thing I've ever asked anyone that mattered. Somewhere behind you, or ahead of you, in a town you'll never find, four travellers are coming down out of the hills, arguing about something small, so hungry they can't see how beautiful it is, and one of them is turning, even now, at the very last, to look back over her shoulder. And I can't hold her any longer. My hands are not the kind that hold; they never were; I made a whole life out of watching instead of holding, and this is the bill. You've read her. She's in these pages, in the wind and the hand and the scar of light, more of her than exists anywhere else in the world. Go back. Find her. Hold the face I'm losing. Be, for her, the thing I never learned to be: in front of the glass, and looking, and there.

She looks happy.

She was. They all were.
