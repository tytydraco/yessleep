We moved to Hartsburgh when I was 7. It's a nice little Midwestern town -- not so small that you know everyone, but small enough that almost nobody outside the area has heard of it, and where the three biggest employers are the hospital, the school system, and the local Walmart. Overall, it's a pretty normal place, for most of the year at least.

I found out about Hartsburgh's weird little quirk the year we moved in. Everyone was getting ready for normal Halloween festivities, but it was all scheduled for the day *before* Halloween. Our school Halloween parade and party, the town's parade, even trick-or-treating. Kevin Schneider even told me he heard from his older brother that the (in hindsight, probably illegal) party the high schoolers were throwing was gonna be on the night of the 30th, not Halloween proper.

It wasn't just that. The teachers reminded us there was a 5PM curfew for the 31st. It was clear that, for whatever reason, one they weren't explaining, they didn't want us going out on the night of the 31st.

I asked my parents why that was, but we'd only lived here for a few months at that point, so they hadn't found out any more than I had. They assured me it was just some tradition or something, and my dad said he'd ask a few of his coworkers about it.

I tried asking around at school, of course. None of the answers I got were that helpful.

"It's the ghosts." Randy Kluwe explained.

"Ghosts? What kind of ghosts?" I asked.

"They're from the orphanage that burned down. Back in like 1900." he said.

I knew Randy was the kind of kid that claimed his uncle worked for Nintendo and had all sorts of nutty stories. In hindsight, I never would've asked him, but I think at 7, I actually kind of believed some of what he said. Unsurprisingly, he grew up to be a cop, and once wrote me a ticket for going 24 in a 25.

The teachers I asked mostly just said it was a 'town tradition' or out of 'safety concerns', but didn't elaborate. Only two of them gave me anything more -- Mrs. Hadley claimed it was because the town reserved the 31st for a charity event with the local hospital, and Mrs. Mayne said it was because kids and teens pulled too many pranks on Halloween, so they moved it to a different day.

Even at 7, I realized those explanations contradicted each other. I think I believed Mrs. Hadley more, but even then I still had questions. What *was* the charity event? Plus, schools usually gave the kids flyers to bring home for their parents if they wanted to contribute whenever one of those was held. Why hadn't we gotten them?

That night, my dad came home from work, but he seemed a little perturbed. I asked him if he found out anything.

"It's a charity event." he said, seeming oddly distant in the moment. "The hospital brings the kids trick-or-treating that can't go out normally, and the other kids go out on the 30th to give them some space."

"Oh." I mused. "But why don't they go out on the 30th and we get the 31st?"

"It's just... tradition, I guess." he trailed off. He suddenly looked at me with a serious expression I rarely saw on him. "I want you to listen to what they said. Go out on the 30th, and stay home on the 31st. Please."

"But why?" I asked, the question every parent dreads most. "Why aren't we *allowed* out that night?"

My father thought for a moment.

"Most of them have tuberculosis." he said flatly. "They don't want it spreading."

At that age, I knew absolutely nothing about tuberculosis, and this was probably the first time I'd ever heard the word. In a weird way, this satisfied me. I think I just heard a huge, medical-sounding word and accepted it as something I wouldn't understand yet. At least, that's what I thought deep down. Outwardly, I still protested and asked my dad some variation of "why" about 10 more times before he finally changed the subject and went to go see what was for dinner.

October 30 rolled around, and it was time for our Halloween festivities at school. We had a small parade where all the kids walked the playground in our costumes, the parents visiting with their camcorders ready. After that was the Halloween party in each classroom, they gave us a little candy and a few baked goods. I was a Power Ranger, Kevin was Optimus Prime, Tim Dobin was Ghostface, although the teachers wouldn't let any of us wear anything on our heads, so everyone thought Tim was supposed to be a witch or something.

That night, we went out for our fake Halloween, thankfully able to wear our full costumes this time. Kevin used the opportunity to show off his new sneakers with the lights in the soles, the ones that flash red when he takes a step. He apparently swayed his parents into buying them for him by pointing out it'd help drivers see us in the dark. Somehow, Randy ended up going with us. I think his parents were friends with Tim's, and his actual best friend, Brandon, was sick that night, so his parents sort of foisted him on us instead.

I should probably explain at this point that Kevin's family had also only just moved to Hartsburgh, so he was only finding this stuff out as I was. I think that's why he ended up being my first friend in town, cause we were both strangers to everyone else. Tim, on the other hand, was just a nice kid, the first one to really accept these new kids with no one else to hang out with. Unsurprisingly, Kevin had his own questions about the holiday weirdness around here, but now I actually had an answer for him.

"That's stupid." Kevin said when I explained what my dad said, "Why do they get the real Halloween to themselves?"

"I don't know." I said. "But we get candy anyway, so I don't really care."

Kevin looked like he had an idea. "What if we just go out tomorrow night too?"

"How are we gonna get away with that if everybody knows about the curfew?" I asked.

"**Somebody** will be trick-or-treating, it's just not supposed to be us." Kevin explained. "If we wear our full costumes, the grownups won't know the difference."

"That's a bad idea." Tim shrugged. "My dad said weird things happen on Halloween around here."

"Weird stuff happens everywhere on Halloween, that's the whole point!" Kevin said.

"Not like here." Randy cut in, playing with the buttons on his Buzz Lightyear costume even though they didn't do anything. "You go out on Halloween, the ghosts'll get ya."

"C'mon, there's no ghosts." I chided. "It's just sick kids or something."

"Nuh-uh, I saw them." Randy shot back. "I looked out the window last year and there was a bunch of ghosts walkin' around on the lawn!"

"It was probably just kids in costumes!" Kevin insisted. "You babies can stay home, but I'm going!"

For a boy at that age, 'baby' was about the worst word that could be thrown at you.

I'm embarrassed to admit that that was enough to get me and Tim to cave. Randy, on the other hand, was staying out of this.

After a bit more griping, we made plans to go out in our costumes the next night. I have to admit, the idea of double the candy *was* kind of enticing.

The rest of the night went by like any Halloween, except the discrepancy on the calendar of course, and we each went our separate ways, our plans to meet up the next night in the backs of our minds.

The next day was... a completely normal school day. The Halloween decorations were still up, but there was no party or anything. Of course, we all talked about the hauls of candy we'd gotten the night before, swapped notes on what streets gave out the most. Things most other kids are doing on November 1.

The rest of the day after school was also pretty normal, except the presence of the local cops waving people to their houses. Chalk it up to being too young, but I hadn't even thought about who would be *enforcing* the curfew until that point, and now worried our plan might be a little tougher than I'd assumed.

But again, the hours leading up to our plan were uneventful. I got home, told my mom about school, did my homework a little early, and then waited until it was time. Sure I had things I could do to occupy my time, but I was feeling strangely anxious. This was probably the most "against the rules" I'd ever gone -- this was an ordinance from the *town government* I was defying here, not just from my parents or teachers.

Finally, the time came, 8PM, and I got ready. My plan wasn't anything graceful, it was literally just sneaking out of the house and hoping my parents didn't catch me.

I crept through the hallway toward the kitchen, wearing my Blue Ranger costume. I had to use the back door, since my parents would be in the living room by the front door, ready to hand out candy to the hospital kids. I slowly twisted the knob on the back door, praying nobody would hear me, and snuck out.

It was so much quieter tonight than it was the night before. No cackle of kids running around and playing, no distant music from Halloween parties, nothing. The air was also cooler than it was the night before and smelled like candles burning from people's jack-o-lanterns throughout the neighborhood. It was overcast except for a ragged hole in the clouds that the moon poked through. I suddenly felt strangely unnerved.

I crept around the side of the house, ducking as I passed the windows even though I'm sure I was too short to be seen out them anyway. I think it just felt more 'spy movie' to me at the time. I felt like I was really getting away with something here, so at one point, I even considered trick-or-treating at *my own* house just to see if I could get away with it, but decided against it quick -- I knew damn well my parents would recognize me.

As I got to the corner of the side and front of the house, I started to strategize how I would sneak away from the lawn without my parents seeing in case they were handing out candy. I pressed my back against the fence between our house and the neighbor's and peeked around to see if anyone was coming.

I was surprised to see there were no police out enforcing the curfew, and wondered for a second if the whole thing was just them pulling a prank on the town every year. At the intersection, I saw a group of kids turning off our street, all dressed in the same white sheets, like bedsheet ghosts. Some of them skipped, but they mostly just walked quietly, some of them even coughing as they went.

I came up with, again, a pretty basic and ungraceful plan, which was to flip around to the other side of the fence, then make a run for it.

So, I did it. I ducked around the edge of the fence to the other side, and found myself face-to-face with another group of the kids in ghost costumes. They were coming down from my neighbor's house -- I swear they hadn't been there just a second ago.

Their costumes were identical, white sheets, draped over their entire bodies. There were no holes in it for eyes or mouth, and the fabric looked filthy, stained with substances of all sorts of unpleasant-looking colors.

They were oddly stoic for kids out trick-or-treating. At first, I figured maybe they were just shy, who knows how often they met kids from outside the hospital. But for some reason I couldn't buy that. They creeped me out. From under their sheets, I could hear a faint noise, like they were wheezing and gasping for air.

"Hey, wanna come with us?" one of them asked, sounding out-of-breath despite not moving.

"Yeah, come with us." another one whispered.

I entertained the idea for just a second. Maybe they were just kids with cancer or something. Maybe I'd be doing something nice by hanging out with them. But for some reason, I just couldn't shake the feeling that something was wrong here. The way they took slow steps toward me, their hushed gasping, the claustrophobic feeling I got, almost like they were surrounding me.

"Uh, sorry... I'm supposed to be meeting..." I mumbled, mostly just looking for an excuse to run.

"Come with us!" one of them chimed in, then coughed.

"It'll be fun!" another of them said with a smile I could hear but not see.

My eyes drifted to another group of these kids turning onto our street at the intersection, followed by an adult in a similar costume.

"Michael!" I heard a familiar voice call from behind me. I didn't stop to look. I turned and ran back to my house, where my mom was hanging out the front door in her Bride of Frankenstein mask. As I ran, I heard a mass of foosteps behind me.

I hurried up the front steps and to my mom, who pulled me into the house and shut the door. A second or two later, there was knocking and banging at the door, along with voices I couldn't make out.

"What were you doing out there?" my mom demanded as my dad rounded the corner into the hallway in his Frankenstein mask. I was too scared to answer at the moment.

My dad grabbed the bowl of candy on the table and made his way to the front door.

"And what are *you* doing?" my mom gasped.

"This is what I was told to do with... them." he said. He sounded calm, if a bit sullen, but I noticed his hands shaking a bit.

My dad opened the door.

"Trick or treat!"

The group in ghost costumes was standing on the steps like regular trick-or-treaters. In the light of the porch, I could see them better. Their sheets were stained a pale gray at the edges, and on their noses and foreheads, leaving an outline where their eyes would be. The spots in front of each of their mouths was stained with some mix of either a hideous, yellow-green or an organic dark pink. There was pale gray dust staining their hands and the orange, pumpkin-shaped buckets they all carried. I heard the chorus of wheezing and gasping from under their sheets, even though the cloth didn't move to match the sound.

My dad approached them like he was trying to grab a rattlesnake by the tail, and dropped a few pieces of candy into each of their buckets. Each one said "Thank you!" after getting their candy and moved on. As their numbers thinned out, I saw another figure behind them.

They were the size of an adult, and were also draped in white, but it wasn't quite the same blank sheet as the kids. The head of the costume looked more like a nun's habit, hung low enough to obscure their face in darkness to the point that it just looked like a black void. As the kids collected their candy and took off one-by-one, I noticed my dad lock eyes with her. As the last child turned and skipped off, the nun nodded and also turned to leave.

When my parents interrogated me, I explained 'my' plan to go out to collect a second batch of candy, leaving out Tim and Kevin's involvement. I don't think they completely believed me, they still kept asking me if I was going out to meet up with friends, but I really didn't want to get them in trouble. Eventually, they relented, and made me go to bed, even watching my door to make sure I didn't try to leave again, according to my mom.

Tim and Kevin weren't in school the next day.

I was worried immediately, but there was nothing I could do besides tell my parents about it. They told the police, and until I was older, that was the last I heard of it. Tim and Kevin never came back, and apparently Tim's family never forgave Kevin's, or me, once they found out about our plan.

That was the last major incident I had on Halloween -- I wasn't dumb enough to push my luck again. Besides, it wasn't a very good feeling, thinking you were responsible for your friends' disappearances. I know it's ridiculous, but part of me felt afterwards like I should've gone with them, even though I'm sure all that would've changed is that I would've gone missing too. Sometimes I wonder what would've happened if I'd told my parents the truth about Tim and Kevin being out that night, if there's anything anyone else could've done. If maybe they hadn't gone out yet. If maybe they could still be saved.

In the years since then, I've looked into Hartsburgh's weirdness the best I could, if only so I could tell myself it wasn't my fault, but there's not a ton I can show for it.

On the playground sometime that December, I noticed something up on top of the high, wooded hill overlooking the town, something I hadn't noticed before.

There was a big, ornate-looking building, one I could only see now in winter because all the trees were bare. The second I saw it, I wondered out loud if that was the orphanage that Randy had mentioned.

"There's no orphanage." Caitlin Sapp chided from her seat on the jungle gym, that smug, all-knowing tone only a kid can produce. "My daddy said it used to be a hospital."

I thought about that for years, but there was nothing I could do about it as a kid.

Finally, a few years ago, I decided to make my way up there. Part of me hoped for answers to a question I haven't asked you yet.

I tried looking into the place as much as I could, but the internet had surprisingly little about it, mostly just that it was called Breezy Hill Sanatorium and that it closed in 1911. I tried my hand with the library, figuring they'd have more local info, but they didn't have much more. All I found out there was that it was taken over by a real Carnegie-style Old Money type, who shuttered the place a few years later cause it wasn't turning a profit. It did jump out at me though, that the place closed on October 31, that and the new owner had the same last name as Hartsburgh's current mayor.

After I mapped out how to get there, I drove up the road entering town, and turned down a dirt road leading into the woods, and further up the hill. I think my car hated me for it, what with all the bumps and noises from all the debris I was running over (and I think I did need a new muffler afterward...), but I got there.

The place was decrepit, but definitely didn't look like it'd ever been burned down, ruling out Randy's 'orphanage fire' explanation. It was three stories tall, two wings spread wide from the center, and covered in windows.

The front doors had long since been ripped off their hinges and were nowhere to be seen, so there was nothing stopping me from just walking in. The inside was somehow filthy and sterile at the same time -- empty of almost all furniture and large debris, but the walls and floor were coated in dust and cobwebs, not to mention graffiti.

I made my way out of what I guessed was the lobby and into what I also guessed was the patient area. Unlike every hospital I've ever seen, there were no individual patient rooms, only huge, long rooms that took up the entire wings of the building. There were a few old-looking hospital beds without even sheets on them, but not much else. I decided not to stick around in there. As I left, I heard a squeak from somewhere in the building, and when I got to the lobby, I saw a stairway downward, where it sounded like it came from. I decided to take it anyway.

While the building above was pretty sunny and well-lit, the basement was unbearably dark, but thankfully also pretty empty. The first thing I came across was a wheelchair standing in the middle of the hall in a pretty deliberate-looking way. I looked it over for a second, then stepped around it, trying to ignore the feeling I'd found the source of that squeaking noise.

Most of the doors also led to rooms that were empty except some discarded wheelchairs and gurneys. One even had a machine that looked like a huge tube with a bed in it, which I realized was an old-fashioned iron lung machine. I poked around for a minute or two in each, but didn't find anything noteworthy besides dust and cobwebs. Again, the place felt fully vacant and abandoned, until I got to the room at the end of the hallway.

I shone my flashlight into the room and saw a huge, metal box with a cabinet door on it. I figured this had to be either a furnace or a crematorium. Either way, as I approached the machine and got a better look at it, I noticed something that stopped me in my tracks.

The front of it next to the door was covered in dusty gray handprints, most too small and low to have been from adults. Only a few were *on* the door itself.

A pang of curiosity told me to open it, but before I could take another step, a sudden, loud **CLANG** from inside it froze me like a deer in the headlights. Another **CLANG** and the door lurching outward sent me running in the other direction. That's when I noticed, one of the handprints *wasn't* just a print.

I ran.

The banging continued as I scrambled into the hallway. As soon as I had, the wheelchair was there, slowly rolling toward me. I'd played football in high school, so jumping over this thing was nothing I wasn't used to, but as I cleared it, even more furniture had spilled out of the rooms and into the hall. I had to kick my way past wheelchairs and climb over gurneys just to get out of there, and as I reached the room with the iron lung, I realized I could hear labored wheezing and coughs from somewhere in the basement. I wasn't stopping to look, everything in me told me to just get out of there.

I must have looked like an idiot to whoever must have been watching. I certainly *felt* like one once I got outside and looked back to see nothing chasing me. At first, at least. As I was walking back to my car, I noticed gray dust on my arm. I looked closer and realized there were dusty gray handprints all over the back of my clothes.

That's the last weird incident I had in this town, but not the last one I wanted to mention.

I stopped trick-or-treating around 14, since I had no younger siblings to take out. So, every Halloween since then, both the 30th and 31st, I handed out candy in my parents' stead. What can I say, I kind of loved wearing a scary mask and jumping out at the kids. Or the living ones at least.

When I got older, my parents filled me in on the rules about the ghosts, and simple as they are, I still obey them every Halloween, even after I moved out. Wear a mask, if the kids knock on your door, you give them candy, they thank you and leave. If they find you outside... nobody knows for certain.

Well, I don't know for certain, but I have an idea.

When I was 17, I was going through the yearly ritual. The little ghost children came scampering up our front steps and I was there waiting for them with a bowl of little candy bars. Looking back, I'm surprised how routine this became considering I was already certain it involved real ghosts.

As I was handing out the candy, I saw the nun standing on the sidewalk behind the kids. I could feel her eyes piercing me, even if I couldn't see them. As the last kid scurried off with their candy, she just stood there staring at me for a moment, just enough to make me uncomfortable. I was expecting her to nod, but instead she just slowly turned her head and kept walking toward the children. My eyes followed her and eventually met the kids, when I noticed something.

Every time that last kid took a step, the soles of his shoes lit up with red lights.
