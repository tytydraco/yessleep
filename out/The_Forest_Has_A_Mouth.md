There is a heavy weight that has followed me all my life, born from a single inescapable moment, an event that occurred deep within the forest reservation behind my childhood home. It was there that I experienced something that no one will ever understand.

The police investigation into my sister's disappearance ended after the coroner's findings. They declared it inconceivable that she'd survive such prolonged exposure to the elements so she was presumed deceased. Yet, despite months of search parties surveying every square inch of those blackened hills, they never found her remains. I’m confident they never will. Only I saw what really happened. The truth is so unbelievable that it’s practically not worth telling. But I assure you, what follows is the exact order of events that led to her death.

To my dearest Samantha, I am sorry.

It was the summer of 83. The unbearable heat led my thirteen-year-old self to go exploring through the mountain forests where fresh springs were plentiful. One afternoon I discovered a particularly lovely pond where large rocks encompassed the edges of the water, perfect for diving off. What I remembered most fondly about that place, though, was the bountiful supply of yabbies that swam through there. My father was quite fond of crayfish, and so it was the afternoon before Father’s Day that I had decided to return to that pond with a net and a bait trap. I intended to go alone but my mother insisted I take my eight-year-old sister. With great reluctance, I did as I was told, and as the sun reached its hottest point above our heads, we hopped the fence and headed out into the forest.

A noticeable element of those hills was the way in which the yearly back-burning of the reservation left the trees scorched and black. After some time the leaves would grow back a bright orange before turning green again a few months later. The orange leaves always reminded me of spitting embers, as if the flames of the back-burning had left a traumatic imprint on the forest’s growth. We were surrounded then by blackened wood and amber treetops, the winding trail towards the pond still a long way yet. And so it was, like all sibling rivalries, that we bickered amongst ourselves in the blazing heat of the summer sun.

That day was so long ago now, it’s hard to remember the exact details of our conversations. What I do remember is how my sister was, always complaining.

‘How much longer? My feet hurt!’

‘We literally just left the house. Are you dumb or are you just fat?’

I wish I hadn’t been so mean to her then, but for her age she was pretty quick-witted with her comebacks. ‘You’re the fat one! I saw all the sandwiches you made. It was a whole tower!’

‘You’ll be hungry later. Then you’ll be begging me for one.’

She was exaggerating, I had only made four. It was a good thing I packed extra that day, though, because there was certainly a lot more walking to do than expected. You see, the deeper we ventured into the woods, the less familiar it began to feel. Begrudgingly, I made the decision to turn back and retrace our footsteps. I must have missed the turn off somewhere.

‘I thought you knew where we were going!’ Samatha whined.

‘I know where we’re going. We’re supposed to turn left at this large rock. It’s shaped like a flying saucer… We should have passed it by now. Do you remember seeing it?’

She shook her head.

And so we turned around and headed back in the direction we came from. It was about a minute after trudging through that same path, however, that I began to notice it wasn’t exactly as I recalled. I could have sworn the trees standing right in front of me were the ones we had just passed but along the ground there were these strange starfish-shaped mushrooms, very similar to the anemone stinkhorn but covered from top to bottom in a blackish goo. They looked like they had burst open, the slimy edges protruding out like wet tentacles. The floor of the forest was filled with them, but we hadn’t noticed them at all on our initial walk through there. Upon seeing them, I began to develop an uncomfortable feeling in my gut. I feared that we might be lost. Perhaps we got spun around.

‘Let’s just take a break for a second, I’m hungry.’ Truthfully, I needed a moment to calm myself. I just didn’t want Sam to worry, so I pulled out our lunch, one sandwich each.

Sam grabbed a stick with the intention of poking the mushrooms.

‘Don’t touch those things. You’ll probably get a disease.’

‘I’m not touching them,’ she countered, and then stabbed one of them deep enough that a dark cloud spat out from its centre.

This made her recoil away from it. ‘Ew! What is that?’

‘Spores. I told you not to touch them. Come and eat.’

She ran back and sat beside me. ‘What are spores?’

‘It’s how mushrooms spread, I think. They’re like tiny seeds which get carried by the wind so they can grow in other areas.’

‘That’s gross! What happens if you breathe in the spores? Does that mean the mushrooms grow inside *you*?’

‘I don’t know. I don’t think so. Just don’t get too close to them.’

I thought deeply for a moment. After following the main road leading up the mountain you turn left into the house which means the house is facing towards the ocean, which is East. We walked in a straight line out the backyard, so we headed west. So all we have to do is head back the other way, towards the coast. Relieved, I pulled out my compass.

The strangest thing, though, is that after straightening it out, the needle spun in a circle. I banged it a few times, being the oblivious child that I was, and of course nothing changed. To this day I only have vague assumptions regarding the sort of electro-magnetic interference that would have caused it to do that. There was something supernatural about that place, something that I will never fully understand.

Admitting defeat, I shoved the compass back into my bag. This was when the initial anxiety began to truly set in. The hinterlands are over 100,000 hectares of dense rainforest. If we continued heading in the wrong direction, we might never make it out, I feared. I tried my best to hide the truth from Samantha. I didn’t want her to worry, so I came up with a plan. For those of you who don’t live in the southern hemisphere, there is a constellation visible in our skies that points true south called the Southern Cross. We had to wait till dark.

‘Hey, Sam.’

‘Yeah?’

‘I haven’t been fully honest with you about this trip.’

She looked up at me with naive eyes. ‘Okay.’

‘We’re not actually here for yabbies.’

She groaned. ‘You dragged me out here for nothing!’

‘No, we’re here for something *even* better.’

Her face lit up at this. ‘Really?! What?’

‘I heard something about this area of the forest. Apparently, you can see fireflies at night.’ Fireflies are extremely uncommon where we’re from. We had never seen them before, only in American movies.

She stood up. ‘What?! I want to see them!’

‘Yeah, me too. Do you want to camp out here with me? It might not be true, but if they’re out here, we can catch some in a jar for Dad.’

She really loved that idea. And so we set up under a shaded spot, and tried our best to build a shelter, waiting for night to come.

Finally the sun began to set, and tangerine rays of drifting sunlight squeezed through the branches as the lingering hours of the day came to an end. I only prayed that the stars would be visible enough. Sam on the other hand was already peeking through the shrubs around us, hoping to catch an early glimpse of a firefly’s light.

‘It’s too early, Sam.’

‘It should be dark enough to see them by now.’

Well, where are the stars then? I thought, finishing off the last few droplets of our water bottle.

A few minutes later, anxiously waiting, the black of the night had crept in at last. I looked up towards the sky and what I saw caused my heart to sink. The tree tops were nowhere near as thick and far-stretching just moments ago, I know that for certain. They had somehow grown, and had twisted amongst themselves, leaving tight gaps where only endless darkness could be seen. I jumped to my feet, torch in hand.

‘Stay by the camp.’

‘Where are you going?!’ She shouted.

‘I’m just going for a walk. Don’t leave this spot.’

The thought of there being an opening somewhere played in my mind on a loop as I sped through the winding forest. It was the last saving grace. There had to be a way to see the stars. There just had to be. But no matter how far I ran, the tree tops wouldn’t budge. We were stuck, lost in the dense woods, with barely a day’s worth of food.

I headed back towards the camp, the flickering light of the campfire being my guide. At least there was that.

I slumped back under the shelter, the feeling of dread washing over me.

‘Did you see any fireflies?’

‘I don’t think they’re coming, Sam. I’m sorry but I think this was a waste of time.’

She came and joined me under the shelter. ‘Okay…’

We spent the rest of our time disappointed, and prepared ourselves to sleep through the night. I prayed that Mum and Dad would send out a search party to save us. They must be worried sick, I thought. Meanwhile, Sam wanted dinner so I gave her another sandwich.

‘Told you so,’ I said, trying to keep the tone light.

Only she ate then, I told her that I wasn’t hungry. In truth, I knew then that we had to conserve our rations as much as possible.

The following morning is when everything changed. I peeked out of the shelter, bleary-eyed, and set my sights upon the hellish landscape before me. Black. Everything. The entirety of the forest floor had been replaced by that same wet goo emanating from the mushrooms. They were all around us now, spewing puffs of spores. You could see a dense fog in the air, and with the treetops still encompassing the skies, it was as if the forest had trapped us inside a vacuum. An enclosure where the only air supply was what laid beneath the canopy.

‘We have to get out of here!’ I shouted.

‘What’s going on?’ Sam replied, rubbing her eyes as she awoke.

I pulled her to her feet. ‘Come on!’

Swinging the backpack over my shoulder, I wasted no time. Clasping her hand tight, we jumped out of the shelter and headed deep into the forest. The direction didn’t matter. Anywhere at this point. Anywhere but here. But it all looked the same. Everywhere I looked, the same black mushrooms, stretching far beyond. Just blackness, and the fog that accompanied it.

‘Please, Jordie,’ she pleaded. ‘What’s happening?’

‘I don’t know, Sam. Something isn’t right!’

‘What do you mean?’

I could no longer hold the fear in. I was physically shaking. ‘What I said about the fireflies last night was a lie. We’re lost. And we need to get out of here. Now!’

‘Are we going to die?!’

‘I don’t- No! No, we’re not going to die. There’s a way out. There has to be. We just need to stay calm.’

So calmed down is what we did. I held Sam close, wiping away the tears from her face. I just needed to think. After all, the fog hadn’t killed us. And breathing felt just fine, perhaps a bit cold in the early forest mist, but otherwise, just fine. That’s when I realised. Find water! If we can find a stream, which the hinterlands were covered with, we could follow it down the mountain which should lead us back out to civilisation.

The only question was, which way to go? I guess it didn’t matter. So further into the forest we went.

‘Why did you lie to me about the fireflies?’ She asked, her tearful eyes shining up at me.

‘Because I was scared. And I didn’t want you to know.’

She was silent.

‘But it’s okay now. We’ll find our way out. I know what I’m doing now.’ I hoped that was true.

‘What is all this? It’s icky! I don’t like it.’

‘Just try not to step in it too much.’

We tried our best to walk along the path, around the wet bulbous things protruding from the ground. I remember having the strangest feeling like we were being watched. But I couldn’t quite place it. We’re alone, I thought. But there’s something otherworldly here. The faster we get out of this place, the better.

We walked along the same sinister black muck for hours. We were getting tired. But I didn’t want to rest. Not until I knew we were safe.

‘Jordie, can we sit down for a bit?’

‘Just a little bit farther.’

‘But I’m tired, Jordie-‘ Which is when I felt her hand slip from mine. She had passed out.

‘Sam!’ I caught her falling body, her weight causing me to tumble to the ground. My hands and elbows were covered black by the tar now. ‘Sam! Can you hear me?’ I shook her body, trying to bring her back to consciousness, which is when I noticed that her skin looked odd. There were red spots all along it, similar to a rash but more organised, like there were patterns across it. Just neatly strategised pieces of dead skin.

She violently awoke. Coughing and heaving. Thank God!

‘Sam, are you alright? Can you breathe?’

She nodded as her coughing finally ceased. Wiping away the drool from her mouth, I helped her back onto her feet.

‘I’m sorry. Let’s just sit for a while.’

It was silent in the forest now. Any bird chirps caused by the commotion had settled. Frankly, it was nice to catch my breath. And I hadn’t eaten since yesterday, I realised. So we rationed out the final sandwich between ourselves, and after another minute, headed onwards through that same old mountain.

I started to feel almost bored after a few more hours, seeing the same black goo stretching on for kilometres ahead. A feeling I’d immediately regret upon seeing the morbid horror splayed out in front of us. It was a flock of birds, lying dead on the ground, as if a healthy nest had somehow collapsed and had fallen from the tree above.

‘Don’t look, Sam. Just keep moving ahead.’

‘What happened to them?’

‘I don’t know.’

It didn’t make any sense. The absurdity of it left me feeling quite sick. I just wanted to get away from it. But I always remember the way it looked. The way it seemed like the remains were being swiftly devoured by the earth around it. And the webbing. That strange mycelium webbing, jutting out from their decaying corpses.

As the day grew on, I noticed that Sam’s pace behind me was getting slower. She was really beginning to struggle under the pressure.

’Jordie, I’m thirsty.’

I was as well. ‘Sorry, Sam. We drank all the water already.’

The dehydration caused by the intense heat of the afternoon had given me a raging headache. We had to be getting close to a stream by now, I thought. Surely. But at this point, the sun was beginning to set again. The most logical course would be to use the final lingering rays of light to build a shelter. I couldn’t bear the thought of spending another night in that god-forsaken place. But we had no choice. Our shelter did well enough for us, but the dampness of all the wood caused by the black muck meant there wasn’t a chance of starting a fire. We spent the first hours of the night cold, hungry, and huddled together under our pathetic camp, praying for sunlight to return.

Sam was coughing, she had been doing a lot of it for most of the day.

‘Are you okay?’ I asked. ‘Are you sick?’

Her voice was weak. ‘It’s not a cold. But it doesn’t feel good.’

‘Just lie down for a bit.’ I felt her forehead. She had the most intense fever.

The forest was mostly quiet, save for Sam’s wheezing. Until suddenly, I heard a nearby rustle in the bushes, and hushed her. She did her best to stifle her breath, and the stillness left behind was enough to hear the sounds of something sniffing. We immediately turned to each other. Looking deep into her petrified eyes, I became certain that she had heard the same thing. Something predatory, and big. It circled the outskirts of our camp, persisting as if it was searching for something. It just wouldn’t leave. And then as it got closer to our tent, I could hear the most bizarre sound. A mechanical clicking. *Click. Click. Click.* It was so unnatural, and yet I was certain that it was something predacious. And it wanted us. I thought there and then that I was about to die. We both did. Then suddenly, for reasons still unknown to me, it sped off like a flash into the woods. I have never been as scared again as I was that night. Sam began crying, and in the dreamy fog of my own mind, I could only think one thing. Please, God, get us home safely.

I didn’t sleep a wink then. Only Sam did, eventually tiring herself out from all the weeping. I just sat there all night long, peeking out of the tent, keeping guard. Without the fire I could barely see what was in front of me, only the abstract foliage illuminated by the dim moonlight. Though, sometimes, my tired eyes would start to hallucinate images moving in the dark, tall monstrous creatures moving about. I was apprehensive. Deep down I knew that if that same animal returned, or heaven forbid something worse, I wouldn’t stand a chance against it. But I couldn’t rest. No matter how sluggish my body and mind became, I couldn’t let the forest take us. And worse still, I began to develop a cough. Just like the one that had been troubling Sam throughout most of the day. I didn’t know then what gloomy afflictions the forest was producing within us, nor how little time we had left before its ghastly symptoms took hold.

Eventually, hours passed and the dawn of the new day had finally crept in, that same shimmering black goo across the forest floor making itself known once again. I peered over at Sam lying next to me, the gentle sunlight revealing her now, and became struck with the gruesome sight of her. She looked like she had the plague. She was covered in pustular overgrowths, and her skin had indents in them, like the skin irritations she had before had somehow festered and were now decaying into blackish wounds. Most terrifying of all, though, were the small strands of mycelium growing inside her, close to the surface of her skin. Tiny roots running alongside her veins. They were popping out of her pores like strands of hair. Every second spent breathing in that toxic air had been slowly eating away at her from the inside out.

We couldn’t wait another moment. I picked her up and hoisted her onto my back. We’re getting out today, I asserted. No exceptions. The alternative was too tragic a thought to bear. I carried her as best as I could, but the lack of water, food, and sleep over the previous days had left me feeble. My legs wobbled beneath me, each step feeling insurmountable, but I had to persevere. I just had to.

‘Everything hurts, Jordie.’

Hearing her painful cries only broke my spirit. ‘I know, Sam. Just hold on.’

I carried on for kilometres ahead, Sam whimpering in my ear the whole time, pleading for the pain to stop. I felt so helpless. I knew that she was dying. I didn’t want to accept the truth but the mutations of the forest were too far along now.

I made it perhaps a quarter of the way up a further incline when my legs finally gave out and we crashed onto the wet ground. It felt so cold. Sam gazed up at me with half-shut eyelids, murmuring quietly. She was saying my name.

‘Sam, I’m sorry. I can’t. I’m trying… I’m really trying.’

‘…Jordie.’

I knew what was coming. ‘Hey, remember that time… when I was really sick. And Mum let you stay home with me? …Remember that?’

She muttered something under her breath.

‘I acted so mad because it was so unfair that Mum let you have the day off school. But really… I was secretly happy about it. I didn’t want to spend the day alone. And you were there for me, but I was so mean to you.’

'…It’s okay.’ She smiled as she said it.

‘I’m here, Sam. I’m here. Please… don’t leave me.’

Her head drifted to the side. I could tell that every word she said was painful for her. ‘I want to see the fireflies, Jordie…’

I could barely get the next words out. For her sake, though, I tried my best to hold back the tears. ‘You have to stay awake to see them. They’re here but you have to stay awake.’

‘…Jordie.’

‘Please, Sam.’

'Jordie…’ Her quiet murmurs faded out like a light. She died in my arms then. The pain she felt had stopped.

Over the many years spent recounting this story, this is the one part I always gloss over. Out of all the supernatural horrors that I experienced, seeing her in that state, the way her soul drifted from her eyes is the one thing that truly haunts me. I collapsed into her, just holding her. It felt like an eternity, just sitting in that desolate place, curled up. I didn’t want to move. But I couldn’t leave her there. I lifted her up, practically dragging myself up the rest of the incline. 

I tried to bring her home, but that’s when the forest found me. The ground shook violently beneath my feet as a heavy gust of wind came flooding across the hills, whistling through the air, sounding almost like a voice. I tried my best to run, adrenaline pumping through my body. Something was coming, but it wasn’t a beast. It was the forest itself. All around me, I could see it. The forest had finally shown its true form. It was alive, and it was staring into me. Eyes everywhere, along the trunks of the trees, and the mushrooms themselves. Just piercing eyes observing my every move. I felt so vulnerable. The canopy above tightened, and the poisonous gas thickened. From the bushes came this wave of rhythmic sound, a skittering across the forest floor. It surrounded me. I couldn’t stop to look but in my peripherals I could make out the source of it. Swarms of large insects, crawling and wriggling towards me. They wanted her. All I could do was keep moving but I wasn’t fast enough. They started climbing up my legs, gnawing into me, acid spewing from their mouthparts, burning my skin. I tripped and fell, and Sam hit the ground. They jumped on top of her. I tried desperately to pull them off, these thick centipede-like creatures with mandibles the size of thread snips. But they were cutting into me. The pain was agonising, and I left her. I wish I hadn’t, but I did. I ran as far as I could, barely able to see what was in front due to the fog.

But then it happened. A burst of blinding light, the ground falling out from under me, and a splash. It was water! And as my eyes adjusted I could make out the familiar shapes. Large rocks encompassing the edges, perfect for diving off. It was the yabbie pond. That stupid yabbie pond that sent us on this treacherous journey in the first place. I couldn’t believe it. All this time we had been wandering in a circle. I swam upstream, hesitating to look back towards the woods, but I couldn’t help myself. And there, just across the pond, peeking out from behind a tree, the beast from the night before. I could hear its mechanical call, the same way it did around our camp. *Click. Click. Click.* It was a deer. It had antlers like branches towering above its head, twitching in place. And the strangest of all were its eyes. It had four of them, all perfectly symmetrical. The forest had possessed it, mutated its form. Like a predator standing dormant behind a glass window, I knew it couldn’t cross the barrier. It just stood there, watching me. Then it turned and wandered back behind the fog. I was alive, but I didn’t want to be. Not without Sam. I didn’t deserve it, I failed to save her. All I could do was lower my head in shame and cry.

After a while, I swam to the edge of the water, and stumbled back the way that we came, back along the trail towards the house. Strangely enough, I noticed that my fears about the reservation had vanished. The forest had somehow shifted. The entity that controlled the woods, the mushrooms, the creatures, all of it. It was gone. I was just so relieved to see green grass again. I could even feel my throat beginning to clear up.

Then, as the afternoon rolled around and the sun began to set, I was greeted by the most bittersweet sight. I couldn’t believe my eyes. There were fireflies. Actual fireflies. They glimmered in the trees around me, levitating like lanterns towards the night sky. They were lighting the way back. I think Sam was trying to guide me, back towards the fence. Finally upon reaching it, I gripped it tight, feeling the smoothness of the wood beneath my tattered fingers. I had made it home.

The events that came after were a mess. I won’t bore you too much with the details of the case. I was hospitalised, psychologically evaluated, and interviewed multiple times by police. They took my clothes as evidence and found small traces of Sam’s blood. Rumours spread of me murdering her but they never found her remains, even after months of search parties. I knew they never would. The entity had left. All the black goo would have dried out, flaking into ash amongst the rest of the charred remains in the forest. Everything else returned to the soil. So it went that the extreme lack of evidence caused her to be ruled as a missing person, presumed dead. Those years were the hardest for me, and my relationship with my parents was never the same after the event. They said they believed me as a child but I know they never did. They were of course grateful I was alive but deep down they always blamed me for my sister’s disappearance. My mother went to her deathbed asking me what really happened. So I thought in her final moments I’d offer her mercy. I told her that Sam drowned in a river trying to save me. She smiled, seeming content with that, and then departed.

To those reading this, heed my warning. Never go wandering deep into the woods. There are things that humans are not meant to see. Things that cannot be reasoned with. They only devour, it is their nature. And they will not hesitate to take everything from you.

I still find it impossible to sleep most nights. The guilt I feel consumes me, and I know that I will always feel this way. If I had been just a little bit stronger, I could have brought her home with me. I had the chance to save her, but I didn’t.

I’m so sorry. I wish you could have seen the pond, and the fireflies, you would have loved them. I wish you could’ve had the chance to grow up alongside me. 

Sam, I wish you were here.
