The first thing Frank handed me was the key to my own car. We were in a car rental lot, next to the minivan the agency had booked for us. I'd parked two spaces down. I thanked him and put the key back in my coat before it occurred to me that I'd been sure it was still there. I checked the other pocket anyway. He waited while I did it. “Everybody says thank you,” he said.

I was driving rideshare in a car with a window that came down by itself on the expressway. I'd quit a job supervising deliveries to start my own courier business. It lasted thirteen months. A guy called about my last van the week Frank hired me. I told him I'd scraped the lettering off already. The scraper was still in its package on my kitchen table.

Frank hired me through a local car service that farmed out private jobs. He asked if I had a recent work physical. It seemed fair enough coming from somebody who was about to spend ten days in my passenger seat. I sent the clearance sheet from my old job. It had the lifting test and my answer to whether I'd ever fainted. He paid the car service in advance. They were supposed to release the money at the end of the trip. He'd booked me my own room at every stop.

He was taller than I'd expected. I'd looked him up and seen a man in a burgundy dinner jacket pulling lit cigarettes out of a singer's sleeve. The man by the minivan had combed his white hair over a spot he'd colored in too dark. He wore good shoes with elastic laces. When he bent for the handle of his case, his right knee gave. He caught himself on the van and stood there for about five seconds before he asked if I'd mind getting it.

The black case was about the size of a carry-on, with metal corners and a spot on the lid where a nameplate used to be. I could barely lift it. Frank wanted it on the floor in front of his seat, where he could keep a foot on it. I offered to clear a space in the back. “He's had worse seats,” Frank said. I looked at him, and he said, “So have I. Go on and put it down.”

He said he wanted to look in on a few people from the old show before we got to his old theater out west. He still owned the building, though he hadn't put on a show there in years. Whatever was wrong with him had started in his right hand. Now it was in his legs and making it hard to swallow. His doctor had given him an idea of how much time he had. Frank said he meant to spend some of it eating pie in states he'd only ever seen through a bus window. He gave me the atlas his wife Jean had used to book their tours. Next to one of the motels she'd written that they should get a divorce if he ever made her stay there again. That first evening he called two numbers out of the back of the atlas. He left one message about a dog somebody used to have. On the other call he listened for a while, said, “No, I understand,” and put the phone away. I thought he was getting in touch with people who'd stopped expecting to see him.

Frank taught me a card trick in a diner one morning. He'd watched our waitress look toward the cook every time the bell rang. He got her to sign the two of clubs. When the bell went, I slipped her card under my side plate and held up a different one. She'd put a smiley face under her signature. She checked for it herself when I let her lift the plate, then laughed and grabbed my elbow. She wanted me to do it for the guy at the register, who'd been having a bad week. Frank let her tell us why. Then he paid for the guy's breakfast and handed me the receipt to slip under his plate.

Frank claimed he'd done a private show for a country singer's bus driver, who pulled out his own deck after dinner and showed him something he'd stumped her with. “It was all right,” Frank said. He kept trying it with his bad hand for most of the afternoon. At breakfast the next morning he asked a waitress if she'd mind turning down a television the size of a bedsheet. If you wanted to see a man's pores, he told me, you ought to have to buy him dinner.

Sometimes his hand lost its grip on a fork and he'd ask to see mine for a trick, as if he'd meant to put his down. After a day or two I knew to take the lid off his coffee before I put it in the cup holder. He knew about the business by then. One of the guys I'd employed had let me pay him a month late because his wife could cover the rent. Frank asked if I still talked to him. I said we both pretended to be busier than we were. Frank told me Jean once took two buses in the rain to return a pair of shoes she'd borrowed for an audition. The girl who lent them had told her to keep them. “She wanted to be able to call the girl again,” he said.

A refrigerated trailer pulled in beside us at a fuel stop. The unit on the trailer gave two hard knocks as it started. Frank grabbed my sleeve so fast that the cup between his knees fell over. Two softer knocks came from the case under his feet. He put both hands on the lid. “Keep going, please,” he said. He'd gone the color of the napkin in his lap. I drove to the end of the lot before I asked if he wanted to get out.

He said he'd spent years waiting for the noise of a stage lift stalling with somebody still up on it. A motor could give you a fright a long time after you'd stopped working around it. I looked back and saw the driver cleaning his windshield with the refrigeration unit running. Frank took the napkins I handed him and kept the case between his feet while he dried his shoe.

That night I brought him the good pillow from my room. He'd told me he could sleep if he was propped up. The connecting door was open a crack. Through it I saw him sitting on the carpet with his back against the bed and the case across his legs. The lid was up an inch. I heard a wet click, the kind you make with your tongue if you pull it off the roof of your mouth. “There you are,” he said. “Easy. I can feel you trying.” He waited long enough for a person to answer. “I know,” he said. “I've been asking too much.”

I set the pillow down and knocked on the frame. He was on his feet when he opened the door. It must have hurt him to get up that fast. A bit of white stuff stuck to the heel of his right hand. He folded it into his palm before he reached for the pillow. He asked if I wanted to see what Jean looked like when they met. I'd once driven a woman to a pharmacy to return an oxygen tank. She'd brought her husband's ashes along because she didn't know where to leave him. I decided there was something of Jean's in the case. I sat down and looked at the pictures.

In the first photo Jean was leaning on him to fix her shoe in an empty theater. Frank remembered the song playing in the lobby and what she'd said when he asked her to marry him. Then he showed me one from the stage. He was grinning beside a woman with confetti stuck to her cheek. She had her right hand clamped around her left wrist. Her cuff had come open, and something dark and shiny showed inside it. There was a corner of a cabinet behind her.

“That one went perfectly,” Frank said. I asked if she'd hurt herself. He took the photo back and looked at it with the same expression he got when he was trying to close his bad hand. “That's Ruth. You'll meet her in a few days.” He slid it into an envelope in the atlas. There were others tucked in there, each marked with a woman's name in Jean's handwriting.

We reached Ruth's place late in the afternoon on the sixth day. There were two jars of jam in a bag hanging from the mailbox for the mail carrier. I took Frank up to the porch and went back for the case. He asked me to bring the atlas too. He said Ruth would recognize Jean's notes. He kept smoothing his coat while he waited. I thought this might be a woman he'd been in love with.

A man with some gray in his beard opened the door. Frank smiled at him and said, “Paul.” Paul got as far as smiling back. Then he saw the case. “She told you no when you called,” he said. A woman with her left hand tucked under her right elbow pushed the door wider. She looked past Frank at the case. “How long since you could get it to shut all the way?” she asked.

Paul wanted Frank to stay on the porch, but Ruth had me find him a chair inside. Through the kitchen door I could see a lemon cake with a corner cut out of it. Frank looked at it and swallowed. Ruth asked if he'd eaten. He said he'd had a tuna sandwich at a gas station. “You hate tuna,” she said, the way you'd remind somebody he'd left his keys in the door again. I was about to offer them both cake when the case knocked twice.

The clasps were shut, but the lid lifted at the hinge and settled. It did it again. Frank's hand slipped off his knee and he caught it by the wrist. Ruth leaned on the lid with her good arm. “Paul, get the strap off the coat rack,” she said. He stood there looking at her. “The one we use for my arm. It's long enough.”

At that point I still thought there might be a machine in the case, something they both needed for whatever was wrong with them. I helped Paul get the strap through its buckle. Frank's right hand lay open on his thigh. The white stuff from the hotel ran down out of his cuff. At the base of his thumb it spread so thin I could see it jump with the twitch in his thumb. Then I saw it jump when his thumb was still. I tightened the strap until the lid held.

“Come look, since you're looking anyway,” Ruth said. I followed her into the kitchen with Paul, who stood in front of her when she started to take her cardigan off. She told him I was already the one who'd brought the case in. He asked if I'd touched Frank's wrist. When I said I hadn't, he took the sweater from his mother and held it so the sleeve wouldn't catch.

Her left forearm was about half as wide as the right. The wrist hooked in. Her thumb and little finger curved toward the space where the other three should have been. She set her elbow on the table and tried to make a fist. Three knuckles rose under the skin on the inside of her forearm, pointing back toward her elbow. The skin over the middle one went white. It took her a moment to get her breath before she could ask Paul for a tea towel to rest her arm on. It had hurt like that for decades.

Paul told people she'd burned her hand making jam. He'd thought she'd hurt it when she fell off a stage on tour. “That was my hip,” Ruth said. “This was the cabinet.” I brought her the picture from the atlas. She put the picture flat and touched the bit of cabinet behind her. She said she'd put her left arm through a slot in one end. Six feet away, Frank had opened a glass door and a hand had come out. She could feel a woman from the audience forcing a wedding ring over one of her knuckles, even though her elbow hadn't moved. “You can let go now,” she'd told the woman. The woman said her hands were in her lap. Ruth could still feel something turning the ring. When Frank brought her out for the bow, he pulled her cuff down himself.

“I told him I could feel my fingers going in the wrong way,” she said. “He told me to clap for the woman who lent us the ring. I clapped as best I could.” She got her arm off the table and looked at Paul. “The next week I taught Marcy how to stand in there. She bought you that pool you used to have on the back porch.” Paul asked if she meant the one with the yellow slide. For a second he sounded like he wanted to go out and play in it. He asked if Marcy knew what had happened to the hand. “She could see I couldn't open it,” Ruth said. “I told her he was changing the trick before she went on.” Paul picked up the corner of the photo and put it down again. “Is Marcy coming today too?” he asked.

A chair scraped in the other room. Frank was down on the carpet with the case across his right arm. He was trying to tell me how to move him, though it took him a few tries to get anything out. One clasp had sprung. The strap was the only thing keeping the lid from opening more than an inch. A wet fold filled the gap, like the lip of a horse pressed up against a board. While I watched, a piece of it flattened and slipped under Frank's cuff. It drew the loose skin above his wrist into a ridge. His fingers stopped jerking. The rest of him went right on shaking.

Ruth came in with the tea towel still over her arm. She told me to press on the loose corner. The thing in the gap was warm under my palm. For a second it gave. Then it pushed back hard enough to straighten my elbow. Frank got his sleeve flattened against his wrist. I heard another knock from inside the case, and the fold sank out of sight. When he could speak he asked Ruth to get in the van. “I did enough of that for you,” she said.

Paul had his phone out. Frank asked him to wait. He turned his right hand over so Paul could see where the white edges went under the skin. “If somebody pulls it off, I bleed,” he said. “If my heart stops, it'll let go and look for another one.” He said he'd brought one piece with him. He still had the rest of it on water at the theater. Paul asked him how long it had been since a doctor saw it. Frank said he'd been in and out of hospitals for months. The trick was to keep the sleeve down when they stuck a needle in the other arm. Paul turned his screen off without putting the phone away.

Ruth said they ought to shut the water off and get the lines out of the cabinet while there was still time. She asked who had the keys. Frank had mailed them to Nina, who used to work on the show. She'd agreed to meet him and help with the cabinet. When Ruth asked what he'd told her about the water, he said he'd told her it was part of an old effect. The cabinet had been off the bill by the time Nina started working for him. Ruth reached for her sweater. “I think I'd better meet Nina,” she said.

After Ruth left the show, she'd heard from Marcy once. A card had come one winter signed in somebody else's handwriting. Paul asked why she'd ever go back with Frank. She said she'd tried to take the cabinet apart once before. She'd got as far as the sidewalk and heard water running inside. This time she wanted to see the water shut off. I said I could call the car service and have them send somebody for the van. Frank sat up enough to look at me. “Have them release all your pay,” he said. “Tell them I ended the trip.” Then he asked if I'd pull his jacket out from underneath him. I did. He left it at that. I'd been getting ready to argue with him about the money. I had a whole answer for him if he tried to hold any of it back. It bothered me that I never got to use it.

I stayed because Ruth was going to the theater either way. I could at least get her there myself, and I meant to be there when she wanted to leave. Paul made her put on her seat belt before he handed her the sweater. He had a couple of calls to make and said he'd follow in his car. “If I come in and ask you to leave, will you come?” he said. “I know, honey,” Ruth told him. It was the same voice she'd used about the tuna. He kept his hand on the roof while I started the van. Frank had the strapped case between his shoes again. He missed the seat belt buckle twice and asked me to do it for him.

It was dark by the time we reached the theater. It was the narrowest building on the block. Nina was in the alley with her truck's flashers on. She had reading glasses stuck through a buttonhole. She said she'd built sets and run sound for Frank. Now she fixed machines in a commercial laundry. “When a dryer starts making a noise, people want you to do something about it,” she said.

Nina had the cabinet on two dollies. Frank had asked her to bring a truck with a lift gate, so they could get the cabinet out without a crew. She wanted to know if it was supposed to get any lighter with the water off. She'd found the shutoff in a cleaning closet, beside a bucket with a hose running into it. “I shut the tap and watched the hose empty the bucket,” she said. “Is there a pump in it?”

The alley door opened onto one side of the stage. Nina had wedged the door open. The cabinet stood a few yards in, over a floor grille, with its leaded glass facing the seats. A small work light was clipped above the window. There were two old trunks beyond it and a trapdoor at the far end of the stage. The end of the cabinet nearest us had a slot lined with something that looked like the rubber on a mail drop. Ruth went straight to it. She asked Frank if it had stayed in there all these years. “In the cabinet and in the lines,” he said. “It needs the water to get around.”

Ruth wanted a look at the hoses before we tried to roll the cabinet any farther. Nina brought a standing lamp over and looped the spare cord around its base so we wouldn't roll a dolly over it. She and I took a corner each. I'd moved vending machines before. Nina asked if they'd ever sent me out with a pallet jack with one good wheel. I said they had. “I guessed from the swearing,” she said. I hadn't realized I'd been doing it out loud. She handed me a shop rag for the brass pull I had hold of. Water was coming out around the screws.

We lowered the cabinet onto the dollies to get a better grip. Nina aimed her flashlight at the grille. Two braided hoses came up through it, looped under the cabinet where you couldn't see them from the seats. Somebody had spliced a piece of clear hose into one. In that piece I saw rows of pale ridges turn toward the light, one after the other. I was telling Nina to get back when the hose snapped taut. Her end of the cabinet lifted, and the dolly shot out from under it.

I reached for the end that was coming down toward her and put my fingertips in the slot. The lip closed on them and drew me in to the elbow. I felt something go under my nails. For a second I thought I'd put my hand into a brush. I tried to turn it, but the tips of my fingers stayed where they were. I braced my knees against the wood and pushed. The cabinet rolled a few inches on the other dolly and took me with it.

The corner had caught Nina above the ear. She got a chock under the remaining dolly and came around to the glass. With Nina's end of the cabinet on the floor, I could see the glass from where I was stuck. Nina lifted it by the catch. She pulled her head back when she saw what was inside. It took her a second to look at me. “Where's it got you?” she asked. I tried to tell her it was moving up my fingers. All I got out was her name, twice.

Frank told me to lean in so it had less of me to pull. There was a wet handprint on the velvet inside the window, with the fingers spread farther apart than mine could go. A strip of tissue slid down over it. Hard little bars showed through the strip, bending in behind my knuckles. I felt the middle finger draw backward into my palm. The tip came out through a crease at my wrist, beside the lip of the slot. I could see it from outside. When it moved, the nail snagged on my sleeve. I felt the cloth from inside the finger.

I tried to hit it with my left hand. Nina caught my wrist before I reached the glass. “You hit it and those fingers come off,” Frank said. He put the case on the cabinet and undid the strap. His right hand slipped off the good clasp, so he braced the wrist against the slot. The lip lifted at once. Frank pushed his cuff up with his chin. The white strip opened over a dark piece that fluttered like a fish's gills.

One end of the strip joined the lip of the slot. The end still on Frank drew tight until his hand went purple. I felt something slacken around my wrist. Ruth leaned under the glass and found a square brass shaft. “The return still works?” she asked. Frank said yes. She dug a wrench out of Nina's bag because he'd taken the handle off.

Ruth asked if I wanted her to try to put my fingers back. She said it would probably hurt worse coming out. I wanted her to do it before I had to answer. She waited until I said yes, then turned the shaft to a scratch somebody had filed in the brass. Water rushed out into a pan under the glass. The bars started to lift. My middle finger eased back along my hand, and the skin bunched around it where the knuckle should've been. Frank's knees went. Nina let the glass drop so she could catch him by the belt. His wrist came clear of the slot, and the bars came down again.

The back of my wrist hit something solid in the slot. A seam opened in the skin just below the knob of bone. Something thin went in there. I put my other hand on the inside of my elbow because I thought I could stop it when I felt it pass. My skin rose under my palm. Then my heart seemed to beat twice on the same beat, and I watched a row of little knots cross my chest under my shirt. I felt each one go.

Nina eased the wrench a little farther around. The bars lifted just enough for Ruth to ease my hand through the slot while Nina held me up. My middle finger flopped inward when Ruth let go. A flat cord trailed from the seam in my wrist back into the cabinet. Frank said it had found a vein. Nina tried lifting it, and I felt it pull just below my collarbone. I asked her to put it down. She did. I tried one step toward the door and the pull put me on the floor.

Frank was sitting against the wall by the alley door with the case open on his lap. It looked packed with dirty gauze. I could see little openings in the thicker parts along the corners. Frank put his wrist over one of them and drew back his cuff. He waited until he found a place it could get its mouth on him. His breathing settled. He made a fist with the hand he'd had to catch when it slid off his knee.

There was a phone on a tripod beside the display window. My arm was on the screen, and the timer was still going. Nina stopped it. She picked up an envelope from the crate she'd set the tripod on. She said Frank had sent it with the keys and told her to open it once the women were there. She slid out two sheets and set them on the boards where I could see them. The first was a schedule with three women's names. Ruth was first, then twenty minutes for recovery, then Marcy. There were phone numbers in the margin. At the bottom Frank had written to keep the camera running through the applause. Ruth was the only one who'd come.

My name was on the second sheet. He'd copied my answer about fainting from the work physical and circled the weight I'd lifted on the test. Below that was a sketch of a man with an arm in the slot. A line ran from inside the cabinet to the place where I'd felt my heart jump. He'd written his own name next to the man, crossed it out, and put mine there. Under that it said to have the driver lie still for ten minutes after the connection came out.

“What did you need me for?” I asked. Frank said the water was enough to keep things alive in the cabinet. To put a piece of somebody back, it needed blood until their own could get to it. “Mine worked last time. I wasn't sure I could do it again.” He told me he'd found the thing in a drain under the stage. A rat was alive on one side of the grate with two of its feet on the other. The toes curled when he touched the rat with a broom. Jean wanted him to call somebody at a university. Frank took the grate to a man who built cabinets for escape acts.

Ruth was holding my hand straight where I couldn't. Frank said he'd send Marcy the film if it worked. She might watch it if she knew Ruth was in it. “How many times did you actually get it to work?” Ruth asked. Frank said twice with Jean. “You got the thumb back on twice,” Ruth said. “Could she feel it?” Frank looked down at the worn spot on the lid of the case. “I used to do up her coat in the morning,” he said. “I'd get it done before she asked.”

I asked when he'd been going to tell me. Frank said he'd nearly asked me in the van a few times. “What did you think I'd say?” I asked. He looked at Ruth's arm. “I thought I'd ask after you saw what I'd done to her.” I wanted to tell him I'd had him figured out since the noise at the fuel stop. I'd spent that whole drive looking over at him after I said anything funny to see if he'd laughed.

Nina called for an ambulance. She gave the alley entrance and told them my arm was caught, which was close enough. Then she asked Ruth if we could pinch the cord at the cabinet and cut it there. The cord gave a slow twist between Ruth's fingers. She said she'd seen a man go at one with shears. The part inside him tightened and tore when he pulled away. “Get the water running through the return again,” she told Frank. “You saw it let him partway out.” He got his left foot under himself and stood.

Two knocks came from under the floor. They were the same ones I'd heard from the case at the fuel stop, only now I felt them through my shoes. One corner of the cabinet rose and stayed up. Water surged one way and then the other in the clear hose. Each time it backed up against a gray wad in the line, the cord in my wrist tightened. A loop of hose straightened under one of the trunks and dragged it sideways. Wet strips started squeezing through the gap under the lid.

Frank pointed across the stage at a brass wheel by the trapdoor. He said there was a tank underneath and the wheel would let what water was left run into the cabinet. Nina went to open it. Frank set the case on the cabinet and took the wrench in his left hand. He brought it back to the scratch Ruth had used to open the return. There was a second mark a little farther around. Ruth put a finger beside the first one. “This is as far as you need to go for him. The other one opens the panel.” Frank said he just needed to get me loose. He looked straight at her as he turned it past the second mark.

A brass panel below the glass swung down. The air from behind it smelled like the inside of a cast I'd worn as a kid. A thick sheet slid over the rim. I could see bars like the ones that had closed around my knuckles, running down both sides. It went right past me and struck Ruth on the back of her wrist. She tried to tuck her arm under her chin. “I told you I could feel it,” she said to Frank. I could hear her over the water even though she'd barely raised her voice.

I got up on one knee and took hold of her cardigan with my left hand. For a second I had her against me and the sheet had her by the arm. Then the cord hauled me down to my knees. Frank kept saying, “I can do it this time.” I saw Ruth look at him. She tried once to pull her arm away from the sheet. Then she put her good hand on my shoulder. “Help me get it off,” she said.

Nina came back from halfway to the trapdoor. She picked up the spare wheel chock and hit the bars where they crossed the rim. The sheet folded where she hit it. One of the hoses jerked hard enough to split a joint at the grille. A strip came out of Frank's case and went down the front of the cabinet, following the water. It wrapped itself around the split and squeezed until the leak stopped. Then the whole fitting pulled out of the floor. Frank tried to put his shoe over the end of the hose. His foot slid off and he went down against the cabinet. The case slid off after him and landed by his leg. The sheet that held Ruth reached across to the back of his neck.

Nina got to the wheel. I heard her shout, then the sound of water moving under the boards. The cord in my wrist was tight enough to rub the raw edge of the cut. I'd have been glad if that was all I felt. Something under my collarbone took a better hold every time my heart beat. I leaned forward until I could breathe, and when I tried to stand I couldn't feel the boards under my feet.

Somebody at the alley door said, “Mom.” I saw Paul's hand on the frame. Ruth yelled for him to stay where he was. One of the strips from the trunk had nearly reached the door. Its end was tangled in the lamp cord. Paul grabbed the slack by his feet and pulled. The stand went over across the strip. He kept pulling until the strip tore loose at the trunk and curled back under the fallen lamp. I heard the bulb break. The strip was still moving in the light from the alley.

Ruth was on her knees at the open panel. “Give me a hand shutting this,” she said. I got my left hand under the edge while she put a shoulder to it. The panel started to close across the sheet. The part inside the cabinet tried to pull away from the pinch. On Ruth's arm, the skin over the three knuckles went white. Then it opened and the fingertips came through, nails first. The sheet drew them down toward the empty places in her hand. I felt the cord thicken at my wrist. Ruth caught the fingers with her good hand so they wouldn't have to hang by themselves.

Frank was still slumped against the side of the cabinet with the sheet over his neck and his right wrist in the case. I heard him speak to the part in the case in the voice he'd used at the motel. I couldn't hear what he said. Ruth planted her feet. One of the lower hinges had bent. She got her shoulder under that side of the panel and I pushed with her. The panel jammed against the rim with the sheet caught in it. The piece that held her arm tore away from the rest.

The three fingers lay in a row beside the two she'd always had, held to the palm by pale tissue. Ruth lifted an edge of it with her thumb. The color started to go out of the middle finger, so she laid it back down. Paul was still calling to her from the door. She told him he could come now. He reached for her good elbow until she showed him where to put his hands. He took hold exactly where she asked.

Nina came back from the trapdoor and stepped over the open grille. She brought the wrench back to the first mark, and a little water ran into the pan. Then it stopped. “The wheel's open,” she said. “Where's the water going?” The hose that had pulled out of the floor lay over my leg. I lifted the end. Pale tissue filled it up through the threads. I tried to give it to her and dropped it. I couldn't pick it up again.

Frank looked at the plugged hose. “It'll come to me if you give it a way in,” he said. He took his wrist out of the case. The white strip stretched after it until it came loose at the corner. I thought he'd try to peel it off. He stood just far enough to put his right hand through the slot I'd got mine out of. He pushed it in to the elbow. His palm touched the glass from the inside. “Try the return now,” he said. Nina turned the wrench and the drain ran red. The cord in my wrist thickened, and I could move my shoulder without feeling it pull under my collarbone.

I got my left hand in Frank's jacket to take some of his weight. His knees slipped before I had hold of him. His arm slid partway out of the slot, and the drain stopped running. Whatever had loosened in my chest took hold again. Frank pushed his arm back in until the shoulder of his jacket caught on the slot. I thought he was going to tell me how to keep him there. “You were very good with the woman at the diner,” he said.

Nina slid her hand along the wrench until I could get mine on it, then she put both arms around Frank to hold him up. She asked him if he wanted us to keep going. He nodded. I could feel the wrench trying to come back up, and every time it moved the cord pulled tight. Frank's left hand came along the edge of the cabinet, looking for something to bear down on. I remembered getting him out of the van that morning. He'd put one foot between mine and take my hand, and I'd pull on three. If I'd let the wrench come up, I could've taken his hand. I felt the tips of his fingers on the back of mine and pushed down as far as it went.

Through the glass I saw Frank's little finger bend into his palm and disappear. The others went the same way. A crease ran up the sleeve, past the elbow. I waited for him to make a sound. The sheet at his neck settled as he sagged against it until he couldn't lift his head. His sleeve went flat. The cord warmed against my wrist. When it started to come out I could feel the places it let go, right up to the last one under my collarbone. A length of it fell across my thigh. I put my cheek against the side of the cabinet while the rest of it came out. I could feel the cool wood against my cheek. My face was where I thought it was.

Nina wrapped my wrist in her sweatshirt. I asked her to see about Frank. She got two fingers under his ear and held them there for a while. Then she came back and got her arms under mine. I put my left heel down and pushed when she did. We went out sideways. I could still hear the end of the cord dragging itself along the edge of the cabinet. We were on the ground in the alley when Paul brought a man from the ambulance. Behind us all I could hear was water going down under the stage.

Frank was dead when they carried him out. Later a deputy showed me a picture of his right sleeve. It had kept every crease all the way to the cuff, even where there was nothing left inside it. He asked how long Frank had been sick. I told him I'd been tying Frank's shoes toward the end of the trip. He'd put on his old dress shoes to see Ruth. He liked me to tie the bows low enough that he could pull them open himself. The deputy wrote down he'd needed help dressing. He said the crew had found the tank with the drain open and a length of hose down it. He wanted to know what had been in the hose because they hadn't found anything to bring out.

Nina turned over the recording. I asked her what she'd done with the numbers Frank wrote next to the women's names. She called them from the hospital while I was waiting to get my wrist looked at again. She told me one woman hung up on her. I heard her on the next call from the hallway. “Nothing,” she said. “He's gone. I thought you might be waiting for a call.” After that she made the little noises you make so the person on the other end knows you're still there. A nurse pushed a cart past. When it was quiet again Nina was still sitting there with the phone to her ear.

Ruth lost two of the three fingers she'd carried out. With the third she could feel warm water if she got it in far enough. Paul put up a rail on the other side of her porch steps so she could use her good hand. She had him move it closer to the house so she could get past him when he brought groceries. The first time Nina and I visited, Ruth came down the steps with both hands in her pockets. The steps were dry. Paul stayed up top with a grocery bag tearing down one side and let her do it.

From the back my right hand looks fine. If I turn it over you can see the scar and where the middle finger crosses under the next one. The hand therapist touched the tip of that finger once and I felt it above my elbow. She got a fresh sheet of paper to mark the place. I stayed out there for a few weeks after the hospital let me go. Nina drove me to therapy. After she parked I'd sometimes lean over and try to turn her key. She knew I wanted to see if I could do it before I went home. She'd leave the radio on and wait while I worked my finger under the ring. I work dispatch back home now. I called the guy I'd paid a month late to ask if he knew anyone who was hiring. He asked how I'd been first. It took me a while to answer, and he stayed on.

Last week a new driver shook my right hand before I got a chance to offer him the left. I felt his fingers close around mine, then the edge of his wedding ring slide up the inside of my arm. I jerked back. He asked if he'd squeezed too hard. His hand was down at his side by then, and I could still feel that ring under my sleeve, all the way to the elbow. “You can let go now,” I said.
