I never expected to even have a honeymoon until I met Charlie. 

I was never a romantic. Ask my ex girlfriends. They'd all tell you that was a major reason, if not the number one reason, things ended between us. I just wasn't a boyfriend guy - I couldn't understand the desire for flowers, the urge to hear sweet nothings, the necessity of chivalry. I found opening car doors demeaning. Long back massages beneath the glow of candle light felt a little creepy. 

Sue me. 

She changed all of that. 

In the past I'd had long screaming matches on the topic of equality: I would say, don't you feel belittled by all this? Wouldn't you rather just be two people? And my girlfriend would say, my god you are such an asshole, etc. 

On our first date, Charlotte brought me a bouquet she'd picked on the way to the restaurant. She pushed it into my hands, a truly jubilant smile making her cheeks scrunched and ruddy in the dim orange lighting. 

I snorted instinctively, although I couldn't quite place what was funny about it. Maybe I was just a little misogynistic. Maybe I just thought it was weird to receive a gesture as the man, whatever. She didn't react to my snarky laughter. Didn't give me even an inch. 

On our next date, we went to the fair. I won her a stuffed animal. 

On our first anniversary, she got me fancy cologne and a box of chocolates. I got her a ring. 

It was just easy with her. She made me realize maybe I wasn't anti-romance: maybe I just never felt all that romantic about anyone else. We had fights, and she never pulled her punches with me, but she never said I wasn't enough... because I *wasn't* not enough. Not making her feel loved became my worst fear. She deserved everything she wanted. 

I never thought I'd have a fancy wedding. I pictured myself getting married in the courthouse. 

When I said that to her, she laughed and rolled her eyes. 

"Your family has so much money, Oli. Not having a wedding is, like, the dumbest idea you've ever had." Then she leaned in, running her fingers through my hair and kissing my cheek. "Just think of it as throwing a huge, expensive party." 

And so I did. And we threw a huge party with everyone we knew. And I had a great time. I even cried at the alter, something I'd made fun of countless guys for before. 

It just came naturally. 

When I told her I also never thought I'd have a honeymoon, she reacted about the same. 

"You never wanted one because your whole life is a honeymoon, sweetie." 

We compromised. 

No Italy. No yachting in Barbados. No bull-running in Spain. Charlie always thought that felt kind of cruel, anyways.

We would go camping. 

And after a few hours of research, we found the perfect place. 

"Seems a little less private than advertised," I muttered as I pulled my Chevy into our lot, nodding towards a campsite mere feet away from where we had planned to set up. 

It was only 1 PM, and they already had a fire going. An older woman sat in front of it in a fold up chair, roasting what looked to be a sausage on a metal poker. 

Charlie gave me a light shove, tutting. "Don't be such a grump, boy. It's summer." I saw her grinning from the corner of my eye as I maneuvered the truck, backing in. "Love is in the air..." 

Honeymoon Hill was surrounded by trees. We had driven for an hour down winding forest roads to get here, and the meadow was the first expanse of land without thickly packed pines we'd seen since leaving the freeway. 

It was beautiful, I had to admit. You could see across the top of some of the woods: the sunset was going to be otherworldly. 

There was a loud knocking on the glass next to my face. I jumped, cursing under my breath. 

The old woman was standing outside my door, beaming up at me. I fixed my expression quickly, rolling down the window. Charlie snickered at me. 

"Hi there!" The woman crooned. Up close I could see she had bright blue eyes, and she had pulled her windbreaker hood down, revealing that her hair was nearly entirely white. She had to at least be in her seventies. "I guess we're your neighbors for the weekend, isn't that nice?"

I nodded, smiling back a little awkwardly. Charlie laughed again, leaning over me to take over. 

"We're on our honeymoon!" 

"Oh!" The woman's hand flew to her chest, as if clutching at her heart. Her mouth formed a perfect little 'o'. "My goodness! Well, forget me, then, I'd better leave you two alone!" 

"No, no, it's okay!" Charlie intercepted, to my mild dismay. She loved people. "What's your name?" 

The woman smiled again, pleased. People loved Charlie, too. "Well, it's Beverly, dear." 

Charlie placed her hand on my shoulder, which made me feel a little bit like a dog. Like she was settling me down. I couldn't help but pout. I felt plenty settled. 

"That's a beautiful name. I'm Charlie, and this is Oli." 

Beverly clapped her hands together. "Charlie and Oli! How sweet. Oh, you must meet my husband."

I opened my mouth to protest, to tell her we probably ought to start setting up if we wanted the tent built by dark, but she was already spinning back toward her RV. 

It was pretty decked out, I realized. They had a canopy hanging from the side, shading their little outdoor living room area, and when Bev opened the door, I could see that the inside was much larger than it looked. I shifted a little uncomfortably. 

"Hey," Charlie purred, finally turning her attention back toward me. "What's wrong?" 

I shook my head, unbuckling my seatbelt. "Nothing's wrong." 

"Hey," she said again, catching my wrist before I could push open the door and climb out. She smiled at me, her half smile that made her face look rounder, and I felt something inside me soften. "We'll have plenty of alone time. And... I love it here. This is perfect." 

She always knew what would make me feel better. Hesitantly, I smiled back. "Really?" 

"Yes. Perfect. Thank you for bringing me here." 

I leaned over the center console, and I kissed her. 

"Well, here are the kids my wife is raving about," came a gruff voice from outside the cabin. He sounded how I might imagine Santa Claus would sound. I pulled away from my wife, resting my forehead against hers and closing my eyes, sighing. She giggled. 

John, Beverly's husband, insisted on setting up our tent for us. Charlie and I watched as he grunted over the spokes. I felt a little emasculated, but Charlie wouldn't stop grabbing my arm and chuckling, so that kept me from crossing over the line from mildly annoyed to fully crabby. 

By the time everything was all assembled, the light over the trees was beginning to fade, painting the hill in orange light. John and Bev invited us to join them for dinner, but, thank god, Charlie declined politely. 

She and I carried a bottle of wine up to the highest point of the hill and sat in the grass. As the sun set, she rested her head on my shoulder. I took her hand, feeling the wedding ring I'd given her between my fingers, imprinting the diamonds into the pad of my thumb. 

It was the perfect night. 

I woke to the sound of a metallic door banging open. 

I blinked, rubbing my eyes and slowly sitting up. It was pitch dark outside, and the crickets were chirping so loudly it felt like they were inside of my head. 

I looked down at my wife. She was facing away from me, the blankets wrapped tightly around her like a cocoon, snoring softly. I reached down, pulling a loose sheet up to her neck. She was illuminated slightly by a warm yellow light, I realized, slicing across the back of her head. 

I turned toward the other campsite. Where the sound that had woken me must have come from. 

Through the thin polyester tent, I could barely make out their camper. Sure enough, the door was hanging open. The glow was coming from within, where the lights had been turned on sometime since I'd fallen asleep. 

I frowned, squinting my blurry eyes and leaning closer. 

"You aren't happy, are you?"

At the sound of Charlie's voice, I nearly leapt out of my skin. I whipped back around. She was still lying where she had been: she didn't seem to have moved at all, and I could've sworn she'd been snoring half a second before speaking. 

Her voice was right there, attached to her body, but it also sounded distant. Like it came from outside. 

"What?" I asked, my voice rough with sleep. I shook my head, stretching my arms above my head. "Of course I'm happy, baby. I mean, I guess I wish we weren't sharing our campsite, but-" 

"Your whole life's a honeymoon," she said, cutting me off. 

I paused. Those were the words she'd said to me before, but this time, they didn't carry the same playfulness. This time they were snide. Cutting. Almost pained. 

I lay back down behind her, forgetting about the neighboring campers. I drew my fingers through her long hair, brushing down her back. "What's wrong, Charlie?" 

She didn't answer. When I pressed closer to her, burying my face in her neck, I could hear her snoring. Still, or maybe again. Maybe she'd been sleep talking. 

When I wrapped my arms around her waist, she pushed back against me, humming happily. 

In the morning, when I finally forced myself up and out of the tent to pee, I found Bev sitting in front of their fire pit. I watched as she held a cigarette up to the minuscule flame, lighting it and bringing it up to her mouth. 

I hadn't pegged her as a smoker. 

"Careful," I called out, trying to make my voice soft and friendly like Charlie would, but it still made her jump in surprise. She clasped her chest. She tended to do that. 

"You scared me, my goodness. Forgive me, what's your name again?" 

I smiled a little weakly. I was positive she remembered Charlie's name. 

"It's Oliver." 

"Yes, yes. Oh, that's right." She took a drag of her cigarette, her hand shaking a little, likely from the cold. She was bundled in a puffy jacket, pinning it tightly around herself. "Come sit with me for a moment, Oliver." 

I hesitated, looking behind me at the tent where my wife was still sound asleep, then shrugged. I took a seat on the stump beside her camping chair, rubbing my hands together. 

She offered me her cigarette. I took it. 

"How long have you known Charlie?" She asked me, her eyes trained on the dying fire. I smiled. 

"Two years," I said, bringing the cigarette to my mouth. "People say that's not long to know someone-" 

"People are silly," she cut in, her voice surprisingly curt. "People don't know." 

I nodded, watching her. Something was off about her this morning. "That's what I think, too." 

She was quiet for a minute. I handed her back the cigarette. She took it, looking down at it for a while before she spoke again. 

"Yesterday was our forty-ninth anniversary." A small, unreadable smile pulled at her wrinkled lips. She finally looked at me. "Isn't that just crazy, Oli? Forty nine years." 

"Wow," I offered. "That is crazy. That's a long time." 

She nodded at me, her blue eyes searching over my face. "You would think you'd know someone by then." She stubbed out the cigarette on a rock, groaning as she leaned over. I watched the last traces of smoke trickle from the butt before it was extinguished. "But there's always more. There's always more to know. There are all these things that they think about, things they keep up in their head, that they may never tell you. Isn't that just awful? You can never just be one. Isn't that so strange? That you can never be one?" 

I didn't know what to say. I tucked my hands into my jacket, nodding slowly. 

She waved her hand, dismissing me. 

"I'm sorry, listen to me. Babbling on like a fool. Let me not take you from your honeymoon, sweetheart." 

She pushed herself to her feet, walking away before I could say anything. She left me there, sitting in front of her fire, my face frozen in surprise. 

It took about an hour for Charlie to convince me to go on a hike, but once it was happening, I was glad. The fresh air was amazing compared to our usual city smog, and the sunlight that dodged through the tree branches made our skin warm and illuminated. 

The only thing I could do without was how hard it was to keep up with her. She kept disappearing up ahead of me, and with how thin and obstructed by brush the paths were, I found this quickly frustrating. 

"Charlie!" I called out after the fifth time, stopping to catch my breath. "Charlie, I'm gonna stop for water, okay?" 

I was silent, waiting. No response came: all I heard was the flutter of birds in the trees, and the wind. 

I rolled my eyes, a little annoyed. Weren't we supposed to be *together* on this trip? It felt like things were always pulling her away from me. 

I sat down against a tree, reaching for my water bottle. I had just unhooked it from my belt when I heard something. 

A rustle, and then a voice. 

*"Oliver? Oli!"* 

It was distant, nearly indistinguishable, even though the rustling had felt fairly close. I shot up, peering down the path. It hadn't sounded like it was up in front of me, either. 

"Charlie?" 

For a moment, nothing. I spun around in a circle, searching the trees, as if that was going to do anything. 

"Charlie, are you okay?" 

*"Help!"*

I froze.

Her voice was coming from several angles all at once, like it floated on the wind to reach me. It sounded wrong, I couldn't quite place my finger on why... maybe it was too cheerful, and yet it was too strained at the same time. 

*"Help! Help!"* 

Instinct kicked in and I started to run down the path, my water bottle dropping to the ground and rolling into the brush. She couldn't be too far ahead of me, could she? There was no way she was as far as she sounded. 

Suddenly, her voice was right in my ear. Right behind me. A whisper. It drowned out the sound of my feet on the path and my heavy breathing. 

"It isn't enough." 

I slid to a halt, nearly tumbling backwards. I whipped around. 

She was there. She was standing at the end of the path behind me, at least the furthest down that I could see... not directly behind me. I could barely make out her face, she was nearly just a sillouette, but I could tell that she was crying now, her body trembling with the sobs wracking through her. 

"What are you talking about?" I asked, forgetting to shout it, but she heard it anyways. She shook her head, crying harder. 

"You don't love me, Oliver! Why can't you ever show me you love me?"

"I do love you," I breathed, panic pulling sharply at my chest. I began advancing toward her, almost in a daze. "I do love you! How can you say that? I show you I love you all the time!"

"You didn't want to have a wedding. You didn't want to go on a honeymoon." She whimpered, hugging herself like a child as I got closer, turning her face away from me. "And I don't want to be with someone no one likes. You should just go, Oliver. Just leave. I don't want you here. *GO!*" 

I opened my mouth to protest, to tell her I'd never leave her here, to beg her to tell me what was going on, when something sharp hit me on the back. I winced, my strange haze broken, and spun around. 

"You asshole!" Charlie shouted at me, crossing her arms and frowning. I looked down. She'd thrown a stick at me. "What the hell are you doing?" 

"I..." I trailed off, glancing over my shoulder. 'Charlie' was gone. I blinked, shaking my head. Was I going insane? "I'm sorry..." 

"I don't know if you're trying to mess with me or what," Charlie murmured, stepping closer to me. I felt a strange urge to back away. "But it's not funny. You're being so mean." 

I frowned, the first real expression I was able to make. "I am?" 

"Yes," she hissed. Then her face broke down, her mouth turning in in the corners and her eyes drooping sadly. "Yeah, Oli. You never make fun of me for money stuff. Not even as a joke." 

I ground my teeth. I glanced over my shoulder again. Nothing. 

I forced myself to snap out of it, stepping toward her and pulling her into my arms. I didn't know what to say. How to explain that I *hadn't* made fun of her, or at least I didn't remember it. "I'm sorry," I mumbled into her hair, warm from the sunlight. 

She sighed, the tension in her body slightly loosening. She hugged me back, hiding her face in my chest. "How did you get back here, anyways?" 

"What do you mean?" 

She looked up at me, her eyebrows furrowed. "You kept running up ahead of me. I couldn't keep up with you. How did you get back here when you were in front of me that whole time?" 

When we got back to the camp, we were both exhausted. So exhausted, in fact, that it took us far too long to notice that our neighbor's camp was entirely packed up. 

The RV roared to life, which was what finally what caught my attention. Charlie and I looked at each other, confused. 

I followed her as she ran up to the driver's side window, knocking on it like Bev had that first afternoon. Bev rolled it down, her whole body trembling like a leaf. 

"You heading out?" Charlie asked, a little sadness tinting her voice. She peered around the cabin. "Where's John?" 

Bev started to shake her head. She didn't stop shaking it. She reached out the window, clasping Charlie's hand in her own, squeezing tightly. Her face was alarmingly pale. I took a step forward instinctively. 

"Go," she whispered. Her eyes flickered back toward me, locking on mine. I felt a cold chill wash over me. "Go now. It's getting smarter." 

She seemed to hear something and her head jolted like a deer, whipping to the rear view mirror. I followed her gaze. 

John was sprinting toward us, faster than an old man should be able to, up to the back of the RV from down the hill. Bev let go of Charlie's hand, letting out a little shriek. I grabbed Charlie by her shoulders and pulled her back from the camper before it started moving. 

Bev peeled away, kicking up dirt and grass as the tires spun wildly. John kept coming: he didn't call out to her, beg her to stop, nothing. He just ran, chasing until she had picked up too much momentum to catch. 

Then he turned back toward us. I held Charlie close to my side, watching in shock as he stumbled toward us. 

His face wasn't nearly as kind as it had seemed before. It was too shadowed, too *long*. 

"Are you okay?" I called to him, my voice shaking a little. 

He didn't answer me. He just looked for a while, standing in the center of the dirt road, his stance a little crooked. 

Then he walked down the hill and disappeared into the woods. 

I begged Charlie to leave that night. I need it on record that I truly did. I told her that something was incredibly wrong with that place, that we could have a redo somewhere else, that we should listen to Beverly and *go now.* 

She told me we should stay and leave in the morning. She said this was our chance to have some privacy, we had the hill all to ourselves, and that it would be way too dark to drive even to the closest motel. 

I tried to protest. We argued for what had to be at least an hour and a half. But her words from earlier kept flashing into my mind, making me feel desperate to prove her wrong: *you didn't want to have a wedding. You didn't want to go on a honeymoon. You should just go, Oliver*. 

I wasn't leaving without her, and she wasn't leaving. 

So we stayed. 

We did have a nice evening. I'll give her that. We played cards by the fire, and we listened to music, which helped me forget what had happened a little. It helped me stop glancing toward the trees, wondering when John might emerge again. 

But then it was time for bed, and everything came rushing back again. 

Charlie fell asleep fairly quickly, but I didn't. I couldn't. I listened to her snore, the sound melding with the hum of the outdoors and the trickle of rain bouncing off the plastic. I stared at the ceiling of the tent, willing my heart to stop racing. 

I must have drifted off eventually, though, because I woke up. It was still dark out. I couldn't place what had woken me, but I felt panicked the second I opened my eyes. 

I felt around in the dark for my wife, letting out a breath of relief when my fingers landed on her waist. She stirred, rolling toward me. 

"Sorry," I breathed. "Sorry, baby. Didn't mean to wake you up." 

She hummed in response, pressing close to me. I held her close, reaching for her hand under the covers. 

I heard something outside. A voice, from far off. I froze, my fingers half clasped in hers. Charlie froze too. 

"What was that?" She hissed, holding onto my arm. "Oli?" 

"I don't know," I whispered back. I sat up, slowly rising. 

"Wait." Charlie's voice cracked, and she reached for me. "I don't think you should go..." 

I chewed on my lip, taking her hand and squeezing it. 

"I'm just going to check," I whispered, unzipping the tent. 

I fumbled for my phone, turning the flashlight on and shining it toward the sound. 

Down the hill, I saw her. I knew what I'd see, but it still came as a shock... it still made my entire body feel like it'd been set ablaze. 

My wife stood at the bottom of the hill, facing the thicket of trees. She was talking... shouting, really... to someone in the darkness. Or something. 

I looked back into the tent, at my wife, sitting up and hugging the blankets to her chest. I shone my flashlight at her, which made her wince and cover her face with her hands. 

I stared at her long, slender fingers, and something cold struck me. 

There was no ring. 

Charlie never took off her ring unless she was doing the dishes, or cleaning the toilet. 

I turned without a word and ran down the hill. I grabbed Charlie from the edge of the trees despite her protests, nearly tossing her over my shoulder in my haste. 

We left without the tent. I placed her in the passenger seat and buckled her seatbelt without a word, and we were gone. 

I now understand better what we experienced. 

I think I understand what those things wanted. 

They wanted to trick us. They wanted to drive us apart. They wanted one of us to break, to stray, to leave without the other. They wanted to replace one of us with one of their own, and do whatever they wanted to do to the real one once they were left behind. 

Maybe eat them. Maybe worse. 

I've spent a lot of time thinking about what that something worse might be lately. 

Because I think I've made a horrible mistake. 

I've noticed lately that Charlie never has her wedding ring on. And now that I think about it, I don't know if I've seen it on her since we left Honeymoon Hill. 

Charlie never took off her ring. 

I'm writing this not only as a warning. I'm writing this in case you see it, Charlie. If I'm right... god forbid I'm right... if you're still out there somewhere, if you survived, if I truly left you there with those things... 

Please, please come home. 

I love you.
