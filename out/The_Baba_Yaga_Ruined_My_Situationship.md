I’m twenty-two. He was in his late 60s.

Emphasis on the *was*.

First time I met him was at a Žižkov jam session. There’s a spot I go to every Sunday. Open mic, cheap drinks, lots of artsy folks and talented musicians. I don’t actually play anything. Never had the urge to learn. I do love the music though. And I do love to dance. So, every Sunday, I smoke a joint and groove out by the stage with a dapo star.

When I first met him, I almost asked him if he had any drugs. He was sitting by the stage, drenched in purple light, tapping his foot along with the music. There’s not a lot of old timers who drop by on Sundays. I thought he was Gandalf, an old Canadian drug dealer who used to be a Žižkov staple back in the day. I was just about to clasp him on the shoulders and ask him if he had anything interesting in stock. But, luckily, I realized my mistake before committing to touch.

Not Gandalf. Just a different old guy with a beard.

He tapped his knee along with the music while I danced and spun my dapo. Spinning the cloth on my finger often gets its fair share of interested looks. I caught a couple curious glances from him. While he was wondering about the dapo, I, too, found myself wondering about him.

This gentle looking old guy. With his beer belly and suspenders and corduroy and untouched wine glass, what had brought him here? The man seemed utterly harmless and my curiosity had gotten the better of me. When there was a break in the music, I sat down and said hello.

To my delight, Ondřich was more than happy to chat. He was Czech, but had spent a couple years working in Philadelphia after the revolution. He had the strangest accent and was lovely and sweet and polite in a way only old people are. When I asked him what brought him to the jam, he beamed with excitement.

“Well, young lady,” Ondřich said, with a dentured smile, “I am here for a date!”

And just as he said that, she crept in. A hag, bent with age and wrinkled by time. She wore a garish dress of old sequin and her hair had the consistency of cobweb. Only her eyebrows looked new. They were painted on, poorly, like two angry crimson slugs. Beneath one eyebrow rested a clear glass ball. Beneath the other, an angry eye that stared straight at me.

“Out of my way, peasant,” she growled, as she pushed past me.

The hag had sharp elbows, but she immediately softened the moment she was before Ondřich. She threw her bony arms around him and covered his neck with kisses. At first, Ondřich seemed confused, but eventually he rested his hands on her shoulders and hugged her back.

As the music started up again, the two of them sat down. I did not like the hag, and she clearly didn’t like me, but I considered her none of my business. So, I just went back to dancing and spinning the dapo.

As hard as I tried to ignore her, however, I could not.

Even at a distance, even with the music going wild — I could not ignore her voice. She screeched like nails on chalkboard. Past the drums, I could only make out the gist of what she was yelling about. Something about cats and peasants and a cabin out in the woods. The hag just kept talking and talking and talking, not giving Ondřich any chance to respond.

The few times I looked over at the table, Ondřich shot me a sad look. I felt bad for him. Even though he was old, he seemed kind and wasn’t entirely bad looking for his age. I would hope that the dating market for the elderly wasn’t as brutal so that he would have to settle for someone so ghoulish.

When the hag got up to go to the bathroom, I danced over in Ondřich’s direction. By the time the song had reached its crescendo, I was standing right next to him. Once the first wave of applause died down, Ondřich started talking to me.

Desperate to be taken from the table, like a man drowning, he asked me a bunch of questions about the dapo. Having been on my fair share of bad dates, I obliged him in conversation. I even asked if he would like to try spinning the dapo himself.

As the drummer on stage started searching for a new beat, Ondřich clambered up from his chair. While he was sitting down, he looked old. But his age didn’t truly show until he stood up. As he made his way from behind the table he uttered little grunts of effort and when he finally stood he was hunched over with his beer belly on full display.

He took a couple of attempts at spinning the cloth, but his sense of balance was lacking as well. Each time he dropped the dapo, however, Ondřich persevered. He laughed and joked and didn’t let any failure dissuade him.

The old man was suddenly merry, yet, all his mirth disappeared the moment the hag returned.

The hatred in her expression was tenfold of when she first saw me. Even her glass eye seemed to be burning with anger. This time, however, she had no love for Ondřich.

“Your hands lack finesse, *peasant*,” the hag said, to the rumbling of drums. “You would make for a poor lover.”

And with that, as the music grew, she reached down into her sparkly dress, past her sagging breasts and pulled out a dapo of her own.

Black silk in the shape of a pentagram. Small colorful beads were stitched into the dark cloth, leading to a massive painted eye at the center. Light reflected strangely off the flowstar. It was as if it was eating up all the pink and purple of the stage.

As another tune came from the musicians, the hag spun the dapo star. The cloth moved strangely in the air and she, too, moved strangely. The moment the dapo star started to spin, her haggard body snapped to action. She moved like she was in the prime of her life. Her tendons and muscle and wrinkled skin could not contain the hag.

“I was once like you, *peasant*,” she said, balancing the spinning cloth on her finger, the glass eye burning purple and pink. “And then I got bitten by a snake!”

In mid-spin she snatched the dapo away. Something in the air changed. Even the band skipped a beat. It was as if a presence had left the room.

“If I am too old for your love, *peasant*, then you have wasted my time.” Ondřich started to apologize, but the hag silenced him with a single, bony finger. “You know as well as I, *peasant*, that as the years pass by, they flow faster. To waste an evening of a *harlot* like her is one thing —”

I excused myself outside for a smoke. I wasn’t about to get into a shouting match with a crazy old witch. Before I even had a chance to find my lighter, however, Ondřich and the hag were already outside.

He was apologizing to her. She was having none of it. Out of a nearby alley she pulled out a rusty old bicycle and set it on the road.

“If you wish to be young, *peasant*, so be it. But remember, as the years pass by, they flow faster. Remember that well. I curse you for wasting my evening.” She propped up her haggard body onto the bicycle. Her glass eye shined in the glare of the street lamps. “Čáry — Máry — Fuk!” she yelled, and then threw the dapo star at Ondřich.

And just like that, she bent over her bicycle and pedaled down the road towards Florenc. Ondřich was left holding the strange cloth she pulled out of her dress. He was still mumbling apologies into the night when I joined him.

I assured the old man that his date was crazy and that we weren’t doing anything wrong. He shrugged his shoulders and did admit that it wasn’t a very good date, but he still felt unwell about her having a bad time. Out of curiosity, I asked how they’d met. Facebook dating. Ondřich’s wife had passed away ten years prior. It was hard getting back on the dating scene.

Just as Ondřich and I got to talking, a group of my friends came outside for a joint. They invited us to join. The old man was happy to chat but, on account of his asthma, he didn’t smoke. Once the rest of the group was sufficiently toasted, we went back inside.

Ondřich got another glass of wine and joined our table. He told us stories about how he and his wife used to travel around Asia, and how in his twenties, he also used to frequent the Žižkov underground. He played the saxophone. Sadly, his lungs could no longer handle the instrument with grace.

The music reached a wonderful groove, as did my blood chemistry. I got up to dapo and dance. After a while, I noticed Ondřich tapping his knee a way off from the stage. With trembling hands, he tried spinning the dapo the hag had thrown at him.

It took him a while to get it spinning, but before the song was up, he could get a couple of rotations out of the dapo before it fell limp. By the end of the evening, he could get the cloth to spin for seconds at a time. His face glowed as he spun the dapo. Like a proud child.

Spinning the dapo, Ondřich seemed to shed a couple years.

We chatted a bit more before I went home. Ondřich asked me if I had a Facebook. I accidentally called it the *geriatric website* but I gave him my WhatsApp and promised to teach him some dapo tricks once he got good enough.

I thought I had made a nice older friend. Nothing creepy.

In the morning he sent me a message making sure I got home safe and thanked me for a pleasant evening. Polite enough. Then, after a couple of days of silence he wrote me another message that set off some red flags. I ignored it. By the end of the day, he texted me something about my *young body*.

With a sigh, I blocked him.

Ondřich genuinely seemed like a nice old man. It made me sad for him to get creepy. I didn’t dwell on it much though, as I had exams. The next two weeks of my life were consumed with textbooks and redbull and anxiety.

I completely forgot about Ondřich, and then, one Sunday, I found myself back in the purple and pink lights.

That Sunday, my friend and I went out for dinner to Pavel’s Bistro. We arrived late, and sluggish with a mild case of food coma, but the jam was in full session. The drums were on beat, the guitar was crisp and there was a new guy on the saxophone that commanded my attention.

Late 30s, maybe. Jet black beard. A white dress shirt unbuttoned a few buttons too many. It was well past 10, but the man was wearing sunglasses. Had he not played the saxophone so goddamn well, I would have found them lame.

But the music coming out of that sax was raw energy. I danced and spun my dapo and hovered a bit closer to the stage than usual, hoping that he would notice me. He played three songs and then hopped off of stage, narrowly passing me. I tried complimenting him on the playing, but the applause drowned me out.

He passed by without a word.

A couple minutes later, however, the sax man returned. He placed a glass of white wine on the table closest to the stage, danced over to me and took out a dapo of his own. A black pentagram covered in beads.

I recognized the cloth before I recognized the man.

“Ondřich!” I yelled.

“I go by Ondra these days,” he said, lowering his sunglasses.

The same kind eyes. The same black dapo. The same man… just a lot younger.

We danced together for a song or two, but then we retreated to the bar. He offered to buy me a drink and I invited him for shots in return. For a moment I thought that maybe I was drinking with Oldřich’s son, or a younger brother, but the sax player assured me we knew each other. Seeing the old man transform so drastically over just a couple of weeks made me uneasy, yet the more we drank the fuzzier my memory became.

Soon, all I could think of was the man standing before me.

I am not one to go home with a guy on the first night. And I guess, in a way, it wasn’t the first night I was meeting him. After a couple more drinks, however, things got flirty. One more song sealed the deal for me.

That night, I went home with Ondra.

They say saxophone players are horrible in bed, but Ondra was an exception to the rule. He was attentive, patient, gentle and firm when it was required. Ondra was a good lay, but his aftercare left a lot to be desired. Just as we’d finish, he would get out of bed, stand up and spin his dapo.

Naked, no music, no dancing — just spinning the cloth on his finger. He wasn’t performing. He wasn’t trying to impress me. Ondra was seemingly spinning the dapo purely for the joy of it.

The spinning was strange, yet, after a couple of minutes of balancing the cloth, he would always return to bed with renewed energy and vigor. After the second spin of the dapo I stopped finding it weird.

I was just excited for him to come back to me.

After that morning, I unblocked Ondra’s number. We chatted for a couple of days and then I came over to his place for drinks and dinner. When he met me at the door he looked even younger than when I met him the second time. He was just as handsome and just as charming though, so I didn’t give it much thought.

Even as he cooked, he spun the dapo. He worked both the cloth and the spatula with a juggler’s precision. As I watched in awe, trying to chop onions, I asked him where he’d learn how to cook.

“My wife and I used to take cooking classes wherever we traveled,” he said, with a smile. But then, behind those thoughtful eyes, something shifted. For a moment, he nearly dropped the dapo. The cloth lost all its motion. He was elsewhere. But then, just as quickly, he shrugged off the thoughts and regained his dexterity.

We ate little and we drank little. We spent most of the night in Ondra’s bed.

It wasn’t until the morning that the strangeness of my situationship got to me. He was back on his feet, twirling the pentagonal dapo. The beads on the cloth had changed. They were plumper and gibbous. The eye at the center of the cloth seemed to be much more detailed and livelier. Yet it wasn’t any change in the dapo that made me leave Ondra’s apartment that morning.

It was witnessing the change in Ondra.

The morning sun hit his face as he spun around the cloth. At first I thought it was a trick of the eye, but the more I watched the more certain I was.

Ondra’s black beard, it was receding back into his face.

No matter what I said, Ondra wouldn’t stop spinning the cloth. I poked fun at his obsession, asked him whether he missed the old hag that gave it to him — yet he just kept on spinning.

“I just like spinning the dapo,” he said, smiling. “It relaxes me.”

After some more gentle attempts, I asked him directly to stop. He didn’t. The best he could do was spin a little slower. That wasn’t good enough for me.

I couldn’t stop looking at his beard. I couldn’t stop watching those hairs retract back into his skin. Once it became clear he wasn’t going to stop making me feel uncomfortable, I went home.

He texted me before I came home. He kept on texting me. But the texts were different from the long thought-out messages he sent me before. They were short, filled with misspellings and text speak and were turning more and horny vulgar by the hour.

I ignored most of them, and, after three days of steadily descending legibility, I told Ondra I wanted to meet. Not at his place. Somewhere public.

The whole situation was giving me the creeps. I wanted to end it.

It wasn’t until I reached JzP square the following afternoon that I realized how bad things were. Standing by the church, spinning the black dapo, was a teenage boy. There were bits of fuzz on his upper lip, but by the time I reached him they were gone.

*As the years fly by, they pass faster.*

The words of the hag echoed through my head. Immediately, I gave up on trying to break up with Ondra. Us splitting was a given. Now, all I wanted to do was to stop him from digging deeper into the curse.

“Ondra!” I yelled. “Stop playing with the dapo! It’s cursed!”

“Don’t tell me what to do!” he yelled back, his voice cracking. “And don’t call me Ondra! That name is old and stupid! My name is Odík!”

And just like that, he started to shrink before me.

I tried to reason with him, even attempted to snatch the dapo away from him — yet Odík would not have any of it. He wanted to play with his dapo. The more he spun it, the more unreasonable he got.

The clothes started to sag on his shrinking body. His voice raised a handful of octaves. Odík was screaming at me to get away. I was no longer trying to break things off with an older sexual partner. I was fighting a child over a piece of cloth.

Even as the boy shrunk, the dapo kept on spinning. The dark cloth was blacker than ever before. The beads grew thicker and more curved. The eye at the center of the cloth was bloodshot and mad.

I wish I would have tried harder. I wish I would have tackled the child to the ground and ripped that cursed thing away from him. But I couldn’t. By the time Odík reached the height of my waist, his screams were attracting the attention of the patrons from the nearby terraces. I couldn’t stand their stares and I couldn’t stand the shame.

I started to walk away.

Behind me, Odík started to sing a vulgar song about me. With each step, the pitch of his voice raised. The words of his song simplified. The rhymes turned wonky. For a while the song turned into a chant and then it descended once more.

Into laughter. A little boy’s laughter. High-pitched and joyful and filled with malice. And, with a couple more steps, that laughter turned to tears. The pitch of the cries rose steadily up, up and up until, finally it stopped.

A soul shuddering gurgle, followed by silence.

I turned around. Where once Odík stood, only the dapo remained.

Without thought, I ran to the center of the square. The eye on the cloth was burning mad and staring straight at me. With the dapo out of motion, I could see the beads clearly. They had taken on a concrete shape, a terrifying shape — each of the beads was a fetus, curled up, connected to that horrid eyeball by bits of string.

As I approached the dapo, I could see the slightest bit of motion beneath it. Something was stirring right at the center of the eye. For a moment I hesitated even touching the cloth, yet by the time I worked up the courage there was nothing there.

All that remained of the old man was a wet splotch on the hot [cobblestone](https://www.reddit.com/r/MJLPresents/).
