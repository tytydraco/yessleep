I woke up early that day. The trailer park looked especially dilapidated. Rebecca yelled, “Hey, Bobby!” as she carefully stepped over some used baby diapers before tossing her trash into the bin. My trailer, in contrast, looked clean and neat compared to nearly everyone else’s.

Richard, my elderly neighbor, had asked me to buy him some groceries, promising he’d pay me back. So I went to the grocery store and bought everything he needed. When I returned, I pounded on his dirty, broken door.  
Small cans and bottles littered the ground, making it difficult to walk without stepping on something. Richard obviously never bothered cleaning the outside of his home. The grass had grown wild, several windows were cracked, and one corner of his porch had begun to collapse.

A cold breeze passed through the park. As I looked around, I noticed it had just begun to snow.

“Wait! Wait a minute!” Richard yelled.  
I could hear him struggling to get up from his chair. I stood there for another minute or two before he finally answered the door.

“Ah! Bobby! Thanks for the groceries!” he exclaimed. I could hardly understand him over the wind rattling his windows.

“Come in! Come in!”  
I stepped inside. His floor was littered with small pieces of trash, and the only truly lived-in spot was the area surrounding his rocking chair, where he spent most of his day watching television.

“Where do you want me to put ’em?” I asked, looking around.  
Richard was a feeble old man, and despite having known him for a few years, I hardly knew anything about him.

“Over there,” he said, pointing toward his stained refrigerator.

I placed the groceries inside, careful not to set them on top of any trash. Richard returned to his chair, which groaned beneath his weight as he settled into it.

“Thank you, thank you,” he said, his attention already returning to the television.

“I’ll be sure to pay you back soon, my boy!”

I left soon after. Richard never paid me back. I didn’t care.

As I walked to my trailer, I looked up at the sky. It wasn’t its usual shade of blue. Today, it looked sickly and moldy.  
I hurried inside, and the cold seemed to disappear the moment I shut the door. The soft whistles of the wind faded behind me. I took off my shoes, grabbed a beer from the fridge, and sat down on the couch. I’d lived here for most of my life, so I tried to keep the place well maintained.

My favorite show was on, and it kept my attention for the next few minutes. I took another long gulp of beer and finished the can.

But as I drank, something slid from the can and into my mouth.

Whatever it was, it certainly wasn’t meant to be there. I shot up from my chair and spat it onto the floor. I could see it clearly.

It was a slightly deformed worm.  
“What the fuck!” I yelled, already gagging.

How could it have gotten there? I gagged again and rushed outside. Once I was out in the cold, I finally threw up. Even then, the awful aftertaste remained.

I threw out the rest of the six-pack, figuring there must have been something wrong with the manufacturer. Then I went inside, brushed my teeth, and tried to get rid of the lingering taste.

I tried to watch my show, but I couldn’t concentrate. Eventually I went to bed, thoroughly disgusted.  
I didn’t sleep well that night. Morning couldn’t come soon enough.

I had work to do that day. I’d be helping one of my neighbors, Michael, cut down a tree near his home. He’d promised to pay me about five hundred dollars. I figured I’d do anything to get my mind off the worm.

I gathered my things, stepped outside, and headed over to his trailer. I knocked on the door.

“Hello? It’s me, Bobby!” I yelled, growing annoyed after the tenth knock. I could hear someone approaching before the door finally opened.

“Aye, Bobby! We gonna start?” a man asked in a drunken tone. It was Michael. The man looked tired, with noticeable bags under his eyes. Having six children probably had something to do with it.

“Yeah, yeah. I’m ready,” I replied.

“Alright, man. Give me a minute!” I heard one of his children screaming somewhere inside the trailer. He was irresponsible and acted like he was twelve. I still felt bad for his neglected children.

“Look, man. That one,” Michael said, pointing toward a large tree beside the trailer.

We got to work soon after. I wasn’t an expert at cutting down trees, and neither was Michael, but we knew the basics.

“Well, man. Pretty odd shit been goin’ on around our place,” Michael said as he tied a rope around the tree.

“Yeah? Like what?” I asked.

“Well, I found Julia, my youngest, eatin’ a fuckin’ worm yesterday!” he said, laughing.

“A-a worm?” I replied cautiously.

“Yeah! Haha! But we all did that as kids, right?”

Michael chuckled as he grabbed a chainsaw and started it. I took the rope, tied it to the hitch of my truck, and climbed inside.

It took a few minutes to pull the tree down, but we made relatively quick work of it. It snapped with a deafening crack, leaving the stump splintered in the ground.

“Pretty nice, Bobby. I’ll cut this sonuvabitch up and sell the wood!” Michael yelled, stopping the chainsaw.

“Well, where’s my cash?” I called back as I leaped over the fallen tree.

“Ah! I forgot about that…” Michael seemed annoyed with himself. He reached into his pocket, pulled out about five hundred dollars, and handed it to me.

“Very nice,” I said, eyeing the cash before putting it in my pocket.

Michael pulled out his phone and began trying to call someone. That was my cue to leave. Before heading home, though, I figured I’d stop by and talk to Richard. I knocked, and he pulled open the creaky door.

“Ahh, Bobby. You need anything?” Richard asked.

“Thought I’d just talk to you for a moment,” I replied.

He motioned for me to come inside. He was watching an old western on his outdated television.

“All this shit with the economy, it’s crazy, ain’t it?” Richard said as he sat down.

“Mhm,” I replied, heading for his fridge. I pulled out a beer, hesitated, then took a sip and checked the can. Nothing.  
“Well, Bobby. Been a while since I talked to your father. How is the man?” Richard asked, struggling to get up from his chair.

“I- well… he… I forgot to tell you, but…”  
I stumbled over my words as I helped him up.

“He’s… he got cancer a few years back. He never really got much better,” I said, choking up.

I hadn’t told many people about my dad’s death, but Richard was one of the first.

“He’s gone? I’m surprised, Bobby. I am surprised,” Richard said.

He reached into his cabinet and pulled out a can of food. The hinges squeaked.  
“He was there… when my son passed. The car accident,” he added.

“They’re somewhere better now, aren’t they?” Richard said, his voice cracking slightly.

“Let’s hope so,” I replied.  
I leaned against his rocking chair, which let out a loud groan.

“Well, you and Michael were out there?” Richard asked.

“Yeah, cutting down a tree. Not much,” I chuckled.

“Eh, he’s an odd one. He came by earlier and told me his youngest ate a damn worm!”

“Yeah, he told me.”

“Goddamn worms, eh? Found a few in the bathroom not long after he left. I might have to call an exterminator or something, but that’d cost a bit.”

“Found one in my beer yesterday. Weird, ain’t it?”

“Weird indeed. Well, you got a woman yet?”

“N-not yet.”

“Well, I best get goin’. See ya, Richie.”

“See ya!” he yelled back.

It was lonely out here. A large empty field lay in front of the park with a sign announcing a new suburb. Couldn’t wait.

I hurried into my trailer. It looked familiar. It was warm, and I knew my father would be proud of what I’d done with the place. I sat down on the flimsy couch and looked around.

My eyes settled on a spot on the floor. It looked as though something pink had been crushed into a dull paste. As I looked closer, I could see the head of a worm that hadn’t been crushed.

“Another fuckin’ worm!” I yelled.  
I cleaned it up with a paper towel and threw it away.

I needed to clear my head, so I sat outside on one of my dirty lawn chairs. The sun had already set. Between each snowflake I could see a star, and between each star a snowflake. For what it was worth, it was beautiful. Amid the whistling wind I could hear Michael and Rebecca having another shouting match.

When the snowfall intensified I went back inside. Just before I flung myself onto my bed I realized I had to take a shit.

I opened the bathroom door and lifted the toilet lid. There was no water. Instead a multitude of worms crawled out from the pipe. Some disappeared beneath the others while the rest climbed over one another, only to slide back into the bowl once they reached the slick walls.

“Jesus H. Christ,” I whispered.  
There must have been at least fifty visible. I decided to deal with it in the morning.

When I woke up I made breakfast and looked out at the thin blanket of snow. I decided to go deer hunting to get my mind off the strange occurrences. I drove to a secluded spot, walked through the snow-covered woods, and spotted a buck.

I raised my rifle and fired. The buck dropped into the snow. That would be enough to feed me for a few weeks. I carried it back to the truck and tossed it into the bed.

When I returned to the park I decided to put off preparing the deer and visit Richard instead.

I knocked three times.

“Bobby, come on in,” Richard called, pulling open the door. Surprisingly, his trailer looked as though it had been cleaned.

“You cleaned up, I see?”

“Yeah, well, those pink bastards wouldn’t stop crawling across the floor, so I thought I’d clean up for a change. Couldn’t hurt.”

“I’ve seen a few around my place too. In the drains. Even in my beer.”

“Feels like a damn infestation,” Richard muttered. “I see more every day.”

I could tell he wanted to talk about   
something else.

“I went hunting today. Got a deer.”

“You like huntin’?”

“Yeah. One of the few things I still enjoy. That and keepin’ the trailer clean.”

“Why stay here, Bobby? This place is   
terrible. I’d leave in a heartbeat.”  
I chuckled, but it came out thin.

“My father bought me that place before he… you know. He spent most of what he had on it. Feels like if I left, I’d be leaving him behind. That, and I’m too poor to go anywhere else.”  
Richard was quiet for a moment. Then he checked his watch.

“It’s not even three. Go enjoy yourself a little. Don’t spend the whole day cooped up here.”

“Yeah. You’re right. Thanks, Richie.”  
I shut the door and drove into town for lunch. The diner was nearly empty. I ordered steak and eggs and a Dr. Pepper. After I finished I reached into my pocket for my wallet and felt things crawling against my hand.

I threw my jacket under the table, paid, and got out fast. The worms were in my clothes now.

Back at the park I grabbed a flashlight and two bottles of bleach, peeled back the skirting of my trailer, and crawled underneath. Rocks dug into my stomach. Near the pipes I found them—thousands of worms gathered around a crack in the ground, some disappearing into it, others climbing over one another in a slow pink mass.

I poured both bottles of bleach over them. They writhed and went still by the hundreds. Then my elbow knocked the flashlight away. In the dark I felt them on my arms, then inside my jeans, then between my toes. I kicked my way out, stripped in the cold, and ran inside before anyone could see.

I called the exterminators. The man on the phone sounded tired.

“We’ve gotten a few calls like that. We’re pretty busy. Try back later.”

“You’ve said that ten goddamn times!” I yelled back.

“Well maybe because we’ve had to work all across the state, asshole! You should see all the other cases of fucking worms in peoples homes!” He yelled back before hanging up. 

That night I slept in the truck. Over the next few days the park emptied. Families packed up and left one after another. Michael and Rebecca were among the last. He leaned out the window of their overloaded camper and told me I should go too. I told him I’d be fine.

By then it was down to me and Richard.  
I checked on him when I could. His voice grew weaker. He stopped answering the door. Once I looked through the broken window and saw the top of his head still rocking slowly in the chair. I told him through the glass that I’d bring groceries the next day. I thanked him for listening about my dad when no one else had. He didn’t answer.

The power and water went out. I kept buying bleach and salt, shoving rags into the drains, pouring what I could into the cracks in the floor. For every worm I killed, more came. Soft squelches and faint crackles rose from under the floorboards at night.

One afternoon I woke in the truck past three. The ground was slick with them. I went straight to Richard’s door and knocked hard. No answer. I forced it open.

The smell hit me first. Richard was still in his chair. Worms moved under his clothes and across his decayed face. His skin had begun to rot. His mouth hung open. His eyes stared at nothing as worms crawled under his eyelids. 

“AGH!” I screamed, stumbling back. 

I grabbed his shoulder. His body was cold and stiff. When I turned to run, the blanket caught my shoe and pulled his body to the floor. His skin had dried into the chair itself. I scrambled out, stepped on them the whole way, and didn’t stop until I reached the empty field and the for-sale sign.

I puked. But I’m still here.

The snow keeps falling. The park is quiet now except for the sound of the ground under my feet—soft popping and cracking, like something shifting just below the surface.

What happened to Richard? What the hell am I going to do?

At this point, I’m not sure.

And when I look around, I can see the worms have followed me out here too.
