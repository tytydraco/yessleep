The summer we stayed with my uncle, my brother and I had one room, bunk beds, and a man who'd never had kids standing in the doorway every night at nine saying the same thing.

"I don't want to hear a peep out of you."

Same words. Same spot. Said like a threat. Then the door shut and his footsteps went down the hall and stopped, which we figured meant he'd gone to bed.

My brother waited ten seconds. Then, from the top bunk, so quiet it was mostly breath:

"Peep."

I about died. I said it back. He said it louder. I said it louder. We got to maybe a normal speaking voice before the floorboard outside the door creaked, and then we were two dead boys, and the door banged open, and there he was in his underwear, hairy and furious, finger out. "I swear to God, if I hear one more." He never finished it. He never had to. Door shut.

That was the best game we ever had.

We got good at it. We learned how loud you could go before the board creaked. We learned he never came in on the first one or the second one, only the third, so you got two free. We learned the threat never changed and never got finished. We learned not to laugh until the door shut. We thought we were driving him out of his mind.

Three weeks in we had our best night. We'd been building to it. My brother went first, loud, and I went louder, and he said it at full volume, "PEEP," like a kid yelling across a pool, and we both lay there waiting for the board and the underwear.

No board.

No door.

We lay there grinning in the dark like we'd finally worn him out.

Then, from the hall, small and careful:

"Peep."

It wasn't him. I knew his voice from three weeks of it. This was doing ours. Badly. Like somebody copying a word in a language they'd never heard spoken, getting the shape right and nothing else.

And I opened my mouth to answer it, because that was the game, and the game was three weeks deep in me. I had the P on my lips when the door opened.

My uncle, in the dark, in the underwear, and not one bit angry. In a voice I'd never heard him use, low, like there was somebody sleeping he didn't want to wake.

"Don't answer it."

My brother came down off the top bunk without a sound and got into mine, behind me, and stayed there.

Then my uncle stepped back into the hall and dragged the chair across the doorway, the wooden one that always sat out there, and he sat down in it facing away from us, toward the stairs, and he stayed there until it was light out.

He was there the next night. And the next. We didn't peep anymore. We listened, and once you're listening you can hear it sometimes, out past the chair, down by the stairs, trying the word. Getting it a little better each time.

The chair had always been in the hall. It had always sat a little away from the wall, facing the stairs, like somebody had just got up out of it. He'd been sitting in it every night since we got there, and the line at the door was the only part of it we ever saw.

He never talked about it. Not that summer, not after. He died eleven years ago. The chair went to the dump with the rest of the house.

I've got two boys now. One room, bunk beds. Every night at nine I stand in the doorway and say it, same words, same spot, the way he said it.

Last week I bought a chair.
