When my grandfather died at eighty-four years of age, there wasn't much left to me. Only his old golden pocket watch and a heavy, somber signet ring made of dull red gold. The stone was a deep carnelian, engraved with a fine, almost organic motif.

Since I was a child, this ring had held a fascination over me. Grandfather had never removed it from his finger. Not even when working in the garden or taking his bath. The skin beneath the metal was white, dry, and appeared as if the gold had grown together with his flesh over the decades. When the undertakers prepared him for the viewing, they had to cut the ring from his finger with a delicate saw. The indentation on his corpse left no bone visible, only a dark, desiccated cavity in the tissue.

The evening following the burial, I sat alone inside my study. The house remained in silence; outside, a cold rain of September beat against the windowpane. I took the ring from its small velvet case. The jeweler had repaired the fine cut in the band with flawless precision. The ring rested with a surprising weight in the palm of my hand, keeping a strange, almost feverish heat of its own.

I slid it onto the ring finger of my right hand. It glided with a strange ease over the knuckle. The gravity of the metal felt pleasant—a comforting, almost consoling connection to my grandfather.

With the thumb, I stroked over the smooth red stone. The warmth of the red gold seemed to pass immediately into my skin. A soothing, deep pulsation expanded inside my hand. It was so relaxing that I closed my eyes and held my breath.

Yet the pulsing did not cease. It grew steadily tighter, more urgent.  
I opened my eyes. The thumping did not originate from my own blood. It came directly out of the ring.  
A sudden, tight spasm constricted my finger. I wished to pull the ring off, but the metal did not move a single millimeter. The band of red gold tightened visibly, pressing itself with unnatural force into my skin until the tissue beneath turned white.

"Damn it," I muttered. I seized the ring with my left hand and pulled with force.  
A sharp, burning pain shot through my forearm. On the underside of my ring finger, precisely where the metal met the skin, the flesh had opened itself. Tiny, hair-thin threads of living red gold pushed themselves greedily beneath my skin.

I stared with disbelief at my hand. The metal was not merely cutting into the skin—it was searching for nourishment. I felt the fine, glowing tendrils digging through the fat layer of my palm. It was a terrible, internal scraping, right along the tendons.

In panic, I attempted once more to tear the ornament from my finger with my left hand. But as my fingertips touched the warm carnelian stone, fine golden thorns pressed out from the sides of the ring and drove themselves deep beneath the nails of my left hand.

I was trapped, my hands coupled together by my own flesh.

I looked down at my chest. Beneath my shirt, fine, red-glowing lines traced themselves out. They spread fan-like across my ribs, sliding directly under my sternum. The tearing pain in my arms gave way abruptly to a terrible, icy numbness.

My heart made a stumbling leap as the metallic roots clamped around the tissue, then submitted silently to the foreign, heavy rhythm of the stone.  
My smartphone lay barely thirty centimeters away upon the desk surface. The screen lit up. When I attempted to extend my hand to call emergency, my muscles no longer responded to my will.

The golden thorns withdrew themselves with a faint, wet snap from beneath the nails of my left hand—the infection was complete, the second hand rendered submissive.  
Yet, upon reaching my heart, something else opened itself.

A foreign, ancient mind seeped like dark ink into my thoughts. There were no words, only images, coldness, and a bottomless, centuries-old sense of belonging. I felt the memories of dozens of men before me whose bodies had been hollowed out in this exact manner. And suddenly, I understood the smile of my grandfather. It had not been tenderness. It was the terrible, pure relief of a host who, after forty years of torment, had finally been relieved of his duty.

The fingers raised themselves slowly all on their own from the table. I could only watch in silence as my hands were guided by the golden strands beneath my skin. With tenderness and a frighteningly gentle precision, the ring led my right hand upward and stroked slowly across my cheek.

In the window glass before me, I saw my reflection: while tears of panic ran down my face, the foreign nerve impulses formed my lips into that same simple, peaceful smile.  
I was no longer the heir. I was merely the farmland for the next generation.
