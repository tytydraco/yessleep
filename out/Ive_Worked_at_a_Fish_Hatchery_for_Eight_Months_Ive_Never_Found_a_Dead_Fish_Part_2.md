I returned to work on Monday.

Not to the hatchery, obviously. Management had arranged for several of us to work out of the administrative office until maintenance was completed, and I'd been assigned a desk in a room occupied by three employees responsible for processing production records. My responsibilities consisted primarily of reconciling inventory estimates and correcting discrepancies in spreadsheets that had apparently accumulated over several years.

Nobody seemed particularly concerned about the closure. One of the women working beside me complained that the reassignment had added twenty minutes to her morning commute. Another had left a jacket at the hatchery and wanted to know when she could retrieve it. Somebody had brought doughnuts, and for most of the morning, the conversation revolved around a football game I'd missed over the weekend. I remember listening to them and wondering how many of those people had walked across the same stretch of gravel where I'd nearly disappeared.

I'd decided before arriving that I wasn't going to tell anyone what I'd witnessed. I didn't know whether management understood what occupied the property, and until I could establish who knew what, announcing that I'd watched the ground swallow a deer seemed like a particularly effective way of making myself a problem. For the time being, I intended to behave exactly as I had before the incident.

It was almost lunchtime when I noticed that several production ponds had been assigned different reporting codes. I asked the woman sitting beside me what they meant. She leaned over, examined the spreadsheet, and explained that certain ponds were subject to supplemental reporting requirements.

“Do we collect different information from those ponds?”

“No. Same stuff you collect everywhere else.”

“Then what's the separate code for?”

“Different reporting destination.”

I asked where the reports went.

“Some federal program, I think. We've got a few of those.”

That wasn't particularly unusual. Commercial hatcheries occasionally participate in government-funded research, restoration projects, and cooperative stocking programs. We'd raised fish for organizations outside the company before. What interested me was the distribution of the reporting codes. Nearly all of them belonged to ponds in the southern portion of the property, including the one we'd attempted to drain shortly before I found the fish in the grass.

After lunch, I began looking through the shared directory for the corresponding reports. Our original sampling sheets were available, but the completed reports were stored separately. When I attempted to open the folder containing them, a message appeared informing me that my account didn't possess the necessary permissions.

These were water quality reports. I'd collected some of that information myself.

Apparently, I was authorized to collect it. I just wasn't authorized to know what happened to it afterward.

My interest wasn't motivated entirely by fear. As a biologist, I desperately wanted to understand the coordination between the ponds and the material I'd found inside those fish. I couldn't even establish whether I was dealing with one organism or a colony. Nothing suggested that it recognized property lines. For all I knew, the highway outside the entrance crossed another portion of it.

How do you recognize an animal preparing to strike when the animal looks like the ground you're standing on? I imagined the gravel closing around my wrists and those transparent filaments penetrating my skin. I wondered whether I'd feel them moving.

The thought of being dragged underground frightened me. The thought of remaining alive afterward frightened me considerably more.

On Tuesday morning, I asked one of the administrative supervisors about the supplemental reporting codes. I told him I'd encountered several unfamiliar entries while processing inventory records and wanted to make sure I was entering the information correctly. He examined the spreadsheet, scrolled through several rows, and asked where I'd obtained the file.

I explained that it had been included in the material I'd been assigned.

“You're not supposed to process those.”

He reached past me and removed the relevant rows from my working copy of the spreadsheet.

“Someone else handles them.”

I asked whether they were associated with a separate research program.

“Did somebody tell you that?”

I said no. I'd assumed the additional reporting requirements might be associated with an outside research organization.

“Federal contract. Nothing you need to worry about. If those come through again, just leave them alone.”

He started walking away before stopping beside the door.

“You're normally out at the hatchery, aren't you?”

I told him I was.

“How long have you been there?”

“Eight months.”

He nodded and left.

During lunch, I began looking through publicly available information concerning the company's government contracts. Most concerned fisheries restoration and the production of animals intended for release into public waters. One agreement, however, appeared to concern the continued operation and maintenance of the hatchery itself. The available description referenced environmental monitoring, biological resource management, and the maintenance of existing research infrastructure. I couldn't locate a detailed statement of the project's objectives or identify any published findings associated with it.

What I did find was a reference to the year in which the original arrangement had been established.

I'd already established that the hatchery reopened in 1986, eight years after the disappearances. Until that moment, I'd assumed the reopening marked the end of whatever investigation had taken place. Now I was considering the possibility that it marked the beginning of something else.

That evening, I returned to the newspaper archives. When I'd first looked into the disappearances, I'd been more interested in what the investigators had uncovered than in the investigation itself. This time, I arranged the articles chronologically, paying particular attention to the officials involved. It wasn't until I reached the weeks following the seventh disappearance that I noticed the people conducting the investigation had changed.

Earlier articles identified local law enforcement officers and volunteer search coordinators. Later articles referred to outside investigators without providing their names or identifying the organizations they represented. Statements from the sheriff became increasingly brief, and references to the missing employees gradually gave way to discussions of environmental conditions and public safety. The final article explained that access to the property had been restricted while specialists conducted an environmental assessment.

There was a correction published in the following week's newspaper. Apparently, an earlier article had included a statement from a volunteer who claimed to have heard one of the missing employees calling for help near the southern ponds. The newspaper explained that the account could not be independently verified and apologized for its publication. I couldn't find the original article. The relevant page was missing from the digitized edition, although that alone wasn't enough to convince me somebody had deliberately removed it.

The first indication of direct federal involvement came from a property record dated February 1979. Shortly after the closure, the hatchery's operating company dissolved, and several parcels were transferred to a newly established corporation. Attached to the transaction was a temporary easement granting the United States government access to almost the entire property for surveying, environmental remediation, and activities associated with public safety. The easement expired in December 1985.

I couldn't locate the environmental assessment or establish what remediation had supposedly been performed. There were incomplete catalog entries, references to documents that hadn't been digitized, and records that had been transferred to other collections. So I began looking for historical aerial photographs.

The earliest aerial photographs showed that several of the southern ponds existed before the hatchery was built. One depression even appeared to change position between surveys, although I couldn't rule out errors in alignment or seasonal flooding. I thought about the pond that had refilled overnight. Perhaps those depressions served a biological function.

The photographs taken during the closure were considerably more disturbing. In 1981, almost the entire southern portion of the hatchery had been cleared. The woodland was gone, several ponds had disappeared, and long trenches extended across areas that had previously contained production infrastructure. Near the center of the cleared land was an excavation approximately three hundred feet across.

I retrieved the newspaper photograph I'd described in my previous post, the one showing those five enormous impressions in the bottom of a partially drained pond. By comparing the surrounding features, I established its approximate location along the northern edge of the excavation. Several trenches extended from there, curving southward before dividing into narrower channels. One appeared to extend underneath a temporary access road before disappearing into the surrounding woods.

I wondered whether the investigators had been following something.

There were no aerial photographs available for the following three years. In the next image, taken in 1985, the excavation was gone. The trenches had disappeared, the access roads were being reconstructed, and several ponds that had been completely removed during the investigation were once again present. Their shapes were remarkably similar to those visible before the closure.

The surrounding woodland hadn't recovered.

The depressions had.

Several were irregularly shaped, difficult to access, and unsuitable for commercial production. I couldn't understand why anybody would bother recreating them. Perhaps they hadn't.

Over the next few days, I began paying closer attention to how the administrative office operated. Most employees knew very little about the supplemental reporting requirements. They knew which documents needed additional signatures and which maintenance requests had to be forwarded elsewhere. Nobody seemed to find that particularly unusual. Understanding why wasn't part of their job.

I came across a maintenance request concerning one of the small weatherproof enclosures mounted throughout the hatchery. I'd driven past them hundreds of times and assumed they belonged to the automated water quality systems. According to the request, the enclosure contained a communications unit, a camera, and several instruments connected to probes installed beneath the surrounding substrate. The probes weren't located in the water. They were buried in the levee.

The installation specifications were inaccessible through my account, but I found another maintenance request associated with a similar enclosure beside the old settling pond. The attached photograph showed a small camera mounted above it. It wasn't directed toward the road or any of the equipment that might reasonably need protection from theft. It was directed toward the bank.

From its position, the camera would have had an unobstructed view of the area where I'd watched the deer disappear. It would have seen me fall and struggle to pull my foot out of the gravel. It would have seen the movement that traveled across the ponds before I escaped. I couldn't access the recordings, but the maintenance log indicated that the installation had been functioning normally throughout the preceding month.

Until that moment, I'd believed I was the only person who had witnessed what happened beside that pond. Now I was wondering how many people had watched it happen, and why none of them had contacted me.

My next discovery came while reconciling several years of production estimates. The work involved comparing projected harvest quantities with the actual number of animals removed from each pond. Several of the older southern ponds consistently failed to meet their projected harvests, which wasn't particularly surprising. What surprised me was how the company accounted for those losses.

Most were assigned conventional categories: handling mortality, disease, predation, environmental stress, and unexplained mortality. Several of the southern ponds had an additional category. The corresponding quantities were deducted from our standing inventory before the projected harvest was calculated. I asked one of the employees whether she knew what the abbreviation meant.

“Contract allocation.”

I asked whether those fish were being transferred to another facility.

“I don't think so.”

“Then where are they going?”

“I just know we're not supposed to count them toward production.”

She told me that records documenting their removal would probably be included in the supplemental reports. I didn't bother trying to access them again. Instead, I compared the quantities with our stocking records and discovered that several of our least productive ponds received additional fish whenever their estimated standing biomass approached a particular threshold. The threshold remained remarkably consistent despite changes in species, production schedules, and projected harvest quantities.

Now I wondered how many of the fish I'd spent eight months raising had ever been intended to leave the property.

I contacted the county records office on Thursday and explained that I was researching the historical development of a commercial aquaculture facility. I wanted to examine engineering documents associated with its reconstruction in the mid-1980s. The woman I spoke with warned me that records from that period were incomplete. Some had been transferred to another repository, although she couldn't establish which one. She eventually located a collection of engineering documents and emailed them to me that afternoon.

Most contained exactly what I'd expected: diagrams of drainage structures, specifications for concrete, and recommendations concerning the stability of earthen embankments. One site plan, however, contained shaded areas that didn't correspond with any proposed construction. The legend identified them as restricted excavation zones. In one location, excavation was permitted to a depth of nearly twelve feet. Less than a hundred yards away, the maximum permitted depth was eighteen inches.

I recognized the second location. It was the cleared lot where I'd found the fish in the grass.

Near the end of the collection was a document titled *Operational Considerations for Long-Term Site Stability*. Individual ponds could be drained for routine management, provided adjacent ponds remained filled. Under no circumstances were all southern ponds to be drained simultaneously. Maintaining water levels can influence the stability of earthen structures, so the recommendation itself wasn't particularly strange. The explanation accompanying it was.

*Previous attempts to maintain the southern basins in a dewatered condition resulted in progressive displacement of surrounding substrate. Displacement ceased following restoration of water levels and the introduction of aquatic biomass.*

The document contained a table outlining minimum stocking quantities for several areas of the property. The figures were expressed in pounds of biomass rather than numbers of individuals, and no particular species was identified. The modern quantities weren't identical, but the ponds subject to minimum biomass requirements corresponded with the ones assigned supplemental reporting codes. Near the bottom of the historical table was a handwritten annotation.

*Replacement quantities are to be calculated against observed acquisition, not recorded mortality.*

Contract allocation.

I finally understood why the missing fish weren't being recorded as ordinary production losses. Their disappearance was apparently part of the work we were being paid to perform.

Several sections of the engineering collection referenced observations made during the original federal investigation. The source documents weren't included, but portions of their findings had been reproduced to justify restrictions imposed during reconstruction. Searching for the corresponding document numbers produced nothing useful. One excerpt concerned the decision to prohibit excavation along an eastern embankment in June 1980.

*Following the cessation of excavation activities, personnel reported vocalizations originating beneath the eastern embankment. No personnel were present within the excavation at the time. Additional excavation was authorized. No individuals were recovered.*

Nearly two years had passed since the disappearances began. The vocalizations could have belonged to somebody entirely unrelated to the missing employees. They could have been misidentified animal sounds or noises produced by water moving through underground cavities. But whatever the investigators heard had been convincing enough to make them start digging.

Another excerpt concerned restrictions on the removal of biological material. An observation dated October 1982 described an adult bovine specimen that had become partially incorporated into the substrate along the eastern margin of an experimental basin. The animal remained responsive to external stimuli despite extensive loss of abdominal tissue. Attempts to recover it had been discontinued following increased activity in adjacent observation areas. Continued responsiveness was documented during subsequent inspections.

The initial observation was dated October 3. The final inspection referenced in the document occurred on October 19.

Sixteen days.

I don't know precisely what the investigators meant by responsiveness. They could have been referring to involuntary muscular contractions rather than conscious behavior. Regardless, the persistence of any physiological response under the conditions described was extraordinary. A subsequent assessment of recovery procedures contained two sentences that I haven't been able to stop thinking about.

*Successful extraction should not be considered synonymous with successful recovery. Specimens removed beyond the established degree of incorporation have consistently experienced immediate physiological failure.*

*Continued responsiveness is not a reliable indicator of recoverability.*

For the first time, I had a possible explanation for what happened to the fish I'd removed from the grass. I'd assumed the animal died because of the injuries it had already sustained. But if those transparent filaments had become integrated with its circulatory system, perhaps they'd been performing functions ordinarily carried out by its missing organs. Removing the fish might have severed the only mechanism keeping it alive.

The investigators hadn't described a single unsuccessful recovery attempt. They'd written that specimens removed beyond a certain degree of incorporation had consistently experienced immediate physiological failure. They'd done it enough times to establish a pattern.

I returned to the observation from June 1980. The report hadn't said that nobody was there.

It said no individuals were recovered.

I received another email from the county records office the following afternoon. The woman explained that several supplemental pages had been included in the collection in error. She apologized for the inconvenience and attached a corrected version. The replacement was eleven pages shorter. The references to the original investigation were gone, although several operational recommendations had been retained with minor alterations.

*Physiological condition* had become *environmental condition*. *Acquisition activity* had become *subsurface activity*. *Biomass introductions* had become *stocking operations*.

The numerical recommendations remained unchanged. The restrictions concerning excavation were identical, as were the minimum stocking quantities and suggested intervals between inspections. Somebody had decided that the procedures could remain publicly available as long as the reasons for them were removed.

I copied both versions onto an external drive.

Then I opened the employee handbook I'd been given when I first started working at the hatchery. New employees were expected to accompany an experienced technician during their first month, even after demonstrating that they could conduct sampling and operate equipment independently. We were assigned specific inspection routes and encouraged to complete them in a consistent order. Certain portions of the southern property were inaccessible without an experienced employee present.

One of the original engineering documents contained a recommendation concerning personnel exposure.

*Introduction of unfamiliar personnel should occur gradually and, where practical, in the presence of individuals associated with established operational routines.*

Another paragraph described a reduction in acquisition attempts following repeated exposure to personnel engaged in predictable activities. It concluded with a warning.

*Reduced acquisition frequency among familiar personnel should not be interpreted as permanent immunity.*

I'd spent nearly every morning of my first month following Dale. He'd shown me which roads to use, where to park, which ponds to inspect first, and where to stand while collecting samples. Occasionally, when I'd suggested taking a different route to save time, he'd insisted that we continue the way we'd been going. I'd assumed he was a creature of habit.

Now I wondered whether the purpose of those thirty days had been to familiarize me with the hatchery or to familiarize the hatchery with me.

I retrieved several of our routine inspection forms. Every morning, somebody was expected to drive the southern access roads and record changes in water elevation, displacement of sediment, unusual wildlife activity, and damage to surrounding vegetation. There was also a category I'd never particularly understood: unexplained absence of wildlife.

One of the historical observations described reduced vertebrate activity preceding significant acquisition events. Personnel had been instructed to record the absence of wildlife alongside visible substrate disturbances and changes in basin elevation. Every category on our inspection form corresponded with something the investigators had identified as potentially significant.

We hadn't been inspecting the condition of the roads. We'd been looking for symptoms.

I'd submitted dozens of those reports. I'd documented deer standing motionless beside abandoned ponds, sections of road becoming uneven despite the absence of rainfall, and changes in water elevation that couldn't be explained by the operation of the inlet or outlet structures. Somebody had been collecting those observations, and I wanted to know what they'd concluded from them. I wanted to see the measurements recorded by the buried instruments. I wanted to know whether the changes we'd documented indicated feeding behavior, metabolic activity, or something more complicated.

I wasn't trying to discover something nobody had ever studied. I was trying to understand something that somebody had apparently been studying for nearly half a century.

Before leaving work that Friday, I asked whether I could participate in the supplemental research. My supervisor explained that we provided operational support, but the research belonged to the contracting organization. Recruitment was handled separately. He couldn't tell me anything else.

The medical implications of an organism capable of sustaining otherwise fatally injured animals were extraordinary. I wondered whether the government remained involved because the hatchery presented a threat or because it represented something valuable. Perhaps both.

The following Monday, I arrived at the office to discover that my temporary assignment had changed. I'd been given several years of equipment inventories and maintenance receipts to organize. The supervisor explained that they'd caught up on most of the outstanding production work and needed additional help elsewhere. When I attempted to retrieve one of the spreadsheets I'd been working with the previous week, I discovered that my account no longer possessed the necessary permissions. The supplemental reporting folders were no longer visible.

I didn't ask about it. Perhaps my permissions had simply been adjusted to reflect my new responsibilities. But shortly before lunch, one of the supervisors stopped beside my desk and asked how I was adjusting to the reassignment.

I told him it was going well.

“Getting used to being indoors?”

I laughed and told him I preferred working outside.

“Probably good to get away from that place for a little while.”

I asked what he meant.

“The hatchery. You've been out there eight months, haven't you?”

I told him I had.

“Never hurts to have a change of scenery.”

Then he asked whether I was sleeping alright.

I told him I was sleeping fine. He smiled, told me to let him know if I needed anything, and continued down the hallway. It might have been nothing more than a supervisor checking on an employee who'd recently been reassigned. But I kept thinking about the camera beside the settling pond. I wondered whether somebody had asked him to find out how I was doing.

I still hadn't heard from Dale.

I'd contacted him several times since the reassignment, initially assuming he'd been transferred to another facility. None of the other technicians had heard from him either. Eventually, I asked the administrative employee responsible for coordinating our temporary assignments where he'd been sent.

She checked the personnel records.

“He wasn't reassigned.”

I asked why.

“His employment arrangements are different from yours.”

“Different how?”

“He's assigned through another organization.”

I asked whether she meant the organization responsible for the supplemental monitoring.

“I don't have access to that information.”

I'd worked beside Dale for eight months. We'd eaten lunch together, complained about broken equipment, and spent entire afternoons driving between ponds. I'd watched him struggle with the same paperwork the rest of us hated. I'd considered him another technician. Apparently, that wasn't how the company classified him.

Later that afternoon, I came across a document attached to a maintenance request concerning equipment that needed to be retrieved from the hatchery. It contained a list of personnel authorized to enter the property during the closure. There were eleven names. I recognized only one.

Dale's.

Several of the others appeared to be associated with technical services companies, while one affiliation corresponded with a federal research office I'd encountered while examining the company's contracts. I searched several of the names. One belonged to a man whose professional background involved geophysical monitoring. Another was associated with a laboratory specializing in cellular physiology. A third had published research concerning tissue regeneration in marine invertebrates. None of those were the people I'd expect to be responsible for replacing pumps or repairing drainage structures.

I asked the employee who'd forwarded the request whether Dale was still working at the facility.

“He's authorized to be there.”

I asked whether he actually was there.

“I couldn't tell you that.”

I explained that I'd been trying to contact him. She suggested I speak with my supervisor.

It occurred to me afterward that I'd been asking the wrong question. I'd asked whether anybody had heard from Dale, whether he'd been reassigned, and whether he'd received additional instructions concerning the closure. I'd never asked whether anybody had actually seen him leave.

I contacted one of the technicians who'd been working near the northern ponds shortly before the facility closed. He remembered seeing Dale's truck beside the old equipment shed. Management had instructed the employees to collect their belongings and leave, and he'd assumed Dale had received the same instructions. The truck was still there when he left. When I asked whether he'd actually seen Dale that morning, he thought about it for several seconds.

He couldn't remember.

I remembered the irregular pump-like noise behind Dale's last phone call. Perhaps he had been at the hatchery when he warned me not to return.

I drove past the hatchery shortly before sunset. It was my second visit since discovering the maintenance sign, although this time I had no intention of approaching the fence. I wanted to establish whether Dale's truck was still there, and I believed I could do that without leaving the highway.

The gate remained locked. Several unfamiliar vehicles were parked near one of the main buildings, including two white pickup trucks and a trailer carrying what appeared to be monitoring equipment. Two individuals stood beside one of the northern ponds. Both wore ordinary work clothes, and one appeared to be holding a tablet while the other examined something near the water.

Farther down the property, beside the old equipment shed, was Dale's truck. I recognized the faded paint along the driver's door and the toolbox mounted behind the cab. I continued driving until I reached a paved pull-off farther down the highway, where I remained inside my truck with the engine running.

From there, I had a partial view of several production ponds. Their water levels were considerably lower than I remembered, and broad strips of exposed sediment extended along the banks. I retrieved my binoculars. The two individuals were still standing beside the nearest pond. One had moved closer to the exposed sediment and was examining a narrow opening several feet above the waterline.

I watched him crouch, extend an instrument toward the opening, and hold it there. The sediment around the opening began to move. Even from that distance, I recognized the smooth contraction of its edges. I'd seen the same thing during our attempted drawdown.

The man withdrew the instrument and stood. His colleague recorded something on the tablet. Neither appeared surprised. They continued walking along the bank.

I lowered the binoculars. For eight months, I'd been working beside people who would have considered that movement impossible. These two had reacted to it with the same degree of interest I'd ordinarily devote to checking a water temperature. Whatever they were observing, they'd seen it before.

For several seconds, I actually considered driving back toward the entrance and asking whether they needed assistance. I wanted desperately to know what the instrument measured and what the contraction indicated. Then I remembered the pressure around my ankle and the report describing an animal that had remained responsive for sixteen days.

The people standing beside that pond might know considerably more about what would happen to me if I became trapped than I did.

I drove home.

I contacted the sheriff's office that evening. I explained that I was concerned about a coworker I hadn't been able to reach, provided his name and the location of his vehicle, and described the circumstances surrounding the closure. I didn't mention the historical investigation or the things I'd witnessed. The dispatcher took my information and explained that somebody would look into it.

Several hours later, a deputy called and explained that they'd contacted the organization responsible for the property. According to the information they'd received, Dale was authorized to remain at the hatchery during the closure.

I asked whether anybody had spoken with him.

The deputy explained that they'd been assured there was no reason to believe he was in danger.

I asked again whether anybody had actually spoken with Dale.

There was a brief pause. The deputy said he didn't have that information.

But I knew the property was being monitored. If Dale was somewhere on that property, there was a reasonable possibility that somebody knew exactly where he was. They might know what condition he was in. They might even be watching him. And for reasons I wasn't authorized to understand, they might have decided that his condition didn't warrant intervention.

I retrieved the original engineering documents and returned to the recommendations concerning personnel exposure. There was a paragraph I'd previously overlooked.

*Where practical, replacement personnel should be introduced before the permanent withdrawal of established individuals. Abrupt removal of familiar personnel has been associated with increased exploratory activity in previously stable operational areas.*

The following recommendation was considerably shorter.

*Personnel continuity should be maintained during interruptions to routine biomass introductions.*

The hatchery was closed. The technicians responsible for feeding and maintaining its fish populations had been transferred elsewhere. I didn't know whether anybody was still introducing animals into the ponds, and the only employee I couldn't account for was the one who'd spent nearly twenty years working there.

Perhaps Dale hadn't been overlooked during the reassignment process. Perhaps somebody had decided that removing him from the property presented a greater risk than leaving him there.

I've spent most of tonight trying to decide what to do. There is still a considerable part of me that wants to understand what the researchers have discovered. They might possess anatomical descriptions, tissue samples, behavioral observations, and physiological measurements that would answer questions I've been struggling with since the incident. They might know how old it is, where it ends, and whether it possesses anything resembling a nervous system. The answers might exist in a database somewhere. I might have been sitting within a few offices of somebody authorized to read them.

That possibility is almost unbearable. I've spent eight months contributing to a research program I didn't know existed, collecting information I wasn't permitted to interpret, and performing responsibilities whose actual purpose was deliberately withheld from me. Despite everything I've discovered, I still want to know more.

But I can't allow that curiosity to determine what I do next. I've made copies of everything I've found and arranged for somebody I trust to retain them. I'm not going to climb the fence in the middle of the night and start wandering around the southern ponds. I need to establish whether there's a legitimate way for me to return, and whether the people responsible for the research know what happened to Dale.

I keep returning to the observation from October 1982.

Sixteen days.

When I first read that report, the possibility of an animal surviving for so long beneath the ground made me physically ill. I'd imagined myself in that condition, incapable of escaping and dependent upon whatever was gradually consuming me. Now I find myself hoping that the investigators were right. I hope whatever occupies that property really can sustain the physiological functions of another animal. I hope it can maintain circulation, prevent the deterioration of tissue, and preserve consciousness long after the injuries it inflicts should have resulted in death.

Because if something has happened to Dale, that might be the only reason he's still alive.

I keep imagining finding him somewhere on the property. I imagine recognizing his clothes beneath the grass, kneeling beside him, and discovering that he's still conscious. I imagine him asking me to help him, and I don't know whether helping him would mean pulling him out or leaving him where I found him.

What frightens me most is that there might already be somebody who knows the answer to that question. Somebody who has studied the process, documented its progression, and established exactly how long a person can remain alive after becoming incorporated. Somebody who could tell me whether Dale can still be saved.

And I might never be allowed to speak with them.

Dale was the only person who understood what had happened to me. He knew enough to warn me, and whatever responsibilities he'd been given at the hatchery, he'd apparently considered my safety important enough to tell me not to return.

I can't leave him there without knowing.

I'm going back to the hatchery.

Not because I believe it's safe.

Not because I believe it remembers me.

But because somewhere on that property, there might still be a man listening to the sound of passing vehicles, wondering how many people are going to drive past before somebody realizes he never left.
