My wife had been sick for months when she died. I was sitting beside her, holding her hand. She had barely spoken that morning, but I kept telling her I loved her and that I was there. When her breathing became slower, I moved closer and held on tighter. Then she took her last breath. I kept holding her hand long after she was gone.

Eight months later, I bought the townhouse. I had tried to stay in our old house, but eventually I couldn't stand it anymore. Everything reminded me of her. Her shampoo was still in the shower, a book she'd never finished was still on her nightstand, and at night I'd sometimes wake and reach across the mattress before remembering there was nobody there.

By then I was barely functioning. I went to work because I had bills to pay. I ate when I was hungry enough. I slept badly and spent most of my time alone. I wasn't trying to die, but there wasn't much about being alive that interested me anymore. I needed a fresh start, even if I didn't really believe one was possible.

The townhouse was new construction in a development on the edge of town. Three floors, gray siding, white walls and stainless steel appliances. Some of the neighboring houses weren't even finished yet. There were piles of lumber beneath plastic sheeting and stretches of bare dirt where lawns would eventually be.

There wasn't anyone to help me move, so I hired movers. When they finished, I watched their truck disappear down the street and closed the front door behind them. For the first time in months, I was standing somewhere that didn't remind me of her. I thought that would help. Instead, I'd never felt more alone.

My life became very small after that. Work, home, dinner, television, bed. I didn't know my neighbors, nobody visited, and entire weekends sometimes passed without me speaking to another person. The townhouse was quiet enough that I quickly learned its ordinary sounds: the refrigerator clicking off, the furnace starting, water moving through the pipes and the occasional creak somewhere in the walls as the new building settled.

That's why I noticed the footsteps.

They started about a month after I moved in. I was downstairs watching television when I heard several slow creaks from the floor above me. They seemed to move from one side of the bedroom to the other. I muted the television and listened. There was another creak, then nothing. I went upstairs and checked the bedroom, but it was empty.

It happened again a few nights later, then again the following week. Always slow, never more than a handful of steps. I told myself it was the house settling, and eventually I stopped going upstairs to check.

Then one evening I came home from work and smelled her shampoo. I had just reached the third-floor hallway when I caught it. It was faint, but I knew that smell. For years I'd smelled it on her pillow and in her hair when she rested her head against me. For one second, I forgot she was dead. I thought she was in the shower and almost called out to her.

The bathroom was empty. The shower was dry, and the smell was already fading by the time I stepped inside. I stood there for a while before sitting on the edge of my bed and crying.

After that, little things started happening more often. I'd wake in the morning and find the bedroom door partly open when I remembered closing it. I'd hear a floorboard creak in the hallway while I was lying in bed. Once I woke and found the blanket wrinkled on the empty side of the mattress. None of it was dramatic, and somehow that made it worse.

Then I saw her.

I'd fallen asleep on the couch downstairs with the television on. When I woke sometime after midnight, the screen had gone dark and the room was lit only by the streetlights outside. I looked toward the stairs and saw someone standing on the landing above me.

It was a woman. I couldn't see her face, only a shape in the darkness, but something about the way she stood made my chest tighten. I knew those shoulders. I knew the slight tilt of her head. I whispered her name, and she was gone.

I turned on every light in the townhouse and stayed awake until morning. 

For the next few nights, I slept downstairs. I couldn't understand why I was so frightened. I'd spent eight months wishing for one more minute with her. I'd dreamed about her so often that sometimes waking up felt like losing her all over again. But wanting someone back and seeing them standing at the top of a dark staircase were very different things. Death had made someone I loved more than anything feel unfamiliar.

A week passed without anything happening. I had almost convinced myself I'd seen a shadow when I saw her again. I had turned off the television and was walking toward the kitchen when I caught a reflection in the black screen. 

Someone was standing behind me.

I turned immediately, but the room was empty. I'd only seen her for a fraction of a second, but it had been enough. I sat down on the couch and covered my face with my hands. I wasn't as frightened that time. Mostly I was sad.

After that, the fear slowly began to change. I still startled when I heard footsteps upstairs, and my heart still raced when something moved at the edge of my vision, but I stopped sleeping downstairs. I started listening for her.

Sometimes I'd come home from work and stand in the entryway for a few seconds before taking off my coat. I'd say, "I'm home," even though nothing ever answered. At night, I'd occasionally talk to her. I told her about work, how much I hated eating alone, and things that happened during the day that she would have found funny. Mostly, I told her I missed her.

Sometimes nothing happened for days. Then I'd catch the smell of her shampoo on the stairs, hear two soft steps in the hallway or wake in the middle of the night certain someone had just passed the bedroom door.

Once, while carrying laundry upstairs, I saw her standing at the end of the hallway. She was there for maybe two seconds, long enough for me to stop and see that she was looking at me. Then I blinked and the hallway was empty.

She looked sad, and that stayed with me. Whatever was happening in the townhouse never felt angry. Nothing chased me or threatened me. Doors didn't slam and objects didn't move across rooms. It felt lonely, and I understood lonely.

One night near the end of winter, I woke because I felt the mattress shift beside me. When I opened my eyes, she was sitting on her side of the bed.

I couldn't breathe. The room was dark, but there was enough light coming through the blinds for me to see her profile. She looked like herself, not the way she'd looked during the final months of her illness. She looked like my wife.

She was staring toward the window. I whispered her name, and she turned toward me. My eyes immediately filled with tears as I told her I missed her. For a few seconds, neither of us moved.

Then I reached for her.

Before I could touch her, she was gone.

My hand landed on the empty mattress. I sat there staring at the place where she'd been, crying quietly in the dark. Eventually I lay back down, but I don't think I slept again that night.

Something changed in me after that. I stopped being afraid of her. For the first time since she'd died, I started wanting to come home. Not because I was getting better, but because she might be there. That distinction mattered.

Weeks passed, and I saw her only in fragments. 

Sometimes I'd look down the stairs and think she was standing at the bottom, only for her to be gone a moment later. Once, while rain ran down the kitchen windows, I saw her near the counter. I looked away for only a second, and when I looked back, there was nobody there.

Sometimes I woke in the middle of the night and thought I could feel someone lying beside me. I never reached over immediately. Instead, I'd keep my eyes closed and let myself believe for a few seconds that she was there. Those few seconds became the best part of my life. I knew how sad that was, but I didn't care.

Then spring came, and she started disappearing. At first I didn't realize it was happening. A week passed without footsteps, then another. I stopped smelling her shampoo. The hallway remained empty. The mattress didn't move beside me anymore.

The townhouse became quiet again. It was the same silence I'd wanted when I moved there, but now I hated it. I started staying awake later, lying in bed watching the doorway and waiting for a shadow to move. Sometimes I'd whisper her name into the darkness. I'd still come home from work and say, "I'm home," but nothing happened.

Weeks passed, and I began to understand what was happening. Whatever had been keeping her here was ending. I'd gotten used to the impossible comfort of knowing that she might still be somewhere in the house, and I didn't want to lose that. I wanted the footsteps. I wanted the fleeting shadows. I wanted to smell her shampoo on the stairs. I wanted my wife back, even if this was the only way I could have her.

Then one night, sometime before dawn, I woke suddenly. I didn't know why. The bedroom was quiet, with gray morning light beginning to show around the edges of the blinds. Then I felt the mattress sink beside me.

I turned and saw her sitting on her side of the bed.

There was something different about her this time. The sadness I'd seen before was gone. 

She was smiling.

It was a small smile, the kind she'd given me a thousand times when she was alive. Across restaurants, from the passenger seat of the car, or from her side of the couch when we were both doing separate things and happened to look at each other. That smile had once been so ordinary. Now it broke me.

I sat up, already crying. "Please don't go."

She looked at me. For weeks I'd been afraid this was happening. The footsteps had stopped. I hadn't smelled her shampoo or caught a glimpse of her in the hallway. Now she was here again, and somehow I knew why.

"I don't want to lose you again. I just want you back."

My voice broke. She tilted her head slightly, and that tiny familiar movement made me cry harder. I didn't care how impossible any of this was. I would have listened to footsteps in an empty house for the rest of my life if it meant some part of her was still there.

But looking at her, I finally understood that she wasn't supposed to stay. Whatever had brought her back had only ever been temporary, and I had spent every night waiting for her. Every footstep, every shadow in the hallway, every time I woke and looked toward her side of the bed, I'd been waiting for my wife to come home.

I reached toward her and thought I felt something beneath my fingers, cold and soft and barely there. Tears ran down my face as I told her I didn't want her to go.

"But I know you have to."

Her expression softened.

I closed my eyes and remembered her laugh, the sound of her coming through the front door and the way she'd move closer to me in bed when she was cold. I thought about all the ordinary moments I'd never known I was supposed to memorize because I'd believed there would always be more of them. I wanted one more morning with her, one more dinner, one more stupid argument about something neither of us would remember the next day, and one more night falling asleep beside her.

But that wasn't what she'd come to give me.

She'd come to say goodbye.

"I love you," I whispered.

For a moment, I could still feel that faint cold against my hand. Somehow, without hearing her say a word, I knew she loved me too. I didn't know how I knew. I just did.

I started sobbing. "I'm here. I'm right here."

I kept my eyes closed. Every part of me wanted to open them and look at her one more time, but I knew if I did, I'd ask her to stay. I loved her too much to do that.

"It's okay," I whispered. "You can go."

I cried harder as soon as I said it. I told her I loved her again, and for another moment I thought I could feel her hand against mine.

Then I couldn't.

I stayed there with my eyes closed for a while longer. When I finally opened them, her side of the bed was empty.

I reached for her without thinking, but my hand landed on the empty mattress. I stared at the place where she'd been, and then I broke down. 

I curled over her side of the bed and cried into the pillow. Some part of me immediately wanted to take it back. I wanted to tell her I hadn't meant it. I wanted the footsteps again and the smell of her shampoo in the hallway. I wanted to wake the next night and find her sitting beside me.

I wanted my wife.

But the room stayed quiet.

Slowly, I understood that the silence was what I'd given her. I had spent so long wishing she would come back, and now I hoped she wouldn't. Not because I wanted her gone, but because I loved her enough to let her go.

I lay there crying as the morning light filled the bedroom, my hand resting on the empty side of the bed. She had found me when I was completely alone. She had stayed for a little while, and before she left, somehow she'd made sure I knew the one thing I had been most afraid death had taken from me.

She still loved me.

I pressed my hand into the empty pillow and closed my eyes. "I love you too."
