​The story I’m about to tell you happened back in 1997, in a small village in the Peloponnese, Greece. It’s the place I come from, where I lived until I was 21 years old.

​Ever since fifth grade, I had known this guy named Kostas. Since I was sitting alone at my desk, our teacher made us sit together. Kostas was the new kid in the village, and you know how it is in rural places. When someone new arrives, they either get pushed straight to the margins or they become the most popular kid, since their presence breaks the boring routine for a bit.

​Kostas was somewhere in between. He didn't make tons of friends, but he wasn't pushed aside either. It was almost like he was made to fit right in with the rest of the village immediately.

​Even though back in primary school we didn't say much beyond the usual stuff, we really bonded in middle school. We both liked motorbikes, and we both supported the same football team, which actually wasn't very popular in the Peloponnese.

​Long story short, Kostas and I became best friends. Then came high school, and after that, our studies. Every September, we would leave for the cities where we were studying. Me for psychology in Athens, Kostas for math in Patras. During the holidays at Christmas, Easter, and summer, we were inseparable again.

​By then, we had already gotten our bikes. Nothing fancy, but they got the job done. At night we’d ride around, visiting a different village every day. This was back before GPS and google maps, so we felt like explorers. We never knew what we’d see in each village, what was special about each tavern, or where the best beach was.

​That night still haunts me. As I said, the year was 1997, late November. Surprisingly, it wasn't particularly cold, but it was extremely humid and foggy. It was around 5:00 PM, and we decided to head up to Agia Katerina, a village we’d never explored before.

​On the way, we passed farmers. Some were working their fields, others were resting under some shade, others were watering. I didn't pay much attention, nor should I have, probably.

​Just before the small bridge over the stream, exactly two kilometers before Agia Katerina, I saw a strange figure standing on the right edge of the bridge. He was wearing a black cloak, not a very popular piece of clothing among the farmers in our parts. Whether I wanted to or not, I slowed down my bike to take a better look as I went past.

​What struck me as even stranger was that he was holding a scythe. Not one of the small ones, but a huge scythe, the kind farmers used to harvest crops before they learned about tractors.

​Kostas rode past him quickly, but I geared down even more to stare. Who could that be, and what was he doing in our area? There used to be nomadic herders once, but the last ones had settled down permanently around 1970, over 20 years before that.

​As I got closer, I was genuinely terrified. I’ve never told anyone about this incident, ever, because they'd think I was crazy. The guy had no face. It wasn't that his face was sad, dark, or angry, it was simply non-existent.

​I tried to rationalize it. We were at the end of the twentieth century, and I didn't want to believe in old ghost stories. Was it a scarecrow? Was it some prankster from the village trying to mess with people? A thousand thoughts raced through my head to justify what my own eyes had just seen live right in front of me.

​We stayed in Agia Katerina for two hours. It was a tiny village. Back then it had about 160 residents; in the last census, it’s down to about 50. There wasn't much to it. In the main square, there was a cafe where we sat down and drank a cold Coca-Cola while listening to a local musician playing his bouzouki. Next to it was a grill house, which the cafe owner told us made amazing sausage, but we weren't very hungry that day.

​We walked around the village for a bit, and once we got bored, we headed back on the route that haunts me to this day. For a moment, I thought about shouting to Kostas to tell him not to go that way, to ask him to take the other road, or even to wait and head back home tomorrow morning. But what could I tell him? That I saw a weird scarecrow on the bridge that scared me?

​We left. The first few kilometers, despite the fog, were surprisingly peaceful. We reached the bridge and crossed over. Oddly enough, the scarecrow wasn't there. For a second I panicked, thinking something bad was about to happen. But we covered another two kilometers, and everything seemed fine. It was probably all just scenarios in my head; maybe the scarecrow never existed at all.

​Just before reaching the main local town, exactly 20 kilometers before our own village, it happened on a sharp curve. A truck driver, who was driving without proper lights at night, collided with Kostas. He hit him, and Kostas was thrown hard into the ditch. My mind froze. I got off my bike, threw off my helmet, and ran toward him, but it was too late. Kostas was breathing very slowly. I got him onto my bike and rushed to the town clinic, but I didn't make it in time. Kostas had already lost too much blood, and his injuries were severe. Despite the incredibly noble efforts of the doctors, my childhood friend passed away five days later.

​Needless to say, I left the village after that and I haven't returned since.

​I haven't told this story to anyone, as I said, because they’ll take me for a lunatic. Was what I saw just a strange scarecrow? Was it just the work of a prankster who happened to be in the wrong place at the wrong time? Was it all in my head? Or did I actually see the Reaper? The Reaper waiting for us to finish our ride just to take Kostas to the other side.

​It's one of those stories I think about every day, one of those things that no matter how many years pass, stays nailed into your memory. Even though I managed to get my degree and now help other people deal with their traumas, I haven't gotten over mine yet.
