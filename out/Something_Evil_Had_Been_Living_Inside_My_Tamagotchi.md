For anyone too young to remember them, Tamagotchis were little virtual-pet toys from the late ’90s and early 2000s. Small little plastic eggs with tiny black-and-white graphics, three buttons, and a keychain loop at the top. They were mainly about taking care of your virtual pets. You fed the creature inside, played minigames, cleaned up after it, and tried to keep it 'alive' even though eventually, it would pass away. Problem with the first batch was that there was no way to 'pause' the game and so you'd have to go back and check in on it. 

Every classroom had at least one kid whose Tamagotchi would start chirping from the bottom of a backpack back in my day. Teachers hated them. Eventually, many later versions added ways to pause the game. 

I had one as a kid. For a brief moment. 

I remember my friends bringing theirs to school. I remember being jealous of anyone who managed to keep theirs alive long enough because well...

...I don’t remember much about mine.

I only remember my parents refusing to replace it.

Apparently, I lost it after about a week. I begged for a new one, or maybe new batteries - I can’t remember exactly what the issues was, and my parents always told me 'no'. They said I had proven I was irresponsible.

That was that. I got over it eventually. Or so I thought...

Until three weeks ago.

I had reached one of those strange points in adulthood where I had more free time than I knew what to do with. One night, while browsing online listings for old game systems and other childhood junk I absolutely did not need, I found it.

An original Tamagotchi.

Not one of the newer rereleases. This was an old one: faded purple shell, rounded plastic edges, three cheap little buttons under the screen. The listing claimed it was “tested and working,” although the pictures were grainy enough that I could barely see the display.

It was more expensive than it should have been. But it wasn't going to be breaking the bank. And, nostalgia got me.

When I lost mine as a child, I had been really jealous of my friends who still had theirs. I had vague memories of wanting another one badly enough to cry about it at least once. More than that, I wondered what it had been like to actually use mine before I lost it or whatever. 

So I bought it.

It arrived about two weeks later in a small cardboard box.

At first, nothing seemed wrong. It was exactly how I remembered Tamagotchis looking: egg-shaped, light enough to feel almost hollow, small enough to disappear in your palm. A bit worn out, but it looked like it worked. 

And it was already running.

I pressed the middle button, expecting it to ask me to choose an egg or reset the device.

Instead, the screen flickered.

There was already something inside.

At first, I assumed it was a normal Tamagotchi character. The display was old, the pixels were dim, and I couldn’t make out much beyond a pale, rounded shape in the center of the screen.

But it was facing me. Above it, an icon blinked.

A skull and crossbones.

I pressed every button. Nothing happened.

I held down different combinations, trying to reset it. Still nothing. The creature remained on the screen, unmoving save for a small to-and-fro, while that unfamiliar skull icon flashed above its head.

I thought it might mean the previous owner’s pet had died.

That happened eventually, no matter how carefully you took care of one. You could feed it, clean up after it, play games, and keep it happy for weeks. Eventually, you would wake up to a tiny gravestone or angel on the screen. At least, that’s what I remembered.

I searched online for the skull icon.

I found old instruction manuals, collector forums, scanned advertisements, and videos of people restoring vintage Tamagotchis. None of them mentioned a skull-and-crossbones symbol. The older models had death screens, but they were supposed to show a little angel or a gravestone.

Not that thing.

While I was searching, the device began to beep. One sharp chirp. Then another.

Then a rapid sequence of beeps that didn’t match any Tamagotchi sound I remembered. It almost sounded like something crying out in pain. I started mashing buttons. 

The Tamagotchi kept beeping.

Its screen kept flickering.

That was when I decided I had received a defective or modified device. I put it back in its shipping box, filed a complaint with the seller, and mailed it back the next day.

The next morning, the Tamagotchi was sitting on my bedside table.

It was exactly where I usually left my phone charger.

The shell was smudged with fingerprints. Its keychain loop was bent. The skull-and-crossbones icon flashed over and over on the screen.

I stood there for a long time, trying to convince myself that I had made some stupid mistake. Now, I can be forgetful. I will admit that. I misplace my keys. I leave laundry in the washing machine too long. I have gone to the grocery store and realized halfway there that I had forgotten my wallet.

But I had not imagined mailing that thing away. It was far too elaborate to have simply hallucinated something like that. 

I checked the tracking number. The package didn't exist apparently. 

I threw the Tamagotchi into my kitchen trash.

Not carefully, either. I buried it under coffee grounds, spoiled leftovers, food packaging, and a tied garbage bag. Then I carried the trash downstairs and threw it into the dumpster behind my building.

I remember the lid slamming shut.

For the rest of the day, I tried not to think about it.

That lasted until I went to the library looking for something to entertain me. 

**Beep.**

It came from my jacket pocket.

For one stupid second, I felt nostalgic. I remembered classrooms full of kids trying to silence their Tamagotchis before a teacher noticed.

Then it beeped again.

Louder.

I reached into my pocket.

The Tamagotchi was there.

The screen was still on.

The skull-and-crossbones icon was gone.

In its place was a field of static - black and white pixels twisting and crawling over one another. It looked less like television snow and more like a broken image trying to force itself through a screen too small to contain it.

**Beep. Beep. Beep.**

A woman nearby gave me a dirty look. She put a finger to her lips and pointed to the sign saying 'This is a Library. Please put your phones on silent.'

I tore off the battery cover.

The batteries fell into my palm.

The Tamagotchi kept beeping.

I went out of the library.

Once I got home, I took a small hammer to it. It broke apart. 

The beeping stopped. I threw the fragments of the device outside. 

And that was the end of it. I'd hoped. 

That night, I woke, disoriented. 

Then I heard something moving in the kitchen.

At first, I thought someone had broken in.

A cabinet door opened. Something heavy scraped across the floor.

My silverware drawer slid out with a slow metallic squeal.

I grabbed my phone and started to call emergency services.

Before I could press the button, I heard a sound.

**Beep!**

I opened my bedroom door a crack. 

For half a second, I saw the outline of a strange creature scurrying near the fridge. 

There was the sound of wet breathing. 

I can’t describe it properly. My eyes refused to settle on its shape. At first it looked like a shadow, but shadows don’t have depth. This did. It was a mass of blackness folding inward on itself, as if it had too many joints bending in too many directions.

It was taller than my refrigerator.

Its head - or what I thought was its head - was tilted toward the counter.

It was rummaging through my cabinets.

Then it stopped.

The kitchen went silent.

Slowly, very slowly, it turned toward my bedroom.

I shut the door.

Something hit it almost immediately.

The impact shook the frame.

A second hit followed.

Then a growl.

Not an animal sound. Not exactly. It was wet and distorted, like a dog growling through a broken speaker.

**Beep.** 

The door buckled inward.

I pushed my dresser against it. Then my desk chair. Then myself.

It has been trying to get in for hours.

Sometimes it scratches at the door. Sometimes it throws itself against it hard enough to make the hinges scream. Sometimes it goes silent long enough that I think it has left.

Then I hear the small, familiar sound of a Tamagotchi beeping somewhere on the other side.

I think I know what the skull meant now.

Whatever was inside was not just dead. It was cursed. 

I broke it but that didn't destroy it.

It released it from the device instead.

And now, I'm terrified of what it plans to do now that its free.
