My neighbourhood was a quiet one, full of morning pleasantries on neat pathways as workers scurried off to their individual destinations. They had to travel considerable distances for employment, as our village had only a bookshop, a pub, a church and a school. "Still, it's everything you need, really," my mother often claimed with her never-ending cheeriness.

I worked at the bookshop. So sheltered was my existence that I had become the very person I despised: someone living and working in my village, a small world with small encounters. The books gave me my travel, my wealth, my romance, my sex, an escape to other times, lands and perspectives. I had always read fervently, and naturally, my mother was great friends with the shopkeeper, who had needed an assistant as she grew older. "You were destined to work there," Mother often chirped.

When the first woman disappeared from our village, apparently no one was surprised. Mother and her ilk discussed her disappearance over chintz tablecloths and pots of tea, commenting that she had no doubt run off with a man, as "she was the sort". Her husband was an unpleasant drinker (shunned from those polite morning pleasantries we spoke of earlier), and she had begun an affair with a colleague from her workplace.

"You can't really blame her," my mother said, although her face seemingly had other ideas.

And so, no more was said about her. My days continued to be filled with the smell of leather-bound, dusty books and managing the busy spell of my day at lunchtime, when a maximum of three customers would come in.

The second woman's abrupt departure caused "more of a fuss" (Mother's words). She was thoughtful, reserved and educated. "More like us," Mother remarked.

The woman had lost her son recently, so it was thought that she had perhaps left to escape her grief and the painful reminders of his childhood that the neighbourhood provided. No one connected the disappearances. There was the organisation of the local fête and the pressing matter of which youngsters had scrawled obscenities on the bench near the church, both of which warranted far greater attention.

"People just don't parent anymore," Mum bemoaned.

The last two women caused a little more of a stir, despite the fact that Mrs Jenkins at number 8 hadn't baked any apple turnovers for the aforementioned fête, as she had promised.

They were younger women, renting a double room above the pub. Mother had called them "lively".

I had a conversation with the blonde girl one evening. She had noticed the book I was carrying under my arm, carefully protecting it from the light rain as I left the shop.

"I've read that," she had said, but when I questioned her about the ending, it was clear she hadn't. She had, in fact, simply wanted a light for her cigarette.

Still, I enjoyed the chatter about her travels, her university days and her ex-boyfriend. I couldn't contribute, but I was an eager, amicable listener.

Again, these women were gradually forgotten. "Probably owed rent and left quickly," Mother deduced.

They weren't the sort of women Mother and her associates would be greatly concerned about. Some younger local lads had found it surprising that they had left without having a goodbye drink with them, but they had neither the determination nor the intelligence to pursue their worries.

Hopefully, by now, you are anticipating that I will confess to being their murderer. With my axe to grind over my minuscule, mundane, pathetic existence and my overbearing mother, perhaps you thought I truly ground said axe?

With my passion for books, had I developed parasocial relationships with serial killers? Had their convoluted, dastardly stories, nestled inside the true crime section of our small, unassuming bookshop, filled my mind with grandiose thoughts of "being someone"?

Well, you are wrong.

I will always be that quiet young man from the village, with a passion for literature and a life that starts and ends on my doorstep. With my books, with the neat pathways, with my mother.

"Please, you won't tell anyone, will you?" are the words she says most frequently nowadays
