I'd like to say I knew something was off from day one, but honestly? I just thought Pam was just a bit of a grump.

Let me back up. I'd spent four years grafting, double shifts, skipped holidays, living off pasta and spite, all so I could rent somewhere that didn't have a main road outside it or a neighbour who practised drums. And then I found 33 Blossom Drive. Quiet, quaint, the kind of place with hanging baskets and a postbox that looked like it had opinions. I cried when I got the keys. Actually cried, on the doorstep, in front of the letting agent.

Moving day. I'd left my car out front while I lugged boxes in, and I was halfway up the path with a lamp under my arm when a woman came flying out of next door. Small, grey bob, cardigan buttoned wrong, and a look on her face like I'd set fire to her cat.

"You're blocking the driveway," she said. Well, she tried to say it firmly. It came out wobbly, like she'd rehearsed it in the mirror and lost her nerve halfway.

I looked at the car. I looked at the driveway. There was easily room for a bus. "Oh God, sorry, I'll move it now."

"Thank you," she said, and she genuinely looked relieved, which was weird. Like I'd handed her something she'd been scared to ask for. Her eyes kept flicking back to her own front window. I put that down to nosiness, because every village has one.

That was Pam.

A week later, a letter from the council. Complaints of "excessive noise" and "a persistent odour of cannabis" at my address. I read it standing in my kitchen with a mug of tea and went a bit red, because, okay, fair. One night of music while I unpacked, and one cheeky joint in the bath to celebrate. One. I'd even cracked the window. Apparently that wasn't enough.

So I felt properly bad. But I also felt a bit confused, because a letter? From the council? Over one night? I marched next door, ready to say sorry, and caught Pam in the front garden with a pair of secateurs she wasn't using.

"Pam, hi. Quick one, I've had a letter from the council about music and a weed smell. And I'm really sorry, that was me, my first night, I got carried away. But if it was bothering you, you could've just knocked. I'd have sorted it straight away."

She went very still. "Oh. Well. It wasn't us who..." She stopped herself, glanced at the house, and lowered her voice. "My husband George isn't really the confrontational type."

"Right," I said. "And you?"

She gave a tiny laugh that didn't have anything in it, darting her eyes back to the house. "Oh, and my son, Tim. He works strange hours so he didn't appreciate the noise."

"Again I am so sorry. It wont happen again."

She said "That's very kind" with a politeness so careful it was almost painful, like she was handling a plate that might shatter. Then she scurried inside and shut the door so quietly I barely heard the click.

For a couple of weeks, nothing. I kept the music low, kept the washing line tidy, even bought a doormat that said "Welcome" in the hope it would soften the street's overall vibe.

I'd cross paths with George now and then. Tall, stooped, grey, with a face like a man permanently waiting for bad news. I'd wave, say "Morning!", and get nothing back, just eyes straight ahead. I decided he was deaf, or just old-school, or hated me. All plausible.

Then my friend Dani came round one Saturday and parked at the edge of their driveway, with the whole other half free. I was making tea when I heard a noise outside, and George was standing at the end of the drive, shaking, red in the face, jabbing his finger at Dani's car.

"Move it," he hissed. "Move it now."

"Sorry, mate, I didn't think..."

"Now."

Dani looked at me over the car roof with her eyebrows somewhere near her hairline. "Is he okay?"

"No idea," I said. And that's what bothered me afterwards. Angry people I get. But under the anger he looked terrified. He moved like someone who'd already imagined the consequences.

The crying came a few days after that.

I was in my kitchen with the back door open, and I heard it. A child crying. Proper, hiccupping sobs, the kind that make your chest tighten. Then Pam's voice, high and pleading, and George's, lower, saying something I couldn't catch. They were in their back garden, arguing in hushed, frantic tones.

Now, I'd like to say I minded my own business. I didn't. I crept out to the fence and peered over the top.

Where I expected a crying child, a pleading Pam and an arguing George, there was just Tim, standing alone in the middle of the lawn in a grey jumper, perfectly still. Forty-eight-ish maybe, soft-faced, neat hair. And he was already looking straight at me with a blank, piercing stare, like he'd known I was there for ten minutes and had been waiting.

"Hi!" I said, with the confidence of someone who's got a pound coin stuck up their nose. "Sorry, I thought I heard..."

Nothing. The crying had stopped. The whole garden had gone silent, even the birds. Tim continued staring as I awkwardly slinked back down into my own buisness.

I went back inside and made a second tea I didn't want.

After that there was three more letters in two weeks. Noise. Cannabis. Noise and cannabis, which I felt was just showing off.

I started wearing headphones for all music. I took my weekend smoke, to Dani's flat, forty minutes away, and came home smelling of nothing but her scented candles. And still the letters came. One even gave a time: 11:40 p.m. on a Tuesday, when I'd been asleep since ten with a face mask on.

I sat on the edge of my bed with the letter in my hand and, for the first time, I felt properly frightened. The specificity of it got me. Whoever was writing these knew my house and my routines and was making stuff up anyway.

I'd had enough. I put my shoes on and went next door.

Pam opened the door before I'd even knocked, like she'd been stood behind it the whole time.

"I need to speak to George," I said. "Properly, face to face. I'm calm, I promise, I just want to sort this out."

"He's not in, love," said a voice.

It was Pam's voice. Same warm, wobbly tone. The problem was it came from upstairs, and Pam was right there in front of me, and her mouth hadn't moved.

I'll be honest, I didn't think. I just stepped past her into the hallway.

It was spotless. That's the first thing. Show home spotless, the kind of place where nobody's allowed to sit down. Shoes lined up by the door with the toes all pointing the same way. A clock ticking really loudly. The telly was off, the radio was off, everything was off. You know how a house usually has a hum? This one didn't. It was like walking into a held breath.

George was at the bottom of the stairs, hands clasped in front of him like he was waiting for a bus. As the voice upstairs carried on ("He's popped to the shops, I'm afraid, he won't be long"), I looked right at his mouth. Shut. Tight. Lips gone white.

Then the voice changed. Gruff, tired, flat. "Leave it, Pam. Tell her we'll look into it."

And that was George. Every crack in it. Every bit of rasp. Except George was stood three feet from me, saying nothing, staring at the carpet.

"Right," I said, because apparently that's what I say when my brain stops working. "Is someone going to tell me what's going on?"

"Pam," said the voice, sweet as anything, "why don't you put the kettle on for our guest?"

Pam flinched. Properly flinched. She started for the kitchen, but George made a tiny noise in his throat, and she stopped dead, and the pair of them looked up the stairs at exactly the same moment.

A floorboard creaked. Then another.

"Or do I have to remind you of Laura?"

That one belonged to neither of them. It was a man's voice, lower, calm, almost bored. And I watched the colour just leave the pair of them. Pam's hand went to her mouth. George shut his eyes like he'd been slapped. Whatever that name meant, it was the worst thing anyone could've said in that hallway.

Then the crying started.

Footsteps on the stairs, slow, and a child sobbing, that hiccupy, can't-catch-your-breath kind of crying. I know what a child sounds like when they're scared, and this was exactly that. It came down the stairs towards us, getting closer, and my whole body went cold, because I was expecting a little girl.

It was Tim.

Middle aged, grey jumper, neat hair, one hand trailing along the banister, tears actually running down his face, and that child's voice coming out of his mouth. Please, I'll be good. I'll be good, I'm sorry, please don't.

He got to the last step, looked up, and saw me.

And it just stopped. Like someone had flicked a switch. He wiped his face with his sleeve, smoothed his jumper down, and smiled at me. Warm. Friendly. Polite.

"Oh!" he said, in a voice I hadn't heard before, his own, soft and pleasant. "You must be Micha. Sorry, you caught us at a bit of a funny moment. Family stuff."

I couldn't speak. My mouth had gone dry.

"Mum, the tea," he said gently, and Pam practically ran into the kitchen. He turned to George. "Dad, you look tired. Sit."

George sat. In the armchair by the window, straight-backed, hands on his knees, eyes on the floor. Tim watched him do it, and I saw something cross his face that I didn't like at all. Satisfaction. The look of someone who's just watched a dog sit on command.

"We feel terrible about all this," Tim went on, and then, and I want to be really clear about this, George's voice came out of Tim's mouth, perfectly, mid-sentence, without him even changing his expression. "We never wanted to involve the council. We just didn't know how to bring it up."

Then back to himself. "Dad's so shy. Aren't you, Dad?"

George nodded. Slowly. Like it hurt.

Pam came back with a tray. Four cups, and I noticed she'd put out the good ones. Her hands rattled the saucers all the way across the room. Tim let her stand there for a second before he took the tray off her, and then he just looked at her, and she apologised. Out loud. In her own voice. Thin and quiet. "Sorry, Tim."

"Voices, Mum," he said, very softly.

She shut her mouth so fast I heard her teeth click.

I noticed things after that, because I couldn't help it. The photos on the walls, for one. Family photos, loads of them, Pam and George and Tim at the seaside, at Christmas, at some wedding. And in every single one, one person had been scribbled out. Thick black pen, pressed in hard enough to tear the paper in places. Same person every time, I think. Just a small shape, about the height of a kid.

"We do get such lovely neighbours," Tim said, stirring his tea. "They never seem to stay, though. It's a shame. People just can't handle a quiet life these days, can they?" He smiled at me over the rim of the cup. "I hope you're going to be different."

If he'd threatened me, I could've said something. Instead he was friendly, and he was enjoying himself. He sat in the middle of his parents' living room like a king, with the two of them fussing around him, trembling, and he never once raised his voice.

"I should go," I said nervously.

"Of course." Tim stood. "Mum, see her out."

Pam followed me out, and I saw him go to the front window and stand there, watching us, tea in hand.

Pam pulled the door to behind her and I took her hands at the gate, they were shaking like leaves.

"Pam," I said, quietly. "I can help. I can call someone, I'll stay with you right now, I don't care what he said..."

"Don't." Her real voice again, cracked, rusty, like it hadn't been out of the house in years. "Please. You don't know what it would cost us."

"Who's Laura?"

Her face crumpled. For a second I thought I'd lost her. Then she took her hands back, one finger at a time.

"You've been a lovely neighbour," she whispered. "Please just let it go. It'll only be worse for us if you don't."

She went back in. The door closed. Quietly.

I gave my notice that week. Got my deposit back, at least, but I lost the garden, the postbox with opinions, and the house I'd spent four years grafting and living off pasta for. Dani helped me load the van and didn't ask a single question, which is why she's the best.

I looked back once from the driver's seat. Pam and George were at the front window, side by side, small and grey and silent, watching me go.

And behind them, in the dark of the upstairs window, stood Tim. Perfectly still. That same deathly stare, steady, unblinking, like he was memorising my face.

As I pulled away, I heard it, faint, through my own closed window, though I know it can't have been real.

A child, crying.

I didn't look back again.
