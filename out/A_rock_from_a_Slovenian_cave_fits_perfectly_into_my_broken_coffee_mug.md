I work in dimensional metrology, which is essentially the science of measuring things. Most of our clients are automotive companies, although every now and then we receive something from a university or a research institution that doesn't have access to the sort of equipment we use.

In February of last year, we received a shipment of eleven mineral specimens from a geological research group in Slovenia. There was nothing particularly interesting about them. They were mostly calcite formations of various sizes, individually wrapped in foam and numbered with little adhesive labels. I was supposed to scan them, generate three-dimensional models and send the files back to the researchers.

I'd just come back from a cigarette break and was drinking my coffee when I noticed something peculiar about one of the smaller specimens. It was a curved piece of yellowish mineral, approximately four centimeters long, with an irregular surface at either end. It looked remarkably similar to the piece missing from the handle of my coffee mug.

I'd had that mug since 2017. My sister had brought it back from a holiday in Scotland, and I'd broken the handle against the kitchen sink a few years later. It was still usable, so I'd never bothered replacing it.

Out of curiosity, I picked up the specimen and placed it against the broken handle.

It fit.

I called my coworker over and we both had a laugh about it. He took a picture of me holding the mug with the mineral specimen in place, and I sent it to my sister. She told me it was probably a sign that I should stop being such a cheap bastard and buy a new mug.

Before putting the specimen away, I decided to scan the damaged section of the handle and compare the two surfaces. I had some time to kill, and this was exactly the sort of stupid thing we occasionally did with our equipment when nobody was around.

The differences between the surfaces were within the measurement uncertainty of the scanner. Even the little irregularities along the fractured edges corresponded to one another.

I attached the comparison to the email containing the rest of the research group's files, along with a brief explanation of what had happened. I figured somebody over there might get a kick out of it.

Three days later, I received an email from a man named Peter.

He wanted to know when I'd acquired the mug, whether I knew where it had been manufactured, and under what circumstances the handle had broken. He also asked whether the specimen had been damaged during transportation.

I answered his questions and didn't hear from him again for nearly two weeks.

When he eventually got back to me, he asked whether I'd be available for a video call.

Peter was an elderly Slovenian man with very thick glasses and a rather peculiar way of speaking. He spoke excellent English but would occasionally stop halfway through a sentence and stare at something offscreen for several seconds before continuing. I initially thought there was a problem with his internet connection.

We talked for about forty minutes. He explained that the specimens had been recovered from a small limestone cave in southern Slovenia during several expeditions conducted between 1998 and 2007. The chamber containing them had been inaccessible prior to the first expedition, concealed behind an extensive accumulation of mineral deposits.

He showed me some photographs of the excavation. It was an ugly little cave, with narrow passages and muddy walls. In most of the pictures, somebody was holding a flashlight while another person worked on their knees with small geological instruments. There was something unpleasant about the photographs, although I think that had more to do with my own claustrophobia than anything else.

Peter asked whether I was familiar with Raup's model of shell coiling.

I vaguely remembered it from university.

He showed me two photographs of seashells belonging to different species, Thatcheria mirabilis and Architectonica perspectiva. One was tall and pointed, while the other was relatively flat. Despite their very different appearances, their geometries allowed them to be screwed into one another with remarkable precision.

He explained that the animals had developed their shells independently according to compatible mathematical principles. Neither organism needed to encounter the other for such a correspondence to occur.

Then he asked me whether I thought the same phenomenon could occur between a natural object and a manufactured one.

I told him that, statistically speaking, I didn't see why not.

He seemed satisfied with that answer.

We ended up talking fairly regularly over the following months. Peter was retired but still involved with several research projects at his university. He lived alone, collected old radios and had an encyclopedic knowledge of European geology. He also had a tendency to call me at inconvenient hours, apparently forgetting about the time difference between our countries.

He once called me while I was making dinner and spent nearly an hour explaining the geological history of the Dinaric Alps. I understood maybe a third of what he said.

He was a strange guy, but I liked him.

One evening in April, Peter asked me to wait while he went into his kitchen. He returned with what appeared to be an old brass spoon with its handle bent into an arc.

He explained that sometime in the early eighties, the handle of one of his kitchen drawers had broken. Rather than purchasing a replacement, he'd taken an old spoon, cut off most of the bowl, bent the remaining metal and drilled two holes through it. He'd used it as a drawer handle ever since.

He held it close to the camera. The brass had darkened considerably with age, and there were several scratches and small indentations where he'd hammered it into shape.

Then he shared his screen.

It was a photograph of a section of limestone with an elongated depression running across its surface. At either end of the depression were two little cylindrical protrusions.

They corresponded to the holes Peter had drilled in the brass.

He had discovered the formation in 2003.

I asked him to send me the photographs and, if possible, a scan of the spoon. He did. I spent part of the following weekend comparing them.

There were a few minor discrepancies, primarily in areas where the brass had accumulated wear over the years, but the overall correspondence was extraordinary. Even a series of uneven hammer marks near one end of the handle appeared in the mineral impression.

I asked Peter how old the limestone was.

He corrected me. The age of the surrounding limestone wasn't particularly relevant. What mattered was the age of the mineral deposits that had formed over the impression, effectively sealing it away.

Those deposits had been dated by two independent laboratories.

The youngest estimate was approximately 2.8 million years.

I remember sitting on my balcony that evening, smoking a cigarette and looking through the dating reports he'd sent me. I kept returning to the same figures, trying to understand whether I'd misinterpreted something.

Eventually, I called him again.

"You're telling me that this thing was there three million years ago?"

"At least," he said.

"And you made the handle yourself?"

"Yes."

"Out of a spoon?"

"Yes."

Neither of us said anything for a while.

I asked whether he'd ever published the discovery. He told me that he hadn't, and that he had no intention of doing so. There was no scientifically defensible interpretation of the correspondence, and presenting the findings without one would accomplish very little.

Later that night, I looked him up.

I'd known he was a geologist, but I hadn't appreciated the extent of his academic career. He had dozens of publications going back to the early eighties. His work had been cited in geological surveys, textbooks and articles published by researchers all over Europe. He had supervised doctoral students and contributed to international research projects.

I'd almost hoped to find something about ancient civilizations or paranormal phenomena among his publications. Something that would allow me to dismiss him as an eccentric with a questionable relationship with science.

Instead, I found forty years of perfectly respectable academic work, most of which looked painfully boring.

Over the next few weeks, I began helping Peter catalogue photographs of the cave formations. There were hundreds of them, some taken with digital cameras and others scanned from old photographic prints. Most depicted irregular deposits that didn't resemble anything in particular.

I occasionally went through them at night while watching television. My two cats would usually be asleep somewhere on the couch, and I'd have my laptop balanced on my knees, scrolling through photographs of rocks halfway across the world.

That's how I found the third correspondence.

There was a photograph of a small mineral formation with a shallow, rectangular depression. One corner of the depression had an unusually rounded protrusion.

I recognized it almost immediately.

Several years earlier, I'd accidentally left the remote control for my television too close to an electric heater. The plastic casing had softened and partially collapsed inward, producing a peculiar deformation near the bottom right corner. The remote still worked, and I'd been using it ever since.

I retrieved it from the coffee table and held it beside the photograph.

I felt uncomfortable enough that I closed my laptop.

The following morning, I sent Peter some pictures of the remote. He asked me to scan it at work, which I did a few days later.

The correspondence included the little molded lettering on the back of the plastic casing, distorted in precisely the same way by the heat.

Peter didn't seem particularly excited about the discovery.

He asked me whether I could remember what I'd been doing on the evening the remote had melted. I told him I couldn't remember much. It was winter, I'd turned on the heater, and at some point I'd put the remote on the floor beside the couch.

He asked whether I normally kept it there.

I told him I didn't.

That evening, I found myself thinking about the series of events that must have preceded the deformation of that particular piece of plastic. The temperature outside, my decision to turn on the heater, where I'd been sitting, the program I'd been watching, the seemingly insignificant movement of my hand when I put the remote down.

I wondered how many of those events would have needed to occur differently for the remote not to match the formation in Slovenia.

It was an unpleasant thing to think about, so I eventually stopped.

Peter and I didn't discuss the correspondences much after that. We still spoke occasionally, although our conversations became increasingly mundane. He complained about his neighbors, I complained about work, and he would sometimes show me whatever old radio he was attempting to repair that week.

In August, he sent me a photograph of one of the original excavation sites. The image showed a narrow section of the chamber extending beyond the area from which the specimens had been recovered.

I asked him how far it continued.

He said they didn't know. Much of the passage remained obstructed by mineral deposits, and excavating it would require considerable funding and authorization from the local authorities. He doubted it would happen within his lifetime.

I asked him whether he thought there were other recognizable formations further inside.

He didn't answer the question directly. Instead, he told me that during the original expedition, they'd encountered similar deposits on practically every accessible surface of the passage.

They had only examined a very small portion of them.

I think that was the first time the scale of the discovery really frightened me.

Until then, I'd thought about the mug, the spoon and the remote control as three extraordinary coincidences. But they were also three ordinary objects belonging to two ordinary people who happened to have access to a particular collection of geological photographs.

There was no reason to assume they were particularly significant.

There was no reason to assume that the thousands of formations we hadn't recognized were any less precise.

In September, Peter called me on a Sunday afternoon. He looked tired, and for once he didn't seem interested in talking about anything unrelated to his research.

He asked whether I'd ever had a CT scan of my chest.

I told him I hadn't.

He asked whether I'd be willing to undergo one.

I thought he was joking. When I realized he wasn't, I asked him what exactly he expected to find.

He said that he couldn't tell me without potentially influencing the results.

I told him that was a ridiculous answer.

He apologized and offered to pay for the examination himself.

He recommended that I speak with my doctor.

Something about the whole exchange irritated me. I felt that Peter had become so preoccupied with his research that he'd forgotten how inappropriate his requests were. I stopped responding to his emails for a couple of weeks.

By the time I finally arranged the examination, it was already November.

I sent Peter the imaging files the evening they became available through the clinic's online portal. I hadn't received the radiologist's report yet. I wrote something along the lines of, "There you go, now you can leave me alone."

He acknowledged receipt of the files about twenty minutes later.

Then he stopped answering my emails.

The doctor called me two days later and asked me to come back to the clinic. There was an abnormality on the scan that required further investigation. He referred me to a specialist, who arranged several additional examinations and eventually a biopsy.

While waiting for the results, I tried calling Peter again. I wanted to know whether he'd found whatever he had been looking for. More than anything, I wanted him to tell me that the whole thing had been another meaningless coincidence.

He sent me an email yesterday morning. He apologized for his silence and explained that he'd been reviewing some of the original photographs from the 1998 expedition. He attached one of them, along with a copy of a geological cross-section indicating where the photographed formation had been discovered.

The photograph showed two impressions in a pale limestone wall, side by side. They were considerably larger than the other formations he'd shown me, and there was a measuring tape stretched horizontally beneath them. I recognized the general outline from the anatomical images I'd been looking at over the previous weeks.

They were the impressions of a pair of human lungs.

There was a small, irregular depression near the upper outer edge of the left one. Peter had circled it and written its approximate dimensions in the margin of the photograph.

Twenty-six by nineteen millimeters.

I received my biopsy results this afternoon. I have a malignant tumor in the upper lobe of my left lung, measuring twenty-six by nineteen millimeters. I was six years old when Peter took that photograph. Nine years later, behind the gymnasium at my high school, a friend offered me my first cigarette. I remember spending a few seconds deciding whether or not to take it.
