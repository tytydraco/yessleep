I need to write this down because I don't think I can carry it alone anymore, and because maybe someone reading this will understand what it's like to lose everything slowly before you lose it all at once.

I opened my bakery six years ago. I put every rupee I had into it, every rupee my father left me, every favor I could call in from people who believed in me back when I still believed in myself too. For the first two years it did fine. Not great, but fine. Then a bigger chain opened two streets over, and slowly, month by month, my regulars started disappearing. I told myself it was temporary. I told myself people would come back once the new place lost its shine.

They didn't come back.

I don't think people who haven't run a failing business understand what it actually does to your mind. It's not one big moment of despair. It's smaller than that, and somehow worse. It's standing behind the counter for eight hours and selling four items. It's doing the math every single night — rent, flour, electricity, the loan payment — and watching the numbers refuse to work no matter how many times you rearrange them. It's lying awake calculating how many days you have left before you can't make payroll for the one employee you still have.

I started skipping meals, not because I couldn't afford them exactly, but because every rupee felt like it belonged to a bill instead of to me. I stopped sleeping properly. I'd catch myself standing in the shop at 2 a.m., unable to remember walking there, just staring at an oven that hadn't turned a profit in months.

My wife, Anika, wanted a baby. She'd wanted one for two years by that point. Every time she brought it up, something in my chest would close like a fist. I couldn't explain to her, not properly, that the idea of bringing a child into a life I couldn't even keep afloat myself felt like a cruelty I refused to commit. I wasn't trying to hurt her by saying no. I was trying to protect a child who didn't exist yet from a father who couldn't provide the most basic things for the wife he already had.

She didn't see it that way. I understand now why she couldn't.

The fights got worse over those last few months. Small ones at first, then bigger ones, always about the same thing dressed in different clothes. I'd come home exhausted, humiliated by another day of empty counters, and she'd bring up the baby again, and I'd feel that same fist close in my chest, and I'd say something cruel instead of something honest, because cruelty was easier than admitting how frightened I actually was.

The day it happened, I'd had a customer scream at me in the shop over a wrong order, calling me useless in front of the two other people standing there, and something in me had already cracked wide open by the time I walked through my own front door that evening. Anika brought up the baby again within minutes of me getting home. I don't remember what she said exactly. I remember the sound of my own hand before I understood what I'd done.

She didn't scream. She didn't cry, not right away. She just looked at me with an expression I will never forget for as long as I live, packed a bag within the hour, and left.

She never came back.

I want to be honest here, because I think honesty is the only thing I have left to offer anyone reading this. I don't deserve sympathy for what happened that day. I did something no amount of stress or fear excuses, and I have had to live with that fact every single day since, long before anything strange started happening to me at all.

The strange part started almost immediately after she left, and for a while I genuinely thought it was some kind of mercy, like the universe had decided I'd suffered enough and was finally cutting me a break.

My bakery started filling up. Not gradually — almost overnight. Customers I'd never seen before started coming in daily. School children stopped by after class, couples came in for evening treats, and even the man who'd screamed at me weeks earlier walked back in one afternoon and apologized, genuinely, for how he'd spoken to me. I started making enough to cover my bills for the first time in over a year. I started sleeping through the night again, for a little while.

There was a woman who came in most afternoons, always around the same time, always ordering the same thing. We started talking. I let myself imagine, God forgive me, that maybe some version of a normal life was still possible for me. Maybe I could rebuild something out of the wreckage I'd made.

I should have paid closer attention to the fact that everything good that was happening had started the exact same week Anika walked out the door.

It began about two months ago. Small at first, easy to explain away. I'd be closing the shop late and notice someone standing across the street, just standing there, watching the storefront. I told myself it was nothing. A person waiting for a ride. A person who liked the smell of fresh bread drifting out into the evening air.

Then it started happening at my house.

The first night I saw someone standing outside my bedroom window, I convinced myself I'd imagined it. The second night, I couldn't. A man I recognized — a customer, someone who'd bought bread from me just that morning — standing perfectly still on my lawn, staring up at my window, not moving, not blinking, for what felt like far too long before he simply turned and walked away.

The next night there were two of them. The night after that, four.

Every single one of them was someone who had bought something from my shop.

I stopped sleeping again, but for a different reason this time. I started keeping the lights off in my bedroom, watching from behind the curtain instead of trying to rest, counting the figures gathering on my lawn like a man counting debts he couldn't remember incurring. They never came inside. They never spoke. They just stood there, sometimes for hours, all facing my window with the same flat, patient stillness, before turning and walking off just before dawn, one by one, back toward wherever they'd come from.

I went to the police twice. Both times, by the time an officer arrived, the lawn was empty, and I could see in their faces that they thought I was a man cracking under the pressure of a business and a marriage that had both recently fallen apart. Maybe they weren't wrong to think that. I wasn't entirely sure myself anymore.

I tried confronting one of them directly, once, a woman I recognized from the shop who came by every Tuesday for the same loaf of bread. I walked straight out onto my own lawn and asked her, as calmly as I could manage, why she was standing there. She looked at me for a long moment, and then she said something I haven't been able to forget since.

"She said you'd finally understand what it feels like to have people who won't leave."

I didn't understand what that meant until three weeks ago, when an old college friend of Anika's messaged me out of nowhere, careful and hesitant, asking if I was doing alright. I asked her what she meant. She hesitated for a long time before she finally told me.

She said that after Anika left me, she'd gone to stay with a relative in another town for a while — a relative, it turned out, who practiced something the rest of the family only ever spoke about in whispers. She said Anika had asked this relative for help, not to hurt me physically, but to make sure I never got to feel peace again the way I'd denied her the chance at the family she wanted. She said the relative had warned Anika that this kind of thing always costs something, always takes more than it gives, but Anika had gone through with it anyway.

Five, her friend said. She'd been specific about the number. Five things bound to my home, disguised in whatever forms would draw closest to me without raising suspicion — which meant they wouldn't come as strangers. They'd come as customers. As the very success I thought I'd finally earned.

The prosperity in my shop was never a mercy. It was bait, drawing exactly the kind of foot traffic that would let five things slip a little closer to my life with each visit, a little more familiar, a little less like strangers standing on a lawn and a little more like neighbors who'd always been there.

I don't know how to end this, because I don't think it's ended yet. The figures still come every night. I counted six last night for the first time. I don't know if that means something changed, or if I simply miscounted the five that were always going to be there from the beginning.

I keep thinking about the day I raised my hand to a woman who only ever wanted a family with me, and I keep thinking that whatever is standing on my lawn every night might not even be the real punishment. I think the real punishment is that my bakery is finally successful, finally full of the life and warmth I always wanted for it, and I can't enjoy a single day of it, because I understand now that every customer walking through that door might be one more thing I invited a little closer to my own home without ever meaning to.

I built a business to give my family a good life. I ended up destroying the family instead, and built something that isn't a family at all standing outside my window every night in its place.
