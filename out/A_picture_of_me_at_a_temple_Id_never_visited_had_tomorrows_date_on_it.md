I didn’t go to Ashara searching for God. I went because eight-year old Kiara had some homework.

 

She was bouncing on the plane seat across from me, feet swinging, eyes wide. “Ayan, what if I get first prize?”

 

I grinned. “Oh, you won’t.”

 

Her face crumpled.

 

I nudged her, “They’ll have to disqualify you kiddo. Too much cuteness, it’s unfair to the other kids.”

 

Mira, by the window, aimed a world-class eye roll at me. Naina, Mira’s older sister, just shook her head. Naina's husband Rohit, chuckled. Kiara, their daughter, giggled. I wasn’t related to any of them. I was just Mira’s best friend. Somehow, over the years, they’d quietly adopted me anyway.  



Kiara’s assignment was simple. Know Your Origins. The whole class had to learn about their family’s roots. About the culture, stories, and the place.

 

For Kiara, that place was Ashara. For me? It was really just a fantastic excuse.

 

I’d always wondered what Ashara was like. Partly because one of my favourite movies were filmed here. Partly because people can’t shut up about how beautiful it is. And partly to see the land that once saw baby Mira and thought, “Let’s cut this one loose in the wild.”

 

So when I heard they were taking the trip, I acted like any responsible and mature adult would. I went to their house, created a full blown melodrama till they concluded it was easier for me to tag along than hearing me rant.

 

And, look, Ashara really is beautiful. The hills. The rain. The tea gardens. The endless stories. Actually, it’s the stories that got me most. I just didn’t know one would end up following me home.

 

The first few days were perfect. Gavaran , the capital city of Ashara felt like it remembered things the rest of us had forgotten. Kiara hunted facts for her project. Naina shared bits from her childhood. Rohit turned into our unofficial tour guide. Mira turned storyteller. Every street, every hill, every river, each one had a fable or a ghost, or both. I soaked it all up.

 

Then we reached Nyara temple.

 

Even from far off you could see the crowd. Pilgrims, aghoris, tourists, tantriks, police. Every road spilling over with people. Inside, after the blessing, a priest handed us prasad and mentioned the temple would shut down for three days starting tomorrow.

 

I shot Mira a look. “What’s going on?”

 

She looked at me like I’d failed a basic test. “You don’t know?”

 

I shrugged. “No.”

 

“Tomorrow is the first day of Pranubati.”

 

I frowned. The name rang a bell. Oh, right. That festival.

 

“The menstruation festival?” I said, maybe a little too loudly.

 

She gave a little frown and a sharp elbow in my belly. A nearby old woman glared at me.

 

“Have some respect,” Mira whispered.

 

“I’m trying to understand,” I said.

 

So she started explaining the whole thing. The mythology, the goddess, her annual period, why the temple closes, then reopens. Everything.

 

At first, I was curious. Then, honestly, I got confused. By the end, I was just irritated.

 

“Wait,” I said. “So it is believed the goddess menstruates once a year during this time and the temple shuts down for three days?”

 

“Yes,” Mira replied.

 

“For real?”

 

She just held my gaze. The whole thing sounded absurd.

 

That night, the argument continued at the hotel. Me vs. Mira at first. Then Naina waded in. Then Rohit.

 

“You’re missing the point,” Mira insisted.

 

“I get the point,” I told her. “It just doesn’t make sense.”

 

Naina folded her arms. “Go on, explain.”

 

I pointed at the temple on the hill. “Nyara is the goddess of fertility, creation, sexuality. Everyone calls menstruation sacred. So… why close the temple?”

 

Nobody answered right away.

 

“If she’s divine the rest of the time, she’s divine now too,” I pressed.

 

“She is,” Mira said.

 

“Then worship her,” I shot back.

 

“We do.”

 

“No, you don’t.” I didn’t mean for it to sound so harsh, but the words just slipped out. “The temple literally shuts.”

 

“It’s symbolic,” she said.

 

“It’s contradictory.”

 

Silence.

 

I leaned in. “Look, maybe I’m wrong. But isn’t this suspiciously close to how real women were - still are - treated during their periods?”

 

Now Naina frowned. “In what way?”

 

“I mean, for ages, women were pushed aside, separated when they had their period. There was always a reason or excuse. Rest, health, tradition, whatever. Different words, same result.” 

I pointed at the temple again. “Now the goddess menstruates, and the same thing happens. The worship stops. The doors close. Access denied. And we call this respect?”

 

Finally Rohit chimed in. “You think this is patriarchal, don’t you?”

 

I hesitated, then nodded. “Maybe not on purpose, but yeah.”

 

The word just hung there. Patriarchal. Not exactly light conversation, especially mixed in with religion and tradition.

 

Mira looked uncomfortable, not angry. More like she wanted to be anywhere else.

 

“You’re turning a sacred tradition into woke politics,” she said.

 

“I’m asking one thing,” I replied. “If menstruation is truly sacred, why is it the only time we can’t go inside?”

 

No one answered.

 

The next day, I took my question to a priest outside the closed temple gates. Tried to argue my way in. Big mistake.

 

He was old, thin, and somehow looked angry at everything. “You think you know better than centuries of tradition?”

 

“No…”

 

“Then stop questioning.”

 

“I question people. Not gods.”

 

He narrowed his eyes. “You’d be surprised how little difference there is.” Something about him grated on me. Maybe the certainty, maybe the arrogance, maybe because I’ve seen that I-am-the-final-authority look before, from politicians, teachers, managers, priests. It always says "Obey first, understand later. "

 

I hate that look.

 

He pointed at the temple. “The goddess requires seclusion.”

 

“Why?”

 

“Because that is her will.”

 

“How do you know?”

 

His jaw tightened. “Faith.”

 

I almost laughed, but stopped myself. Instead, I just walked away, even more convinced that nobody actually had an answer. Just lines they’d been taught to repeat.

 

I bumped into a younger priest.

 

“Sorry,” I told him.

 

He was gentle. Easy to talk to.

 

“You upset him,” he said lightly.

 

“The angry priest?” I asked.

 

He smiled. “The very one.”

 

I ended up laughing.

Then he asked, “Why does all this get under your skin?”

 

I wanted to answer, but it wasn’t simple. This wasn’t really about religion, politics or tradition. It was about hypocrisy. I kept thinking about the woman I knew. How often people said menstruation was natural. Sacred. Then acted like it was shameful. Treated it differently. Always a contradiction.

 

“No goddess deserves to be hidden away,” I finally said.

 

The young priest just listened. Didn’t interrupt, didn’t bristle.

 

“If she’s powerful, worship her. If she’s sacred, worship her. If she’s menstruating, still worship her. If she actually stands for women, isn’t closing the temple an insult?”

 

He nodded and said something I wasn’t expecting “I understand.”

 

I blinked. “Really?”

 

He nodded again. “Though, I disagree.”

 

“Why?”

 

He glanced at the hill. “Some doors aren’t closed to keep people out. They’re closed because people mistake seeing for understanding.”

 

The reply irritated me. It was the kind of thing that sounds deep but dodges the question. So I just stood up.

 

“That’s not an answer.”

 

He gave a faint, sad smile. “No. It won’t be.”

 

I tried to put it out of my mind, but it stuck. Not because he disagreed. I’m used to that. But because he never even tried to defend the tradition. He didn’t quote scripture. He didn’t get angry. He just listened, then dropped a puzzle on my head and left me to figure it out.

 

“People mistake seeing for understanding.”

 

It sounded profound and meaningless at the same time. I hated that. The kind of answer that’s impossible to challenge, but also impossible to accept. By lunch I was still chewing it over. By dinner, annoyed that I couldn’t let it go. At midnight, still wide awake, staring at the ceiling.

 

Rain tapped the windows. Everyone else was asleep in their rooms. Mira had sent me her usual quota of reels on Instagram and had wished me goodnight. I replayed the argument over and over.The contradiction just wouldn’t let me be.

I tossed, stared at the ceiling, gave up, wandered out onto the balcony. Gavaran’s lights glittered under the clouds. I could just make out the shape of the temple far away.  Just… there. Watching, almost.

 

Just as I was about to go inside, I caught movement on the street. Someone stood below, staring up at me. Still. The rain made everything blurry. Might’ve been a man. Could’ve been a woman. Maybe they waved? And then lightning flashed and poof. The road was empty. 

 

I just stood there. Nothing but rain and the city and my imagination. I shook my head. Way too many horror movies. Way too many discussions with Mira.



I went back inside.

 

Then the dream occurred. Not a nightmare. The difference matters. A nightmare whips you around. The dream felt constructed, like someone was showing me something.

 

Stone corridors, endless, down into the earth, lit with oil lamps. Every few steps, there’d be a doorway. Inside each one, a woman. Young, old, queen, farmer, child, grandmother- thousands. None of them spoke or moved, just watched as I passed. Not angry nor welcoming. Just waiting.

 

At the end, an ancient, massive door covered in symbols. I knew I was meant to open it. I reached for the handle, fingers grazing it, and I bolted awake, sweating, heart racing. 3:03 AM. For one split second, it felt like someone was standing right beside my bed, watching me. That vanished quickly. Whats left was embarrassment.

I mean, come on. Grown man, scared by a dream.

 

Next day, we visited the Pranubati fair - a fair that happens adjacent to the temple each year during the Pranubati festival. Kiara needed photos for her homework. She turned into a tiny journalist. She interviewed pilgrims, monks, shopkeepers. Even tried to interview one unlucky goat.

 

Eventually, she wandered over to an old woman under a tree.

 

“Why is the temple closed?” Kiara asked.

 

“Because Mother is resting,” the woman smiled.

 

Kiara thought for a second. “If she’s resting, why are all these people waiting outside?”

 

The old woman hesitated. So did everyone listening, including me. No answer. Conversation moved on. But I couldn’t let it go. In two lines, an eight-year-old had nailed the contradiction that was driving me nuts.

  

That afternoon, I gave up pretending. I was obsessed. The arguments, the dreams.....this wasn’t just a debate anymore. I needed to know. Maybe I wanted to be proven right. Maybe I just desperately wanted a straight answer. But every answer slipped away. Tradition. Faith. Culture. They sounded meaningful until anyone tried to pin them down.

 

Rain hammered down, turning the city into glass and shadow. I was alone in the hotel lobby, smoking under a no smoking sign. My tiny rebellion. By the front desk, a stack of old books. Tourist guides, local history, folklore. A thin one grabbed my attention. A yellowed, faded cover. "Forgotten Traditions of the Nyara temple."

 

One more drag, I snuffed the cigarette, grabbed the book. Scanned the table of contents. “The Third Night Ritual.” Page 117.

 

I flipped to it. The numbers skipped straight from 116 to 128. Eleven pages missing. Nothing, not even a torn edge.

 

I shut the book, realizing my hands were shaking.

 

Naina called me for lunch. I kept the book to myself, especially from Mira. I already knew her answer. Coincidence. Old book. Move on.

 

Maybe she’d be right. But my gut wouldn’t let it go.

 

I told Naina I wasn’t hungry. That ofcourse didn't work. Elder sisters are like mothers who are just more technolgy savvy. She dragged me anyway, and after a long excuse I finally managed to escape. I claimed I needed to rest, then slipped out and started scouring Gavaran’s older sections. The libraries, bookshops, archives, anything remotely connected to local history.

 

Mostly, I found facts about Pranubati festival. Nothing about a third night ritual. Not until I came across this ancient historian above a musty little bookstore. He smiled at the mention of Pranubati. Nodded about Nyara. But when I asked about the ritual of the third night, his face closed up.

 

“Where did you hear that?” He snapped, quick and anxious.

 

“Just read it in an old book,” I shrugged.

 

He watched me like I’d just announced I was carrying a bomb.

 

“You should enjoy your vacation,” he said, then stood up. That was the end of that.

 

Back at the hotel, I went to Naina’s room. Kiara jumped up with her notebook.

 

“Ayan!”

 

I scooped her up and kissed her cheek. She handed me her project notebook. Pages of interviews, photos, little facts, sketches. Honestly, for an eight year old, it was impressive.

 

I flipped through. Then froze. One drawing showed the temple, the crowd, hills. But above it, Kiara had drawn a woman. Enormous. Towering over everything. Long hair, many arms, no face...just blackness where the face should be.

 

“Who’s this?”

 

Kiara looked puzzled. “The lady.”

 

“What lady?”



She grinned, “The one from my dream.”

 

My insides twisted. “What dream?”

 

“That lady was standing near your bed last night in my dream”

 

Suddenly the room felt cold. “What?”

 

She giggled. “You sleep funny.”

 

“What do you mean?”

 

“The lady said so.”

 

Every nerve in me screamed. Kids say weird things. But her timing, the detail, my dream...

 

“She said you ask too many questions.” She beamed.

 

I laughed. Too hard, too fast. That uncomfortable grown-up laugh.

 

Then Kiara said something that wiped the smile right off my face.

 

“She also said you’ll come meet her soon.”

 

Mira glanced at me. Like a cop glances at a criminal . She took me outside at the hotel garden. I had to tell her everything.

 

The book.

The missing pages.

The historian.

My dream. 

Everything.

 

When I finished, Mira looked more annoyed than anything else.

 

“You’re spiralling.”



“Am I?”

 

“Yes.”

 

“You don’t think any of that’s weird?”

 

“No.”

 

“The pages missing?”

 

“Old book.”

 

“The historian?”

 

“Eccentric.”

 

“Kiara’s dream?”

 

“She’s eight.”

 

I hated that she was being rational. Reasonable people are impossible to deal with when you’re desperately searching for answers.

 

Eventually, she sighed, reached over, and took my hand. The gesture was so normal, for a second I almost forgot all this strange business.

 

Almost.

 

“Ayan.”

 

“What?”

 

“Maybe you should stop trying to solve everything.”

 

I couldn’t help but laugh. “Have we met?”

 

She finally smiled. For a moment, everything felt normal again. Safe, even. But her smile faded as she noticed something. A woman, standing across the garden, watching us.

 

She didn’t move. She just stood there, staring, not even blinking. The moment our eyes met, a group of men crossed between us. Just two seconds. When they passed, she was gone.

 

We stared in silence. Finally, Mira spoke.

 

“Did you see?”

 

“Yes.”

 

“What the hell was that?”

 

She sounded afraid. And hearing Mira scared unsettled me way more than seeing that woman because I’d seen her angry plenty of times, but never scared.

 

I couldn’t sleep, not after that. Gave it up around three in the morning.

 

The city was silent, and the rain had finally stopped. Moonlight lit up the room. It actually felt peaceful for a brief moment, until my phone vibrated.

 

Withheld number. One message. No text. Just a photo.

 

I opened it.

 

At first, I didn’t get it. Then recognition washed over me. Nyara Temple, from above. It looked recent. Just a normal picture, really. Except, when I zoomed in, I saw a figure standing at the temple door. Hands in pockets, head tilted. Staring right at the door.

 

It was me.

 

Wearing these exact clothes.

 

The timestamp?

 

Tomorrow.

 

I definitely didn’t sleep after that. Who in their right mind could, after seeing themselves at a temple they’ve never even visited at night? I spent at least an hour trying to come up with a rational explanation.

 

Photoshop. AI. A prank by Mira.

 

Every explanation sounded worse than the last. The timestamp kept bothering me. Just one word. Tomorrow. Not even a date. Like the picture was showing where I’d be, not where I’d been.

 

By breakfast, I looked like hell. Naina noticed right away.

 

“You look like you’ve been interrogated.”

 

“I barely slept.”

 

“Still obsessing over the temple?”

 

I started to reply, but what could I even say? Nobody would believe me and honestly, I wouldn’t either. So I just shrugged, “Maybe.”

 

Meanwhile, across the table, Kiara was at her notebook, drawing as usual. She didn’t even glance up.

 

“Ayan.”

 

“Hmm?”

 

“The lady says you’re getting close.”

 

The spoon fell from my hand. Nobody else reacted. They didn’t hear what I heard. 

 

I forced a smile. “What lady?”

 

“The hill lady.” So casual, like she was talking about the weather. “The one with no face.”

 

The whole table went silent. This time everyone heard her.

 

Naina glanced at Mira, Rohit cleared his throat. Only Kiara seemed puzzled by the sudden tension.

 

“Where did you hear that?” Naina asked, gentle but worried.

 

Kiara looked up, blinking. “Hear what?”

 

“The faceless lady.”

 

She tapped her head. “I see her.”

 

Nobody laughed. The mood changed. All the grownups suddenly looked uncomfortable. Very uncomfortable.

 

Later that afternoon, I found the younger priest. Or maybe he found me. He somehow always seemed to be around when I needed him. He was waiting near a small shrine, looking like he knew I’d run out of logical explanations.

 

When he saw me, his face darkened.

 

“You are becoming pale.”

 

I unlocked my phone to show him the photo, but it wasn't there anymore. I sat down on the bench nearby. I told him everything.

 

“Did the image frighten you?”

 

I stayed quiet.

 

The priest studied me. “Did it make you want to go?”

 

That stopped me. Because the answer was yes. I hated it, but it was true. The photo turned my obsession into something more. The temple wasn’t a mystery anymore. It was unfinished business. Now it needed an ending.

 

The priest saw it on my face and just sighed, old and tired and utterly defeated. “That’s what I was afraid of.”

 

“Tell me about Nyara,” I blurted out before I could stop myself.

 

He looked toward the temple. “People have called her many things.”

 

“The goddess?”

 

“That's definitely one interpretation.” Too quick, too ready, as though he’d expected the question.

 

“What more?”

 

He hesitated. For once, he looked genuinely unsure. Like he was weighing whether to help or protect me. Finally, he said, “Imagine a mountain.”

 

I blinked. “What?”

 

“A mountain.”

 

“You’re going cryptic on me again, aren’t you?”

 

“Humor me.”

 

I groaned, but played along. “Fine.”

 

“Picture thousands of people around the mountain.”

 

I crossed my arms. “Okay.”

 

“One person says it’s beautiful.”

 

“Okay…”

 

“Another says it’s dangerous.”

 

“Alright.”

 

“A third calls it sacred.”

 

I nodded.

 

“A fourth says it’s cursed.” He turned to me. “Which is right?”

 

“All of them. Or maybe none of them.” The answer just slipped out.

 

He smiled. “Exactly.” And then his face got serious. “Now imagine the mountain’s alive.”

 

Suddenly, the wind just died like someone threw a switch. No birds, no insects, nothing. The silence pounded against my ears.

 

He looked at the temple and whispered, “The problem isn’t that people misunderstand her. The problem is every person understands a different part.”

 

Something shifted behind him down by the trees. A figure. A woman.

 

Watching. Perfectly still.

Watching. Perfectly still.

 

Even from that distance, I saw it wasn’t right. Her posture, her proportions, the way she held herself. The priest saw her, too. Every muscle in his body tensed up.

 

Slowly, she raised a hand and pointed right at me.

 

The priest muttered something in local language, maybe a prayer, maybe a curse.

 

The woman smiled.

 

Then she was just… gone. Not hiding or walking away. Just gone like someone erased her.

 

The priest’s face had gone white. 

 

“What was that?”

 

He didn’t answer.

 

“Is this a cult?"

 

Nothing.

 

“You need to leave Ashara.”

 

I laughed, awkward and hollow. “Yesterday you were all about riddles.”

 

He kept staring at me. “Yesterday she wasn’t looking at you.”

 

I should’ve left Ashara. The priest was right. Any sane person would have booked a ticket and gotten as far away as possible, and maybe made a joke about the weird vacation where an old temple played tricks on their mind.

 

I didn’t. Instead, I did what I always do. Poked my nose.

 

Looking back, that’s where I lost. I lost the moment I convinced myself I was still in control.

 

Back at my room, I managed a short nap but woke up to seventeen missed calls.

 

My mother.

Two colleagues.

Even an old college friend.

 

Confused, I started returning calls. First one lasted thirty seconds.

 

“Ayan, what happened?”

 

“What do you mean?”

 

“The post.”

 

“What post?”

 

Silence.

 

“Stop joking.”

 

My stomach twisted. I opened Instagram.

 

There it was. New post, 3:13 PM. My account. The Nyara temple. Closed doors. Caption: SHE IS NOT WHO THEY THINK SHE IS.

 

Three thousand likes, hundreds of comments, thousands of shares.

 

I froze.

 

I hadn’t posted it. I was asleep at 3:13pm . The timestamp confirmed it.

 

I deleted the post, checked my gallery. It wasn’t there. As if it never lived on my phone at all. 

 

Mira burst through the door.

 

“Why are you posting stuff like that?”

 

“I didn’t.”

 

“Not everything’s a joke.”

 

Before I could even answer, she grabbed my wrist.

 

“Come on, let’s go to the garden. Kiara wants to play football.”

So we headed out and played.

 

Honestly, Kiara’s a monster on the field.

At one point, she just booted the ball as hard as she could, and it slammed right into Mira’s nose.

 

I threw my hands up. “GOOOOAAAAALLLL!”

 

Kiara jumped in too. We both took off doing Ronaldo’s iconic celebration.

“SUIIIII!”

 

Mira wasn’t amused.

Blood started pouring from her nose.

 

It wouldn’t stop.

That killed the game. Suddenly we were all huddled around her with tissues, water, and about a thousand useless pieces of advice.

 

It dragged on.

So much that nobody really noticed Kiara for a while.

 

At some point, Naina glanced around.

“Wait, where’s Kiara?”

 

We all looked over.

She was standing in the garden.

 

She hadn’t moved far. Just ten feet from where we’d been playing.

 

The weird part was nobody had looked at her for nearly twenty minutes.

She stood under a tree.

 

Perfectly still.

Staring at something.

Or someone. At least it seemed like it.

 

“KIARA!” Naina yelled.

 

Kiara whipped around and jogged over, happy as ever.

 

“Where’d you go?” Naina asked.

“I was talking.”

“To who?”

 

Kiara pointed at the tree.

“The hill lady.”

The whole air just froze.

 

I felt every hair on my arms stand up. Not because of what she said, but because of what came next. Kiara looked at me, dead in the eye, and repeated a sentence the younger priest had told me. Word for word.

 

“People mistake seeing for understanding.”

 

It felt like the place tilted. Everyone else chalked it up to coincidence, something she must’ve overheard. Except for one thing. The priest had said those words to me, alone. No one else was there.

 

I’d had enough.

 

Night came. Tomorrow the temple would reopen and resume normal operations. But tonight I’d go inside. Alone.

 

After everyone turned in, I walked back up the hill. It was quiet, just a handful of lanterns lighting the way. I found myself standing in front of those heavy doors. The same doors I’d debated, accused, and raged against for days.

 

Part of me still wanted answers. Another part, though, was starting to think maybe I’d been asking the wrong questions all along.

  

“You’re close,” a voice said.

 

I turned. The young priest was there, looking exhausted and worn down.

 

“Close to what?”

 

He stared at the sanctum. “Her.”

 

I felt my irritation flare up. Something normal at last. “Who is she?”

 

He gave me a sad little smile. “Wrong question.”

 

“Then what is the right question?”

 

He looked me in the eye. “Why does she want you?”

 

The words landed hard. I’d spent all this time thinking I was the one hunting a mystery, trying to get answers.

 

What if it was the other way around? What if I was being led. Every dream, photo, coincidence. A trail of breadcrumbs right to this moment.

 

 

“I’m going in,” I told him.

 

He nodded. “I know.”

 

“No lectures?”

 

“No.”

 

“No threats?”

 

“No.”

 

“No warnings?”

He gave a soft, tired laugh. The laugh of someone who already knows the ending. “You passed that point days ago.”

 

I glared at him. “So stop me.”

 

He shook his head. “I can’t.”

 

“Why?”

 

He didn’t hesitate. “Because she invited you. No one can enter against her will. And here you are”

 

I stared at the entrance one last time, my certainty flooding back. If the goddess was sacred, she deserved worship, not seclusion. If she represented women, she deserved dignity, not isolation. If she represented creation itself, no custom had any right to shut her away.

 

I put my hand on the stone. Pushed.

 

The door slid open with no resistance.

 

The guards just stood there. Motionless, alive but unmoving, their breathing the only sign they were real. They were either in a trance or they couldn't see me. I didn't wait to find out. I walked forward.

 

Darkness waited inside. My whole body screamed at me to turn back, but I kept going. I opened the closed chamber.

 

I blinked.

 

Nothing happened. No horror from beyond, no hidden artifact, no strange ritual. Just a rock, a cave, a little spring in the middle.

 

That was it. The same thing we’d all seen our first day in Gavaran, when we wandered into the temple.

 

I started laughing, half-shocked, half-relieved. So all that tension, those awful dreams, for… this? Maybe I’d had it right all along. Maybe it really was just custom and old tales.

 

Then the air shifted.

 

Not colder, exactly. Just older. It smelled like wet stone and fresh dirt, the scent of someplace that's never touched sunlight. Never even wants to. No glow, no spectacle, nothing big and dramatic. Just darkness and the spring, and someone sitting there.

 

A woman. She sat in the water, waist-deep, perfectly still, facing away. She turned.

 

Her face didn’t hold. It was a man, then a woman, then someone I’d never met, and again, and again, cycling so fast it felt almost dizzying. It wasn't about picking a mask; it was flipping through the faces like she’d already grown bored with them, one after another, nothing sticking, nothing meaning to.

 

And then the water changed too. Not just rippling but showing.

 

In the reflection of the water, I saw a man, kneeling, praying for his rival’s ruin. He vanished, replaced by a woman asking for the same thing, different name, same poison. Then a man, desperate for a son, and a woman behind him asking also. Same prayer, same reason, just so a house wouldn’t call her a failure. I saw a man decreeing what a woman’s body could and couldn’t do that week. Then an older woman, repeating that same rule to a younger girl, never once questioning it herself.

 

The visions started racing. All these faces, crowding together, water smelling off now, metallic and old. The walls of the cave seemed to curve closer, stone listening in, not liking what it heard.

 

“You thought this was about patriarchy?,” she said, and her voice lost all kindness. It filled the air, so deep I felt it in my jaw. “Men asking. Women suffering. That neat story. Easy to hate.”

 

The water lurched, smacking my knees with cold that was beyond anything physical.

 

“They taught each other. Mothers. Daughters. Women guarding rules that withered them, because it was the only sort of power they were ever handed. You want a villain. I’ve watched all of you, every year, longer than your kind’s even known what years are. Not one of you waiting outside that door is innocent.”

 

“You wanted to see me, really see me,” she said, and her voice got quieter. A quiet that was somehow worse. “Here’s what there is.”

 

I tried to speak, but I couldn’t.

 

“Three hundred sixty-two days a year,” she said, “they come here. All of them. Not to meet me. To use me. Wealth. Revenge. Sons. Magical cures for things that shouldn’t be cured. Rules over a woman’s body, written by men, carried forward by women, year after year, till nobody remembers who started it.” The water went calm, faces gone now, just her and me and the dark. “For three days, I close the door. Not because I'm unclean. Because for three days, I get to exist without being asked for anything.”

 

“Then why let me in?” I asked. My voice sounded small.

 

“Because you were so sure you were different.” Her voice had a touch of pity, the kind people have for someone still clinging to a belief they themselves outgrew long ago. “You spent three days insisting I deserved to be seen. I wanted to know if you meant it, or if you just wanted to be right. So I let you see.”

 

I remembered the old priest at the gate. You’d be surprised how little difference there is. Back then, I thought he was just defending dogma. I didn’t get that he was talking about me, too.

 

“You’re no different from him,” she said, and I had nothing to say back. Every argument about the temple, the goddess, the patriarchy, the priests collapsed into the same ugly little thing  a man kneeling beside the water, asking.

 

She looked at me like the water looked, right before everything went still. Not angry, not warm. Just watching, waiting to see what I’d do with what I’d been given.

 

“Now that you’ve seen my silence,” she said, 

“What story will you tell?”

 



I woke on the stone floor at dawn. Temple bells rang out a new day. The festival had ended, and life slipped back into place, like none of this had happened. Pilgrims crowded in, priests wandered by, whispering their songs. No one seemed to notice anything different.

 

The young priest was there, watching. Eventually, he asked, “What did you see?”

 

I looked right past him, at the pilgrims ready to enter the temple now.

 

“I think I finally saw what I was really asking for,” I said.

 

He didn’t push. Just nodded, that gentle patient nod people give someone who’s arrived, late, at the answer they already knew.

 

After getting a pretty thorough scolding from Naina and Rohit for vanishing overnight, we finally made it on the plane. I grabbed the window seat and watched Ashara shrinking below us. Green hills fading away until all I could see was a thick layer of clouds.

 

Kiara sat next to me. There were a handful of seat rows, the steady roar of the engine, and an eight-year-old waging war against a plate of vegetables.

 

"Finish your vegetables," Naina said. Probably for the tenth time.

 

Kiara shook her head.

 

"I'll eat only if Ayan tells me a story."

 

I had to laugh. "Alright, but you have to keep eating your vegetables while I tell it."

 

She took her time. Thinking it over, like she was making a deal in a boardroom or something. Finally, a serious little nod.

 

She picked up a tiny piece of cucumber and nibbled. Hesitated, then took another microscopic bite.

 

Then, turning toward me, she dangled her legs over the seat. Her feet nowhere near touching the floor. Her eyes sparkled, bright and trusting, that kind of curiosity only little kids have. She was all in, listening, waiting.

 

Outside, the clouds went on and on, stretching past the window in every direction.

 

Kiara swallowed her bite, locked eyes with me, and asked, "What story will you tell?"
