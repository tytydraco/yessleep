It's around 10:02 PM. I'm about twenty minutes from the Pennsylvania border and twice that from the nearest state police station.

I think something is following me.

Or maybe I'm getting closer to it. I'm not sure which possibility is worse.

I work as a field technician for a geological and environmental consulting company. Most days, I'm driving between remote sites, inspecting drainage systems, monitoring unstable slopes, things like that. I've spent enough time around old mines and failing hillsides to know what the ground looks like when something is wrong.

Every night when I come home from work, the mountains seem a little closer.

At first, I thought I'd just been lucky with traffic. My commute was supposed to take an hour, and suddenly it was closer to fifty minutes. Then forty. Then thirty. I should've been happy. That's an extra half hour of my life I'd been getting back for free.

But the drive wasn't getting faster. The road was getting shorter.

The first thing that disappeared was a bend along the river. The road just carried on where it should have curved, and the street that used to branch off to the right was gone. I remember the houses along it and the little birdhouse mailbox at the corner that always leaned toward the road. I'd passed it over a thousand times. 

There was no closure sign, no construction equipment, no Department of Highway, no rubble. The road just continued straight through the place where the bend and the side street should have been.

I told myself I was tired. A few days later, my truck overheated on the way home, so I pulled over near the northern water tank our county had installed. While I waited for the engine to cool, I looked toward the mountain behind it.

A pale strip cut across the forest.

It looked like a mudslide. We have had a "destructive" summer in terms of rain, so it wasn't an unfair assumption. The problem was that there were no exposed roots or broken trees. The edges were clean. It was a long, smooth scar curving across the mountainside, with both ends disappearing behind the trees.

I took a picture, mostly because I wanted to see if it looked as strange on my phone as it did in person.

The next morning, I had a job near an old mine site a few miles north of town. A contractor had reported a change in drainage, and I was there to check the monitoring points.

The creek was running in the wrong place.

Its old channel was nearly dry, with a few shallow pools left between the rocks. A new one cut across the floodplain forty yards away. The bank was steep and clean, with no debris to suggest a flood. Trees stood along its edge, all leaning at the same angle, their roots exposed on one side.

I checked the GPS coordinates of the monitoring point. The point was where it was supposed to be. The creek wasn't.

The upstream gauge showed no unusual flow, and there hadn't been enough rain to move that much earth. I photographed the new channel and sent my supervisor a report describing unexplained channel migration and possible ground displacement.

I did, however, leave out the mountain.

Over the next week, I found more changes. A culvert sat crooked in undisturbed soil. A fence line that had once run straight now bent in an arc. At another site, the steel rods we used to monitor slope movement all leaned toward the valley. Their angles increased the farther I walked along the ridge, as if the ground were tipping away from something.

From there, I could see the pale scar clearly behind the water tower. It had widened. Farther along the mountain, two more pale strips crossed the trees.

They curved through the forest at odd angles, disappearing beneath the canopy and reappearing farther along the slope. I couldn't find any obvious pattern to them.

So I took another photograph.

This is Appalachia. Old mines collapse. Water finds new spots. Hillsides settle. The ground has been dug into and blasted apart for generations. Each change had a reasonable explanation on its own. Together, they didn't.

That evening, I compared the picture I'd taken at the water tower with a new one. The scar hadn't just widened. Its center had shifted, and the ridge above it sat lower than before. I checked the county's elevation maps against the water tower and a nearby transmission line. I couldn't make the shapes match.

The headaches started around then: pressure behind my eyes, worse when I looked toward the mountains. I blamed the heat and lack of sleep. I took painkillers and drank more water. Nothing helped.

A few days later, I stopped at a gas station on my way to work. I bought a bottle of water, paid, and left. I still had the receipt in my cup holder when I drove home that evening.

The station was gone.

Not closed. Gone.

There wasn't even a foundation left. Just grass beside the road. Even the utility pole at the entrance had disappeared.

I pulled over and scrambled to pull the receipt out of the cup holder. The name of the station was printed at the top, along with the time and date. I looked across the empty patch of land, then back at the paper.

The next morning, I went back. My phone's map still showed the station, but there was nothing there to photograph except the empty patch of land.

When I looked up, the mountains seemed close enough to touch. The pale scars ran down toward the river, their exposed surfaces smooth and faintly lined. For a moment, I had the horrifying impression that I was looking at skin.

I drove away.

That afternoon, the county sent me to inspect a crack in the pavement near a small bridge. It ran from one shoulder to the other, almost perfectly straight. The ground on either side sat at different elevations.

I crouched beside it. Under the road was a pale, dense material threaded with dark lines. I thought it might be a mineral vein until I noticed the lines weren't branching off. They curved around one another, disappearing beneath the edges of the pavement.

I touched the edge with my measuring rod.

A vibration traveled up the shaft and into my palm.

I pulled the rod away. The vibration stopped.

For a moment, I stayed crouched there, listening. Then it came again, low enough that I felt it in my teeth. The dark lines beneath the road shifted against one another.

The far side of the crack rose. Maybe half an inch. The pavement lifted with it, and a stone rolled into the grass.

I stumbled backward and nearly fell. I waited for the rest of the hillside to collapse.

Nothing else happened.

I photographed the crack and left.

On my way home, I crossed the same bridge. The crack was gone. The pavement was smooth, the grass undisturbed. I stopped on the shoulder and checked my pictures. They showed what I'd seen, but in the last one the ground had risen slightly on one side. Behind it, the mountain was crossed by pale lines that joined into a single, broad curve.

I went home and stayed up comparing the photographs. The changes didn't radiate from one point, as they might after an earthquake or a mine collapse. The curves converged beneath the highest ridge north of town.

I enlarged the map until the details blurred. Whatever I was looking at, it wasn't a pattern I'd seen in any survey. I shut the laptop. I'd been awake too long. That was the only explanation I could live with.

The next evening, my truck overheated again near the water tower. I opened the hood and waited for the engine to cool. Across the river, the forest began to rise.

At first, I thought the trees were bending in the wind. But there was no wind, and they weren't bending. The ground beneath them was lifting.

A ridge pushed up from the valley floor, carrying trees, boulders, and a section of fence. The fence posts stayed upright as the earth rose. The river struck the new slope and split around it, spilling into the low ground on either side.

Then the forest opened.

Beneath the soil was a broad, rounded surface, gray and faintly lined, stretching farther than I could see. It emerged from under the mountain like the back of something buried too close to the surface.

I could make out a wrist.

Beyond it, five enormous fingers shifted under the earth. The ridges of the mountains followed the shapes of the joints. Pale scars ran along the exposed surface and disappeared beneath miles of forest. I couldn't see where the hand ended. I couldn't see what it was attached to.

One finger flexed.

The whole valley moved with it.

The road across the river split into sections. Trees slid sideways with the ground beneath them. A utility pole leaned and vanished behind a fold of rising earth. The sound reached me a moment later, a deep pressure that rattled the truck and came up through the soles of my boots.

Then the movement stopped.

The river kept flowing through its new channel. Dust drifted above the exposed slope. The trees settled, still standing on a hillside that hadn't existed ten minutes earlier.

I got in the truck and drove home.

My commute took eight minutes.

The ridge behind my house was different. A pale curve ran down its face, visible above the rooftops across the valley. I locked the doors and called my supervisor to say I was sick. He told me to take the next day off.

I didn't sleep.

At 10:02 PM, I started writing this.

A few minutes ago, the floor shifted beneath my feet. I went to the kitchen window. The ridge behind my house had risen again, lifting the trees with it. A pale line was opening in the grass outside, running toward the foundation.

I can hear the roots tearing.

The road to town is shorter than it was an hour ago. I can see the water tower from my window, though it should be hidden behind the ridge. I don't think whatever is beneath these mountains knows we're here. I think it's only moving in its sleep.

The pale line has reached the house.

The floor is rising.
