I do the graveyard shift at a little AM station out here on the peninsula, the kind of station that still plays actual records some nights because the owner's too cheap and too sentimental to fully digitize the library. Two to six in the morning, my voice and the occasional call-in and whatever's left of the classic country catalog. It's a lonely shift. I like it that way. I want that on record before I tell you the rest of this, because I need you to understand I am not someone who scares easily, and I am not someone who was looking for a story.

The board at our station is old. Analog, mostly, patched into digital where it has to be, held together with the kind of duct-tape ingenuity you only find at stations too small to afford real maintenance. Somewhere on that board is a dial for a frequency the station hasn't broadcast on since before I was born, 1974, according to the FCC records our owner keeps framed in the hallway like trophies, back when the station ran a second signal that got folded into the main one decades ago. The dial's still there. It doesn't do anything. It's not connected to anything. It's a leftover, same as half the equipment in that building.

Three weeks ago, at 3:40 in the morning, it lit up.

I want to be clear about what that means. That dial has a little indicator light that shows when a frequency's live, receiving or transmitting. I have never, not once in the two years I've worked there, seen that light do anything but sit dark, because there is nothing on the other end of it. Nothing has been on the other end of it since Nixon was in office.

It came on steady red, and underneath the record I had spinning, some old Merle Haggard track, I remember that detail with a clarity that unsettles me more than almost anything else, I heard something bleed through the studio monitors. Not on the air. Not on anything I was broadcasting. Just there, faint, underneath everything else, like a second radio playing in another room.

Voices. More than one. Talking low, too low to make out words, in the particular cadence of people making ordinary conversation, the up-and-down rhythm of it recognizable even when the content isn't. And under the voices, static, not clean radio static, but something wetter, if that makes sense, something with more texture to it, like the sound had weight.

I checked every cable in that studio. I want you to know that, because it's the part that still keeps me up some nights, I did the responsible thing first. I checked connections, checked the board, checked whether some other frequency was somehow bleeding through a bad solder joint. Everything was where it should be. That dead dial had no wire running to it at all. I traced it myself, on my hands and knees behind the board with a flashlight, and it terminated in nothing. A stub of copper wire, cut clean, going nowhere.

The temperature in the studio dropped while I was down there. All at once, the way a walk-in cooler door swings open. My breath started fogging in front of the monitor lights, and underneath the ordinary smell of a decades-old studio, dust, hot tube amps, the ghost of forty years of cigarette smoke nobody's managed to fully air out, came something coppery and wet, thick enough that I could taste it at the back of my throat.

The voices got clearer. Not louder, clearer, the way something comes into focus. I still couldn't make out full sentences, but I caught pieces. A time. Somebody reading a time, over and over, patient, the way you'd read a schedule out loud to make sure you had it right.

*Three forty. Three forty. Three forty.*

I looked at the studio clock without meaning to. 3:40 am exactly, the second hand sweeping past twelve like it had been waiting for me to check.

The record finished. Dead air, actual dead air, the thing every radio person is trained from day one to fear more than almost anything, because dead air means you've failed at the one job you have. I should have cued the next track. I should have said something, anything, into the mic to fill the silence. Instead I sat there on the studio floor, flashlight still in my hand, and listened to that other station's dead air layer itself under my own, two silences on top of each other, and I understood with total, wordless certainty that whatever was on the other end of that stub of copper wire knew that I was listening.

A thought arrived that wasn't shaped like my own thinking. It didn't come through the speakers. It came from somewhere behind my sternum, already fully formed, the way the last word of a sentence sometimes arrives before you've finished thinking the rest of it.

*You found it. Now you belong to the frequency.*

I got up off that floor faster than I've moved in years. I don't remember choosing to cue the next record. I just remember my hands doing it, muscle memory taking over where the rest of me had gone somewhere small and animal and only interested in noise, any noise, something to drown out the second station bleeding through my monitors.

The red light went dark sometime during that next track. I didn't see it happen. I looked up from the board and it was simply off again, same as it had been every night before, same as it's been every night since.

I told our owner about it the next morning, half expecting him to tell me I'd dreamed it on no sleep and too much studio coffee. He didn't laugh. He got real quiet instead, the kind of quiet that told me I wasn't the first person on that shift to bring him a story like this, and he told me, reluctant, like it cost him something, that the station used to get letters, decades back, before his time even, from listeners who swore they'd picked up a second broadcast layered under the main signal late at night. Voices reading times. Nobody ever figured out where it was coming from. The FCC investigated once, in the seventies, and closed the file with no explanation on record.

He told me if it happens again, to just keep the music going and not to listen too close to what's underneath it.

I've taken his advice. Mostly. There have been two more nights since, always right around 3:40 am, always that same steady red light and that same wet, textured static bleeding through under whatever I've got spinning. I keep the volume up on my own broadcast now, loud enough in my headphones to drown most of it out.

Most of it. Not all of it.

Because even with the music up, even with my own voice filling the dead air the second it threatens to open up, I can still hear them sometimes, faint, underneath everything, reading that same time over and over, patient as a schedule that has never once missed a stop.

Three forty. Three forty. Three forty.

I don't know who they're reading it for. I've started to worry it isn't for each other.
