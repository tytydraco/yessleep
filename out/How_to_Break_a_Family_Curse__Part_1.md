***That Bastard, Frank***

Matriphagy is an extremely rare phenomenon in nature where offspring consume their own mother to survive. This unusual tactic is most often found in spiders. After giving birth, the mother spider liquefies her own internal organs for her children to feed on while they grow.

Matriphagy is completely voluntary and, once the process has begun, irreversible.

The mother spider gives everything she has to thankless mouths that will never know the pain and agony she went through for their survival.

In a way, that’s how my own Mother was.

I am the eldest of three siblings. The middle-child, Frank, was born about a year after me and the youngest, Louise, was born five years later. Mom and Dad were amazing parents. Dad taught me basic life skills and how to be a good leader for the family. He was a great man, but had his flaws. Booze, mainly. Late at night, Mom would catch him sneaking a drink and their arguments about it would wake me up. It never became hostile, that I can remember. Dad was genuinely regretful for falling for the tempting lure of liquor.

He died in a car crash a few years ago. Mom said he swerved into the oncoming lane to avoid a deer, but I knew that wasn’t true. Dad was killed instantly and the truck driver walked away with nothing more than a scratch.

In the following weeks Mom got incredibly sick and was hospitalized. The medical bills quickly ate away at what little money Dad left for us. Mom turned to the local church for food assistance. They gave her whatever she asked for, but we were still just scraping by. She still had to get a second job to pay for other bills, working all day, every day to provide for us. Every morning she would have a plate of eggs, bacon and pancakes on the table before heading to work. Our aunts and uncles would rotate taking us to school and on the weekends it was up to me to take care of Frank and Louise.

I was young then, but even so I understood the toll it was taking on Mom. I never took a meal for granted and I never asked for more than I needed. The same did not apply to Frank. He was greedy, selfish and never satisfied. Mom would dig through her already thinning wallet to give Frank money for the newest local fad, never spending the money on herself. As he grew older, Frank would spend less and less time at the house. Nightly I’d hear him stumble down the hall and into his room right next to mine, empty bottles and cans scattering as he flopped into bed. Somehow though, he’d always have it cleaned before Mom woke up.

One night, Frank came home with a half drank bottle of whiskey. I caught him before he could enter the house and scolded him for being reckless. He wasn’t sober enough to understand me. As he stumbled back to his room I sat on the porch, trying to rack my brain around why the dark liquid had such a hold on Dad and Frank. I reasoned, foolishly, that there was only one way to find out.

Soothing, liquid fire coated my throat as I drank my fill, finally understanding why Dad loved the stuff so much. My mind fogged over and all my worries drifted away. For the first time in a long time, I felt connected to Dad. Like he was right beside me drinking the night away. By morning the booze soaked bliss had gone away, leaving behind an aching headache and no memory of the night before. In my heart I knew I could never let that happen again and I promised myself I wouldn’t let the same vice that claimed Dad’s life take mine too. 

Louise was the exact opposite of Frank and I. She was quiet and kept her nose buried in a book. When Frank was out with his friends, I’d sit in the living room watching TV with her or scroll on my well-worn phone while she read. When the siren call of an ice cream truck passed by our house she would ask if we could get something for Mom.

When Louise was old enough to take care of herself, I got a job at a local diner, the same Mom worked at. She begged that I go somewhere else, but I persisted and said that the tips were good there. We both knew that was a lie, but it was the only good excuse I could think of to spend more time with her.

I’m ashamed to admit though that the majority of my earnings from there went into my own interests. Mostly into a new car. I justified that it would help me take care of Louise, but really it became an escape for when Frank pissed me off too much. 

Frank’s junior year of high-school was when he was at his worst. Despite his rude demeanor, off-color jokes and borderline psychotic tendencies, Frank managed to con his way into the hearts of the whole school. The only positive thing I’ll say about Frank is that he was one hell of an athlete. He was the school’s star quarterback in football, point guard in basketball and even broke a few track records. Everyone, save for Louise and I, loved Frank. If you didn’t, then you would be outcast. We only got a pass because we were “Frank’s Family.”

Of course though, none of his abilities were natural. Some nights when I passed by his room, I could see lights dancing under the door. Other nights he’d quietly chant gibberish. I just assumed at the time it was a show or anime he was watching. In hindsight, I should have known he was a practicing occultist much earlier. 

At every waking moment Frank was doing something to my detriment or to put someone else down. Karma had to catch up with him eventually, and it did.

One weekend, Mom asked me to take Frank along on my shopping errands. She wanted him to know the routine for when I went off to college so Frank help out around the house. He would only slow me down and cause trouble so I pleaded that he stay home. She insisted, and being the good son, I reluctantly obliged.

To be honest, that day went about as well as any other day. I even found myself enjoying Frank’s company for once. Conversation drifted to our differences and in a strange turn of character, Frank seemed genuinely sorry for all the trouble he caused. It was going smoothly, until I saw Frank take a swig from a bottle of whiskey I didn’t remember paying for.

“Where did you get that?”

“I’ve had it.”

Frank’s words were already starting to slur.

“No you haven’t. Get rid of it before a cop sees us.”

I was 20 at the time, but looked old enough to pass as 21. Frank was only 18 and already balding.

“Dump it out or you’re walking home!”

Frank downed the rest of the brown liquor. As he tipped the booze backwards I noticed a bloody bandage wrapped around his palm. I was about to bring it up when Frank tossed the empty bottle out of the passenger window.

“Whatever you say boss!”

Just as the glass shattered into a million tiny shards on the pavement, a police car zoomed by. My heart stopped. I watched with abated breath, fully expecting the red and blue lights to flash on and pursue us. It kept straight and grew smaller and smaller in the rear view.

“What the fuck is wrong with you?!”

Frank’s face went red, not from embarrassment but from intoxication and holding in a nasally laugh which he unleashed upon me.

“You need to take a chill pill!” Frank snickered. “The pig didn’t see nothin’! Pull over, I gotta take a leak.”

I slammed on the brakes and unlocked the door.

“Whatever man, just hurry up.”

Frank finished his business and hopped back in the car. We only had one more stop to make, but Frank suggested we take a detour to get to our destination faster. After more arguing I agreed to go, if only to get the day over. Day faded into dusk and dusk fell to night. Both of our phones had lost signal somewhere along the way and we were all turned around. Frank unfolded a map from the glovebox and failed spectacularly at reading it.

I crawled up to a stop sign at a four-way intersection and tried to help. The map was upside down. He pulled it back, claiming he knew exactly where we were. I reached for the map again. Frank shoved his hand into my face and pushed back, hard. In the tussle, my foot lifted off the brakes and soon we were sitting in the middle of the intersection.

I don’t remember much after that, only two bright lights, a loud horn and the metal grill of a semi-truck encompassing my vision. I woke up in the hospital a week later. Frank was killed on impact. The driver walked away injury free. Mom was inconsolable. 

Louise and I tried to help her the best we could, but she didn’t speak to anyone until a week after the funeral. She never outright said she blamed me, but I think a small part of her did. The crash replayed over and over in my nightmares, only ceasing when I allowed sweet liquid fire to coat my throat and fog my brain over. Like father, like son.

Two weeks went by, and everything was as normal as they could be.

Then the freak accidents started happening.

They started small at first. A local baker got his hand stuck in an oven and had to have it amputated. He blamed one of the interns for the “prank,” but nobody else was in the bakery that day. Our city's mayor was hit by a runaway tire, breaking her legs and putting her in a wheelchair for a few months. The tire, nor the vehicle it was attached to, were ever located.

Then, as the accidents grew closer to home, they became more severe. Mom’s best friend from high school was attacked by a crow while she was out running. It dive-bombed her and she dodged out of the way into traffic. Into a speeding bus that was late on its route that didn’t exist. A day later, my best friend, David, was killed while working for his father. He was cutting down trees with a chainsaw and it caught on a tiny nail that had been embedded in the bark. The saw kicked back and cut through his head like butter.

My last text I sent to David was, “You bringing the drinks tonight?”

I was suspicious of how all the accidents were coincidentally connected to us, but didn’t think much of it. At least not until the curse targeted Louise.

She was having an impromptu Fall beach day in the front yard while I sat on the porch. Out of nowhere, the wind kicked up and blew a beach ball into the street. Before I had a chance to react, Louise was sprinting off after it.

We lived in a quiet neighborhood and rarely had traffic, but this day a black sports car was speeding down the street. Louise didn’t see the car coming, but I did. Adrenaline pumped through my veins and I ran after Louise. I wrapped my arms around her and pulled her back onto the lawn. The car zoomed past and swerved to avoid the beach ball. It slipped out of control and rolled over at least a dozen times before slamming into the side of our neighbors house.

None-the-wiser, Louise wriggled out of my arms and ran after the ball again. The neighbors were a young couple, man and woman, who had been married just three months earlier. The wife ran out of the house, now starting to catch fire, covered in glass and blood screaming her head off. Neither the driver nor the husband made it out of the fire.

Confusion and terror overtook me. I couldn’t make sense of it all, until I realized the accidents had only started after Frank’s death. Then I recalled the late nights he’d stay up doing God-knows-what in his room. All of the evidence pointed to one conclusion: Frank had put a curse on his own family.



***My Mother Is a Cultist***

I took Louise inside, locked the front door and closed all the curtains. I double checked everything inside to make sure nothing could fall on, stab or otherwise brutalize my little sister. Then, after being satisfied with the safety precautions, I went to Frank’s room to find evidence to support my theory.

The door to his room was wide open. A thick darkness pooled out of the room, I couldn’t see far past the doorway. A dull hum began to fog my mind as I approached. I reached out to touch the substance, fingers brushing against the dark threshold inside the door frame. It was cool to the touch and smooth like glass. As I stepped forward to pass through the obsidian-esque fog, Mom emerged from the darkness and slammed the door shut, locking it behind her.

She stuffed something down her shirt then turned to face me. Dark bags hung under her eyes and tear stains trailed down her cheeks. She had two, old leather-bound books under her arms.

“Everything ok, honey?”

“Uh, yeah,” I said, trying to hide the concern in my voice. “What were you doing in there? Did you not hear the crash outside?”

“Outside? What happened outside?”

“A car almost ran over Louise and-”

Horror washed over her.

“Oh God! Is Louise ok? Are you ok?”

“Yes, yes Mom. We’re both fine. But I think one of the neighbors-”

Her calm, tempered demeanor returned.

“Oh. That’s too bad. I’m glad you guys are safe.”

She pushed past me. The door to Frank’s room was still locked.

“Hey Mom, can I get into Frank’s room for a sec?”

Mom froze, still as a statue. I wasn’t even sure she was breathing anymore.

“Why?”

“He uh. He borrowed a game a while back and I was just wanting to get it back.”

“I can pop in real quick and get it for you. Just tell me where it is.”

So so still.

“Oh no, it's ok. I’ll be quick.”

I turned and walked toward Frank’s door.

Thump-thump-thump-thump.

Fast-paced footsteps approached behind me.

“No.” Mom commanded. “You are not to go into your brother’s room.”

Her presence was terrifying. I stood a good foot above Mom, but the oppressive aura that towered right behind me was leagues taller. I was terrified to turn around.

“Ok,” shaking. “I won’t, Mom. I promise.”

“Good. Now give Mom a hug.”

In a strange way, it didn’t seem like Mom was asking for a hug. Rather, something else was telling me to hug “Mom.”

I turned around, trying to control my shaking, but it was just Mom with a joyful smile on her face. I bent down and hugged her. She hugged me and rubbed my back just like she did with Frank and Louise.

“Oh, Jeremy. I love you guys so much. You know that right?”

“Uh huh.”

“And you know that I would do ANYTHING to keep you guys safe?”

“Y-yeah.”

“Good. Now go get Louise. I’m making Lasagna for dinner!”

The curse had got to Mom. I knew I had to get into Frank’s room now. Late that night, after Mom and Louise went to sleep, I got up and tried Frank’s door again. It didn’t budge. I couldn’t risk breaking down the door and waking up Mom so I had to find the key.

I waited at her door for a good 30 minutes, making absolutely sure she was asleep. I pushed the door open slowly and began to creep across her room at a snail’s pace. At any movement or sound from her, I was prepared to drop to the ground. Mom was a heavy sleeper, but I didn’t want to risk anything.

Quietly, I searched the room, finding the key on Mom’s nightstand attached to a necklace. Right next to it was a set of picture frames containing old pictures of Louise, Mom, myself and Dad.

I hadn’t seen Dad’s face in years. He had a thick, red beard, like a dwarf’s. He was short like a dwarf too. I guess I got most of Mom’s genes. She was tall and skinny. Mom used to have long, dirty-blonde hair. Now, it was greyed and thinning. No doubt a result of the immense stress she had been under. In every picture where Mom and Dad were together, they were smiling. They made each other so happy.

Among the pictures of us kids, Frank’s pictures were absent. There were no pictures of Frank around the room at all. Mom had taken them all into her arms and was asleep with them. She coddled the glass and wood frames like they were her own children, sobbing softly in her sleep.

I hesitated in taking the key for a moment, but my mission was too important. I snatched the key-chain and exited the room just as carefully as I had entered.

Frank’s bedroom door unlocked easily and creaked open. That same obsidian wall stood before me. I pushed against it gently and the membrane gave way. I hadn’t seen Frank’s since Dad’s death. It was totally different from what I remembered. Sports posters and bikini calendars I had helped Frank put up were replaced or covered by cheap bookshelves etched with arcane sigils and stuffed with old, decrepit books. The air was pungent, salt and moisture that coated my nostrils and throat.

In the dead center of the room was a massive octagram drawn into the wooden floor with a dark, red substance. Probably blood. I didn’t stop to inspect it further. Something else caught my eye. In the center of the octagram was an old, black, leather-bound tome. It looked no different from the other books lining the shelves and the books Mom had, save for its scarlet edged pages.

Despite the occult symbols and strange presence of the room, I didn’t feel threatened or in danger. The room almost seemed to recognize me. Accept me. Accept my blood. The same blood that ran through Mom and Louise’s veins and the same blood that once ran through Frank and Dad’s. The book’s covers and spine were blank and smooth, like the dark wall of fog in the door frame. On the inside cover was a printed title.

“*An Astute History of Occult Practices and Why You Should Never Perform Them, Third Edition Revised”*

I flipped through the faded, scarlet pages which were warm to the touch. A start contrast to the ice-cold leather cover. Most of the text in the book was a language I didn’t understand, but various notes left by Frank translated portions of it. What I read in that book, along with the extremely detailed pictures, was all I needed to confirm my suspicion that Frank had caused this curse that plagued our family.

Sitting in the middle of the octagram I flipped through the pages, trying to make sense of the alien text within. Deeper into the book Frank’s notes became less frequent. Frank had performed three rituals so far: one to make people like him more, the second to enhance his athletic abilities, and the third to let him speak to the dead momentarily.

Just to see if Frank had written any more notes I flipped through the remainder of the book. As I did, a small bookmark fell out of the back cover. On the front was written, “*From A.B.”* I picked up the bookmark and unfolded it, a letter.

*“Hey Frankie-Pie! Here’s that book you asked about. Just remember our deal! ;)*

*Here’s an easy sentence to help you get started with translation.”*

A series of glyphs were written below this, along with Frank’s translation”

*“Your heart is mine forever! Kidding! Or am I?”*

I didn’t know anybody with the initials “A.B.” that Frank had mentioned. I would have searched through some of the other books in the room, but the sun was starting to rise meaning Mom would be up soon to make breakfast. Placing the bookmark back, I spotted a small library stamp at the bottom of the back cover in fine, blue ink.

*“Property of Montauk Historical Society of the Supernatural.”*

Montauk. Montauk. Montauk.

I committed the name to memory then placed the book back exactly as I had found it. I exited Frank’s room and locked the door behind me. Just in time as down the hall Mom’s morning alarm went off. I hurried to my room and closed the door. Soon after, on the other side, footsteps paced past. 

Then silence.

Something was wrong.

Had I made too much noise? Did the lock or door not fully shut? What about the key? Where was the key?

I patted myself down. Nothing. A shadow crept under the door crack.

I left the key in the lock.

“Jeremy? Are you awake?”

I was completely still. My breath was shaky but quiet. There was no way Mom could know I was in Frank’s room.

THUD. THUD. THUD.

“Jeremy. Wake up, Jeremy.”

I started walking towards my bed, slow and steady.

Step by step by step.

The safety of my sheets were inches away.

Underneath my weight, the floorboards groaned and creaked, loud enough for Louise on the other side of the house to hear.

I dived into bed and turned over under my covers. The door swung open and slammed against the wall. Mom gilded over and towered over me. I didn’t turn around to look, but could feel her cold gaze piercing through me.

“Jeremy. Did you go into Frank’s room?”

“What?” I responded in my best “just woke up from sleep” voice.

“Did you go into Frank’s room?”

“No Mom, I didn’t.”

She loomed over me still.

“Can I go back to sleep now?”

I turned over to face whatever stood above my bed, but only saw the silhouette of Mom in the doorway.

“Of course sweety. Sorry to wake you.”

The next few days I spend hours scrolling through sketchy websites and conspiracy forums trying to find any information on Montauk. I found a few posts from people saying they had visited the place, but no one had an address. I messaged users directly in hopes of getting anything to point me in the right direction, but the only replies I got were “bro doesn’t know,” or “lol normie,” or other more derogatory messages.

I was just about to give up, when I received a random message with a set of coordinates attached.

“*Don’t tell anyone I sent you this. I don’t want to end up like the others, but you gotta see this place.”*

Searching the coordinates online brought me to a small, white mansion next to a lighthouse on the edge of a cliff. In front of the house was a wooden sign covered in faded and chipping teal paint. Written across the front in pristine white cursive, like it had been painted the day the photo was taken, was a name.

“*Montauk Historical Society of the Supernatural*”

Jackpot.
