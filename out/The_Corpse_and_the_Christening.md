I’m a speechwriter; you’ve probably seen my work in the mouths of quite a few famous politicians.

People imagine it’s easy, the same way they figure they could get on a stage at a comedy club and make people laugh, but originality is a scarce resource.

At least ten people have quipped the same thing about my daughter Ayla.

She was born like a Buddha. No crying. Clear-eyed and alert, and after that she didn’t coo or babble. Not a word in 18 months. 

‘Well, Scotty,’ they remark, ‘if you write her a speech, maybe she’ll speak!’

Cyd, my wife, began to panic, so we took her to a speech therapist. There was nothing congenitally wrong with her, and she showed no signs of autism.

He jokingly said we had a lazy babe, but it wasn’t like that; it was more like she was sitting on some cosmic joke whose wit we couldn’t discern… 

A month ago, my grandma died, and it hit me hard. 

As a speechwriter, I have a habit of slipping into hyperbole, but not in this case… That old woman was evil. 

When I was a kid, her favourite trick was getting me to clean the attic during the boiling summer vacation. I’d spent all day lugging boxes down a precarious ladder to the garage, and then invariably she’d say, ‘Actually, I don’t have the space. They need to go back.’ 

She was like a Nazi camp guard who made the inmates carry bags of salt from one end of Auschwitz to the other. 

And if I dared kick up a fuss, it was always the same reply in her nasal whine. 

**‘Yours is not to reason why, yours is but to do and die.’** 

Tennyson. God knows where she heard it. Funny too because the poem is from the same year Lincoln gave his 3-hour Peoria speech. 

There is also the great dark thing, but I would prefer that stayed locked up in the attic of my mind, or I might fully lose it. 

Anyway, there was a kind of grim irony in what happened next. Ayla’s baptism was booked in for November 20th, and what do you know, my half-wit aunt booked grandma’s funeral for the same day. The fucking corpse and the christening. 

We were delayed getting to the church because an almighty snowstorm had blown in. 

I shivered when we got there, and not because of the cold. In the cemetery, they’d already dug the hole Grandma was going into that afternoon: a trapdoor to the underworld. 

Ayla was a pretty picture in her white gown, but we’d wrapped her up in a parka because it was so chilly inside. The heating had gone kaput. 

A good temperature for dead bodies, but not so much for the living. 

As the priest took the altar, the congregation's breath hung around the stained-glass windows like mist on an English moor.

He made his blessings for us sinners, then went to carry out the thing itself: ‘I baptise you in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy…’ 

And he paused because he reached down into the font, but the holy water was a solid block of ice. 

… 

We ate lunch nearby and tried to thaw out.

Ayla was the centre of attention. Aunts and uncles tottered over, joking about the 2-for-1 on funerals and christenings, then tried to coax her into speaking so their name was her first word. 

I sat in a kind of daze. 

I think I became a writer because it offers you something harder to come by in life: narrative structure. 

A story, a speech, has a beginning, a middle, and most importantly, an end. 

I’d never had closure with grandma. For the last 20 years of her life, I’d ghosted her, and it had given her a kind of power because she could bitchmoan to all and sundry that her good-for-nothing grandson never visited. 

The restaurant was across the road from the church (funny because the toilets were full of guests changing from white to black), and I braved the elements again and returned to the church. 

She was there, my old gran waiting for her send-off. 

It was an open casket, her request, and the undertakers were fussing around the coffin. 

They looked a little surprised when they saw me; in my stupor, I was still carrying Ayla. 

Of course I should’ve left. A funeral is no place for a baby, but then that word, closure, came to mind again. It was oddly poetic: Grandma had been and gone, and Ayla was just arriving. 

‘We’ll give you a minute,’ the undertakers said and then left us. 

I approached the coffin; she was different from what I’d remembered. She’d spent the last 18 months ‘basically brain-dead,’ according to Mom. 

Death does a disservice to the living, to the rememberers, to the traumatised. Now, she had no wrinkles, her eyelids softly closed, and white hair tied in a neat bun. Nobody would have believed the tyrant she'd been in life. 

And I knew that because Ayla looked down at her corpse and didn’t flinch. In fact, she seemed oddly entranced. 

Yes, closure: Grandma was dead and almost buried, and the future was a bundle in my arms. 

And then baby Ayla looked at me and said, ‘Scotty.’ 

‘Honey! What did you say?’ 

‘Scotty,’ she repeated in her babyish voice. 

Granted, she’d skipped *dada*, but her first word had been my name. 

I kissed her on the cheek, and she tried to move away, a new look in her eyes, something… all at once new and unfamiliar.  

Then she pointed at Grandma and, in a strong voice that grew more nasal, said, ‘What happened to me?’
