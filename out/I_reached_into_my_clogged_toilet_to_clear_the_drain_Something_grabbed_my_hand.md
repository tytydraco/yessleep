It was nearly three in the morning when the stomach cramps woke me up. I barely made it to the upstairs bathroom, my vision blurry with sleep, my stomach churning in knots. After relieving myself, I pressed the chrome lever of the toilet.

The water didn't drain. Instead, the bowl filled up with a thick, murky, stagnant surge that rose to within an inch of the porcelain rim. The solid waste was jammed deep into the narrow S-bend, completely choking the pipe.

I didn't have a plunger. I lived alone in a old, converted brownstone, and the landlord hadn't gotten around to replacing the broken plumbing fixtures.

Desperate to avoid an overflow onto the hardwood floor, I grabbed a heavy plastic trash bag, wrapped it tightly around my right arm up to the elbow, and sealed it at the bicep with a thick rubber band. It was disgusting, but I had no choice. I had to manually break the blockage at the throat of the trap.

I knelt on the cold tiles and plunged my gloved arm into the icy, murky water.  
My fingers reached down into the narrow, smooth porcelain throat, pressing against the heavy blockage. I pushed hard, trying to break the density of the clog to force it down into the main stack.  
Then my fingertips brushed against something that shouldn't have been there. It wasn't soft. It was slick, cold, and wet—like peeled leather.

Before I could pull my hand back, the blockage didn't move *down*. It moved *up*.

A set of long, wet, skeletal fingers shot up out of the black water, curling violently around my wrist. The grip was impossibly tight, like a rusted iron vice clamping down over the plastic bag.

I let out a strangled gasp and tried to yank my arm back. But the angle of the S-bend gave me zero leverage. Whatever was inside the pipe pulled *downward*.

The force dragged my forearm deeper into the narrow porcelain neck. My shoulder slammed hard against the toilet bowl. I screamed as the porcelain edge dug into my collarbone, but the grip on my wrist only tightened, grinding the bones of my arm together with a sickening crunch.

*CRACK.*

Pain exploded in my right forearm as the radius bone gave way under the sheer, mechanical pressure. I shrieked, my face pressed against the cold porcelain rim. Through the murky water, I saw what was pulling me. A pale, hairless face—flatted, blind, and dripping with sewage—was squeezing its way up through the four-inch pipe. Its jaw opened impossibly wide, revealing rows of translucent, needle-thin teeth, locking onto the plastic-wrapped skin of my wrist.

In a frenzy of pure survival instinct, I reached out with my free left hand and grabbed the heavy ceramic toilet tank lid.

With a guttural roar, I brought the heavy lid smashing down onto the porcelain bowl right where the wrist-thick neck of the entity was emerging.  
The ceramic lid shattered into three jagged pieces. The impact crushed the entity’s grip, severed two of its elongated fingers, and fractured the entire toilet bowl.

The pressure released. I tore my broken right arm free from the drain, tumbling backward onto the wet bathroom floor.

Black, foul-smelling water exploded out of the cracked porcelain, flooding the floorboards. From inside the broken base of the toilet, a wet, wet rasping sound echoed—a wet *slurp* as the rest of the wet, segmented body rapidly retreated backward down into the main sewage pipe.  
I lay on the tiles, gasping for air, clutching my grotesquely bent right arm against my chest. Blood was soaking through the torn plastic bag, mixing with the dark water on the floor.

I haven't gone near a bathroom since. I wrapped my arm in splints and tape, sitting on my living room couch in the dark with a butcher knife on my lap. Because every time the pipes down in the walls groan or gurgle, I can hear those needle teeth scraping against the inside of the metal tubes—climbing back up toward the light.
