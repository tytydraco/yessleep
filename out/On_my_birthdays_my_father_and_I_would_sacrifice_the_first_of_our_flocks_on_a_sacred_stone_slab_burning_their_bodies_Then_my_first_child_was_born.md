I remember the first time my father brought me to the slab. This was how I knew it, for he never gave me any other name for that pitted table of stone. 

I grew up on a small, isolated farm in the bowels of the Midwest. My father was a good man, and so was my mother, but we often found ourselves in the midst of an irate community, unwilling to accommodate us or make us feel welcome.

“Witches” they called us. “Satanists, Wizards, voodoo.”   
Those words shouldn’t have stung as they did, but they made my the skin of my cheeks red with all that they implied. We were good Christian people. No church would accept us, but we spent our Sundays in a church of our own making, studying the Testaments New and Old. My parents took every opportunity to tell me and my siblings to be of the kind that Christ would raise up.

“Be a good man,” my father would say. “Do for the good of the community. Our Lord is right. Our Lord is just. Our Lord is merciful. Be as he is.” 

If there ever was a person close to godliness it was my father. He got up early to work, came home late to his family, and still somehow made time for his children. I was his second-born, and he spent unceasing efforts to keep me from going out and losing myself in the world. “We are not from there. Remember your home in heaven. Remember your home here.”

In my younger days, I was instructed in all the small tasks of farm maintenance. It was expected of me to continue on our family legacy. My ancestors had tended this land for over a century, and I was to be part of an ever continuing lineage. And our legacy wasn’t just farming. We felt responsible for the very health of the soil, the very strength of the rocks that we built upon. It was our foundation, our livelihood. While we planted straight lines of corn, we cultivated the wild things as well, let them grow at the fringes of our acreage. It was in those wild things that fertility lingered.

A man makes love not as a man, but as an animal. Only in losing himself can he create a more organized place, create more of himself to fill the void. 

My father made sure I understood this.

The first I saw the slab was the day I turned sixteen.

It was still morning when my father woke me. I expected some sort of celebration, but instead he stood over me, loomed, his face dark like it had never been before. 

“Come. We’ve chores to do.” 

I followed him out into the dark morning air. 

He brought me to a small parcel of corn that I had grown over the past year. On my fifteenth birthday I had received the seeds as a present, was told to make them grow up strong. It was an uphill battle. There was blight in our fields, and the fields of our neighbors. Many of the ears that had grown up were shriveled and misshapen, twisted and blackened. Rotting, and pus-dripping, full of sulfur stink. I felt shame at the corn. But my father didn’t say as much about it as I thought he would. He could be gentle in love, but always clear in his instruction and correction. 

At the parcel, he instructed me to pluck the finest ear of corn from the stalks. I knew the very one the moment the words left his mouth. It was my one good success. I had tended to it as a small sprout, giving it more animal dung and water than the others. Sometimes, after the chores were finished, I would stare at it, seeing the work of my hands and speaking softly to the ear and to myself of how golden, how perfect it was. 

I knew it was a sin. Pride. But it felt good to be recognized, to have achieved something. 

The ear struggled to hold on, and when it ripped away from its mother stalk, I felt a small part of myself tear inside. But it was meant to be plucked, to be eaten. Otherwise it would rot like and be good for nothing, not even animal feed. 

“Come,” was all my father said once it was done. I followed him from our cultivated land to the edge of the farm. A wild bramble existed there. Dark, full, sprawling. Blackberry bushes grew tall, wiry spikes lifting high up and stretching like the earth had just woken from a eons long sleep. Such a motion from the plants reminded me of what our life truly was: cultivating the aches, pains, and small imperfections of the ground to eek a living from the soil. 

I expected to go around the bush, as I’d never been requested to tend to the wild parts of the earth. But instead my father went straight through, pushing aside some brambles while letting others tug at his clothing and skin. 

The brambles did the same to me as I followed. My blood spilled out of scratches as I struggled to keep up with the old man. The sun was rising, and even with the golden ball pouring over the edge of the world, the place we entered grew darker with each push against the vines. I couldn’t tell if my father knew which way he was going in the chaos, or if he was wandering just as our ancestors did in the good book, allowing himself to be led by the voice, the whim of God. 

Eventually, we came to a place where the brambles ended in a slight alcove. They surrounded us above and around like some prickled cavern. A hole in the wild. 

In the center was a large stone slab pockmarked with burns and pits.  

My father gestured to it. “Take the corn, cut it into pieces, and place it thereupon.” 

I did as was instructed, taking a knife from my belt and running its way down the corn. Its golden kernels made a popping sound as they came into contact with the sharp metal edge, as if each were protesting its removal. 

Once it was done, and a small golden pile lay on the stone, my father produced a flint. “Burn it.”  
I did so, unsettled, but asking no questions. They caught aflame immediately. Smoke spiraled into the brambles, and what happened next I cannot fully describe in words, except to put it in terms natural to the flesh. The world…shuddered, if it can be believed. It wasn’t an earthquake, or some great calamitous shaking of the ground. No, it was as if everything for a moment shifted, and then came back to its present place. A single breath. A sigh. 

We stared in silence at the burned offering, as I now knew it to be, neither one of us talking.  
Later that night we celebrated my birthday. I was given a piglet, a female, and told to raise it up to its first litter. 

As I went about caring for my single pig, my corn changed. Where the ears had once been withered, desiccated, and dripping they now grew thick, bulging kernels. It was as if the disease were eradicated overnight.  

Some part of me supposed this to be the result of my father and I’s visit to the bramble. But I never asked him. I was afraid. What we had done in those bushes had felt…wrong in a way. One I couldn’t explain. 

A year passed, and on the eve of my 17th birthday my father came to me again. My sow had given birth only a week prior. He told me to pick the healthiest, fattest piglet of the bunch. Again, I knew immediately the one I must choose. My heart rebelled. Even with the abundance of corn, the pigs had all turned out runts, underfed and scrawny, covered in their own feces. All except for one. I had been cautioned against naming the animals since we raised all of them for slaughter, but I had secretly called the piglet Ryan. Even as my father asked me, I knew I didn’t want to bring Ryan. But that was precisely the piglet I knew my father was asking me to offer. 

I almost lied. Almost brought another piglet instead. I knew what would happen to that small creature should they enter the bramble. But I knew my father. I knew God. Lying felt like the greater sin. 

It took almost an hour of staring at Ryan’s small, pink body, trotting around his limp brothers and sister, suckling at his mother’s teat with little wet shrieks, for me to gain the resolve. I reached my hands in and plucked him from his enclosure. 

Together, my father and I proceeded into the brambles. More seemed to tug on my skin as we went through, almost as if the wild itself were telling us to turn back. But I endured the stings, the pokes. Ryan squirmed in my arms, fought against my grip. His squeal made my heart sink and shrivel. Bitter and salty drops gathered at the corner of my eyes. His cries were like those of a human child, sobbing for his mother. 

When we reached the slab, father gave me the same instruction as before: “Cut it in pieces, and place it thereupon.” 

Butchering pigs was not a new task for me. But as I divided Ryan asunder, and the blood pooled on the pockmarked stone, it felt different. I shouldn’t have named him. My tears intermingled with the blood, until eventually the pig lay in six separate portions. The primal cuts. Again, my father handed me the box of matches. 

“Burn it.” 

We left once the smoke started to abate. My father had the decency to leave me alone to my weeping as we came back. 

In time, the sorrow faded. 

Ryan’s brothers and sisters become strong after his brother’s death. They grew big and fat, larger than all the other pigs that our family had raised. I didn’t name them. I had learned better. 

Years passed, and my father and I fell into this ritual of sacrifice. I graduated from pigs, to cows, to even a horse. I learned to distance my mind, to keep my heart from feeling as I raised the knife. I loved them, but it was a walled love. Though I might feed them, care for the creatures, I knew I was just as capable of killing should it prove profitable to the rest. 

And our farm thrived as it never had before. 

I became a man, and soon it was time for me to choose a wife. There was a farmstead near our own, distant cousins from when our families traveled from the old country. There was a young woman, Elizabeth, that I had noticed ever since I was a child. I was lucky enough that she shared my affections. After a short talk between our parents, we were married, and soon expected our first child. It was an uncommon blessing. Many of the families around us struggled to conceive. The next generation of our family was nonexistent, our wombs shut up, and our seed inert. 

They tended to us closely during the pregnancy, every indulgence taken to ensure Elizabeth’s comfort and the healthy development of the babe within her. 

The day of my son’s birth was a glorious day. My family, Elizabeth’s family, and the whole community celebrated. Love like I never knew came over me, deeper, vast as the firmament. This was my child. My boy. I would do anything on this earth for him. The wall I placed around my heart crumbled with his reedy sobs. He was healthy, and strong, and he had his mother’s blonde hair. 

We named him Sam, after my brother, who had died as an infant. 

Day turned to night while me and Elizabeth spent time getting to know Sam outside the womb. In the haze of new parenthood, I almost didn’t notice my father enter the bedroom. He stood in the doorway, his face dark in a familiar way. When I saw him, my heart died a portion. 

“Son. Come.” He turned and went outside. 

I followed, as I had been trained to do. We went outside to the small clearing between the barn and the house. The sky was a black canvas, smitten with small wavering silver dots. Cloudless, windless. Even the animals were quiet as my father cleared his throat. “Something bad’s come in the air.” 

I nodded. I knew what he referred to. The family across the way had lost a child to a coughing sickness. Most of the children, even in the nearby city, had a similar ailment. Many had already died. More still had their lungs crippled from the disease. 

“Bring Sam.” 

My heart dropped. My knees crumpled and I fell. “No. Please.” Tears poured down my face, and my father’s hand rose as if to strike me.

But instead, he rested it in on my back in comfort. 

I knew what it meant. My father was not one to give an unnecessary command. He asked for what was required, and argued for nothing more. In that moment of comfort, in the cold night where we stood, I knew what he was saying was right. It must be done. 

Ascending the stairs to Elizabeth took all my will, all my musculature. At the opening of our bedroom door she awoke, her arm still around our son. I came to her and held out my grimed hands. 

“Let me take him.” 

“What did your father want?” Elizabeth didn’t know about the slab. About what was inside the bramble. But she had seen the sorrow and resolve in my father’s face. From her look, it scared her as much as it had filled me with dread. 

“He…wanted to give a piece of advice. And to see the boy.” 

Elizabeth drew in her arm so that it touched Sam’s tiny hand. 

I reached in, lifting the baby. Sam stirred in his sleep, and squinted his eyes. Then relaxed.   
Elizabeth clutched onto the sleeve of my shirt. “He can see him in the morning.” 

“I’ll be right back.” I had to pull to get away from her grip. 

She watched me leave. When I closed the door, I could hear the beginning of bitter sobs.  

I don’t remember going outside. Or even what my father said to me when I came to him with Sam in my arms. Before I knew it, we were moving amongst the brambles. Their thorns raked across my skin more sharp than before, blood stinging my eye from a cut above it. 

Sam slumbered in my arms, protected from their onslaught, not putting up so much as a fight. He must remain perfect, if the rite was to be completed. 

We entered the alcove of vines, me and my father. We both bled heavily, as if the salty red drops were the tears we couldn’t shed. With his head, my father gestured to the stone. As if in a dream, I took Sam, and lowered him down. 

He awoke, and squirmed. 

My father placed a knife in my hand, the leather cold against my palm. 

“Cut. Burn.” He paused for a moment. “For the good of all of us.” 

It froze in my hand, the knife, as I brought it to my son’s neck. But before I could commit the last, grave act, something happened. 

Sam started to cry. 

His face screwed up, and his arms flailed back and forth, full of fury and energy. 

And in that moment, I knew I was lost. I was filled again with that love I had shared with Elizabeth only hours before. That care. That determination to do whatever needed to be done to protect my boy. What did I care for other children? This was my son. He was the only thing, the only one that mattered. 

My face flushed, I twisted… 

And buried the knife into my father’s gut. 

I pulled it from him before he could even finish his groan. His arm stopped mine as I tried to pierce him again.  

“Stop! Son!” 

We grappled, back and forth around the stone, Sam’s cries added to my frenzied pace. I slashed father on his arms, his neck, his shoulder. Blood poured and dripped, splattering the stone and all the surrounding bushes. The earth quaked beneath us, shivered as if it were in ecstasy, or else in the greatest pain. 

My father grew weaker, and I was able to drive the knife into his chest. Once the hilt hit his ribs, a strange glow filled his eyes. He reached out with both arms, and embraced me. Pulled me to his chest. 

Then tipped us toward the slab. 

Shouting and screaming, both mine and my father’s, filled the bitter eternity it took us to fall. Too late, I realized what he was doing. Our bodies connected with the stone. 

And with something warm and soft in the middle. 

My son’s cries ceased.  

It was silent in the bramble. I didn’t say a word or cry out, even as I pulled my us off the slab, and placed my still bloody hands gently on my Sam’s limbs. They were still, already turning cold. I turned with the knife raised, ready to invoke all sorts of pain upon the man who called himself “father.” 

He was already limp and unfeeling like his grandson, a single ghost of a word still pressed to his lips. 

I burned it. Burned it all, but Sam. 

The bramble blew smoke into the heavens in gushes. I watched it until it was ash. Until the slab was exposed. The fire was not near hot enough to burn a body entire, but when the fire dimmed, my father’s form was nowhere to be seen among the coals. 

In the next hour, I put my son in the ground. Then I left. 

I’ve never been back to that farm. I couldn’t face Elizabeth. 

My existence since has been one of wandering. Scrounging and feeding off the refuse of others. I’ve kept myself from love, from being loved. Our family had withered away, last I heard. Broke in pieces and sent to all quarters of the globe. The coughing sickness that came before Sam died wiped out that next generation, and much of the previous. 

It’s been decades since. That patch of earth, once fertile, is now a barren waste. 

Already my life fades from me. I hope my death is soon, that I’ll pass on to the sweet oblivion that waits for me. But I’m too afraid to do it by my own hand. Too unwilling to take another life. It had become too easy for me, too natural.  

Living with this weight is my punishment, my torment for murder. 

Last night I dreamt of Sam. I held him in my arms, and I carried him away. Far away beyond plains and brambles, toward a glowing fire in the distance, high enough to reach the heavens and hot enough to turn our flesh red and warm. I ran to that fire, and felt Sam turn softly in his sleep in my arms. Maybe there, things would be different. He’d be alive. I’d be alive. I got so close to touching it. So close to putting my fingers in the coals. 

Then I opened my eyes. Underneath the tree where I slept, my hand was out of my sleeping bag, reaching into the cold forest air. 

Where there had been nothing before, was now a wall of thorny vines.
