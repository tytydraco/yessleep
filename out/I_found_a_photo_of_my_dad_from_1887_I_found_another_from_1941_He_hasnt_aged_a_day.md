I do genealogy as a hobby. Not the spit-in-a-tube, wait-six-weeks kind—the real, dusty-fingers, scrolling-through-microfiche-in-a-library-basement kind. My mom died when I was fifteen, and building out the family tree was my way of keeping her close. Of proving she existed. I’d trace her line back through Polish immigrants and Pennsylvania coal miners, and for a few hours, I’d feel like she was still somewhere in the weave of it all.

My dad’s side was harder. He never talked about his family. When I’d ask, he’d wave a hand and say, “We’re just plain people, sweetheart. Nothing worth digging up.” I figured he was embarrassed—maybe there was poverty back there, or scandal. So I left it alone.

But a few weeks ago, a genealogy site I subscribe to added a batch of digitized historical society photos from rural Ohio. My dad grew up in a small town outside Zanesville. I typed in his surname—my surname—and waited, not expecting much.

Two results. Both photographs.

The first was a group portrait taken in front of a general store, dated 1887. A dozen men in dusty coats and bowler hats, squinting into the camera. I almost scrolled past it. Then I saw the face in the back row.

It was my dad.

I laughed out loud. A doppelgänger. One of those weird genetic coincidences where a great-great-grand-uncle looks exactly like a living relative. I zoomed in, studying the jaw, the set of the eyes, the slightly crooked left earlobe. The resemblance was uncanny. The man even stood the same way my dad stands—weight shifted to the right hip, hands loose at his sides, a faint, patient smile that doesn’t quite reach the eyes.



I downloaded the photo and sent it to my dad with a text: Found your twin from 1887 lol.

He didn’t reply. That wasn’t unusual. He’s a slow texter.



The second photo was a wedding portrait from 1902. A bride and groom stiffly posed in front of a painted backdrop of a garden. The bride was young, dark-haired, clutching a bouquet of wilted-looking flowers. The groom was my father. Same face, same stance, same crooked left earlobe, as if someone had cut him out of the 1887 photo and pasted him into this one, thirty-five years later. He was even wearing a nearly identical dark suit. The bride looked faintly terrified—eyes wide, mouth a tight hyphen. I told myself it was just the era. Nobody smiled in old photographs.

But my skin was starting to crawl.

I dug deeper. I spent the next three days doing nothing but tracing my paternal line, pulling census records, obituaries, church registries. My great-grandfather, a man named Silas, died in a farming accident in 1943. Before he died, he and his wife—my great-grandmother Eleanor—took a family portrait in the fall of 1941. The site had a scan of it.

There he was. My father. Arm wrapped tight around Eleanor’s shoulders. She was crying in the photo. You could see the tear tracks, the smudged mascara, the way her body leaned slightly away from him. Next to her stood a little boy—my grandfather—staring at the camera with the hollow, exhausted eyes of a child who had seen too much.

The man in the 1887 general store photo. The groom in the 1902 wedding. The patriarch in the 1941 portrait. Three different dates, three different families, three different names written on the backs of the prints. The same face. My father’s face, unaged, untouched, watching the camera with that same faint, patient smile.

I printed the photos and spread them across my kitchen table. My hands were shaking. There had to be an explanation. A prank. A family tradition of naming lookalike sons after their fathers, maybe, like a lineage of clones. But that didn’t explain why the dates spanned fifty-four years with no visible aging. It didn’t explain the bride’s terrified eyes, or my great-grandmother’s tears, or the thin, hard set of my grandfather’s mouth in a photo he should have been too young to understand.

I thought about my dad. Really thought about him. His pale skin, almost translucent in the right light. His preference for heavy curtains, dim rooms, evenings. The way he’d never come to my soccer games if they were played before dusk. I’d always thought he just burned easily. He told me he had a sun allergy. A rare skin condition. I’d never questioned it.

But I’d also never seen him in direct sunlight. Not once in thirty-one years.

I called in sick to work. I drove to my dad’s house—the house I grew up in, a tired ranch-style place on the edge of town with a big, overgrown backyard and a porch light he never turned off. I let myself in with my old key.

He was in the living room, sitting in his usual armchair, the one positioned carefully out of the reach of any window light. The television was on, some black-and-white movie. He looked up and smiled.

“Didn’t expect you today, sweetheart. Everything okay?”

I didn’t sit down. I stood in the doorway, clutching the manila folder with the printouts pressed against my chest like a shield.

“I found some old family photos,” I said. My voice sounded thin and reedy. “Can you look at them?”

“Sure. Let’s see.”

I handed him the folder. He opened it. His expression didn’t change. He looked at the 1887 photo. The 1902 photo. The 1941 portrait. His smile stayed in place, faint and patient, the same smile as the man in the pictures.

“Huh,” he said. “Family resemblance is a funny thing.”

“Dad. It’s not a resemblance. It’s you. It’s exactly you. The same face. The same earlobe. The same everything. How is that possible?”

He closed the folder and set it gently on the side table. He folded his hands in his lap. The room felt very cold.

“I told you we’re plain people,” he said. “Nothing worth digging up.”

“Who are you?” I whispered.

He tilted his head, studying me with an expression that was equal parts affection and pity. “I’m your father.”

“My great-grandmother is crying in that photo. She’s afraid. What did you do to her?”

A long silence. The old house creaked. Outside, a cloud passed over the sun, and the room grew dimmer still.

“Eleanor was a good woman,” he said finally. “She figured it out, too. Near the end. She tried to take your grandfather and leave. But I couldn’t allow that. A boy needs his father.”

I felt the floor tilt under me. “My grandfather. You’re not his father. You’re not my father. You’re… you’re not even…”

I couldn’t finish the sentence. He didn’t help me finish it.

“Every generation,” he said quietly. “One child. One family. Someone to carry the name forward. Someone to take care of.” He looked at me with those tired, familiar eyes. “Your mother suspected. She got too close to the truth, just like you are now. That’s why she had to go.”

The room went very still. I could hear a ringing in my ears, high and thin.

“The car accident,” I breathed. “That was you.”

He didn’t nod. He didn’t need to. The smile was still there, soft and sad and utterly inhuman.

“I would never hurt you, sweetheart,” he said. “You’re my daughter. You’re the one I’ve been waiting for. The one who’ll continue the line. I’ve taken care of this family for over a hundred years. And now it’s your turn.”

I bolted. I ran for the front door, fumbling with the deadbolt, my breath coming in raw, panicked gasps. I threw the door open and staggered out onto the porch—into the late afternoon sun. The light hit my skin, warm and golden and safe. I turned around.

My father stood in the doorway. The shadow of the porch roof fell across him like a curtain. He didn’t step forward. He just watched me, hands loose at his sides, that patient smile still in place.

“You can’t stay in the sun forever,” he said. “And I’ve got nothing but time.”

I got in my car and drove. I’m writing this from a motel sixty miles away, the curtains wide open, the desert sun bleaching the parking lot white. I haven’t slept. I don’t know what to do. I can’t go to the police—what would I say? My father is an immortal shadow-thing who’s been breeding a family line for a century? They’d lock me up.

But here’s the thing that scares me most. The thing I can’t stop thinking about.

In the 1941 portrait, my great-grandmother is crying. My grandfather looks hollowed out. And standing just behind them, barely visible in the dark of the room, is another face. A child’s face, half-hidden behind a doorframe. A little girl, maybe five or six, with my grandmother’s eyes and my mother’s chin and the exact same crooked left earlobe I see every time I look in a mirror.

I was in the photo before I was ever born. He’s been waiting for me my whole life. And he’s right. I can’t stay in the sun forever. The sun always sets. And when it does, he’ll be there. He’ll always be there. He’s my dad, after all. And a father never leaves his daughter.
