**Content Warning: depression, alcoholism, murder, blood, hallucinations, severe self-neglect, starvation, dehydration, and death.**  
  
I am happy.

I am the father I never had. Everyone loves me. My children are hugging me, thanking me for the presents I gave them. My son has his arms wrapped around my waist, while my daughter stands on my feet, laughing as I try to walk with her.

I cannot hold back my tears. It has been too long since I felt something like this. I cannot even remember how I got here, but it does not matter.

My wife is coming through the doorway, holding a birthday cake. Candles tremble above the white icing. She is singing, and the children join her.

I know the song. I know her voice.

So why can’t I see her face?

I am looking directly at her, but there is only a blurry mess above her neck. Skin without features. A painting ruined by water. Come to think of it, I cannot see the children’s faces either. Only their hair. My son has short brown curls. My daughter wears two red ribbons.

Where am I? How did I get here?

The house begins to shake. The candles stretch into long white lines, and the wallpaper peels away from the walls in wet strips. Something deep inside my chest twists the wrong way.

I don’t have a son.

I don’t have a wife.

I don’t have a daughter.

The singing stops. They are staring at me.

Their faces are no longer blurry. There is nothing there. No eyes, no mouths, just deep, piercing holes pushed into soft skin.

My blood begins to boil. I cannot move. Why am I sitting in this chair? Why can’t I scream?

God, please help me.

Please—

BEEP. BEEP. BEEP. BEEP.

Sheets. Pillow. Bed.

The black stain on the wall.

I take a few deep breaths. It is my bed, my sheets, my skin. I can move. I remember.

It was a dream. A nightmare, perhaps.

I should get up, but the bed is too warm. The blanket weighs a thousand tons, and my legs feel as though somebody filled the bones with sand.

I want to move. I really want to.

Why am I lying to myself?

I have barely left this bed in years. I eat only when the pain in my stomach becomes stronger than the exhaustion. I piss into bottles when walking to the bathroom feels impossible. I shit only when my body gives me no other choice.

The world lost its colours a long time ago. The bed is my only friend.

People always tell you the same things. Go outside. Get some fresh air. You are still young. Think positively.

Please, pile it on. I know how difficult it must be for you to look at someone who is sad.

When you lose somebody, people allow you to fall apart. When a doctor gives you a terrifying diagnosis, people understand why you crumble into dust.

Do you know how hard it is to be sad for no reason?

Everything is fine. Everyone is happy. And you cannot even look at them.

Jealousy overcomes every instinct to be polite.

I want my home. I want my children. I want the wife from my dreams. I would do anything for them.

That is another lie.

I would do nothing. I do not have the will.

Every movement feels like a rusty machine running on reserve power. Like walking underwater while pretending the pressure is not crushing your lungs.

I was not always like this, you know. I had dreams. I wanted to sing. I wanted to dance. I imagined standing beneath stage lights while people shouted my name.

But those dreams were lies too. Things I invented to impress people. Personalities I wore so somebody might find me interesting enough to love.

I am seventy-three years old, and I still have no idea who I am. Every step feels like broken glass.

I was not always like this.

I—

I wasn’t.

The black stain on the wall is staring at me.

It is roughly the size of a human head, darker around the edges, almost grey in the centre. Sometimes I can convince myself it is only mould.

But mould does not bleed through seven layers of paint. Mould does not return after you cut out the plaster. Mould does not change shape while you sleep.

I tried to forget what happened, but that fucking wall reminds me every day.

I had to do it. I had no choice. I would have died.

Maybe I should have.

Maybe dying would have been better than becoming a rotting corpse held together by a single thread.

I cannot stand the looks anymore. The pity. Another old man left to die without purpose.

Well, I am going to do something about it.

I am going to clean that shit stain off my wall if it is the last thing I do.

I throw the blanket aside. It takes nearly five minutes to sit up. My head spins, sweat rolls down my back, and the room tilts. For a moment, I think I am inside the dream again.

But the children are gone. The faceless wife is gone.

Only the stain remains.

“Fuck you,” I tell it.

My voice sounds like paper tearing.

The cupboard beneath the sink contains three different cleaning sprays, a bottle of bleach and a stiff wire brush. I bought them months ago, during another brief moment of courage.

The bleach burns my nose. I scrub until my shoulder screams. Grey water runs down the wallpaper, but the stain does not change.

I scrub harder.

Tiny pieces of paint collect beneath my fingernails. My knuckles crack against the wall. Blood runs across my fingers and mixes with the bleach.

The stain seems darker now.

Wider.

Almost pleased.

Behind me, somebody laughs.

A child.

I turn so quickly that my knees give out. There is nobody in the room.

I lie on the floor, trying to breathe. The laughter comes again, but not from behind me.

From inside the wall.

I crawl away from it.

“No.”

The stain stretches downward, just a little, like a drop of black liquid running against gravity.

I leave the house.

It is the first time I have gone outside in eight days.

The sun hurts my eyes. Birds sing from the roofs. Flowers hang from every window. Somebody has painted the bench across the road bright yellow.

People call this the happiest town in the country. They are proud of it. A newspaper wrote about us once. Low crime, high employment, strong community. Families stay here for generations. People smile at strangers. Children still play outside. Couples hold hands in the street.

I hate all of them.

The hardware shop is four streets away. I need stronger paint, something made for water damage. Maybe concrete sealant. Maybe a steel plate.

I do not know.

I only need that stain gone.

At the first crossing, I see the little girl from my dream.

She is standing beside her mother, holding a silver birthday balloon. Two red ribbons are tied into her hair.

My feet stop moving.

The girl turns toward me. For one horrible second, I expect to see two empty holes where her eyes should be.

Instead, she smiles.

“Hello,” she says.

Her mother pulls her closer.

I walk away without answering.

Inside the shop, a woman is examining wallpaper samples. She has my wife’s hair.

Not my wife.

The woman from the dream.

Long, dark curls reach her shoulders. She lifts one of the samples and turns toward the light. I cannot see her face from this angle.

My chest tightens.

I move past her.

A man stands near the paint counter with his young son. He is wearing a faded blue shirt with a white bird printed across the front.

The same shirt I am wearing.

I chose it this morning because I remembered it from the dream.

No.

That is not true.

I chose it without thinking.

Didn’t I?

The man kneels and helps his son open a packet of sweets. The child puts one into his father’s mouth, and both of them laugh.

I remember that laugh. I remember the sweet melting on my tongue. I remember kneeling on this exact floor.

But I have never seen either of them before.

The father notices me staring.

“Everything all right?”

I back away.

On the way home, I see pieces of the dream everywhere. The cake in a bakery window. The yellow car parked outside the church. A green bicycle leaning against a fence. A dog sleeping beneath a café table.

Small things. Meaningless things.

But I remember touching them. I remember owning them. I remember loving them.

That night, I do not want to sleep.

I sit in the kitchen with every light turned on. At eleven, I begin drinking. At midnight, I open the second bottle.

The alcohol makes the room softer. It smooths the sharp edges. My body becomes warm and pleasantly distant.

I watch the black stain. It has grown again. Now it reaches from the height of my shoulders almost to the floor.

Its shape reminds me of a woman sitting with her back against the wall.

I pour another glass.

“You weren’t supposed to be there,” I say.

The stain listens.

“I was hungry.”

Silence.

“It was winter.”

The refrigerator hums.

“I would have frozen.”

Something scratches inside the wall.

“I only wanted the money.”

The stain pulses.

Just once.

Like a heartbeat.

I throw the glass at it. The glass shatters, but the wall makes no sound.

For a moment, I see her face inside the darkness. Old. Blood running from her hairline. One eye swollen shut. Her mouth moving around broken teeth.

You will know happiness.

That was what she said.

Not a curse. Not at first. It sounded like forgiveness.

You will know more happiness than any man who ever lived.

I had laughed at her.

I remember that now.

I stood above her with the iron poker in my hand while her blood soaked into the wallpaper.

“I had no choice,” I whispered.

She smiled.

But none of it will ever belong to you.

I wake inside another life.

I am twelve years old, running through sprinklers with my brother. Then I am twenty-six, standing beside a hospital bed while a nurse places my newborn daughter in my arms.

Then I am forty, dancing badly with my wife in the kitchen. Then I am eighty-five, surrounded by grandchildren who complain that I tell the same stories too often.

I live decades between midnight and morning.

Every touch feels real. Every smell. Every emotion.

I love people whose names I do not know. I watch them grow. I watch them forgive me. I watch them bury me.

Then the alarm begins.

BEEP. BEEP. BEEP. BEEP.

I wake alone.

The emptiness is no longer sadness. It is physical. An organ has been torn out of my body.

I scream until my throat bleeds.

After that, I start going into town every day. I search for them. The people whose lives I steal at night.

Sometimes I find them.

The woman with dark curls is called Sarah. She works at the library. Her husband brings her lunch every Wednesday.

The little girl with red ribbons is named Mia. She turned seven last week.

The man in the blue shirt is Daniel. He coaches his son’s football team.

They do not know me.

But I know how Sarah looks when she wakes in the morning. I know Mia is afraid of thunderstorms. I know Daniel cried in the bathroom after his father died because he did not want his son to see him.

I know their happiest moments.

Sometimes I know things that have not happened yet.

Sarah will become pregnant in October. Mia will get a yellow bicycle for Christmas. Daniel’s son will break his arm falling from an apple tree, and Daniel will carry him two miles because the car will not start.

I begin following them.

Not because I want to hurt them.

I only want to be close.

I sit outside the library and watch Sarah leave with her husband. I stand across the road from Mia’s house when her father hangs decorations for her birthday. I watch Daniel teach his son how to ride a bicycle.

They are happy.

Everyone here is happy.

Except me.

At home, the stain continues to grow.

I paint over it with stain blocker. It returns before the paint dries.

I glue wallpaper across it. The paper becomes transparent.

I nail wooden boards over the wall. By morning, the stain appears on the wood.

I cover it with a mirror. The darkness shows through my reflection.

In the mirror, I look dead.

Behind me, the room is always full of people. Children chasing one another. Couples dancing. Families eating dinner.

When I turn around, they disappear.

So I drink.

At first, I drink to avoid dreaming. Then I discover that blacking out makes the dreams longer.

Clearer.

One bottle gives me an evening. Two bottles give me a year. Three give me a lifetime.

The waking hours become the nightmare.

The dreams become home.

I stop eating again. Food only makes it harder to drink.

I stop going outside. I already know what is there. Happy people wearing the clothes from my dreams. Children carrying my balloons. Women with my wife’s hair. Fathers with my shirts.

The stain spreads across the wall, onto the ceiling and beneath the bed. The room slowly disappears inside it.

Sometimes I press my hand against the darkness.

It feels warm.

On the other side, my children are waiting.

I can hear them.

“Dad?”

My daughter’s voice.

“Are you coming?”

“Yes,” I tell her.

I drink directly from the bottle. My hands shake so badly that half of it runs down my chin.

The room begins to fade.

My wife enters through the doorway, carrying a birthday cake.

This time, I can almost see her face.

“You’re late,” she says.

“I know.”

The children run toward me. Their bodies pass through the black stain as if it is an open door.

I feel their arms around me. Their weight. Their warmth.

My son smells like grass and soap. My daughter presses her cheek against my chest.

“We missed you.”

“I missed you too.”

Behind them, another room waits. A home filled with photographs. Birthdays. Christmas mornings. School plays. Arguments forgiven before bedtime.

Every stupid, ordinary thing I threw away before I ever had the chance to own it.

The alarm begins somewhere far away.

BEEP. BEEP. BEEP. BEEP.

The walls tremble.

My wife looks afraid.

“You have to wake up,” she says.

“No.”

“You always wake up.”

“Not this time.”

The alarm grows louder. My body is trying to pull me back. Back to the rotten bed, the empty bottles, the cold room and the black stain.

Back to the truth.

My daughter takes my hand. Her fingers are tiny and real.

“Stay,” she says.

So I do.

Days pass. Maybe weeks. Time works differently here.

I teach my son to shave. I walk my daughter down the aisle. I dance with my wife beneath strings of golden lights.

I become the father I never had.

I become a man people love.

Sometimes I hear pounding from far away. A door being forced open. Voices calling my name.

But they are so distant.

They belong to somebody else.

In another world, neighbours find an old man lying beneath a wall covered in black mould. They say he has been dead for days.

No food in his stomach. Almost no water in his body. Empty bottles surrounding the bed.

They cover their noses and speak quietly about depression, loneliness and the tragedy of growing old.

Nobody notices that the black stain has vanished.

Nobody notices the smile on my face.

My family is singing. My wife places the birthday cake in front of me.

For the first time, I can see all their faces.

The candles shine in their eyes.

“Make a wish,” my daughter says.

I already have everything I ever wanted.

So I close my eyes.

And I make sure I never open them again.
