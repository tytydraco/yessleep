I was twenty-three when all this happened, working at a tire shop outside a little town in the Blue Ridge Mountains of West Virginia. I'd been working there since graduating high school. And if things hadn't turned out the way they did, I probably would've stayed there until I was eighty.

There wasn't much to do around town. You could drink, smoke meth, or fuck up your life. I'd managed all three before I was old enough to rent a car.

My girlfriend, Katie, was twenty-one. We'd been together a little over two years. She was pretty, funny in a way that could catch you off guard, and stubborn as hell. If she thought somebody was talking down to her, she'd argue until they got tired of hearing it.

She worked at a gas station off a two-lane highway, one of those places with two pumps, a busted ice machine, and hot dogs that had probably been rolling since yesterday's breakfast.

That fall, she was taking bookkeeping classes two nights a week at the community college. She wanted a job with health insurance and regular hours, someplace where she didn't have to spend half her shift cleaning bathrooms and getting cussed out by customers whenever a gas pump stopped working. She did her homework in a yellow spiral notebook and got mad if I set a greasy plate on it.

She'd just started the first semester of her sophomore year when she found out she was pregnant.

She told me on a Tuesday night in August, sitting in my truck outside her parents' house. I'd had a rough day, and when she pulled that little white test out of her purse, I remember feeling angry before I felt anything else.

"Are you sure?" I asked.

She gave me a look.

"There's two lines, Travis."

"Yeah, I can see that. I'm asking if you're sure. A test can be wrong."

"I took three of them."

I stared through the windshield.

"Fuck."

She started crying.

I should've hugged her or told her we'd figure something out. Instead, I thought about the money I'd saved for a new guitar, the fishing trip I'd planned with my cousin, and the fact that I was still living in my dad's old trailer.

"Aren’t you gonna say anything?" she asked.

"What do you want me to say?"

"I don't know… maybe that you'll stand by me?"

I told her I wasn't going anywhere.

I meant it at the time. Or at least I wanted to mean it.

A week later, I found her doing homework at the kitchen table when I got home from work. I asked if she planned to quit her classes after the baby came.

"I'm having a baby, Travis. I'm not getting a lobotomy."

She said it without looking up. I laughed, and she smiled a little, and for a minute things felt almost normal again.

Then her parents found out.

They were old-fashioned Baptists who didn’t believe in abortion. Her father sat me down at the kitchen table and told me a man who was old enough to make a baby was old enough to take responsibility for one. He didn't care whether we married in a church, a courthouse, or somebody's backyard, but we were getting married before that child was born.

Katie's mother started looking at dresses. My own mother sided with them.

"That girl's carrying your baby, Travis. You need to do right by her," my mom said.

I tried to explain that getting married because of a pregnancy was a terrible idea.

She said I should've thought about that before I took my pants off.

By October, we'd set a date for the first Saturday in December. I hadn't bought a ring. Katie kept sending pictures of ones she liked, and I kept saying I was waiting for my next paycheck.

The truth was, I didn't want a wedding. I didn't want a kid. And somewhere along the way, I'd realized I didn't want Katie either.

I cared about her. I didn't wish anything bad on her. I just didn't want the life we were putting together.

But I was too much of a coward to tell her.

I suppose that's important to understand before I tell you about the night she called me at work.

---

It was a Thursday, a little after four in the afternoon.

I'd spent most of the day replacing tires on a landscaping company's trucks. I was finishing the last one when my phone started buzzing in my pocket.

Katie.

I let it ring.

She called again before I'd put the lug wrench down.

My boss, Earl, glanced over from the other side of the garage.

"You gonna get that?"

"Probably Katie."

"Well, answer it. Might be something important."

I wiped my hands on a rag and picked up.

"Hey."

"Travis? Are you still at work?"

She sounded nervous.

"Yeah. What's up?"

"Can you come get me?"

"You're not off till seven."

"I know. I called Linda. She said she'd come close for me if I needed her."

That should've told me something. Katie hated asking Linda to cover for her. She'd once worked an entire shift with a fever just to avoid hearing about it afterward.

"What's wrong?"

A pause.

"There's somebody out back," Katie said.

I moved toward the open garage door, away from Earl.

"What do you mean?"

"I keep hearing something near the dumpster. I saw them move behind the building."

"Probably a raccoon."

"It's not a raccoon."

"Did you actually see somebody?"

"Not really. But there's something on the roof now."

I laughed a little.

"On the roof?"

"I can hear it walking."

"How the hell would somebody get up there?"

"I don't know… but they’re up there."

For a few seconds she said nothing. I could hear the hum of the store's refrigerators through the phone.

Then came a dull thump.

"Did you hear that?" she whispered.

I had, but it could've been anything.

Katie didn't like working alone out there. The station was isolated, and a couple of guys had bothered her before. I'd once had to go down and tell some drunk asshole to leave her alone. I figured this was more of the same.

"You locked the doors?"

"Both of them."

"Then stay inside. Call the cops if you think somebody's hanging around."

"I did think about it, but I haven't seen anybody's face. They're gonna think I'm crazy."

"Well, what do you want me to do?"

"Come get me. Please."

Her voice broke on the last word.

I looked back at Earl, who was already putting his tools away.

"All right. Let me see if I can leave."

"Thank you."

"I'll be there as soon as I can."

"Love y—"

I hung up before she could finish the thought.

Earl let me go. We were about done for the day anyway.

---

The drive should've taken ten minutes. I'd gone about five when Katie called again.

I answered through the truck's Bluetooth.

"Hey. I'm on my way," I said.

"TRAVIS!"

She screamed so loud I nearly swerved onto the shoulder.

"What's happening?"

"IT'S INSIDE!"

"What's inside?"

"THAT THING! IT GOT IN!"

There was a thud behind her, hard enough to distort the sound through the speakers.

"Katie, listen to me. Did you call the cops?"

"YES! THEY SAID SOMEBODY'S COMING!"

Another impact. Glass broke.

"Get in the back room. Lock the door," I told her.

"Travis, please!"

"I'm almost there. Go!"

I heard her running. Something rattled, then fell. She went quiet, but the call was still connected.

"Katie?"

A scraping noise came through the speakers. It lasted several seconds.

Then three slow knocks.

"Oh, Jesus," she whispered.

"What?"

"It's down the aisle."

I pressed harder on the gas.

"Can you see who it is?"

She didn't answer.

"Katie?"

I heard her breathing. Shallow, fast breaths, like she was trying not to make any noise.

"It's looking at me," Katie said. "It's too big, Travis."

"What?"

"It's too big to be a person."

A low, rattling sound rolled through my speakers. I couldn't tell whether it was coming from the thing or the building itself.

Katie screamed.

Footsteps. A crash. Then her voice, farther from the phone.

"Travis, it's got—"

The line went dead.

I called her back immediately.

Four rings. Voicemail.

I tried again.

Nothing.

I threw the phone onto the passenger seat and pushed the truck as fast as I dared. I still wasn't thinking about anything supernatural. I pictured some tweaker breaking into the store. Somebody desperate enough to rob a register and hurt the girl behind it.

There was a tire iron behind my seat.

If somebody was hurting Katie, I was going to beat the shit out of them.

---

When I turned into the station, the front door was lying beside the ice machine.

Glass covered the sidewalk and was scattered halfway into the lot. One of the big windows had been smashed inward. Inside, a display rack lay on its side, and merchandise was strewn across the floor.

I pulled up beside the pumps.

From my truck, I couldn't see Katie anywhere.

As I was reaching for the door handle, something struck the edge of the roof of the station, sending a piece of metal clattering to the ground.
I looked up.

A black shape rose behind the roofline, disappeared, then rose again. At first I thought a piece of roofing had come loose in the wind.

Then it spread its wings.

They were enormous, easily fifteen feet across, with long feathers that bent at the tips. Beneath them hung a narrow body, its head sunk low between its shoulders. The legs were too long, bent wrong at the joints, and ended in black, scaly feet.

Two dull red eyes turned toward the parking lot.

Something swung beneath it.

A pair of kicking legs. A blue shirt.

Katie.

The creature had her by the shoulders, its talons buried in her upper arms. She was twisting beneath it, trying to pull free.

One of her sneakers came off.

It hit the pavement beside the ice machine and bounced twice.
Then she saw my truck.

"TRAVIS!"

The creature beat its wings.

Every light on my dashboard flashed. The radio blared, skipping between stations, and the headlights went dark. Something popped behind the dash. The engine died.

Outside, the canopy lights flickered. The digital numbers on the pumps flashed white, then vanished.

I grabbed the tire iron, kicked my door open, and ran.

"KATIE!"

The creature turned its head.

Its face looked almost human in shape, except for the long, gray beak that opened, revealing rows of tiny, needle-like teeth along the inside. A hollow, rattling sound came out of it, low enough that I felt it in my chest.

It began to climb.

"HELP ME!" Katie screamed.

Blood ran down her arms and dripped from her elbows. The thing was struggling to gain height with her. One wing caught the edge of the canopy and tore away a strip of aluminum.

It veered sideways over an old red pickup parked beside the building.
I ran after it and jumped onto the hood.

"KATIE! REACH DOWN!"

She tried. Her fingers came nowhere near mine.

I swung the tire iron at the creature's leg.

The blow landed hard enough for me to feel the impact in my bones. It jerked sideways, and Katie screamed as the claws pulled against her shoulders.

I raised the iron again, but a wing struck my forearm and knocked it out of my hand. I heard the iron clatter across the pavement.

The creature lurched upward.

I caught one of Katie's ankles before it could lift her clear of the truck. Then I got both hands around her legs and pulled.

The sudden weight dragged the creature down. My boots slipped on the hood, and I slammed one knee against the metal. Above me, the wings hammered the air, throwing dust and broken glass across the lot.

"I've got you!"

I hauled her down until one foot scraped the hood.

She tried to stand. The creature wouldn't let her. One talon had punched straight through the muscle of her shoulder. When I pulled, the wound opened farther.

"Stop!" she cried. "It hurts!"

I eased up just enough for her to straighten. She reached for my shirt.

For a second, we were close enough to see each other's faces.

"Please, Travis. Get it off me."

The creature hung over her, its feathers black and matted, pale gray skin moving beneath them as it breathed. It smelled like rotten meat and wet feathers.

I tried to get an arm around her waist.

A wing struck me across the chest.

I fell backward onto the hood. My grip broke, and Katie was pulled out of reach.

She screamed my name.

I scrambled up the windshield. The glass cracked beneath my boot. I lunged and caught her calves again, just before her feet disappeared past the edge of the canopy.

This time the creature lifted me with her.

For one sickening moment, I was hanging over the pavement. My weight dragged it lower, and I got a boot back onto the hood, then the other. My shoulders burned. I could barely feel my hands.

Katie bent toward me and caught my collar in both fists.

"Don't let go," Katie said.

"I won't."

She twisted the fabric so tightly it pulled against my throat.

"I don't want to die."

"I know. Just hold on."

I held her legs and tried to keep my footing. The hood shook under us. The creature couldn't lift us both, but I couldn't pull her free without tearing her shoulders apart.

I looked toward the highway to see if there was anyone who could help us.

Nothing.

No police. No passing cars.

Katie's jeans were slipping through my hands. She was still gripping my shirt, still begging me not to let go.

I wondered how long I could hold on.

And then, almost without meaning to, I wondered what would happen if I didn't.

The thought made me sick.

But I couldn't shake it.

If I’d just let go, there’d be no wedding. No baby. No more worrying about how I was going to support a family on twelve dollars an hour.

All the things I'd been dreading for months would be gone.

"Travis!"

I looked up.

Her face was wet with tears and blood.

This was the girl I'd spent two years with. The girl I thought I loved. The girl who was carrying my child.

And there I was, thinking about how much easier my life would be without her.

I should've been ashamed.

Instead, I felt something dangerously close to relief.

Above her, the creature stopped making that rattling sound.

Its red eyes were fixed on me.

I loosened my grip.

Katie's legs slid upward through my hands.

"Travis! Hold on!"

I let go of her.

But she was still holding on to me.

Her weight pulled me forward. Both her fists were twisted into my collar, the fabric cutting into the back of my neck. I could feel the creature lifting her, one slow inch at a time.

"Help me!"

I reached up and took hold of her right wrist.

For a second, I just held it.

Then I started pulling her fingers away from my shirt.

She stared at me.

"No. Travis, what are you doing?"

I peeled one finger loose, then another.

She grabbed my wrist. Her nails dug into my skin.

Her left hand was still knotted in my collar.

"Please! Please don't!"

I had to use both hands to open that fist.

She watched me the whole time.

"Travis, no!"

"I'm sorry, Katie," I mouthed.

I pulled her last finger free.

The creature lifted her away.

Katie screamed so loudly I heard her over the beating wings.

"TRAAAVIS!"

I stood on the hood of the pickup, my hands at my sides.

She was kicking, reaching for anything she could grab. As the creature passed the edge of the canopy, she caught the gutter with one hand.

For a second it held.

Then the creature pulled.

The gutter tore away from the roof, and she went up with it.

I watched it carry her toward the woods. She was still reaching back at me.

She screamed my name once more above the trees.

Then she was gone.

I listened until I couldn't hear her anymore.

After that, I climbed down from the truck.

My driver's door was still open. There was blood on my shirt, and both my palms were scraped raw. Four fresh scratches ran along my wrist where Katie had tried to hold on.

I kept looking back up at the sky, terrified that the thing would come back for me too.

I sat on the curb and started crying.

---

The first police officer arrived at 5:30.

I know because it's in the report.

Bits of what happened afterward are missing. Apparently I'd crossed the road to a farmhouse and pounded on the door. The man who lived there, a retired schoolteacher named Mr. Bowers, said I was covered in blood and yelling about a giant bird.

By the time the deputies got there, the lights at the station were working again.

They found the front door torn from its hinges, the windows smashed, and blood across the sidewalk.

But there was no sign of Katie, and nothing I could tell them seemed to make any sense.

The security cameras had stopped recording right around the time I arrived. The last footage showed Katie running through the store and looking over her shoulder. Something large passed the broken front window.

Then the picture turned white.

They took me down to the station.

A state trooper named Hensley did most of the questioning. He was probably in his forties, with a buzz cut and a face that looked permanently sunburned.

He set a paper cup of coffee in front of me.

"All right, sir. Start from when you left work."

I told him everything.

Almost everything.

When I described the creature lifting Katie off the ground, he stopped writing.

"How big was it?"

"Fifteen feet across, maybe."

"And you're telling me it picked up a grown woman?"

"Yeah."

"What kind of bird does that?"

"It wasn't a bird."

He studied me for a while, then made another note.

"You tried to stop it?"

"I grabbed her legs."

"And then?" Hensley asked.

"It pulled her up."

"You couldn't hold on?"

I shook my head.

"She slipped out of my hands."

Hensley's eyes moved to my wrist.

"Those scratches. She do that?"

"She was grabbing at me."

"But she slipped?"

"Yes."

He let the silence sit between us.

Then he asked if Katie and I had been having problems.

I told him we'd argued about money, but nothing serious.

He asked whether I'd ever thought about leaving her.

I said no.

He asked if I'd killed her.

I started crying again.

"That thing took her," I said. "I tried to save her."

They questioned me several more times over the next few weeks. They searched my truck, my trailer, and the woods around the station. Both gas pumps had fried circuit boards, and the security system was ruined. There were enormous scratches in the asphalt that nobody could explain.

Katie's mom told the police about the pregnancy and how I'd been looking for a way out of getting married.

That made me the prime suspect in her disappearance for a while. But they didn't have a body, and they couldn't prove I'd hurt her.

For a while, practically everybody in town thought I'd murdered her.
Somebody spray-painted KILLER on my trailer. Earl kept me on at the shop, but customers stared. I stopped going out unless I had to.

---

They found Katie in March.

Five months after she was taken.

Two brothers hunting for shed antlers spotted something near the top of an old pine tree, several miles north of the gas station.

It was a nest.

Or at least that's what the hunters called it.

Katie's remains were inside.

The state police closed the hillside for three days. A wildlife officer came in from out of town. Evidence bags were lowered on ropes. The local paper printed a photograph of the tree and said investigators were considering an animal attack, though nobody could say what animal had built the nest.

The paper claimed it was nearly twelve feet across and rested between three heavy branches more than sixty feet above the ground. The police had to bring in climbers to reach it.

Hensley came to my trailer and told me they'd identified her from dental records.

The next morning, he brought me into the same interview room he’d interrogated me in and opened a folder on the table.

"I want you to look at something."

He laid out six photographs.

The first showed the nest from the ground. It looked like a huge black knot tangled in the treetop.

In the second, I could see what it was made of: branches, old fence boards, plastic, insulation, strips of metal roofing. Bones lay among them. Deer skulls, ribs, little piles of vertebrae. There were shoes and scraps of clothing too.

The third showed Katie.

Her skull lay on its side near the trunk, with a little dirty-blonde hair still attached. Her blue work blouse had been torn into strips and woven through the branches. One skeletal arm stretched toward the edge of the nest.

I saw marks in the bark near her hand.

Hensley slid the fourth picture closer.

Fingernails were embedded in the wood. Some had been torn off completely. I could still see the pink polish she'd gotten the week before she disappeared.

I pushed that picture away.

"Keep looking," Hensley said.

The fifth showed her ribs. Deep gouges ran through the bones, and several had been broken open. There were smaller marks mixed among the larger ones. Marks that looked like teeth.

The sixth picture didn't make sense at first.

Several curved white pieces lay under the branches, surrounded by straw and feathers. I thought they were broken pottery until I noticed their smooth surfaces and jagged edges.

Eggshells.

At least three. One fragment was nearly as long as a man's forearm. Another shell had split almost evenly down the middle.

Whatever had been inside was gone.

Near them lay a scatter of very small bones.

I looked at Hensley.

"What are those?" I asked.

He didn't answer right away.

"The lab believes they're from the baby," he said.

I looked back at the eggshells.

I thought about how hard the creature had fought to keep her. How desperately it had tried to get her into the air.

And about the marks on her ribs. The big ones and the little ones.

I looked at the picture of her outstretched arm.

The fingernails in the wood.

She'd been alive up there.

And she'd had company.

I pushed the photographs across the table.

Hensley watched me.

"Is there anything you want to change about your statement?" he asked.

I told him there wasn't.

I asked if she'd suffered.

He looked at me for a long time.

"I'm sorry, Travis."

---

The police never officially solved Katie's death. The nest and the remains made it hard to argue I'd killed her and hidden the body. That didn't mean anybody could explain what had happened. The papers kept calling it the “Mothman.”

A few reporters asked for interviews. I told them to fuck off.

Katie's family held a funeral in April.

I went.

Her mom hugged me outside the church. She looked like she'd aged twenty years.

"She loved you so much," she said.

I couldn't look her in the eye.

Her dad put a hand on my shoulder.

"I know you did your best, son."

I nodded.

That's all I could do.

---

I moved away that summer.

I got a job at an auto dealership in the city. The money was better, but I was fired after about a year for arguing with the service manager. I've bounced between jobs ever since.

There were women, too. A few serious relationships, though none lasted long. I lived with a nurse named Rachel for almost a year before she found out I was sleeping with a bartender downtown.

She threw my clothes onto the lawn and kicked me out.

I couldn't really blame her.

I'm thirty now.

I work odd end jobs and rent a room from a buddy of mine. I've got a truck I can barely afford, a drinking habit that occasionally gets out of hand, and a girlfriend who's threatening to leave me if I don't get my shit together.

She's probably going to leave.

Sometimes I think about what my life would've been like if Katie had survived.

We'd have a kid in first grade by now.

Maybe another one.

I doubt we'd still be together. Knowing myself, I probably would've screwed things up long before now. Katie would've gotten tired of the drinking, the money problems, the other women.

Maybe she'd have left me.

Maybe I'd have left her.

I suppose it doesn't matter.

I think about her sometimes, especially when I hear somebody say my name in a certain way.

The way Katie had drawn out the first syllable of my name when she begged me to save her.

*Traaavis.*

I can still hear it.

I've told a few girlfriends what happened to her. They always feel sorry for me.

Rachel once asked whether I thought I could've saved her.

I told her no.

She said there was nothing I could've done, and that I needed to stop blaming myself.

I didn't argue.

It's been seven years now. I don't have much to show for it, but I get by. I go fishing, drink with my friends, and generally do whatever I feel like doing.

Some things haven't changed.

I wish I could tell you I regret what I did every day.
