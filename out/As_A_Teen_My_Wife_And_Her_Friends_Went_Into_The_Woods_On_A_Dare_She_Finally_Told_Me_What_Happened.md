We all heard of at least one local legend growing up. Every place has them: haunted buildings, abandoned houses, spots in the woods where you'll see Bigfoot or an alien... When I was a kid, the neighbor's son convinced me that the house down the street was haunted by the ghost of a woman  (I realized he was kidding when it turned out my very-much-alive third grade teacher lived there).

While I've never completely bought every legend I've ever been told, I like to keep an open mind. Every story has some element of truth, after all, and some legends are specifically told to warn people against going to certain places.

After what Rachel told me, I'd be a fool not to believe that.

My wife and I share a lot of common interests, and one thing we love equally is autumn. The crisp air, hoodies, pumpkins, pumpkin spice, apple-picking, Halloween, debating whether or not *The Nightmare Before Christmas* is a Halloween or Christmas film... We love the whole season. There's just something about autumn that we both find peaceful.

One thing we disagree on, though, is the woods.

Our house sits on the edge of our small town. Near our town is a national park (I won't say which park for privacy reasons). It's not too close, but we get a lot of visiting tourists who stop by to buy supplies or rent a motel room before they go camping or hiking. I've never been the outdoorsy type: I find long walks boring, mosquito bites make me swell up, fishing grosses me out, and I prefer to bike ride on well-established roads. Still, even if I didn't like the outdoors, I've still always liked forests for their wild, untamed beauty.

Rachel thought different. She did not like the woods... In fact, she was afraid of them. Her therapist called it xylophobia, or severe anxiety from being in or too close to wooded areas. It was fairly minor, though: driving through a wooded stretch of road or seeing a forest on TV didn't frighten her. Being physically close to, or actually going into the woods, though, scared her to pieces. Especially at night.

I'd always wondered about where this fear came from, but I'd never asked out of respect for her. After all, if she could deal with my fear of heights, then I could deal with her fear of the forest.

The other day, though, she finally told me the story.

It'd been cloudy and gloomy all day, with the forecasters promising a thunderstorm by dinner time. Most people would take as an ominous start to the end of the work week. My wife and I were of the opposite opinion: our ideal autumn night is a stormy one spent watching our favorite movies while drinking hot drinks and listening to the rain and thunder. The fact that it was a Friday night just heightened our joy: we had no where to be the next day, and could stay up as late as we wanted.

We'd been working our way through our watchlist while the rain came down, drinking Rachel's "Fall Fiascoes": a combination of hot chocolate, pumpkin spice, and whiskey. After a Fall Fiasco each and a laughably bad horror movie, the question popped out.

I still don't know why I asked: my guess is that the Fall Fiasco made me feel brave/stupid enough to ask.

"Hey, babe, I've always wondered. Why do you hate the woods?"

All night, she'd been bright and cheery. But the second the words left my lips, she froze. Her face paled, and she seemed to shrink.

Seeing her so scared, I set my drink down and put an arm around her.

"Hon?"

She snapped out of it, shaking herself and taking a deep breath.

"I'm fine, I'm fine..."

"...I'm sorry if I said--"

"You're alright, you didn't know..."

She was silent for a moment before taking another deep breath.

"Maybe... If I tell you this, can you promise it'll stay between us?"

"Of course."

"And you won't make fun of me or say I'm making it up?"

I shook my head, though my interest in the story had picqued.

She took a deep breath, poured a little extra whiskey into her drink, took a swig, and sat back.

Rachel looked me in the eyes, and told the story.

\- - - -

It all happened back in middle school. I was thirteen and had just moved to town, which made eighth grade a little difficult. All the girls in school had been friends for years, so an outsider like me wasn't someone they readily trusted. Still, I managed to make a friend, Beth. Her sister Caroline, or Caro, was two years older, and the most popular sophmore at the high school. Beth and I knew that befriending her sister's friends was a surefire way to be popular when we started the ninth grade. So we did everything we could to try and get on her good side.

Despite some of Caro's friends thinking we were being a bit annoying for trying to hang out with the "cool kids" all the time, she always talked them into giving us a chance. Eventually, we became friends with the closest of her friends, who started to see us as surrogate little sisters. Still, despite being accepted into their group, Beth and I were still desperate to prove we were as cool as the others.

The night of Caro's birthday sleepover, we got our chance.

Birthday sleepovers were a tradition in our friend group, and each one went the same way: first dinner, cake, and presents, then taking over the family den to watch a bunch of stupid movies and do each others' nails. Finally, when we were sure the adults were asleep, we'd tell the scariest ghost stories we could think of.

The night of Caro's fifteenth birthday, her friend LaTanya told us about our town's scariest urban legend: the Deadly Ditch.

When she'd first said the name, I'd scoffed. "What's so scary about a ditch?"

LaTanya had grinned, as if waiting for me to ask. "Oh, it's not the ditch itself that's scary. It's what happened in it... and what lurks there now."

She held a flashlight under her chin, the light reflecting off her eyes and making her afro puffs seemingly glow.

"Back in the 1950s, a little yellow school bus was driving a group of kids to a science museum in the next town. Fifteen children and two teachers had taken the bus to spend the day there. They'd meant to be back at the school before sunset, but the bus got a flat tire shortly after they'd started the trip home. No one would let them use their phone, so the teachers couldn't tell the kids' parents that they'd be late. So, as soon as the bus' tire was changed, the two teachers decided to take a shortcut to get back faster.

"That shortcut went right through the Wind Sweep Woods, the forest that borders on the edge of town. The shortcut was a narrow, winding road through thick trees that make it hard to see what's coming at you. It's a hard enough road for a car to take, but a school bus has it even harder since it's much bigger and harder to steer. Still, the teachers were desperate to get home as soon as possible, and decided to take the risk.

"Thing is, the bus never made it home. Days went by, with the parents getting more and more worried. The police started searching the area for the missing children and teachers, but they found nothing. That is, until five of the missing children came out of the woods.

"All five of them were starving, dehydrated, and covered head to toe in dirt. They were taken directly to the hospital, where the police questioning them on what happened. All any of them would say was 'the deadly ditch'.

"The police went into the woods and found the school bus the group had taken. The front end was buried in the dirt, with the back of the bus sticking in the air at a forty-five degree angle. The two teachers and other ten children were nowhere to be found. When search-and-rescue dogs were brought out to try and track the missing people, the dogs went completely crazy, barking at the bus and peeing themselves in terror.

"The teachers and children were never found. The town's leaders tried to arrange for the bus to be moved from the ditch in the forest, but the thing is, no one wanted to go near those woods after what happened. So the bus remains there to this day, a terrifying reminder of what might've happened to the lost field trip."

She turned off the flashlight, looking pleased with herself. The older girls, having told the story before, gave her polite smiles. Beth and I, having never heard the story before, were trying to pretend like we weren't as frightened as we were.

"I heard that the story's been embellished over the years," Gabriela stated from where she was pouring herself another cup of soda. "The group actually got eaten by bears, the police just didn't want to tell the parents that."

"Bears would've left blood or at least remains behind," Caro pointed out. "Besides, I heard they were abducted by aliens. That's why the bus got stuck in the ditch like that."

"I heard that they got snatched by some serial killer who buried their bodies in the woods," LaTanya admitted. "Not sure if I believe that one, though. Even if five of the kids got away, it's hard to kill fifteen people all by yourself."

"You know what I think?" Beth stood up, trying to look braver than she clearly felt. "I think you three are just trying to scare Rachel and me. That never happened!"

"Yes, it did," Caro replied. "You can ask the school librarian, I think she still has the newspaper article about it."

"Not buying it," I stated. "I won't believe it unless I see it."

I knew I'd made a mistake as soon as I closed my mouth. Beth shot me a look that screamed "What are you doing?!" while the older girls all shared identical evil grins.

"You want to believe us?" Caro asked. "It's not that long of a walk from here--"

"We can be there and back in twenty minutes if we use my dad's truck," Gabriela interrupted.

"I thought you didn't have your learner's permit yet," LaTanya pointed out.

"I don't, but Dad lets me practice in the school parking lot. It's late, no one will see us... and I have the spare key he had made."

She pulled said car key from her purse to show off.

"Why does he have a spare car key?" Caro asked.

Gabriela rolled her eyes. "He has a phobia of locking his keys in his car and not being able to get a locksmith. Now, we doing this or not?"

I really, really didn't want to do it. But it was my idea (however unintentionally) so I was forced to go along or be labeled a cowardly hypocrite.

Next thing I knew, Caro had given her parents an excuse about running to the store for more nail polish, and we were at Gabriela's house, piling into the Hernandez family car: Beth got the passenger seat while Caro, LaTanya, and I took the back. Even though I knew we wouldn't be seen (the neighbors were asleep and Gabriela's parents were at a late movie), my heart was hammering. I didn't like this, any of it: stealing a car, sneaking out at night, going into the forest, NONE of it felt right.

But as silly as it sounds now, being alone all through high school scared me more. So I felt I had no choice but to grin and bear it.

The drive out of town and to the edge of Wind Sweep Woods took less than five minutes, and all too soon we were driving along an old road.

There was no doubt in my mind that it was the same shortcut from the story: it matched it perfectly. Just like LaTanya had said, the road was a winding path that kept banking directly in front of big trees, and made it next to impossible to see where you were going. Even with the car's high beams on, Gabriela had to drive at a snail's pace just to keep on the road.

It was at this point I noticed that Caro and LaTanya were starting to look as nervous as Beth and I felt. I realized that they probably hadn't realized that the place from the story actually existed. Seeing the same scary trees and pitch-black road as the poor kids and teachers did was clearly chipping away at their bravado from earlier.

Gabriela was better at hiding it, but I quickly realized that she was scared, too: her hands were gripping the steering wheel so tightly I was surprised there weren't cracks in it.

Suddenly, the car stopped short.

While Beth and I had remembered our seat belts, Caro and LaTanya had neglected theirs, and only had seconds to protect themselves from smashing their faces against the front seats.

"Gabby!" Caro complained.

Beth shushed them, and pointed forward with a trembling finger.

My heart stopped when I saw what was in front of us, illuminated by the car's headlights.

There it was: the school bus.

Just like the story said, it was sticking out of a ditch at a forty-five degree angle. Years of exposure to the elements had worn away the yellow-and-black paint, turning the entire vehicle a hideous shade of rusty brown. The glass that once lined the windows was all gone, only tiny shards of it clinging to life in the window sills. The headlights were both smashed, their remains barely handing on. Still, despite years of age and weathering, the letters on the side were still legible:

***Northlight Elementary School, Bus #130***

"Holy..." LaTanya started, her voice coming out in a whisper.

"I can't believe it," Caro whispered, "the legend is true..."

"Hey!" I whispered to her, my annoyance briefly overpowering my fear. "You said the librarian had evidence the whole story was true!"

Beth froze in her seat, but we didn't notice.

"Well," Caro admitted, looking a bit sheepish. "I kinda made that up so you wouldn't question it..."

"Guys?" Gabriela whispered, eyes locked on something ahead.

"You shouldn't lie just to make yourself look cooler," I retorted, ignoring the fact that I'd been lying all night about being scared.

"Guys?" LaTanya asked, her voice shaking as hard as she was.

We finally looked back at the bus.

My eyes went wide.

There was a... a light coming from the ditch. It was dim, like the light of a candle starting to burn out. But it was unmistakably light.

And, as we sat in a confused, frightened silence, a voice started coming from the ditch.

A child's voice.

"Hello?"

The voice sounded young, like a little boy, and very scared.

"What the...?" LaTanya whispered.

"Hello?" The voice called again from the ditch. "Is someone there? Please help, I'm very hungry!"

The voice kept calling to us, saying he was scared and very hungry. But the more I listened, the more... *wrong* it sounded. It reminded me a bit of when a parrot talks: it has a voice and it's saying words, but the voice doesn't sound human. The voice we heard then didn't sound right... almost like something trying to convince us it was a human.

"That..." Caro began, her voice barely a whisper. "That... That can't be one of the kids from the field trip, right?"

Gabriela seemed to be thinking what I was thinking, as she kept checking to make sure the car doors were locked.

"After almost forty years?" LaTonya whispered back, her eyes not leaving the ditch. "There's no way they'd still be a little kid."

"Can we go home?" Beth wasn't trying to hide her fear anymore: there was no point when everyone else was just as scared as she was.

"Gabby?" Caro asked, her voice pleading.

"Don't need to ask me twice," Gabriela responded as her hand moved towards the stick shift.

The second she touched it, the crap hit the fan.

The voice from the ditch let out a scream that no living creature on Earth could've hoped to copy. The best way I can describe it is some kind of demon's howl, or a sound you'd expect to hear only in the deepest pits of Hell.

Our hands all flew to our ears in a vague attempt to block out the horrific noise. It stopped as suddenly as it started... and gave way to a much more horrific sight.

Rising from the ditch were two massive stick-like arms, each ending with three thin fingers. The arms appeared to be made from tree roots, and were covered in dirt and forest floor debris. The fingers were each tipped with sharpened points, and the arms were at least ten feet long.

The voice came back, speaking once more. But what we heard wasn't the voice of a little boy. It wasn't even the voice of a human.

"HUNGRY!"

Caro and LaTanya screamed at Gabriela to drive, and she didn't need to be told twice. She threw the car in reverse, trying to put some distance between us and whatever that thing was. We flew back three feet before the back hit a tree trunk.

Now crying in fear, Gabriela put the car into drive and sped forward. Seconds later, one of the massive wooden hands slammed down right where the car had been.

"THE ROAD!" Caro screamed, pointing at the badly supported tarmac. Our driver didn't have to be told twice, and bee lined for it.

"Turn off the headlights!" Beth shrieked. "Maybe it won't see us!"

"I can't drive blind!" Gabriela shouted back. "I'll kill us!"

"HUNGRY!" The creature's arm once again slammed down where we'd been a moment before, using so much force that the back tires briefly lifted off the ground.

"Go, go, please go!" Caro sobbed.

"Wait!" LaTanya shouted. "Look!"

As we all looked out the rear window, we saw the arms retracting into the ditch.

"...Maybe it realized it couldn't reach us?" I hoped. "Maybe it's given up?"

The others agreed, thinking/hoping that this made sense and we were in the clear.

Beth was the first one to see how wrong of an idea that was.

Mere seconds after the arms disappeared, the sound of bending, protesting metal reached our ears. Hearts in our throats, we all looked.

The school bus was... swelling. It's sides were starting to puff out, like a balloon partially filled with air. I was the first to notice the roots that seemed to be entering through the shattered rear window...

Wooden limbs as thick around as trash cans exploded from under the bus' fenders. A long tendril exploded from the roof, snaking into the ditch and attaching to something we couldn't see.

Using it's legs, the bus monster removed its front from the ditch, and turned to face us. The grill split open, creating a massive mouth with wooden and metal spikes for teeth. Two golden orbs appeared in the windshield, looking just like a pair of glowing demonic eyes.

Gabriela had seen more than enough, and stomped on the gas pedal while the rest of us screamed.

As the car shot forward, the monster chased after us.

With all the banking turns and poor visibility, the trip out of the woods became one of the most harrowing drives of my life. While Gabriela miraculously avoided hitting any trees, she had to constantly swerve left or right not only to keep us from wrecking, but to dodge the oversized, grabbing hands of the bus monster.

Caro was crying as she screamed at Gabriela to go faster. LaTonya was searching the car for anything that might better our chances of escaping. Beth and I stared in horror at the thing that continued to barrel down on us. Gabriela was easily doing sixty, yet the creature was keeping up with ease.

Finally, LaTonya seemed to find something in her purse, and picked up a small metal rectangle from the floor.

"Gabby, turn right!"

"What?!"

She started opening the window. "Trust me!"

With a whimper, the driver did a sharp turn, putting LaTonya's window directly in front of the monster. One of the monster's arms started reaching for us.

The metal rectangle turned out to be a lighter, which she flicked open. Then she pulled out her can of hairspray and started spraying.

A stream of fire shot out of the window, engulfing the monster's hand. An unholy shriek echoed throughout the woods.

We all stared briefly in shock as the monster tried to douse it's flaming arm before our driver stomped on the gas, charging forward.

"For once I'm glad my dad smokes," Gabriela breathed.

With the monster behind us, we dared to think we might actually get away. Gabriela drove through the forest, following the road once more.

"You know how I was complaining about not getting that record player I wanted for my birthday?" Caro said after a while. "And how I hated that sweater Aunt Geraldine sent?"

"Yeah?" Beth answered.

"Doesn't seem like such a big deal anymore. In fact, I'm wearing that sweater all day tomorrow just so I never take anything for granted again. Including ugly birthday presents."

It wasn't that funny, but I laughed. I had the feeling if I didn't laugh, I'd start crying.

Finally, Gabriela started to slow the car down. I looked up, hoping to see the lights of the town.

Instead, I saw the ditch.

"What?" Beth asked, her voice small.

"I wasn't paying attention," Gabriela whispered in mounting horror. "I must've taken us in a circle while we were being chased..."

"Then," Caroline asked, her eyes widening. "Then where's the mons--?"

'HUNGRY!"

A massive hand, charred black and with smoke tendrils still rising from it, smashed through the car window, showering the three of us in the back seat with smoking hot wood chips and broken glass. If not for the glasses I wore back then, I might've gotten some in my eyes.

The monstrous hand grabbed Caro around her neck. She let out a scream unlike any I'd ever heard as she was dragged out of the car.

"CAROLINE!" Beth screamed.

Gabriela grabbed the lighter and hairspray from where LaTonya had dropped them on the floor, then jumped out of the car.

By then, we could hear unsettling, horrifying cracking noises coming from high above the car. It reminded me of the sound chicken bones make when snapped apart. Caro's screams of pain and terror filled the air.

"LaTonya!" Gabby shouted. "Take the wheel!"

The remaining sophmore, glass shared sticking out of her afro puffs, climbed from the back to the front, hopping in the seat.

As we watched in horrified silence, we saw Gabriela flick open the lighter and use the hairspray to set the monster's foot ablaze. It yowled in pain, it's shrieks joining Caro's. But when she tried to set another foot ablaze, the bus' grill started to move towards her.

The metal teeth snatched her up as fast as a dog grabbed a Milkbone. But the teeth didn't chew her up: they merely held her. Then, before our horrified eyes, the bus-wearing creature went back to the ditch, the bus' front (with poor Gabriela still trapped in the grill) sinking back into the dirt. The wood and vines all over the bus withdrew, returning back to the ditch. In seconds, the area looked just like it had when we'd first arrived.

The glow from the ditch was still there... but it was a little brighter now.

The three of us stared in shock, trying to process what we'd just seen.

For a solid minute, everything was perfectly quiet.

Then, from the ditch, came a voice. 

Caro's voice.

"Hello?"

LaTonya lost her nerve, threw the car in reverse and started driving us out of there. The shattered window and heavily dented door meant that we couldn't easily block out the noises behind us. The sounds of Caroline (or, rather, the thing using her voice) screaming for us to come back, to help her, she was so *hungry...*

The new driver (who didn't have her learner's permit but knew at least how to operate a car) drove through the woods like a mad woman, narrowly avoiding trees and barely staying on the road in her haste to get us back to civilization. Neither Beth nor I were willing to criticize LaTonya's driving skills: Beth was too horrified and sad at the fate of her sister while I was too shocked by what had just happened.

Finally, after what felt like an eternity, we made it back to Beth and Caroline's house. Her parents, who'd realized that our trip to get more nail polish had taken far longer than it should have, took one look at the damaged car and our terrified faces before calling the police.

We spent the night at the next town's hospital for observation (thankfully, minus some cuts and bruises we were fine) while a few officers talked to us about what had happened, namely where Caroline and Gabriela were. Through our tears, we told them the story.

The younger officer who interviewed us didn't believe a word we said. The older one, though, one who'd been on the force for forty five years, looked at us in horror. He then sent a message to the police department *insisting* that the search for the missing girls only take place during the day, and that no volunteers were to accompany them.

The next morning, while we were being discharged from the hospital, the police found the ditch. The school bus was exactly where we said it would be, though the ditch didn't glow in the daylight. Nor did whatever was in there seem as keen to attack during the day.

After finding two massive bloodstains in the dirt right next to the ditch (something I only learned years later), the rest of the police were far more willing to believe our story. The town council had an emergency meeting, which my parents attended. While the story of what we'd been through wasn't repeated (the mayor merely said that we'd had a terrifying ordeal in the woods), the rumor mill had already been going full force, meaning everyone knew some variant of the true story. It was decided that the best thing to do would be to declare the woods off limits, and to block off the road leading through Wind Sweep Woods.

\- - - -

I stared in shock and sadness, horrified that Rachel had gone through something so traumatic.

She took another long swig of her drink as she wrapped up the story.

"LaTonya, Beth, and I never spoke again: we couldn't. Every time we saw one another, we were reminded of that terrible night... and the friends we'd never see again. LaTonya ended up changing schools and taking summer school classes so she could get into college early. She got into Stanford, packed her bags, and never set foot in our town again. Beth's family ended up moving out entirely: the town had too many memories, they said. 

"I stayed in that town until I got my acceptance letter to college. Shortly after I moved into my dorm, my parents called and said they'd bought a house in a town just ten minutes from my campus: they said there was nothing keeping them in town now that I was gone, and they thought it'd be best to offer support... But I think that was only part of the reason."

"...What was the other reason?" I asked, not sure if I wanted to hear the answer.

"The other reason was that, the same summer I moved out, a group of visiting hikers decided to film an amateur found-footage horror movie in Wind Sweep Woods, using the urban legend of the Deadly Ditch as their premise. They disregarded everyone's warnings and went in... and never came out. Two weeks later, their camera was found."

She drained the rest of her mug before she continued.

"The police didn't show anyone outside the department the footage, but... But one of them told his wife what was on it, and she told everyone else. In the footage, there's a close up on the side of the bus. In it, you can see nineteen marks in the metal... Tally marks."

She started shaking so hard that she set the mug down. I sat a little closer to her, putting an arm around her to offer some comfort.

I can't tell you what my wife went through that fateful night in the forest, nor do I know what it was that hunted her and her friends that night.

What I can tell you, though, is that some legends are told not as silly stories to scare children, but as warnings.

And if you disregard those warnings for the sake of a cheap thrill... You might not live to tell it.
