By twelve-seventeen Sunday morning, Captain Reese had crossed out two of the three ways his men might reach Sam. The first ran beneath a section of floor that had already shifted under their weight. The second ended at the door where an officer had gone down screaming with his arm broken. Reese was drawing a line through that one when a medic came in to ask whether anyone had seen the missing stretcher straps. He answered her without looking up. Every few minutes somebody needed a key, a radio, a clean blanket, an answer. The room kept functioning, and Sam was still in the ground underneath it. I'd been standing over the table so long that the coffee I'd spilled on the plans had soaked through the cuff of my jacket.

The treatment schedule we'd found put his standing correction just after six. I watched the minute hand pass the twelve, then dragged the sheet closer and tried to make sense of a narrow passage marked beneath the refrigeration wing. An old drawing showed it ending short of the rooms where they'd seen him. The newer drawings didn't show it at all.

"There used to be a brine return under here," I told Reese. I traced the passage with one damp fingertip. "Forty inches across, maybe. The pipes would've run under the slab. If the old cover is still there, your men could reach the wall behind the treatment rooms without using that doorway."

Reese bent over the paper, his coffee-stained sleeve almost touching my hand. "And if the cover isn't still there?"

"Then there's nothing beneath them but a drop into a drain channel. We won't know until we look." I hated the answer as soon as I'd said it. It sounded exactly like what he'd spent the night telling me, and I'd been taking every sensible precaution as a personal refusal to help Sam. An engineer at the compressor pit reported that the floor sounded hollow, and Reese told him to put a camera through before anyone cut a hole big enough for a person.

He looked at me while he gave the order. "Your job is to tell us where the building lies to us, Cal. My job is to get everyone back out."

I'd already suggested cutting through a wall without noticing the load it was carrying. Reese had shown me the column above it, and for an awful few seconds all I could see was Sam buried under that much concrete because I'd wanted to save ten minutes. I picked up the flashlight an engineer handed me and held it on the spot he needed to examine. My arm trembled badly enough that he quietly asked for the light to be steadier.

I braced my elbow against a filing cabinet and tried again. On the far side of the room a radio crackled, and all of us looked up before the dispatcher finished saying it was an ambulance asking for directions. No one made a joke about it. A few minutes earlier a similar burst of static had brought word that the officer Mother injured was on his way to surgery.

Detective Vale arrived with a statement from the officer who'd seen the man beside Mother. He still couldn't identify the face, but he remembered two fingers bound together with white cloth on the man's left hand. Sam had broken those fingers helping me unload a sheet of plywood years ago. Even after the splint came off, one finger stuck out whenever he held a coffee cup. I told Vale, who wrote it down carefully, then folded the statement instead of giving me the confirmation I wanted.

"It's consistent with Sam," she said. "We're acting on that. We can't say it's certain yet."

"Did they see what he had on? A watch? His boots?" I heard myself asking as though a detail about his clothes might make it possible to pull him out. The pencil in my hand had snapped. I put both pieces down before Vale had to tell me to.

"They couldn't see his feet. Cal, if they open that room the wrong way, the person in it could get killed." I nodded, but I couldn't look at her. She waited until I did before going back to the radios.

I went outside before I said anything unfair to Vale. An ambulance sat with its rear doors open and a pair of muddy gloves on the step. A generator drowned out the medics' voices. Coldwater had supposedly been empty for seventeen years, yet the people underneath had lived in heated rooms with working lights while everybody drove past the locked gates.

I called Marisol from beside the fence. She answered quickly, but there was a pause before she spoke, as if she'd picked up the phone in the middle of doing something and couldn't remember what it was. I told her they were working on a different way into the room. She asked whether I'd seen Sam myself, and I had to tell her I hadn't.

"Lily asked me if he was still helping that person," she said. "That's what I told her yesterday. That somebody needed help and he couldn't leave."

I put my hand over my other ear to block the noise from the generator. "What did you tell her tonight?"

"That I didn't know when he'd be home." A cupboard door closed at her end. "She said if he missed breakfast he could make pancakes for supper. She saved him the last one. It's in a plastic container in the refrigerator, with a face made out of blueberries. I keep looking at it every time I open the door."

I could hear Marisol pull a drawer open and shut again, then the quiet scrape of a chair. She asked whether she ought to take the girls to her sister's house in the morning. I knew why she was asking, but neither of us said it. I told her she shouldn't have to manage everything alone. She said her sister had offered, then asked me not to promise the girls their father was coming home until I'd actually seen him walk out. After we hung up, I still had the phone pressed to my ear. A medic had to say excuse me twice before I moved away from the ambulance entrance.

Sam and I had worked together for eleven years, first for a contractor who thought safety rules were an insult. Once, a steel door slipped its track and nearly fell on me. Sam hauled me backward by my jacket and tore the collar clean off. I shouted at him because I'd scraped my arm. He shouted louder, then sat on a pallet shaking too badly to light a cigarette. He quit smoking two years later, but he still checked the tracks on every heavy door we worked around. I knew exactly what he'd say if he caught me rushing these men into that tunnel.

A little after one, they opened the compressor pit. The first cut filled the room with dust, and I had to turn my face into my sleeve. Then the bit broke through and a draft lifted the dust back toward us. It smelled of disinfectant. The camera showed an old brick channel, high enough to crawl in, with its refrigeration pipes stripped out and new cables fastened neatly along the wall. Somebody had laid rubber mats over the drain. I caught myself wondering whether Sam had crawled over those same mats, and how far a man's voice would carry through brick.

Twenty feet farther along, the lens found a panel almost hidden by conduit. The operator eased the probe into a narrow gap. Through it we heard the steady clank of a chain and saw bare feet on a platform, toes curling over a painted yellow stripe. Whoever stood there was holding very still. A bell sounded, the feet shuffled off, and another person took their place before the machine had fully settled.

A man's voice said, "Heels back. Start over." The second person's ankles were raw above the heel. They adjusted their stance, breathing audibly through the microphone, and the machine made a small electronic chirp. When the bell rang again they hurried away without being told, and the first person stepped back on. I was looking at a weighing station. I'd spent enough years around salvage scales to recognize the delay while a reading stabilized, but I'd never seen anybody made to stand on one barefoot.

One of the rescuers whispered, "What the hell is that?"

Reese withdrew the camera to wait for another team. Sam kept a scale like that in our shop for scrap metal. He'd repaired its display last winter and always shouted when we stood on it as a joke. The familiar electronic chirp coming from that room made me want to hear him complain about it.

The panel gave onto a narrow recess above the weighing room. After the officers cleared it, Reese had me look at a cracked support beside a steel conveyor frame. I knelt to check its anchors while he spoke to the entry team. A dull vibration traveled through the bolts every time the scale below took another person's weight. The support itself was sound, and I gave him the answer. I stayed on one knee a little longer because the room beyond the wall was quieter than I'd expected. There was no shouting, only people doing precisely what they were told.

Four monitors stood behind thick glass. Two showed corridors, one a bench where people waited with their hands folded, and another a row of yellow-marked weighing platforms. A barefoot young woman in a numbered gray shift stepped onto the nearest scale. A man in a white coat recorded her weight and sent her through a gate without looking at her face.

The next woman had a swollen hand. She tried speaking to the man, but he pointed at the scale. When she hesitated, he rang a bell and everyone on the bench sat up straight. She apologized, stepped onto the platform, and held her injured hand against her chest. He wrote down her weight and gave her a strip of paper. She read it and began to cry.

"Can we talk to them?" Vale asked.

An officer found a speaker beside the controls and said the police were upstairs, that help was coming, and that everyone should stay where they were. A young man on the bench looked toward the camera. The older woman folded the strip of paper until it was no bigger than a postage stamp and pushed it into her sleeve. The man in white went through a side door without hurrying. One of the people waiting lifted a hand, then glanced at the bell and put it down. Nobody made a move toward the exit.

The screens went black while the officer was still talking. Beyond the panel, three bells sounded in quick succession, and then we heard the scrape of several benches moving at once. Reese sent two officers to check the next hall before anyone touched the gate. I could hear a woman coughing on the other side and a man telling her to stop because she was making people look. A few minutes earlier I'd been angry that the rescuers were taking time to check doors. Listening to the people in that room try to be quiet, I found myself watching their hands and silently begging them to hurry.

The far gate was an old fire door fitted with a newer lock. I showed an officer where the original emergency release should still be hidden in the frame. His screwdriver scraped at the cover until it came free. When he drew the door open, warm air met us, carrying cooked grain, washed concrete, and the stale closeness of too many bodies sleeping in one room.

Beyond the gate, four rows of steel partitions divided bunks beside a drainage channel. People in identical gray clothes watched the officers enter. A man in the last row began folding his blanket, keeping his bowl tucked into its folds as though he'd been told another move was coming.

Reese stopped me at the threshold. "I only need you to identify those wall supports. After that, you go back."

I showed Reese the steel jacket holding a cracked column together, then followed his light along the anchors to make sure they weren't pulling loose. A woman came to the nearest gate carrying two tin cups. She had dark hair cut unevenly above her jaw and a white scar across her right thumb. She waited for us to finish talking about the column before asking whether we were from the repair floor. Even then she addressed the question to the officer, not to me.

"We're here to get you out," the officer beside me said.

"All of us?" she asked. When he said yes, she glanced down the row at the others, apparently checking that he'd understood the question. "You'd better find shoes, then. The stairs tear your feet up." She seemed about forty, although it was hard to judge beneath the harsh overhead lights. Her identification band read 06-F. When I asked her name, she hesitated before saying, "Nell. That's what the others call me."

She explained that she'd been carrying water to Tess, a young captive whose ankles were sore from the scales. Tess appeared behind her, gripping a bent hairpin. Nell took it and slipped it into her pocket with the practiced irritation of someone tired of repeating the same warning.

"They're not the floor people," Tess said quietly. She was studying our helmets, boots, and jackets with an intensity that made me feel as though she was trying to memorize us before we disappeared. "Look at their hands."

Nell said the people who came to measure them wore soft gloves. Tess looked at our bare hands, then asked whether it was still cold outside in October. When an officer said yes, she nodded as though he'd confirmed something she'd heard years earlier.

When the medics brought blankets, Nell took the first one and wrapped it around Tess instead of herself. She told one man to bring his glasses and another to stop wasting time on a slipper he'd lost Tuesday. A little girl began crying because the officers wouldn't let her return for her cup. Nell pressed one of her own into the girl's hand and waited until she'd stopped shaking before asking a medic where the stairs led. Nobody in the row moved until Nell told them it was all right to go.

Nell kept a tiny handmade book hidden behind a pipe. It contained names, dates, and crossed-out entries. The bunk assignments changed too often, she said, and she didn't want to forget who'd slept where.

"Some of them moved to the nice rooms," she said. "They got birthdays and proper clothes. You probably found some already."

"We did," I said. "Do you know someone called Miriam?"

Her face brightened. "Green ribbon? Always asking for books? She used to send back puzzles once she was finished with them. I'd tell the floor man that half the pieces were missing, and he'd bring another box. Is she all right?"

She was pleased Miriam and Kit had reached the hospital. She asked if Miriam had seen a real cat yet, then looked toward the dark monitor and put her book away before I could answer.

"There's someone else you need to look for," she said. "Inez. She slept opposite me for twenty years. They took her for presentation last week and nobody will say where." Nell opened her little book to a page softened by fingerprints. Her own number, 06-F, was written beside her name. The line above it read 06-E, Inez, followed by a date but no destination. "Usually they let me write down the new room. They gave her a blue paper this time, and they wouldn't tell me."

Vale asked what presentation meant. Nell said the animals who'd kept their weight up were sometimes washed, checked for injuries, and taken through the clean door in fresh clothes. It was supposed to be an honor. The explanation sounded rehearsed rather than believed.

"Did Inez want to go?" Vale asked.

Nell rubbed at the scar on her thumb. "She did at first. They said she'd done very well. Then she saw the blue slip and asked if she could come back to our room afterward. The floor man wouldn't answer her. She started trying to find her shoes. I told her I'd keep them by her bed, and she could put them on when she got back." Nell glanced toward the bunks. "I've still got them. You don't have to tell her, but she's missed two wash days. That isn't fair to the rest of us."

The officer helping Tess looked away. Vale asked where Inez had gone, and Nell pointed toward the clean door. Only attendants could open it, she said; anybody without a blue slip lost meals for going near it. Then she asked a question that stopped Vale's pen.

"You'd better tell the others we're leaving," she said, looking toward the clean door. "If all the animals go upstairs, nobody will be here when the Mothers come to eat." The officer beside me made a short movement toward her, as though he'd misheard and was about to ask her to repeat herself. Nell was already guiding Tess toward the medics.

Reese called for the group to move. A boy had collapsed near the gate, and two medics were kneeling beside him while another officer made room for a stretcher. Nell crouched to speak to the boy until he let the medics lift him. Reese called for another team to cover the unguarded corridor, and the questions I'd meant to ask had to wait.

Nell left last. She went back for Inez's shoes, canvas slip-ons with blue thread mending a torn seam, and asked an officer to make sure nobody threw them away. He promised he wouldn't. She waited while he repeated it, then tucked the shoes under her arm.

By three, Nell and Tess were on their way upstairs with the others. Tess wouldn't let a medic leave her until somebody confirmed Nell had been put into the same ambulance. Beyond the scales the hall divided. One branch led back toward the decorated rooms we'd already found. The other ended beneath a painted sign reading WASH BEFORE ENTRY, with clean rubber aprons hanging outside a sealed door. The cuff of the nearest apron had been turned inside out to dry. I thought of the woman on the monitor shielding her swollen hand and of the way Nell had talked about the clean door as if being taken through it were a prize. From somewhere beyond the wall came the smell of hot water and boiled onions. I hadn't eaten since the previous afternoon. My body noticed the smell before my mind did, and that embarrassed me more than I can explain.

The clean door was locked from inside. Vale asked an officer to photograph the latch, and a man's voice came from the speaker above the aprons, irritated that the weighing had stopped. He asked if the alarm in Six was fixed because the Mothers were waiting. When an officer identified himself and demanded the door be opened, the man went quiet. Through the wall we heard a faint scrape, as if someone had covered the microphone with a hand.

"Please go back," he said at last. "You don't know what you've interrupted."

"We're evacuating the people you've been holding here," the officer told him. "Open the door and come out with your hands visible."

The man whispered to somebody else, then came back to ask whether the people from Six had been given blankets. The officer said yes. "Good," he said. "They get cold when they're frightened." Before the officer could reply, the speaker clicked off. The answer bothered me more than a threat would have. Whoever he was, he knew enough about frightened people to look after them.

Reese wanted to know if the old conveyor crossed beneath Sam's treatment room. I showed him the crossing on the plan and the maintenance hatch where the brake should be. He sent a camera to check. While we waited, Nell went past on a stretcher, bound for an ambulance, with Inez's canvas shoes against her chest. The medic asked for her arm to take her blood pressure. Nell gave him the other one so she wouldn't have to put the shoes down.

At three-forty, officers reached the clean room from another passage. Reese kept me by the monitors, and I sat down instead of protesting. One officer stopped in the doorway to straighten his partner's respirator strap. Then they went inside.

It looked like a spotless industrial kitchen. Steel counters ran beneath fluorescent lights, rubber mats covered the drains, and a conveyor disappeared through a shutter at the far end. There were three serving trolleys against the wall, their linen covers folded into identical squares. On a shelf stood rows of dishes in several sizes, including a shallow bowl large enough for a grown man to curl up inside. I couldn't make myself stop looking at it. Then the officer moved his camera toward a board over the counter, and I read the headings: HOUSE, REARING, TABLE.

The officer held a clipboard steady for Vale to photograph from our monitor. The entries under TABLE were arranged by weight and dated like deliveries. Some were waiting to be filled, while others bore the stamp COMPLETE. The last completed number was 06-E. I traced it with my eyes twice before I understood I was reading Inez's number, the one Nell had written in her book with a blank space beside it. I heard Nell asking if we could find her shoes. Vale asked the officer to show the number again, and I said, "Don't," even though I was the one who needed to see it.

An officer opened the refrigerator. Clear wrapping hung in heavy folds from metal hooks, and his flashlight flashed across neat printed labels. He reached past the first row, paused, then moved his light back to a parcel near the door. A human hand was visible beneath the plastic, curled slightly toward the palm. The leather band at its wrist was just like Seventeen's. A man outside the camera's view said to close the door. The officer stood there for another second before another hand reached across and pulled it shut. One of them backed into the corridor and began retching. Nobody told him to compose himself.

Vale asked for the identification number. The officer said 06-E. He said it twice because the radio operator hadn't answered the first time. I tried to speak but could only get part of Nell's name out. I had been standing next to her while she worried about finding enough shoes for everyone. She'd folded a blanket around Tess and kept a page in her book for a woman who was never going to come back. I reached for the water bottle beside the monitor and squeezed it before I could unscrew the cap. Water spilled into my sleeve and onto the floor. I sat down in it, still holding the bottle, and started searching my pockets for my phone. There wasn't anybody I could call who would make what I'd just seen less true.

The officers called for the medical examiner and sealed the room. Vale asked whether I was injured. I shook my head and told her Nell was in an ambulance headed toward the hospital; somebody had to let the paramedics know not to promise her they'd find Inez. Vale took out her phone immediately. While she spoke I listened to the dispatcher calmly read back the room number, then ask for the correct spelling of Inez's name. Nell had known it for twenty years, and the woman on the phone couldn't even know why it mattered. When Vale finished, I asked her to make sure Nell wasn't alone when someone told her. She held my gaze for a moment and said she would do what she could. I had no idea whether she'd be able to keep that promise.

Officers found the man from the speaker in an office and brought him into the recess. He had gray hair, a burn scar on his neck, and a neatly buttoned medical coat. He kept looking toward the inner corridor.

"Are the animals being taken upstairs?" he asked Vale before she introduced herself. "All of them? The ones from Six?"

"They're receiving medical attention," she said. "What's your name?"

"Elliot." He looked at Reese, then at me. "You're the people who opened the old wall. I saw the damage report. You should have stopped when you found the first room."

"Why?" Vale asked.

He examined her badge without touching it. "Because those rooms aren't yours. And you can't just move a household because you don't like how it's run. Some of them have been together for decades."

Elliot maintained the treatment records. He objected to the dirty shoes in the clean corridor and to the refrigerator being left open. When Vale showed him the photographs, he stopped correcting her terminology and watched her with his hands folded in his lap. I wanted him to look at the screen long enough to see the identification band.

"There are different uses," he said. "That's always been the arrangement. I'm not allowed to change it."

"Her name was Inez," I said. "She had a friend who kept her shoes." Elliot asked whether Inez was from Six, and I gave him her number. His face softened in recognition. "She was very easy to care for," he told me. I had already started toward him before he finished. My chair caught a cable and went over, and Vale put herself between us. Elliot flinched at the noise, then bent to right the chair, apparently more concerned about the equipment than my hands.

"Who decides?"

Elliot looked at the ceiling, worrying the button on his sleeve. His cuff slipped back, exposing a leather identification band like Seventeen's. The plate read 02-A. Vale noticed it too.

"Were you born here, Elliot?" she asked.

He looked genuinely surprised by the question. "Where else would I have been born?"

Elliot said the Mothers chose him for medical work when he was young because his hands were steady. He'd studied manuals with older attendants and practiced until he could work without supervision. He smoothed his trousers as he explained that he'd been allowed to keep his legs unaltered so he could carry patients. The movement made the leather band slide down his wrist. I noticed the skin beneath it was paler than the rest of his arm.

"Did you perform the procedures on Seventeen?" I asked.

Elliot studied me before answering. "Not the first procedures. I assisted when he was older. Mother never liked him to wake up alone, so she stayed beside him until he settled." He searched my face as if hoping I would approve. "She always brought him his little wooden animals. He was very fond of the horse." I had seen the same carved toys in a photograph at the hospital, scattered beside Seventeen while he cried for her. The horse was missing a wheel. I'd assumed somebody had broken it.

"And the man you've got back there now?" I asked. "Are you the one who's going to do that to him?"

Vale put a hand on my arm, but Elliot was already answering. "He's an emergency placement. He's too old, and he keeps trying to leave. I've explained that correction at his age carries additional risks, but she's determined to keep him. She says he talks in his sleep and cries when he wakes up. She hasn't left his room for more than a few minutes since he arrived."

"He has a wife and two daughters," I told him. "He has a home. He doesn't belong to her."

"I know about the daughters. She told me," Elliot said. "That's why I'm trying to make the change easier for him. You can't take away everything familiar at once. He'd be inconsolable." He looked at us hopefully. "We have a schedule. Please stop upsetting her."

Reese asked who assigned the captives. Elliot said the Mothers approved the lists and humans kept the equipment running. When Vale asked who ordered transfers marked TABLE, he looked at the ceiling.

A woman's voice came through the speaker, deeper than Mother's, drawing out its syllables as if speech required effort. She asked Elliot why the counting had stopped. He shut his eyes, then told her there were visitors and he was trying to settle them. She asked if the household had been fed. Elliot said not yet.

"Then do your work," she said.

Elliot rose to answer, but an officer caught his arm. He sat again and apologized into the speaker, his voice shrinking to that of a frightened child. The heavy movements behind the wall receded before he stopped.

Reese ordered the speaker disconnected and had Elliot escorted away. At the door he turned to me. "Your friend is still walking. You should be grateful we haven't done anything to his legs." He waited for me to thank him. When I didn't, he asked the officer whether the people from Six would be brought back after the police finished with them.

After four-thirty, the camera found the conveyor beside the treatment rooms. Its brake stopped a wheeled bed at a narrow maintenance hatch, and the engineers confirmed the frame could hold it. An adult could fit through. Mother couldn't. Reese ordered a rescue sled brought to the opening.

The plan was to stop Sam's bed as it passed the hatch, cut his straps, and pull him into the channel before Mother caught up. I showed the engineer where the catch would grip the rail. The wrong speed could throw Sam forward. I asked to work the handle myself, but Reese saw how my hands were shaking and said no.

Just before five, Nora sent a picture from yesterday's exhibition. She was standing beside her photograph, holding a ribbon and looking embarrassed that somebody had made her pose with it. In the second message she said she'd left the print at my house because she knew I'd want to see it. I zoomed in until I could make out her handwriting on the card beneath the photograph, then typed that I was proud of her. I started another apology but erased it. She'd already heard every version I knew how to give.

At five-twenty-one, two human attendants wheeled a narrow bed into the treatment passage. Mother filled the doorway behind them. Her pale hand lay on the rail, six fingers hooked around it. Part of her face moved into view above the doorframe, too near the camera to focus. Lily's BEST DADDY drawing was still pinned to the wall behind her. A second microphone caught the low three-note melody Seventeen had hummed at the hospital. Mother reached over the bed and began stroking the man lying on it.

The man on the bed was Sam. I knew him even before one of the attendants lifted his head to adjust the pillow. His hair was matted, his face swollen along one cheek, and somebody had changed his clothes for a loose white garment. His left hand was bandaged. When he turned toward the doorway, I saw the familiar crooked finger sticking out at an angle beneath the tape. He tried to raise himself on one elbow, and an attendant pressed him back down. Mother made a distressed noise, reached through the doorway, and stroked the side of his face until he stopped struggling.

I said Sam's name so quietly Vale had to ask me to repeat it. Then I was pointing at the screen, trying to show Reese the crooked finger as if they couldn't see it themselves. He confirmed Sam's identity over the radio. Sam was alive, his eyes open, but there were straps across his chest and thighs. Mother's hand covered most of one shoulder. I'd seen Sam asleep in his truck after long shifts, his mouth hanging slightly open while the rest of us unloaded. The man on the bed kept trying to lift his head to see where the attendants were taking him.

The engineer watched the bed approach the hatch. Sam turned his head toward the open service panel, where the officers waited with their lights covered. He tried to speak, and one of them motioned for quiet. His eyes moved from the gloved hand to the darkness behind it. Then he started struggling against the straps, as if he knew an opportunity had arrived and feared it would disappear before he could reach it.

The engineer pulled the brake. The wheels hit the catch hard, and Sam cried out. Two officers caught the bed while a third began cutting his straps. An attendant ran back toward Mother. The humming stopped. On the screen her face lowered toward the opening and her hand crept along the rail, searching for Sam.

"Sam!" I shouted before anybody could stop me. He twisted toward the microphone above the hatch and said my name. At first he whispered it as if he were checking whether he'd heard correctly. Then he said it again, louder, while the officers worked at the straps. I pressed my hand to the radio button and told him to follow the rescuers' instructions, to keep his head down, to let them help him. I almost told him I had it under control. Sam had spent eleven years fixing my mistakes, and I couldn't bear the idea of making him believe another promise I couldn't keep.

An officer lifted Sam beneath his shoulders while another caught his knees. Mother struck the treatment door, breaking a hinge with a report that reached us through the floor. The officers turned Sam sideways, but his knees snagged on the bed frame. He cried out, and Mother began sobbing so loudly that the microphone distorted. She hit the door again, and metal scraped against stone as the frame shifted.

"Stop pulling him," she pleaded. "Please. He hurts."

Reese ordered the officers to free Sam's knees. One began cutting the caught sheet. Then a deeper female voice called from farther down the passage, speaking a word I didn't recognize. Mother stopped striking the door at once. She protested with a long, thin cry but withdrew her hand from the bed.

The attendants returned through a second entrance. One pushed a wall switch, and a steel shutter dropped between Sam's bed and the hatch. The closest officer jerked his arm clear. Reese ordered the team to raise it, but the shutter had sunk into a recessed track beyond the reach of the brake rod. Sam struggled to sit. An attendant pulled the sheet across his legs, and he kept staring at the narrow opening where he'd just seen the officers' hands.

On the screen I saw gray dust sift from a line of bolts above the hatch. An officer had one knee on the old cover plate. I'd checked the support earlier, but the shutter had driven its frame sideways, and the bolts were pulling through loose concrete. I knew the pattern. We'd lost a loading platform that way three years earlier, after a forklift struck a guardrail. If the cover went, the officer would drop through the floor and could drag the men holding Sam with him. Reese was still ordering the team to try the shutter. I told him the bolts were tearing out. He stopped speaking, checked the camera, and ordered them back. I watched the nearest officer look from the moving concrete to Sam and reach forward once more before his partner grabbed the back of his vest.

Sam called after them. He wanted to know whether I'd gone away. I leaned over the desk and told him I was right there, even though he couldn't see me. "Cal, tell Mari I tried," he said. For a second I could hear the old Sam, furious and ashamed that he couldn't lift himself off a bed. Then he started asking for his girls. I told him I knew their names, that I'd talk to Marisol, that we were coming back. One of the officers paused with his arm through the hatch and had to be ordered away twice. The microphone cut off while I was still talking. I kept speaking into the dead channel until an engineer touched my elbow and showed me the red light had gone out. I had stepped onto the cables and was pulling the monitor toward the floor.

The camera on the nursery side was still working. Mother reached for Sam again, but the deeper voice spoke from beyond the frame. The attendant with the chart stopped beside the bed and removed a clean identification band from his pocket. Mother shook her head and tried to push his hand away. The attendant waited. When the voice spoke again, Mother backed into the doorway, one long finger dragging across the frame as she went. She was crying, and I could see her reaching toward Sam even as she obeyed.

The attendant fastened the new band around Sam's wrist, wrote on the chart, and passed a sheet to another man. They turned the bed away from the treatment room without performing the scheduled surgery. Sam went through a different gate, beyond the shutter that had stopped Reese's men. From the camera I could see his legs moving beneath the sheet.

Vale found the new marking in a duplicate of the treatment list. Elliot was brought back to explain it. He asked first whether Mother had agreed to the change. When Vale told him another female voice had ordered it, he looked down at the floor. "Then the correction is canceled," he said. "He's been returned to general stock." I asked him to say that again, certain I'd misunderstood. He did, using the same careful tone he'd used to explain how they kept their medical instruments clean.

"What happens to him now?" I was standing beside Elliot's chair before I knew I'd moved. His coat had a small brown stain on one button, and I found myself studying it because I couldn't look at his face. He folded his hands over the band at his wrist. I wanted him to say Sam would be locked in a pen, or weighed, or put to work. There were people upstairs who'd lived that way for decades. I'd begun thinking of a pen as a place where he could at least stay alive.

Elliot looked toward the evidence bags outside the door. "You've seen the lists," he said. "I can't tell you who'll be chosen next." I asked whether Sam already had a date. He said he didn't, not yet. Vale was asking another question, but I heard only the words not yet. Somebody had already printed a fresh band for Sam and brought it to his bedside. I watched Elliot straighten his cuff over his own old number and wondered how often he'd said those same words to other men who wanted to know what would happen to someone they loved.

Reese sent teams toward the lower transfer corridor while I watched the last monitor. Sam sat upright on the moving bed, rubbing the raw skin at his wrists. Every few yards he looked back toward the hatch. I wanted him to see a light, a hand, anything to make him understand we'd followed him. An attendant tucked his legs back onto the mattress. Sam pulled them away. Then the next gate shut, and the screen held only an empty stretch of track.

At six-ten I called Marisol. I told her we'd found Sam alive, that I'd seen his face and heard him say my name. She made a sound halfway between a laugh and a sob and called into the kitchen that Daddy was alive. A chair scraped. She asked if he was hurt. I told her he was, but that he could move his legs, and that the officers hadn't been able to bring him out. She repeated the last part softly, as though it hadn't made sense.

I heard the refrigerator open. Lily asked if she could keep her pancake for Daddy, and Marisol told her to eat it because he'd make her another. Her voice was so ordinary for those few seconds that I pictured the kitchen perfectly: the blue plastic plate, Lily leaning on the open refrigerator door, Marisol standing barefoot on the tile with the phone pressed to her ear. Then Marisol asked, very quietly, what she was supposed to tell them. I'd watched men in clean white clothes wheel her husband toward a transfer corridor, and I knew what had happened to Inez. I held on to the edge of the desk until my fingertips hurt and said the teams were still searching. "You said you talked to him," she told me. "What did he say?" He'd asked me to tell her he tried. I could hear Lily asking if Daddy had asked about the pancake, and I couldn't make myself put those two sentences into the same conversation. I told Marisol he had asked about her. It was true, but it wasn't what he'd trusted me with. After she said goodbye, I stood there with my phone in my hand, trying to remember whether I'd ever passed along one of Sam's messages exactly the way he asked me to.
