I've worked maintenance at a municipal recreation centre for nine years. Until last Thursday, the worst thing I'd ever found in our swimming pool was a used diaper.

The pool has been empty since the beginning of November. We had a leak somewhere around the deep-end expansion joint, so the city brought in a contractor to repair it. They drained the whole thing, shut down the circulation system and closed that section of the building.

I need to make something clear before I get into what happened.

The pool was completely empty.

Not low on water. Not partially drained. Empty enough that you could stand in the deep end and look up at the diving board without getting your shoes wet.

I know because I'd spent three afternoons down there with a pressure washer.

The first strange thing happened on Monday.

I was collecting tools from the deck when I heard someone swimming.

Not pipes knocking or water dripping. Someone doing laps. I could hear the regular slap of an arm entering the water, followed by a little rush and splash.

I turned around expecting to see one of the contractors messing about.

Empty pool. Bare concrete. A couple of buckets near the shallow end.

The noise stopped.

I walked around the edge, looked into the drain, checked the adjoining changing rooms. Nothing.

We have another smaller pool on the other side of the building, and I figured the sound must have carried through the ventilation somehow.

Except it happened again on Wednesday, while that pool was also closed.

This time I was standing right beside the deep end.

Three strokes.

A pause.

Three more.

Then a sound like someone turning over in the water.

I looked directly at the spot the noise was coming from. Nothing moved. There wasn't even any dust blowing across the floor.

I mentioned it to Len, the contractor doing the joint repairs.

He laughed and said empty swimming pools were creepy places.

I agreed with him.

Thursday was supposed to be the last day before the repair compound needed time to cure. I was helping Len pack up his equipment around six in the evening. The centre was still open, but the pool area was locked off and we'd switched off most of the overhead lighting.

Len had left his tape measure on the bottom of the deep end.

He went down the metal ladder to retrieve it while I was coiling an extension cable.

Halfway down, he stopped.

"Did you leave the hose on?"

I told him the hose had been disconnected since Tuesday.

He was holding onto the ladder with one hand and moving the other around in front of him, about four feet below the pool's original waterline.

"Feel this."

I came over.

He reached out again and laughed nervously.

"Seriously. Put your hand here."

I knelt at the edge and extended my arm.

For a second I felt absolutely nothing.

Then my fingers touched something cold.

It wasn't a solid surface. It moved around my hand. There was resistance, exactly like pushing your hand through water.

Except I could see straight through it to the concrete seven feet below.

I pulled my hand back.

It was dry.

Len watched me look at my fingers.

"Right?" he said.

I didn't answer. I reached down again, slowly this time.

My hand passed through empty air, and then there was that sudden cold pressure around my wrist.

I moved my fingers.

Something rippled.

I couldn't see the ripples themselves, but a little piece of blue masking tape that had been floating around the deck began moving in a circle about an inch below the edge of the pool.

It should have fallen to the bottom.

Instead it just sat there, turning slowly.

Len said, "What the fuck?"

And then something bumped the ladder from underneath him.

He jerked backward, lost his footing and dropped.

I watched him fall seven feet into an empty swimming pool.

He landed near the bottom and immediately began drowning.

That's the only word for it.

His cheeks puffed out. His eyes opened wide. He tried to breathe and started convulsing, his mouth opening and closing as his arms thrashed around him.

He was lying on the dry concrete, but his movements were slow and heavy, like someone struggling underwater.

I screamed his name.

He couldn't hear me.

I ran for the rescue pole hanging beside the lifeguard chair. It's one of those long aluminium poles with a curved hook on the end.

I lowered it toward him.

The moment the hook passed below the old waterline, I felt drag on the pole. Real resistance, like I was pushing it through several feet of water.

Len grabbed it.

I pulled.

He moved maybe six inches.

Then the pole stopped dead.

Something was holding him down.

I leaned back with both hands, bracing my feet against the pool edge. Len's body lifted slightly off the concrete.

I could see his shoes.

Something was wrapped around his left ankle.

There wasn't anything visible there, but his sock was compressed into five deep indentations, as if somebody had a hand around it.

I pulled harder.

The pole bent.

For one horrible second I thought it was going to snap.

Then Len came loose so suddenly that I fell backward onto the tiles.

He struck the ladder, caught a rung and somehow managed to climb the last few feet.

I dragged him onto the deck.

His clothes were completely dry.

He wasn't breathing.

I've done CPR training every year since I started this job, and I've always wondered if I'd actually remember what to do when I needed it.

Apparently I did.

I don't know how long I was working on him. Maybe a minute. Maybe three.

Then he coughed.

A shocking amount of water came out of his mouth.

It smelled like chlorine.

He rolled onto his side, coughing and gasping, and I nearly started crying.

I had my phone out to call an ambulance when Len grabbed my wrist.

His fingernails were torn and bleeding.

"Don't look down," he whispered.

I looked down.

Of course I fucking looked down.

The empty pool was no longer empty.

I still couldn't see water. The concrete was right there, perfectly visible, and the repair buckets were sitting where we'd left them.

But there were shapes moving through the space above it.

I could see them because of the dust.

The contractors had been grinding out old material from between the tiles, and there was a fine layer of pale grey dust along the bottom of the deep end.

Something was stirring it.

Long, slow trails of dust were rising from the concrete and drifting sideways, following the movement of things I couldn't see.

There were several of them.

One passed directly underneath us.

The dust curved around a shape approximately the size of a person, with something trailing behind it that was far too long to be a pair of legs.

Then I heard the swimming again.

Three strokes.

A pause.

Three more.

Except now I could hear other movements answering it.

Splashes from different parts of the pool.

Len was trying to stand up.

"We need to go," he kept saying. "We need to go right now."

I got his arm over my shoulder.

We were about halfway to the door when I heard metal scrape against metal.

The deep-end ladder.

Someone was climbing it.

I turned around.

There was nothing visible on the ladder.

But the top rung was shaking.

Then the next rung.

Something heavy was putting its weight on each one, slowly working its way upward.

A few drops of water appeared on the handrail. They hung there for a moment before disappearing.

The metal creaked.

Then I saw the strangest thing of all.

Two handprints appeared on the tiles at the top of the ladder.

Not wet handprints.

Impressions.

The dust on the tiles flattened beneath five long fingers, as if someone invisible had pressed both hands against the floor.

Then those fingers curled.

Whatever it was, it was pulling itself out.

I didn't wait to see the rest.

We ran.

Len could barely walk, so I was practically dragging him down the corridor toward the main reception area.

Behind us, the pool door slammed open.

I heard something hit the corridor floor.

A horrible heavy slap, followed by a dragging noise.

And then another.

It sounded like somebody learning how to walk while carrying something much larger than their own body.

I didn't look back again.

The receptionist called emergency services. Two paramedics arrived first, followed by the police.

Len was taken to hospital for suspected water aspiration. They kept him overnight for observation.

The police searched the pool area.

They found nothing.

One officer actually laughed when I tried to explain that Len had almost drowned in a drained swimming pool. He stopped laughing when Len started coughing up more water in front of him.

Our manager thinks we were exposed to some kind of chemical fumes from the repair compound.

I can understand why he'd prefer that explanation.

But there are two things I haven't told him.

The first is what Len said in the ambulance.

He told me that when he fell, he could see everything clearly. The concrete floor. The ladder. Me leaning over the edge with the rescue pole.

But he couldn't get air into his lungs.

And there were people down there with him.

Not swimming.

Standing on the bottom.

He said they were all looking upward, toward the surface of the water that wasn't there.

One of them had its hands around his ankle.

He told me it kept opening its mouth, but no sound came out.

When he finally understood what it was trying to say, it wasn't asking him to stay.

It was begging him not to pull it out.

The second thing is what happened yesterday.

I went back to collect my personal belongings. I'd already told my manager I wasn't coming back until the pool area had been properly investigated.

The repair crew was gone. The pool was still drained.

But there were fresh marks on the tiles around the deep end.

Long parallel scratches, all leading away from the ladder toward the door.

Some were about a foot apart.

Others were nearly four feet apart.

Like whatever had climbed out was trying different ways of moving.

The scratches stopped at the doorway.

There were more in the corridor.

I followed them as far as the emergency exit.

The door was locked from the inside, just like it was supposed to be.

And on the bottom half of the glass, facing out toward the car park, there were five long impressions in the condensation.

They were about seven feet above the ground.

I quit yesterday.

The centre is supposed to refill the pool on Tuesday and reopen it to the public next week.

I keep thinking about the thing on the ladder.

Not because I think it's still there.

Because Len told me there were several people standing at the bottom.

And when he said they were begging him not to pull them out, he didn't say they were afraid of us.

He said they were afraid of the thing that had already climbed out.
