Most of these are from a point in which I was at my worst of contemplation in our true place in the universe, with the effects of particular observations dragging on for months and influencing ones of similar connotations. 

But I must start things off with a few clarifications. 

I mentioned in a few of my last stories that some discoveries would never be formally published publicly, but I didn’t really explain why since I'm used to the people, I mention this stuff to already understanding why. So here’s the reason: Governments around the world see it fit that some of our findings are altered or flat out classified. While somewhat good intentioned to keep the exploration of the cosmos in people’s imaginations and dreams rather than nightmares. It does suck at times. But here I am now, telling all of you the full story. 

On top of that, if the government ever tells you we don’t have the technology to imagine or make out compositions of not-so-distant worlds – oh yes they do. 

It's hard to get into but in effect alot more is done with a lot less, especially with our mirrors department. Hence, images of alien planets exist and they are… unflattering. Big balls of rock possibly with an atmosphere if were lucky. It seems like the interesting planets are still out of reach of even our most advanced telescopes. But that doesn’t mean the signs of them aren’t present. 

Unlike others, our government in particular wishes to relay this finding. They're just attempting to formulate the best possible disclosure of alien worlds to the public. Since they’ve been trying to do this for almost ten years now, I’m inclined to believe the statement is trapped in a bureaucratic hellhole from which it may never escape. To a point where it resides other discoveries of a similar nature to a similar fate. Will tell people eventually. I image other governments and their agencies have similar reasoning only with the potential added hint of a desire to shape the narrative around it being their counties discovery. Though truthfully, I cannot speak for them. 

What I can speak for is the astronomer community’s ability to collaborate on an international level for the shale of discovery. Which often extends to our ability to cover it up. 

A good example of this comes not from interstellar endeavours, but human ones. 

Amid a radio range project, I picked up a faint but distinct wavelength between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter cradling along the asteroid belt. Despite ignoring it under the impression it was just a blip, it remained – just very subtly fading. That made me think it was a probe of some kind. But after consulting the maps, I found no nation had sent anything to that region of the solar system ever. Something that could easily be explained by the area’s lack of novelty. Which made the signal all the more intriguing since there wasn’t any clear reason for it being there. 

Normally, it would’ve been hard to persuade management for a couple hours of signal tracing with there million-dollar telescope, since unsurprisingly, random unexplained signals aren’t all that uncommon. But I managed to win them over with the classic concern of another country looking for something we didn’t know about. (barely even had to write a proposal since it was a target of opportunity). 

After some triangulation calculations inside the cold spacious observatory dome, we were able to point the telescope at the rough location. 

Let me tell you its alot easier to get an image of a planet then a craft. With the effects being comparable to spotting a grain of rice on a cosmic scale (with a nearby planet being more like a basketball). 

Thus, the picture generated was of a blur just barely disenable as circular around the top and coned at the bottom. 

Confusing at first, but it didn’t take long to form an idea of what it was, since there was only one craft that matched it’s proportions. 

In the 1960s the Vostok program officially flew 6 crewed missions, which were the first to put humans into space. After some off the books digging, I found that two Italians with nothing better to do but spy on the space program with scavenged radio parts had intercepted at least three other missions that had ended in tragedy covered up by the Soviet Union to avoid bad publicity. 

Apparently, the transmissions they picked up recorded the final moments of the failed mission’s crew. 

When I tell you I spent the next week and a half doing anything but listening to that audio I don’t do so lightly. Obviously because it could potentially incriminate what I'd found. But eventually curiosity compelled me. And I really wish it hadn’t. 

My ears were filled with a flaky static that a calm voice cut through with an distress message starting and ending with Russian code keys. 

Any words where too muddled to interpret. Only emotion was conveyed. Frantic, frightened and above all hopeless. But soon the words faded to incredibly laboured breathing as the capsil slowly ran out of oxygen. Long and crocked – more of an inhale. Until even that stopped. Replaced by a heartbeat growing faster and faster through the enveloping static. 

The audio supposedly came from a capsule veering off course into the void and didn’t include any timeline. He could’ve spent weeks slowly starving and losing oxygen with the mental load of being the furthers any human being had ever travelled. 

Expectedly once I tracked it – the resting orbit matched the one I'd found. 

The almost indisputable truth of an multi decade cover up was in my hands. Or so I thought. Our collective capacity to suppress information was revealed to me two days later when an official I'd never meet before asked me a few questions regarding my finding. He denounced that it could be a craft and somehow labelled it as an extinct comet that was emitting seismic acoustics. Above all else he assured me it wasn’t related to the Vostok program (even though I hadn't brought it up) and should most certainly not go spreading that it was. Honestly, I didn’t even want to. 

Now any time I find something with the potential of being a lost mission, I simply sweep it under the rug. Though it gets hard, as theirs far more than you’d expect. 

One of the great mysteries of our solar system I’ve been bless (Or cursed) with investigating is the hexagon storm vortex at Saturn's north pole. A discovery made way back with voyager two’s flyby. Rather disappointingly, our progress decades later was minimal. The storm is bigger than earth and has a vortex rising several kilometres into the air like a tower. Surprisingly these are fairly normal attributes for a gas giant storm. The only real difference outside of shape was its immense heat. The average temperature of Saturn’s upper atmosphere is around –170 Celsius. The hexagon sits at 180 for no discernible reason. We ran every wind simulation possible most of which I was present for so that I could sleep again without pills. But every single one we did turn up nothing. There was no clear way to continually replicate it, and no other features to reference it. 

The main theory the others decided upon was that the hexagon was a ‘final form’ of a polar wind system when it has no land to interrupt it like on earth. Course I didn’t buy it, since no other gas giants have any features remotely resembling it. Meaning its cause was something else entirely. 

Nature seldom makes perfectly straight, two-dimensional objects (Till humans came around). That logic that applies equally to space with the outlier of objects often forming vague spheroids due to gravity. (the moon is more of a lemon after all). 

Something unseen and unknown, potentially deep within Satun is violating everything we know about planetary behaviour for reasons we simply cannot understand. Which is why we throw up plausible ideas. Because the reality is much more terrifying. 

Things got even worse a few months after we’d developed our theories. Another team looking at the pole with one of JWST’s infrared instruments immediately brought us in when they found black bead like spots swirling around the hexagon in the lower atmosphere. We originally thought it was the biproduct of an unknown magnetosphere effect but were quickly disproven at the realisation the beads – despite being hundreds of kilometres wide, were solid yet weren’t disrupting the existing cloud formations in the slightest.  

I didn’t even bother sticking around to help with speculation, since I already knew all we could confirm nothing other than the potential connection between the beads and the hexagon. 

Another time in my exoplanet research I ended up at the forefront of one of the most terrifying interstellar events ever recorded. It was one of my first observation projects where I would track dimming events of a star some 15 light years away. Dimming events occur when a planet moves in front of its star, thus makings its existence known and recordable to us. You'd think the amount of light they have to block would be substantial, but in reality, a world the size of Jupiter would barely block out one percent of the light (Less than the size of a coin on our telescopes). That’s why when a passing object blocked out over twenty percent of its star’s light, it put my stomach into knots.  

It most definitively couldn’t be a planet. 

I watched for hours as gargantuan objects of varied shapes and sizes sporadically swung around the star till some of them started staying. It was so bizarre, I physically couldn’t look away. 

 Eventually one of my superiors particularly forced me out the door with the reassurance they’d keep watch. Unfortunately, this then bestowed upon him the turbulent task of explaining to me that in the six hours I was gone, the star completely disappeared. The covers had encircled it to the point that it vanished without a trace. A couple were inclined to believe it was the star’s final send off before it became a black dwarf. Which would have been a monumental discovery in itself that also violated everything we thought we knew about the concept. Another subjected it was a rouge black hole since one of the guys swore the silhouette quivered right before it disappeared. But it was a possibility presented by another project that was the most convincing, and unsettling.  

A project comparing old star surveys to our current updated ones. Several were missing from the new maps with most having logical explanations, mainly just imagining errors, over a hundred didn’t. They'd simply vanished without reason. Suddenly a very disturbing reality straight out of science fiction stopped seeming entirely unfounded. The potential that those stars had been encircled by some kind of Dyson sphere by an advanced civilization. Something only thought to be the work of science fiction. Something so unbelievable. Something I witnessed firsthand. 

Things only got worse on the mental front for me. A complete shutdown over the fact I was seeing some of the most disturbingly unexplainable events that painted my species as nothing more than specks and I couldn't even talk about it since my government believed humanity wouldn’t be able to handle such discoveries (Honestly, they are probably right). It was around this time my wife and I divorced. We loved each other but it was clear I was spending more time obsessing over concepts she didn’t and couldn’t know about. 

I never spiralled as far Derric. Derric or Dazza, some called him wasn’t like other astronomers. While most of us where often extremely profession and determined people which tended to make us antisocial. Derric was loud, comedic, and often proclaiming of his boredom while working. Numerous near terminations resulting from actions such as arranging a star chart’s connections into a penis. Or editing the words “send help” onto a photo from a Mars orbiter without its team noticing. This often made me one who he’d managed so long in such a strict environment. Though the answer was quite obvious – he was about three times as smart as any other astronomer in any room. Which went to knew heights one fateful day only a few other people know about (so you can’t tell anyone). 

There's this concept known as the Gödel’s time travel theory in which general relativity could permit backwards time travel if spacetime where to rotate enough for light to loop back into its own path. And supposedly there are several of these light paths that could show anything from a couple years ago, to a couple billon years ago. 

It was entirely theoretical for decades, with no sign of any observable proof or interpretable data out there. So naturally, when Derric got his hands on the concept, he worked it out immediately. 

An embarrassing reminder of his intellect to have solved a decade spanning mystery within the afternoon. 

But the state I found him in made me think he regretted it. Curled in a ball in the cornier of his office lacking any colour in his face. 

From there he uttered the last words I’d ever hear from him. 

“Nothing God made could facilitate that.” 

Now we see back in time constantly in astronomy given light takes billions of years to reach us. What Derric had done was find an exotic gravitational lens that allowed him to physically see back in time. To be exact four billion years ago. To the early formation of our solar system. 

In amongst the great golden protoplanetary discs was Saturn's birth. It had rings though not of rock and ice, but gas and heated liquid slowly spinning into the foundation of the planet. Inside this vague shape was the image I believe will be the last thing I ever see. 

A creature of unimaginable scale curled among the gas – of which it seemed to be feeding a red plasma into. Its slim form blurred into pixels by its centre, but its arms where interpretable as long tubes reaching bellow its form, and its head arched over its back without any distinct eyes or mouth. 

Above it were four black pill shapes that spewed unknown material down into a bright outline of a hexagon from which it would funnel across the rings to slowly form the planet. 

 I do not know what it was, or why it was, or how it was. All I know is Derric doesn't do much speaking anymore. But I still do. Because I realised at his expense – compiling these truths, having the idea to share them, is enough to bare the weight of discoveries humankind was never meant to see.
