I work for a self storage company in regional Victoria. I’m not naming the town or the company because I still need the job, assuming I have one after this week.

I’m twenty nine and technically an “asset recovery coordinator”, which sounds more impressive than it is. When someone stops paying for a storage unit, I help with the process leading up to auction. I check the account notes, confirm that the required notices were sent, cut the company lock with a witness present and photograph the contents.

I don’t touch anything unless there’s a safety issue. I take wide shots, document anything obviously valuable and write a basic description for the auction listing.

“Assorted household furniture, boxed kitchenware, clothing, possible tools.”

That sort of thing.

Most units are depressing rather than interesting. People imagine storage auctions as rooms full of antiques or drug money. Usually, it’s damp mattresses, outdated televisions and family photos that nobody bidding online wants.

I’m posting because an auction listing appeared on our website tonight using photographs I didn’t take.

They show the inside of my apartment.

I need to explain how this started before I contact anyone else. I already tried the police once, but I left out enough that they clearly thought I was involved in a theft or wasting their time. I also need this written somewhere the company can’t quietly delete it.

About six weeks ago, our branch manager, Darren, assigned me a unit labelled C-44.

Our facility has four main blocks. A and B are near reception. C and D are farther back, beyond the vehicle storage bays. C Block mostly contains smaller units with roller doors, concrete floors and no internal lighting.

I knew the layout well enough that C-44 sounded wrong.

C Block ended at C-42.

I told Darren that, but he barely looked up from his monitor. He said the system showed C-44, ninety three days overdue, with all notices completed.

I assumed someone at head office had entered the wrong number. That happened regularly. Some customers still had contracts listing unit numbers from before the facility was renovated.

The account belonged to a woman named Elise M. According to the notes, she had rented the unit online eight months earlier and never visited reception. Payments stopped after the third month. Calls went directly to voicemail. The registered letter was returned unopened.

Darren told me to check C Block and call him if I couldn’t find it.

There was a C-44.

It was beyond C-42, where I remembered a blank concrete wall and an emergency water cabinet. The cabinet was still there, but the wall now extended another three metres to a narrow roller door.

I stood there for a while, trying to understand how I could have ignored an entire storage unit for nearly two years.

The number had been applied with one of our standard blue and white stickers. It wasn’t crooked or fresh. Dust had collected along its upper edge.

The unit had a customer padlock, so I called Darren. He came out with Mo, our maintenance contractor, who also served as the required witness when we opened overdue units.

Neither of them seemed surprised by the door.

Darren actually laughed when I said it hadn’t been there before. He told me I spent too much time staring at spreadsheets and not enough time walking the site.

Mo cut the lock.

The unit was almost empty.

A wooden dining chair stood in the centre, facing the roller door. On the chair was a black plastic crate with no lid. Inside were a house key, a cracked phone screen protector, three supermarket receipts and a pair of men’s reading glasses.

Nothing was dusty.

That stood out because even sealed units collect a fine grey layer around the door. The concrete floor looked freshly swept.

Darren was annoyed. Units with little resale value cost the company money because we still had to pay the auction platform and the rubbish contractor. He told me to photograph it quickly and list it as miscellaneous personal effects.

I took eight pictures.

When I reviewed them at my desk, the chair appeared in every shot, but the crate didn’t.

I returned to the unit because I thought I had somehow framed it out.

The crate was still sitting there.

I photographed it again with my work phone. While the camera was open, I could see the crate on the screen. After I pressed the shutter, the saved photograph showed an empty chair.

I tried three more times.

The same thing happened.

My work phone was old and had been dropped more times than I could count, so I blamed the camera. I used my personal phone instead.

That photograph showed the crate.

It also showed something behind the chair.

At first, I thought it was a stain on the back wall. When I enlarged the image, it looked more like the edge of an open doorway. There was a dark vertical gap and, beside it, part of a white doorframe.

The actual back wall was uninterrupted concrete.

I checked it by hand. I even knocked along it because I thought someone might have painted over an access panel.

There was nothing.

I should have reported it then, but reporting unusual things at work is harder than people pretend. Darren already considered me overly cautious. Two months earlier, I had delayed an auction because the account notes didn’t show that a notice had been properly issued. I had been right, but he still complained that I was creating unnecessary problems.

So I uploaded the ordinary work phone images and kept the photograph from my personal phone.

The auction listing received no bids. A rubbish contractor cleared the unit three days later.

I asked the contractor, Steve, whether he had noticed anything unusual.

He said there had only been a chair.

I asked about the crate.

He said there was no crate.

When I checked C Block that afternoon, C-44 was gone.

The water cabinet was immediately beside C-42 again. There wasn’t enough space for another unit.

I didn’t tell Darren. I decided I had confused the layout and that Steve had thrown out the crate without remembering it. The photograph bothered me, but phone cameras produce strange shadows all the time.

A week later, Mo came into reception and asked whether anyone had handed in his glasses.

He described them as cheap black reading glasses with a small silver mark on the left arm.

I remembered the pair in the crate.

I asked when he had lost them.

He said sometime that morning. He had used them while checking an invoice in his van, then couldn’t find them after lunch.

I didn’t tell him I had photographed what appeared to be the same glasses seven days earlier.

That night, I enlarged my personal photograph again. The open doorway behind the chair was clearer than I remembered. Beyond it was a strip of pale flooring and the corner of something red.

Mo’s work van has a red rubber floor lining.

The next incident involved Unit B-17.

B-17 was real. I had opened it before. It belonged to a landscaping company that had gone into liquidation, and it contained bags of fertiliser, broken tools and an old ride on mower.

When I raised the roller door to take the auction photographs, the mower was gone.

In its place were three office chairs and a small laminate desk.

The desk was identical to the one in our staff room.

A ceramic mug sat on top with the words WORLD’S OKAYEST BOSS. Darren’s wife had given him that mug as a joke. It normally sat beside the kettle.

I called Darren immediately.

He came down, saw the desk and accused one of the staff of moving company property into an auction unit. He was angry enough that I almost felt relieved.

Anger meant there was a normal explanation.

We walked back to reception together.

The staff room desk was still there.

So was the mug.

Darren picked it up and examined it. There was a small chip in the handle. He said there was only one because his wife had ordered it from a personalised gift site.

We returned to B-17.

The second mug had the same chip.

Darren stopped treating it as a joke after that.

He told me not to photograph anything and called Mo. While we waited, Darren secured the unit with a company overlock. He also stood in front of it, which I noticed because Darren usually delegated anything involving heat or walking.

Mo arrived about twenty minutes later.

When Darren unlocked B-17, the landscaping equipment was back. The mower, fertiliser and tools were exactly where the earlier intake photographs showed them.

The office furniture was gone.

Darren told Mo that I had opened the wrong unit.

I said I hadn’t.

He spoke over me and repeated that I had made a mistake.

Back in the office, he asked me to delete any photographs I had taken.

I reminded him that he had specifically told me not to take photographs.

That seemed to frighten him more.

For the next two weeks, nothing happened. C-44 didn’t reappear. B-17 was sold and cleared. Darren avoided discussing either incident.

I started checking the facility map every morning. I printed a copy and marked the walls, doors and fire equipment. I counted the units in each block whenever I walked the site.

I also began photographing my own belongings before work.

That sounds ridiculous, but Mo’s glasses affected me more than the duplicate desk. The crate in C-44 had contained those glasses before he said he lost them.

I kept wondering about the other objects.

The key. The receipts. The screen protector.

I still had the photograph, so I enlarged the receipts. Two were too blurred to read. The third showed the logo of a supermarket near our facility and a total of $23.70.

The date was the following Thursday.

At that point, the photograph had been taken eleven days earlier.

I went to the supermarket on Thursday after work. I didn’t have a plan. I walked around studying customers’ trolleys like an idiot, trying to guess which combination of groceries would total $23.70.

At 6:12 p.m., my phone rang. It was my younger sister, Leah.

She asked whether I could pick up dog food because she was working late and had forgotten to buy it. We share responsibility for our mum’s elderly kelpie, Buddy, and I was supposed to visit Mum that evening anyway.

I bought dog food, milk, bread and a packet of batteries.

The total was $23.70.

The receipt matched the one in the crate.

Same store number.

Same register.

I stood at the self checkout comparing it to the photograph until an employee asked whether I needed help.

I kept the receipt in my wallet.

The next morning, it was gone.

I emptied the wallet, my backpack and my car. I checked the supermarket bag and the rubbish bin at Mum’s house.

I never found it.

That afternoon, C-44 reappeared.

I was alone in C Block. I had checked the area less than an hour earlier, and the wall had ended after C-42.

The new roller door was closed but unlocked.

I should have left it alone. I know that.

I had already seen enough to understand that the unit wasn’t simply a room someone had concealed.

But I kept thinking about the receipt. If it was inside, I could show Darren. I could prove that an object had moved from my wallet into a locked storage facility.

I raised the door.

The chair was back.

The crate now contained Mo’s glasses, my receipt and the cracked screen protector.

The key was gone.

There was also a photograph.

It was a standard six by four print lying face down beneath the receipt. I used a pen from my pocket to turn it over because I didn’t want to touch it directly.

The photograph showed the front door of my apartment.

It had been taken from inside my hallway, facing out.

I live alone. Leah has a spare key, but she hadn’t visited for weeks. The property manager also had access, although they were supposed to give notice.

I called the police from outside the unit. I told them I had found evidence that someone had entered my home and placed a photograph inside an overdue storage unit.

I didn’t mention that the unit sometimes disappeared.

Two officers attended. By the time they arrived, C-44 was still there. That was the first time anyone outside the company had seen it while I was present.

They examined the crate and asked who had access to the facility. They treated it as a possible stalking or burglary matter, which was reasonable.

One officer picked up the photograph with gloves.

He asked whether I had taken it myself.

I said no.

He turned it over.

On the back, written in blue pen, were my full name and the date.

Tomorrow’s date.

The officers searched my apartment that evening. There was no damage and no obvious sign of forced entry. My building’s hallway camera had been broken for over a month. The property manager denied entering.

The key in the crate was tested.

It opened my apartment.

The officer handling it looked at me differently after that. He asked whether I had recently changed the lock.

I had, about nine months earlier, after the previous tenant’s brother turned up drunk and tried his old key.

Only three copies existed as far as I knew.

Mine, Leah’s and the property manager’s.

The key from the crate had the same unusual triangular head as mine.

When I checked my keyring, my apartment key was still there.

The police took the crate and everything inside it as evidence. They told me to stay somewhere else until the lock could be replaced.

I stayed at Mum’s house. The next morning, the officer called and said the evidence bag contained the glasses, receipt and screen protector, but not the photograph or key.

He asked whether I was sure those items had been placed inside.

I said both officers had handled them.

He didn’t deny that. He only said the bag had been sealed and there was no sign of tampering.

Darren suspended me that afternoon.

Officially, it was because police activity at the facility had disrupted operations and because there were “questions regarding unauthorised access”.

Unofficially, he thought I had staged the whole thing.

I asked him to check the security footage from C Block.

He said the cameras had recorded me walking past C-42, stopping beside the water cabinet and raising my arms as though opening a door that wasn’t there.

There was no C-44 in the footage.

The two police officers appeared to walk directly through the concrete wall when they followed me inside.

I haven’t seen the video myself. Darren refused to send it, and by the next day he claimed the recording had become corrupted.

I spent the last week at Mum’s place. I replaced my apartment lock anyway. I also changed the locks on Mum’s house and checked Leah’s keyring myself.

The key that disappeared from police evidence wasn’t any of ours.

At least, I don’t think it was.

Tonight, at 10:47, I received an automated email from our auction platform. Suspended staff accounts are apparently still included in listing notifications.

A new auction had been scheduled for Unit C-44.

The description reads:

“One bedroom apartment contents. Furniture, electronics, personal records and miscellaneous effects. Inspection not available.”

There are twelve photographs.

The first shows my sofa and television.

The second shows my kitchen.

The next several show my cupboards, wardrobe and bathroom cabinet. The photographs are recent. A plate I left in the sink this afternoon is visible.

The eleventh image shows me asleep in Mum’s spare room last night.

The last image is different.

It shows the chair inside C-44.

My apartment key is on the seat beside my wallet, my work identification card and the framed photograph of Leah and me that currently sits on Mum’s bookshelf.

There is something else written on the back wall, but the image is too small to read on the website.

I called Leah because she’s working the late shift and I wanted to make sure she was safe.

She didn’t answer.

While I was trying again, Darren sent me a text. It’s the first time he has contacted me since the suspension.

He wrote:

**Do not come to the facility. C-44 is open. Your sister is inside.**

I’m posting this before I leave because Darren has lied to me repeatedly, and the police already think I staged the evidence.

Leah’s phone has just answered my third call.

Nobody spoke, but I could hear a roller door being raised.
