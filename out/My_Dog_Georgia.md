Georgia Famine  Peach

I first met Georgia while I was working as an animal control officer. I had been called out for a stray dog, normal stuff. Nothing I didn’t see at least a few times a day. Usually the calls would come from suburban areas in the city where people could *see* the loose dog and report it. Normally, the more rural an area is the less calls for service we get. Sometimes an injured wildlife call would sneak through the cracks but those were few and far between for country folks. All this to say that I wasn’t anticipating heading to the edge of town for a stray dog on an unnoteworthy Monday afternoon. 

Georgia’s call came from the outskirts of the city, just before the line crossed from Baltimore county into Carroll. An anonymous caller told dispatch that they spotted her in a patch of bramble bushes from their porch. This statement in it of itself should have set red flags off in my head because once we got to the address given to us there were no residential homes for miles. The area was barren. A rusted metal fence fought solemnly against a thick and unruly tree line. Bark bore its way into the tight diamond gaps between the fencing, protruding the metal outward and into messy patches of dried out and withered pricker bushes that scattered across a field of dehydrated and brittle yellow grass. 

I drove along the “work vehicles only” designated dirt path until it stopped at an abrupt dead end. I was not looking forward  to either backing out in the old piece of shit van nor making a potentially hundred point turn. It took me several minutes just to get to the location from the main highway. The dirt road itself was at least three miles long. There were no residential buildings within view from the parking spot that was forced upon me. The only building in sight was an old peanut factory, no longer alive and breathing billowing clouds of salty smelling smoke. The windows were boarded, signs with bolded letters spelling **CONDEMED** even drove off the local homeless population. It was less common seeing drug users and people battling homelessness near Carroll anyway but truly none of those groups would be interested in being in this field. The factory was the only noteworthy thing for miles aside from the occasional car passing on the highway or unkept cluster of woods.  

I can say with certainty that no one was around for miles. 

Which is not uncommon, sometimes when people abandon their pets, their conscience eventually creeps up and prompts an anonymous call to dispatch to spill their sins like a good catholic on their way to confession. It was weird, but at the time it really didn’t warrant any red flags on my end. I had been thrust into plenty of stranger situations before. So, a stray dog spotted by no one in particular in the middle of nowhere would have never set off my alarm bells. 

I hopped out of the van after making my initial scan of the area. I lumbered over to the treeline with my catch pole. Laxidasiacally, I prepped the vinyl lined metal collar to size like I had done hundreds of times before. I set it to my side, holding it in one hand like a bored boyfriend waiting for their turn to swing at mini golf. 

My eyes traced across the forgotten landscape and through the barbed bushes that were more brown than green. Each vine and limb spun together like rotting cotton candy, leaving only a vague warning of the pain it would enact if you got too close to the cluster. I swung the pole haphazardly at a few of the bushes, rustling what was left of the leaves onto the ground while wrestling the pole free from the thorned clutches of each victim to the physical manifestation of my boredom. 

No visual sign of a dog. 

I stopped walking after a minute or so, allowing my feet to create one last crackling of dried foliage before falling into the silence that comes from an absence of anything interesting. The only thing you could hear was the soft breeze of early October and the subtle hum of the car I left idling off in the distance. After a few seconds of nothing I turned to head back to my van, chalking the call up to a “clear on arrival.”

Before I could finish my first step back towards civilization I heard a crinkling of fallen leaves just beyond the fence line. 

“Oh come on man.” I grumbled to myself, knowing damn well I was too fat to climb a fence that had been left to decay for this long. I whistled before committing to clumsily make my way over, secretly hoping to see a squirrel or bird, something that belonged in the treeline and not the dog I had been sent out for. Not Georgia. 

To my dismay, I heard something whistle back. Not as clear and crisp as my own, but a whistle none the less. A lesser mimic like the echo of one’s own words that drift down the corridor of a deep cave. I felt a shiver crawl up the back of my neck at the thought of what waited for me beyond the fence line. The best case scenario would be a friendly crack head that thinks the police are valuable members of society. The worst case scenario would be that I’m forced to discharge my weapon while waiting for backup to arrive to the middle of bum fuck nowhere. Either way, my curiosity peaked enough to get my fatass over the fence. 

I set my pole against a knotty shrub just before the fence, suddenly preferring my firearm to the cold metal leash. I climbed with the grace of a groggy toddler climbing into their parent’s bed after wetting their own. Once I reached the apex of the fence I peered out into the treeline, looking for signs of movement, looking for a person, looking for something capable of whistling. 

That wasn’t what I saw though. Not a person, no, instead I saw the outline of a medium brown and white dog, hunched over in a small dirt gap surrounded by a circle of wild mushrooms and pricker bushes. She was hunched over like she was getting ready to vomit, but she wasn’t emanating the gagging sounds I had become accustomed to when working with nauseous canines. She was silent and swaying slowly back and forth with the mild breeze. 

My eyes lingered from above for a while, attempting to make sense of what exactly she was doing. Plenty of dogs sway when they’re sick or hurting but she looked physically fine. Her coat was clean, like it had just been washed. Her body condition score was a little on the lower side but by no means was she emaciated. Visually she seemed fine. No visible breakage on any of her limbs. Hell, not even an excessive amount of dirt on her paws. Each limb was firmly planted into the ground, no signs of limping. Maybe she was sick? If I could see her face I could check for ocular and nasal discharge but I’d have to get her attention. I let out another quick whistle, hoping she’d tilt her head up towards me. 

I was greeted with another whistle and an unmoving dog. The sound was closer than it had been, if I was a dumber woman I would have assumed it was coming from the dog, but a noise like that couldn’t possibly come out of a dog’s mouth. I called it a whistle but in reality it sounded more like a metallic shriek, a quick glitch before the sound cuts back to normal. The whirl of a radio as it attempts to lock onto the right channel. A shoddy A.I.’s first attempt at a whistle. 

For a moment, I attempted to make sense of this whistling border collie. Was this something I should have done from the top of a ten foot fence? No. Nor was I paying any mind to the rusted metal that had been struggling to carry my weight. It wasn’t something I considered at all until I heard an abrupt snapping sound from underneath me. Just as it registered in my brain, the steady metal beneath me gave out, setting off that weightless feeling in my stomach. The feeling someone gets from the first drop of a roller coaster. 

I fell on my face in one of the bramble bushes that circled the little dog, cracking and scratching my glasses in the process. The glasses smashed against the bridge of my nose before sinking into the dry dirt beneath me. Dust and weathered vines danced against my eyes, a vengeance for using their friends as pinatas just moments before. Without my glasses as cover I was helpless for longer than I would have liked to be. 

It took me a second to wipe the dust from my eyes and the blood from my face but once I did I found that I was face to face with the dog that had brought me here, Georgia. Or at least that was what the neon yellow cow tag stapled to her left ear had labeled her. 

It’s important to note that it took me a second to find my glasses and get my bearings, but, in that moment between lapsed half vision and half darkness my brain managed to carve out a visual of Georgia. Her eyes crept just slightly outside of their sockets, like a stress ball being squeezed ever so gently. Her tongue hung taught in the middle of her gaping maw, twisted upwards towards the pinkish grey roof of her mouth, forking almost like a snake’s. Her teeth were yellow and coated in thick layers of plaque and bile. Strangely, they weren’t canine teeth, and I had been bit by enough dogs to know what canine teeth looked like. No, these teeth were sharper, jagged, almost like a shark's if it had just been in a fist fight. Kind of, really what I remember is more of a hodge podge of teeth. Teeth lining the gums, teeth clumped along the roof of the mouth, teeth where things normally don’t have teeth. 

You get what I’m saying. She was wrong, abnormal, and though my brain only registered her in that instant I had this gut feeling that she had been unmoving in that position the entire time I was watching her. Unblinking. Caught in the moments before a sneeze for at least long enough time to warrant a blink or two. The way her face was contorted wasn’t in response to my sudden stumble into her personal space. No, that was the face of the decade of road kill I had been picking up. The face of sickly raccoons that were clawing at the catch pole as the air conditioned chilled leash tightened around their throats. It was the rotting wicked face of something trying desperately to survive right before meeting an untimely end. 

The feeling that image set off in me was more than enough to get me to hop up from my stomach, broken glasses in hand. Unfortunately, not being able to see where the vines tangled together around me took me immediately from my feet and back to the ground. Ass over face this time. Though this momentary tumble didn’t prevent me from scooting myself away from the bizarre creature. With my eyes squeezed shut as tightly as they had been when I first got pepper sprayed, I backed myself up until the remains of the fence I mangled stopped me. I sat there with my eyes closed breathing shakily, afraid to look back up at Georgia. I felt like a kid again, hiding under my blanket so the monster couldn’t see me. 

Once this realization hit me I managed to regain some composure. I’m not a child. I’m an adult with a gun. I knew I would need my eyes to get out of this without dying. I quickly rubbed out the combination of muddied dirt, sweat, and blood with my thumb and forefinger. I forced myself to blink until my watery vision turned into a more lucid and solid visage. I kept my eyes down towards my boots, avoiding getting another look at that *thing*. Once I could make out my double knotted laces with ease I forced myself to look up. As I did, the image of an average little dog was set before me. 

It was almost picturesque. She was sat in the middle of the clearing, like a normal dog. Her fluffy white tail wagged slowly behind her, like a normal dog. Her head  was ever so slightly tilted, just like I would expect from a confused normal dog. She could have easily been a poster for a cheesy movie where the dog brings the family together. A regular Milo and Otis without the needless deaths and brachycephalic face. I stared, wide eyed and confused. It’s safe to say I was flabbergasted. I stared at her, this very regular dog with a regular face, until I could fully calm myself down. 

Once my breaths slowed from near hyperventilating speeds to that of a fat woman that just climbed a fence for the first time in months, Georgia stepped forward cautiously. Her pink nose sniffed my legs. This was not abnormal. I dealt with dozens of dogs a day, almost all of them liked catching the scent of the ones that came before. Georgia was no exception. Mentally, I felt a bit more at ease, my nightmare caught now by the dull reality. Physically though, there was still a lull between the transition from trauma response back to day to day composure. Normally, when dogs approached me gently like this I would coo and talk sweet to them, but I was still at a loss here. Still holding my breath and waiting for a jump scare. 

I didn’t think to grab my firearm even as this thing got closer to my face. I had my hand clutching the leather clasp of my holster, but I had no real intention to grab it and pull the trigger. The moves this animal was making were behaviors I saw every day after I punched into work. It was a picture perfect example of a stray dog approach that you really didn’t need to worry about. Something that usually wouldn't have me on high alert. Though, my body was still reeling. I was still afraid to move. This feeling wasn’t new to me.

I grew up prone to having night terrors, moments between sleep and wake that left me immobilized by fear. It wasn’t uncommon for me to wake up in the middle of the night screaming. Certain that some unfathomable force or, more commonly a hoard of bugs, were falling down towards me from the ceiling. But this was midday and that had always occurred in the dead of night. When you can still hear the quiet hum of a warm TV that had just been shut off. 

What I mean to say is, this was different. The sun was bright, the air was cool, and worst of all I was fully awake. In that moment though, I knew, with every ounce of certainty in my body, that I had seen something grotesque. I had convinced myself that this creature was bad news just like I convinced myself that the pile of clothes in my bedroom was actually a human man ready to pounce. I came to terms with reality just as I always did. It just took a minute for real life logistics to kick back in. 

“You know you scared the shit out of me, right?” I chuckled.

As soon as she heard my voice it was as if I gave her permission to formally act like she was excited to see me. She crawled into my lap, tail wag at max speed. She placed her paw on my vest as if to demand a hand shake. I obliged with a half hearted exhale as I took her paw in my hand. For a moment, the typical callous paw pads that dogs usually have felt more like the soft and smooth skin of a peach. Though the sensation did subside just before the proper greeting concluded. 

I picked her up and held her as I swooped my leg over the now crumpled fence. She nuzzled her head into my neck like a sleepy child. Once I got to the van, I set her down in the front seat for a minute while I worked on setting up a back crate. Once a little towel stretched thin on top of cold bolted metal, I opened the passenger door and faced her again. She was still a regular dog, not an apparition, not an amalgam of all the animals I failed to save. I smiled to myself, then extended a hand to scoop her out of the front seat. She scurried away from me and curled into a ball, cowering at me for the first time. 

“I don’t wanna go in a cage.” I threw my voice in order to create what I thought she’d sound like. Evidently, I thought she would sound like a nasal little boy. It was dumb, but I was pleased with the funny little voice. It suited her. 

“Okay fine.” I muttered in response to, well, myself. I shut the passenger door. As soon as it closed fully she crawled back into her designated seat. This part of the job always made me smile. It was frowned upon to drive with a dog in the front, but I would sneak it anyway from time to time. I liked the company. I liked knowing that the dogs thought of the situation as more of an average car ride and not scary purgatory where they sat wobbling and waiting for the unknown. No, I preferred when they thought of the road trip as a treat, not a punishment. 

I went to the side of the van to shut the crate door. Once she was out of view the radio kicked on in the front. A quick exhale left my nose. How the dog’s always managed to fuck with my radio station was beyond me, but it seemed to always happen when I wasn’t looking. I folded the towel and set it on top of a small pile of clean towels I had set aside in the rear center of my van. My eyes lingered on a small black body bag that laid in the closed crate besides what would have been Georgia’s. I chose not to think about it by focusing on the noises coming from the radio. Within the sporadic crackling a voice rang out.

“IIIII li-k-ed **that**!” Chimed a jagged and robotic exclamation. Weird, but it wasn’t the bag, so it wasn’t a problem. I shut the side door. As I did, I could see Georgia in my peripheral vision. Her face was pressed against the window. Her snout was facing down and her eyes were fixed on where I was standing. She was panting which left her mouth hanging open in a haunting smile. Her jowls were dripping with saliva and her stare remained fixed on me even as I trudged over to the driver side door. Georgia’s expression didn’t shift but her head turned with me. No 180 spin but it was eerie, like when you find a painting with eyes that follow you throughout the room. I cracked my door open with reckless abandon and raised an eyebrow. 

“What are you eyeballing me like that for, ma’am?” I asked in a thick country drawl. 

Her tail wagged rapidly. The radio clicked without being touched, stopping on a station with erupting sounds of laughter and clapping. I gave the old radio a concerned look and smacked it once. It flipped back to my usual station, all it needed was a bit of brute force. 

“I am pretty hilarious.” I mumbled to myself, slumping into my seat. I shut my door and slipped my seat belt on. Then I leaned over and snapped Georgia’s into place so the alarm wouldn’t hound me the rest of the ride. Georgia shut her mouth when my hand approached. She watched it with neutral curiosity. Before rescinding my hand back to my steering wheel I gave Georgia a quick rub on the top of her head. I even took a second to remove the tag from her ear. Honestly, I was surprised she let me. But she did. Once we were both situated and I was ready to drive off she did my favorite thing that dogs do. 

Georgia curled up in a ball and got herself comfortable. 

Mae, War Mae

There were a few reasons why I ended up taking Georgia home with me. I’m sure you all will think it’s strange to take home a dog that gave me the heebie jeebies but this wouldn’t be the first time. I have another dog named Mae Mae. Mae Mae is a good girl but a very big dog. When her call for service came in, dispatch had labeled it a VICIOUS DOG call. More often than not these calls were never as bad as dispatch made them out to be. They were usually amazon drivers that got spooked by a dog and called dispatch in a huff. 

While they weren’t my favorite calls to go to, they were some of the more interesting ones. Interesting without crossing into depressing that is. Maybe interesting is the wrong word. Memorable, maybe? Either way, they were the sorts of calls that either left a mark on you or made you laugh. Real coin tosses. 

Thinking about it reminds me of when I was a rookie. My first vicious dog call went fine. No one got hurt, none of the officers were bit. But once I had that dog on the end of my catch pole she shrieked the entire time like a terrified fox. She thrashed and whipped her head around with reckless abandon until the leash had tightened so much around her throat that she began to choke herself. 

Let me tell you, it doesn’t matter how badly you want to reach down and help. It doesn’t matter that you are doing this for the dog’s safety and the safety of others. It doesn’t matter how jaded you are. Deep down, in that instant, you feel like a helpless monster torturing an animal. There is nothing you can do but run, dragging the dog to the cage. Even as they gasp and scream. Even when they begin to vomit on your shoes. Even when they collapse and go limp. You can’t reach down to comfort them. You can’t pause to loosen the noose. The only thing you can do to better the situation is move with purpose and get them off the pole as fast as possible. 

That is not what I did. I panicked. I completely froze. The way her body spiraled in circles at the end of the metal rod. The horrible sounds she made before throwing up blood and bile. Everything about it made me choke. A wave of dread washed over me, I couldn’t do it. I didn’t want to be the person to hurt her. Why couldn’t I just hold her? Why couldn’t I reach out? Why wouldn’t my legs move? 

I didn’t tell my trainer at the time but I think he knew. I wet myself, right there on site. My hands gave out and I nearly dropped the pole loose. Before I lost control of the dog completely, my trainer stepped in, Davis. A jaded old man that retired from being a cop to being an animal control officer. I guess he thought animals were easier.. For him it was. For a man that had left a job where he resuscitated the bloated blue corpses of drowned children, flinging half alive dogs into the back of a van was nothing.  

Despite being a stereotypical cop, the kind people would protest against, he didn’t give me a hard time once everything settled down. He was ready to, don’t get me wrong. Almost killing a dog on a typical call is pretty frowned upon. Once we were both in the van he gave me a sharp stare, like he was ready to lecture me. Then once he saw my face he stopped himself. I must have looked terrible. I was sheet white and coated in a thick layer of sweat like I had just gotten over a stomach bug. I still had that look of terror on my face because I could still see and hear that dog choking in the forefront of my mind. I wasn’t shaking, I was completely still. I sat in the driver's seat in silence for a moment before pulling out of the driveway. I pressed my foot against the gas pedal, ready to drive into the street when the car jutted in reverse and sent us into the wooden garage door. That was when I realized my hands weren't stable, my hands were trembling. With that jarring movement all the fear and anxiety spilled out of my mouth and into the air. Davis didn’t need to say anything. He moved me to the passenger seat and let me cry all the way to the next call. 

I should have prefaced that story by saying that the dog didn’t die that night. Davis got her off the pole in time for her to recover from the oxygen deprivation. I would sneak her treats every day I was in the shelter too, as penance. Thankfully, I got more confident with the pole after that call. So, by the time I met Mae Mae I wasn’t worried about potentially needing to use it. You see, once you feel confident and you feel calm the pole becomes less scary. You come to terms with it. You know it’s a necessary evil. A tool to use to avoid lethal force. I am not a fan of lethal force. 

I don’t think Mae Mae was a vicious dog. I think she was hungry. When I arrived at the scene, what I saw made that abundantly clear to me. Mae Mae was one of five breeding dogs stored in a one bedroom apartment. She was also the only one still alive when I arrived. The owner made the claim that the dog had “gone ballistic.” He told me she was dangerous and needed to be put down right away. He told me she had killed the other girls and their puppies. He spouted a lot of bullshit. 

The reality was clear when I got inside the “home.” The place was barren and the wood floors had been warped by the piss soaked newspaper and liquid shit that was smothering every flat surface. There were no water bowls. There was no food. What there was, were the bloodied remnants of four skeletal dogs. Parts of each had been taken out in chunks, not removed or cut into by a person but by the gashing teeth of something desperate. Mae Mae. The sole survivor of what was clearly an act of neglect and cruelty. 

In a desperate attempt to survive, Mae had resigned herself to eat away at the decaying bodies of her long dead sisters. Some pregnant, some not. When I first saw her she was lumbering over the mummified corpse of a white and grey pitbull, at least, that was what I assumed her color was. Really, it was hard to tell at this point. The fat and skin tissue had begun to schlup off the bones of the dog. The act of decay made her look like a jellied skeleton. Rigid from rigor that had long set in but slimy from the melting of the little remaining fatty tissues. 

Flies riddled the air and obscured my view of Mae just enough to keep me on my toes. She was hunched over a body, not actively eating, but her muzzle was stained red with blood and mucus. She was emaciated. Every bone of her body was fully visible, ribs, hips, even areas of her skull were visible through her blood crusted fur. She was pregnant. Her stomach was bloated just enough to see the outline of puppies. 

If you thought the scene was the most deplorable part of all of this you would hate to hear how the case concluded in court. Charged with four counts of felony animal cruelty for the fatalities and one count of misdemeanor cruelty for Mae Mae. The judge gave him a fine of $50 per dead dog and dismissed the charge for Mae. A fine of $200 total, less than some speeding tickets. His defense? 

“Your honor, it was between feeding myself or feeding my dogs.” 

I cannot express this enough. No it wasn’t. Those dogs could have easily been fed if he had surrendered them to a shelter. The judge didn’t think about that and frankly neither do most people. Either way I was pissed. This was one of many ticks that ultimately led to me quitting the police force without notice after a few years. 

This was also the nail in the coffin that led to me moving Mae Mae from a foster in my house to a full fledged member of my one person family. Initially, I had set her up with a pregnant spay, thinking child birth would be too difficult for her with how weak she was. She didn’t like that plan. The night before her surgery I found her in my bathroom tub with a slick fresh puppy. 

I had never helped an animal give birth before, and Mae was still a little stand offish. I was worried she wouldn’t let me help her. But I was worried for nothing. She didn’t react when I slipped into the tub with her. Okay, maybe she side eyed me but that was the end of it. 

 I sat, scrunched up near her butt with nylon gloves in hand. Every time a puppy came out they came out surrounded by a membrane sack of liquid viscera. If you don’t remove them from that sack fast enough they suffocate. Most mothers figure out birth after the first few pregnancies but this was Mae Mae’s first time having puppies. She didn’t get to the puppies right away so I had to manually peel away the thin flesh sleeve with my hands. The goriest version of peeling off a fresh phone screen. Oddly satisfying. 

Once the babies were out and had their first breath I would hand them off to Mae to clean. Then the next one would come out and we would repeat the process. After 9 babies and a few hours of sitting and watching them suckle and coo I assumed Mae was done and started prepping my living room to be a more spacious place to rear Saint Bernards. 

I set out blankets, a dog bed, water, all the things she was previously denied. Then I enclosed the space with some white garden fencing I had got for a project I never started. I was able to pick the babies up all at once. I remember the cute puppy noises they made when I separated them from their mom’s nipples. The sound, combined with Mae’s maternal instincts, made her follow me to her new nursery without any fuss. I made sure everyone was comfortable before heading off to bed. 

I was devastated when I found 11 puppies in the morning. They had been in Mae for too long and died before they were born. The worst part of it all was that Mae didn’t seem to realize they were gone, or she didn’t want it to be the case. She had them nuzzled under her face. It was like she was trying to keep them warm. 

“Oh Mae…” I muttered. I was sympathetic. I never had kids, never miscarried, but I can only imagine how painful it must be to come to terms with a loss. One baby was white like the largest of the living siblings and one was speckled like his mama. I reached down to remove them from the nursery but as soon as I picked them up Mae wrapped her mouth around the white one, bringing it back to her pile of nursing puppies. I lifted the other out of reach before she could grab him too. I wanted to cry. Mae nudged the puppy as if trying to get him to latch. I picked him up again. 

This time I didn’t move slowly enough for her to grab the two with her mouth. I stood up and walked into the backyard. Mae stood up and followed. I slid the glass door closed, leaving her to sit and whine for her babies while I dug small holes for them. The two of us cried the entire time. 

I buried the two in front of my magnolia tree. After Mae’s first attempt to dig them back up I took a sick day and made a wooden fence to surround them at the base of the tree. While I was at it, I planted some little flowers, shrubs and things that liked to be in the shade. It was a pretty little place to be dead. I liked to think that they enjoyed being in the yard where their siblings would eventually play and their mom would sunbathe. Eventually, their siblings all got adopted out to nice people, mostly my cousins and neighbors. Everyone but the white boy was left. I named him Sugar, like the cube. But my dad ended up calling him Kill Shot, after the little patch of brown fur on the top of his head. Unfortunately, Kill Shot is what stuck. He was double the size of his mom before he was even a year old. 

When Georgia first met the two she was only about 25lbs compared to Kill Shot’s 120lbs. I introduced my pair to her at a park by my house. They were all weirdly polite. Georgia was terrified at first. After I had dropped her off at the shelter her demeanor shifted. She was scared of everything. She wouldn’t even approach me when I offered treats or got down to her level. Unfortunately, some dogs are like that after they spend time in the puppy clink. Weirdly, even though Georgia was less friendly with me she wasn’t afraid of Mae or Shot. Instead of hiding from them, she hid behind and between the two of them like they’d protect her. Mae was not a fan. She didn’t outright growl or complain, she just stepped away when Georgia became too much for her. Georgia seemed really at home with the two dogs, she walked in between them like a weird sandwich. I remember she had the biggest smile on her face too. I think that was what got me to sign the adoption papers. She had found a family that made her feel comfortable. 

When we got home everything was pretty normal, mostly, I prepped a crate for Georgia in case she wanted alone time. Usually, a dog crate is a little bit of a fight to get dogs used to. Georgia though, ran to the back end of her new hiding spot like a fish flocks to water. It was a little eerie. I always put blankets on top of the crate so the dogs can have some privacy, like curtains. Georgia must have been a big fan of her privacy because despite keeping the crate door open she spent the night seated in the back of her house shrouded by shadow. The only thing visible was the vague outline of a dog and her glowing green eyes. 

Now, sometimes when dogs are scared they cower and hide in their houses because it makes them feel safe. I assumed that was what was happening with Georgia. She wanted to get a lay of the land before fully engaging with us. Not a problem. When you deal with dogs as much as I used to you pick up a few tricks. My patented technique for coaxing a dog out of the cage is tossing human food near the crate’s entrance. I had ordered shrimp fried rice for dinner that day. Now, I’m not a huge fan of the addition of peas and carrot squares to my rice so I would toss them out to Shot and Mae and then toss a handful behind me to Georgia. It’s important to note, that when you toss the food to a scared animal you don’t move closer to them or get up in their business. You just set it and let them come to you. 

The first time I tossed back food I stayed in my recliner and checked to see if she was collecting her spoils out of the corner of my eye. She was not. Okay, no problem, I also don’t like peas and carrot squares. The second time around, I tossed her a pristine tiny shrimp. That was the ticket. In the corner of my eye I could see Georgia begin to sniff at the air. I kept watching her from my peripheral. It was strange though, hard to describe what I saw but I’ll try to anyway. 

Instead of the vague outline of a dog tip toeing out of the crate, I could still see her shape at the back end of her house. Mae began to grumble. Shot positioned himself at my side. The tension in the air was mounting despite an absence of movement or change. I tilted my head ever so slightly to get a better view. 

Georgia was still seated in the same spot but emerging from the crate was a set of teeth. Not her snout. How do I describe it? Well, you know when you give a horse a carrot and their lips kinda go back a little? It was that. It was a set of bare horse teeth extending out of the darkness. They inched toward the shrimp in this long and narrow cylinder of flesh. Two gaping nostrils sniffed in Mae’s direction prompting her to bark. The mouth let out a soft sigh and moved to sniff the floor boards. I watched the teeth nash down on the little shrimp and then go for the remaining peas and carrots. I  didn’t move. I was too fixed on the pale pink tendril. The mouth was dripping saliva onto my hardwood but more importantly, the appendage itself had a heart beat. It throbbed. 

As I watched on in horror, my mind fell back into memories of the day before. I was waiting in my van at the site of a hostage situation. Two dogs were on site and I waited, bored, for the SWAT team to hand them off to me. Before SWAT grabbed the dogs they grabbed the kidnapper. Or at least what was left of him. SWAT dragged his limp body from the freshly gassed house and I fixed my gaze on the mashed remains of the brain he had blown out of his head. EMS did CPR as a last stitch effort to help a dead man. His brain matter and blood drained out of what remained of his skull and onto the front lawn. I feel bad saying this, but my first thought was how strange the brain looked. It was almost like play-doh that had gone through a pasta maker. These stringy little pieces of pink and grey tissue danced around the remains of an ugly face. He convulsed as the SWAT officer handed me a little shaggy dog. I didn’t even acknowledge him. I was too focused on the second corpse that was being retrieved from the house. A little girl. The one SWAT was hoping was still alive somewhere in there. She wasn’t. She had clearly been long gone. Stiff. Stiff to the point that the plastic horse toy in her hand remained in her clutches even in death. 

“Okay! I think I’m ready for bed!” I hopped out of my chair and spun around to find Georgia out of her house and licking the rice remains off the floor. No sign of a horse mouth. Just a regular dog. Tail wagging, happy body language. This was not the case for my dogs. They remained seated by my chair even after I got up. Their hackles raised. I brushed it off. 

Before bed I brought everyone to the backyard to go to the bathroom one last time. One thing I didn’t consider before letting them loose is that Georgia was more dexterous than her large looming new siblings. The first thing she did after getting outside was hop over the little wooden fence that led to my makeshift garden graveyard. I didn’t think much of it.

It wasn’t until I called for everyone to come in that I realized Georgia was just looming over the plants in the garden. Hunched over like a drunk man ready to vomit. Her snout pointed down between rose bushes where the puppies were buried. She didn’t move. I couldn’t even tell if she was breathing. She was as still as the night air.

Mae Mae was sitting just outside of the fence, whining and watching Georgia. I called for everyone to come back inside. Mae reluctantly left her side and ran up to me. She nudged my hand with her nose and looked out to Georgia. She stayed in place, looking towards the unmoving dog and whining. She wanted me to go over there but I didn’t want to. 

My feet were firmly planted inside the house, barefoot. I had no intention of finding shoes just to go grab Georgia and bring her inside. My expression didn’t falter when I called for Georgia to come again. I had to stay firm, stay dominant. 

Georgia didn’t move. We were in a stand off. Shot and Mae both knew it. They stared up at me then over to Georgia. They were getting antsy just like I was. That is until I heard it. That unmistakable whimpering of a new born puppy. 

I bolted over to the garden with Mae and Shot in tow. The noise was so clear. I know I’m not great remembering things but I could have sworn those puppies weren’t breathing. I must have been wrong. No, that doesn’t make sense. They’d be as big as Shot now. Maybe this was a stray? Though, with how Mae was reacting I had a hard time believing that was the case. She was a good mom. She knew what her babies sounded like. Mae and I could hear the puppies clear as day. I knew it wasn’t just me. Mae wouldn’t be this excited for nothing. 

When we got to the garden I hopped the fence and began shifting through the plants. I will admit I was pretty frantic while looking for these babies.. I got down on my knees and swept the sea of pink flowers and dark evergreen leaves out of the way of the dirt. Mae’s impatient barking edged me on throughout my endeavor. My heart was beating a mile a minute, my body responded to these curious sounds with a gut retching panic. It wasn’t until I slowed my breathing that I realized the sounds weren’t coming from underground. They were coming from Georgia. 

I could feel her hot breath, or lack thereof, against my cheek. I looked up from the dirt in horror to find her pupils dilated and fixed on the dirt, jutting out of her face again like it had the day we first met. Her mouth was wide, coated in a lacquer of drool that slipped off her canines and into the shrubbery. Her muzzle was unmoving but the sounds of the dead puppy’s whimpers echoed from her mouth like a ghostly speaker. I collapsed beside her, a disheveled mess. 

“Georgia!” I blurted out before rubbing my eyes. This was a vision problem and nothing else. This was not okay. This didn’t feel okay. I broke down crying. I sat in the weeds sobbing and shaking until I felt Mae’s reassuring muzzle on my shoulder and Georgia’s cold wet nose against my arm. I didn’t need to look at her to know that everything would be normal. The sound was gone.
