I got braces at twenty-eight. I'd wanted them since I was eleven, which meant more than two decades of pressing on my crooked front tooth in the dark every night, just willing it to shift. It never did. My parents didn't take us to the dentist growing up unless something had gone seriously wrong. We weren't neglected, just poor, and dental care got pushed down the list unless someone was bleeding. Braces weren't a conversation we had.

My teeth were bad. The overlapping bottom row. The front tooth that sat slightly behind the other. The canines that came in too high and jutted outward. I learned to smile with my mouth closed, to cover it when I laughed. There's a photo from my college graduation where everyone else is grinning and I'm doing the closed-mouth thing, head slightly down, looking slightly left. I remember thinking I'd ruined it. I remember thinking that even on a day like that, I couldn't just smile like a normal person.

Dr. Vale was in the parking lot when I arrived for my first appointment, standing beside his car with a surgical mask already on.

His practice had 5.0 stars. Hundreds of reviews. What sold me were the photographs. Finished patients with the most perfect teeth I'd ever seen. The front two slightly longer, each tooth diminishing toward the back in precise increments, very white, very uniform. There was one woman my age whose review I read several times because her before picture looked like a photograph of my own mouth:

*I waited until I was an adult to finally fix my smile after growing up in a family that couldn't afford braces. Dr. Vale never made me feel embarrassed, and now I can't believe these are my teeth. Trust the process. You won't regret it!*

I made the appointment the next morning.

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

Before he could fix my teeth, he had to fix everything else. Eleven cavities. Dr. Vale never asked why I'd waited so long. He just said we had some things to take care of before we could get to the fun part. He wore the mask for every appointment.

Three months later, I was ready for braces.

Dr. Vale showed me the 3D simulation: my bottom teeth untwisting in sequence, my front tooth moving forward, my canines descending, everything settling into a smile I'd never seen on my own face before.

"Those are my actual teeth?"

He laughed. "Whose else would they be?"

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

For the first six months, everything was normal. Painful, but normal. Every time I caught my reflection and saw the brackets, I felt something close to relief. Like I was finally handling something I'd been putting off for half my life. I started smiling with my teeth in photographs for the first time. Actual smiling.

Then Dr. Vale said I needed two premolars extracted to make room.

I'd just spent all this money saving my teeth, and now two healthy ones had to go. I looked it up. It's a real and common thing. I agreed.

The first came out cleanly. The second didn't. Dr. Vale rocked it for a long time, and then something cracked, and he raised the tooth and looked at it before setting it on the tray.

"Beautiful," he said.

"What?"

"A very healthy root." Something in his eyes above the mask I couldn't interpret. "You should be pleased."

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

About five weeks later, one of the neighboring teeth felt loose. I called the office. Dr. Vale told me not to play with it. So naturally I spent three days doing nothing but.

One morning something clicked against the porcelain of my sink. The tooth was sitting in pink foam. When I touched the space with my tongue, it wasn't empty. Something hard was already just beneath the gum, a thin white edge cutting through.

Dr. Vale told me the lost tooth had undergone severe root resorption and what was coming in was residual odontogenic tissue.

"That means I'm growing a new tooth?"

"Essentially."

"At twenty-eight."

"It happens."

Root resorption is real. Supernumerary teeth are real. The images I Googled didn't look exactly like mine, but they were close enough. I convinced myself it was fine. The new tooth grew quickly, straight and very white. Then the tooth on the other side fell out. Something was waiting underneath that one too. Straight. White. Perfect.

Dr. Vale began photographing my mouth at every appointment. *You're responding beautifully*, he'd say.

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

Around month nine he recommended removing my left canine. The root had resorbed.

I said no. That tooth had been with me since I was a kid. I'd hated it every day. I'd hidden it in every photograph and covered it with my hand when I laughed. And now the thought of it being labeled medical waste was more than I could stand.

Dr. Vale didn't push. "We can stop today. It wouldn't look like the simulation."

I looked at myself in the small mirror he was holding. Half my mouth was the smile I'd wanted since I was eleven. The other half was the mouth I'd been hiding since I was eleven.

I let him take the canine. I cried in my car. Six weeks later its replacement came through the gum, straight and white and beautiful, and I stopped thinking about the tooth it had replaced.

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

It was around this time I started actually seeing the Smile Wall.

I'd walked past it dozens of times: a full wall covered with finished patients holding their signs, grinning at the camera. The finished patients didn't have similar teeth. They had identical teeth. Same white, same proportions, same slightly longer front two. Every single patient, regardless of what their before photograph showed, had ended up with exactly the same result.

One afternoon a woman came out of the back and the receptionists clapped. She'd just gotten her braces off. They gave her a sign that said *I LOVE MY NEW SMILE!* and photographed her for the wall. She sat across from me afterward, looking at her phone.

Then her face went slack.

Her lips pulled back. Then closed. Then pulled back again. Three or four times, smiling at nothing. She caught me watching.

"Sorry," I said. "Your teeth look incredible."

"Thank you."

"Are they veneers?"

"No." A pause. "You get used to letting them out."

I stood to follow the assistant back, and when I glanced over my shoulder, the woman had turned toward the corner TV. It was showing footage of a partially collapsed building, workers in orange vests picking through the rubble. She was facing it with her mouth completely open, the full arc of those white teeth aimed at the screen. Eyes open. Not blinking.

I followed the assistant down the hall and told myself I hadn't seen it.

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

A few weeks later my gums began to itch. From the inside. There's no way to address itching gums. I flossed until I bled and ran the Waterpik until my gums were raw because the pressure made it stop for seconds at a time. Normal, Dr. Vale said. Tissue changes.

One evening I was brushing before bed and something clicked against the porcelain. A bottom incisor in pink foam. Another tooth shifted when my tongue found the gap, so I took hold of it. The root came free with a wet, membranous pull. Blood filled the space, and another tooth was loose, and I don't know why I kept going. Maybe because nothing hurt. There was pressure, tissue releasing, but no pain, and without pain my hands just kept working.

Seven. Eight. Nine.

On one of the upper teeth, something came with the root that wasn't blood or tissue. A white filament, wet and thin, the diameter of fishing line. I pulled the tooth further from the socket and the thread pulled taut between the hole and my fingers, and something twitched behind my left eye, a deep involuntary jerk that made my vision strobe.

I touched the thread with one fingertip.

The black came up so fast I didn't have time to let go. Not pain, something worse, like a signal firing through me, like finding out a wire was live by completing the circuit yourself. When my vision came back I was on the bathroom floor and the thread had slid back into the socket and was gone.

I threw up. Then I rinsed until the water ran clear and looked in the mirror.

There weren't eleven holes. There were eleven new teeth, some halfway through the torn gum, some almost fully emerged. All straight. All white. All identical. My face was smeared with blood, my pajamas ruined, my gums ragged and dark. But when I smiled at my reflection I looked exactly like the simulation.

I took a picture. I know how that sounds. I couldn't help it.

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

I found the woman whose review had sent me to him. She agreed to meet me at a coffee shop.

In person, when she smiled, I could see more teeth than seemed anatomically possible. The arc went further back than it should. I asked if her teeth had fallen out during treatment.

"No."

"Did new ones grow in?" "No."

"Did Dr. Vale put something into your jaw?"

"No." A pause. "I'm doing amazing."

There were tears on her cheeks. She was smiling. The two expressions had no relationship to one another. She pressed my fingers to her cheek, against the muscle beneath her jaw, and I felt it, a faint slow vibration, like current through wire. I pulled my hand back.

Then I told her about the thread. About what I'd felt when I touched it. While I was talking, her smile spread. Not the way a reaction spreads, not because she was surprised or relieved or amused. It spread the way a plant moves toward light, automatic, directionless, and she clamped her hand over her own mouth to stop it. Her eyes above her hand were frantic.

She bit down on her own tongue. Hard. I heard it. A wet crunch.

For two seconds the smile was gone. Her eyes changed completely.

"Don't finish," she slurred through the blood.

Then it came back. She pressed a napkin to her mouth, calmly, without looking away from me. "Sorry. I do that sometimes."

I drove home and couldn't sleep. At eight-thirty the next morning I was in the parking lot outside Vale Orthodontics.

I went inside.

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

A month later I demanded to see my scans. My new teeth didn't have individual roots. They had one root, one white structure running along the ridge of my jaw, branching upward through the bone, around my remaining natural teeth, into the tissue of my face. The branches followed the paths of the facial nerves. One ran along the trigeminal pathway and turned upward behind my left eye. Another followed the nerve from my lower jaw toward the base of the skull and, at the foramen magnum, the small opening where the spinal cord passes, continued through.

"Is it in my brain?"

Silence.

"What happens if we stop?"

"At this stage, I genuinely don't know."

"And if we finish?"

"You'll have the smile I showed you on the first day."

"How much longer?"

"Six weeks."

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

The last session was the worst. Dr. Vale removed the rest of my natural teeth without anesthetic, and when I nervously laughed he just waited, and then pulled the first one and I understood. I felt everything: the pressure, the gum releasing, the root sliding out, the warm rush of blood. None of it was pain. When he pulled one molar, a long white filament came with it, unwinding like thread from a spool. Six or seven inches between the socket and his gloved hand.

"Hold still."

He cut it. My right hand seized shut. My left leg kicked the chair once without my telling it to.

"That's connected to my brain," I said.

He didn't answer.

Six weeks later my braces came off. The simulation was correct. From the front it looked like individual teeth, but when I ran my tongue behind them they joined into one continuous smooth piece, like a single bone shaped to look like many.

"I love them," I said.

That wasn’t me.

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

That was three months ago.

I still go to work. I see my family. I think, remember, feel afraid. The thoughts are mine. Most of the actions are mine. Most.

When someone compliments my teeth, my mouth opens before I decide it should. *Thank you!* I don't want to say it. *Dr. Vale did them.* Please don't ask. *He's incredible.* Please. *You should at least do a consultation.* Please don't.

I smiled at my grandmother's funeral. My sister dragged me outside and slapped me across the face. Seconds afterward, while I was recovering, she grabbed my shoulders and looked at me the way she'd look at a stranger. "What is wrong with your face?" she said. The impact had moved my head. It hadn't moved the smile at all.

Sometimes I can press my jaw shut and hold it there. Then the pressure builds behind my eyes and the lips go back and I'm smiling again, wider than last time.

I found a photograph of Dr. Vale without his mask. From a conference, a couple of years ago. His teeth are normal. Slightly yellow. The lower left incisor is crooked.

He'd never do this to himself.

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

Last week Vale Orthodontics emailed asking for a review. I pressed one star.

Pain. Immediate, total, erupting behind my eyes and through my jaw.

Two stars. Pain. Three. Pain. Four. The pain eased. Five. Nothing. Just the hum.

I typed: *Please don't go here. He put something inside my jaw. My teeth aren't teeth. The roots have reached my brain, and I'm still conscious inside all of it.*

Delete. Delete. Delete.

Then my hands typed this, and I watched them:

*I waited until I was an adult to finally fix my smile after growing up in a family that couldn't afford braces. Dr. Vale never made me feel embarrassed, and now I can't believe these are my teeth. Trust the process. You won't regret it!*

My hand pressed submit.

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

I had a check-in yesterday morning. There was a woman in the waiting room I'd never seen before, young, looking at the Smile Wall the way I used to, with the hunger of someone who's wanted something for a long time.

My before picture was now on the wall. They'd added me. There I was, the overlapping row, the too-high canines, and beside it, the after. I have no memory of sitting for that photograph.

"His reviews are insane, right?" she said. "I just booked a consultation. For Thursday."

The thing in my jaw fired deep in the bone, and I pressed both hands over my mouth, hard, arms shaking.

"Sensitive," I said through my fingers. "From the treatment."

She looked back at the Smile Wall. "I've wanted this my whole life. I used to press on my teeth as a kid, hoping they'd shift if I pushed long enough." She laughed. "Stupid, right?"

I took my hands away.

*I know exactly what that feels like.* I wanted to say it so badly. *I did that too. Every night, in the dark, for fifteen minutes, willing them to move. I know what it's like to want this so much. I know what's on the other side. I'm standing right here. Please.*

"It changed my life," I said.

The smile came the rest of the way open. The woman looked at my teeth, and her expression went soft.

"They're so beautiful."

"He's incredible. You won't regret it."

She smiled at me one last time on her way out. A real smile, her own.

I smiled back.

\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

I spent years wishing I could smile like this.

Now I can't stop.
