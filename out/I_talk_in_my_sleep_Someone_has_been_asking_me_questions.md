On Tuesday, at 3:12 in the morning, my sleep app recorded a man asking me if I live alone.

I didn't know I talked in my sleep until six weeks ago. Six weeks ago is also when Eli moved to Denver with a woman named Skyler who makes pottery. Her pottery isn't even that great. He took the air fryer. I'm over Eli, but I'm still not over the air fryer.

I couldn't sleep after he left. Like at all. So I downloaded an app called Drift. You leave your phone on the nightstand, it tracks your sleep, and it records anything louder than a whisper. In the morning, it gives you a score and a little summary, and the summaries are aggressively cheerful.

**✨ Rough night! ✨** 

**Sleep score: 41/100** 💀

**You talked in your sleep 3 times! 📢📢📢** 

**Try a wind-down routine tonight! 💆🏻‍♀️**

It turns out I talk in my sleep. Like, a lot. Mostly about work. I'm a pharmacy tech, which means I count pills by fives for eight hours a day while people yell at me about their insurance, and apparently I don't stop when I clock out. Most of my clips are me mumbling numbers. Once I very clearly said, "Ew. That's not a real dog." Another time I said, "I need to speak to a manager."

My best friend Tessa thinks this is the funniest thing that has ever happened to anyone. She FaceTimes me every morning on her drive to work, and we listen to my clips together like it's a podcast. She rates them. "Ew. That's not a real dog" got a ten. It's her ringtone for my number now.

Right after Eli left, I moved into a one-bedroom at the Ashford because it was the only place I could afford by myself. It's… Well... (picture exasperated sigh here) Fine. The walls are thin. I know my upstairs neighbor's Peloton instructor by name. He's madly in love with her and speaks to her more frequently than he actually exercises.

Two weeks after I moved in, the management company, Corwin Residential, put a keypad lock on my unit. The email said it was for emergency access. It also said we'd be charged $75 every time we used our old manual deadbolts, since those block crucial services.

Tessa texted: *that's not a fee, that's a ransom letter*

The guy who installed mine didn't say his name. He spent a long time looking around my apartment while he worked.

"One bedroom," he said. "Lot of room for one girl."

I lied and told him my boyfriend was moving in soon.

"Right..." he trailed off. Then he told me he'd done all the locks at the Marwick, downtown, back when Corwin managed that one too. When he was done, he punched in a code to test it. Four beeps and a little chime.

"You a heavy sleeper?" he asked.

"Like the dead," I said. Which, in hindsight, was not the best answer.

"Good," he said. "These beep pretty loud."

I paid the deadbolt fee exactly once. Then I stopped using it. I make $18 an hour. Seventy-five dollars is my phone bill.

Okay. Tuesday.

Drift said I talked in my sleep four times. Tessa called at 7:40 AM like always, and I put her on speaker and played the first clip.

Me, mumbling: "...forty, forty-five, fifty..."

"Boring," Tessa said. "Next."

The second one was me saying "no" a few times.

The third one started with breathing. It wasn’t my own. My breathing is slow and kind of whistly because my nose is crooked. This was closer to the phone than I ever am. Then my bed creaked, the way it does when you sit on the left side, and a man said, really softly:

"Hey. You awake?"

And I said, "Mm."

"That's okay," he said. "Stay asleep."

Then: "Do you live alone?"

And I said, "Yeah..."

The clip ended.

Tessa didn't say anything for a long time. Then she texted me, even though we were still on the phone, I think because she didn't want to say it out loud.

By lunchtime I had a whole explanation. The walls are thin. My downstairs neighbor falls asleep with the TV on. It was a show, or a podcast. Drift picks up everything. But the bed creaked. My bed only creaks on the left side when somebody sits on it. Eli used to sit there to tie his shoes. (I swear I'm over him. But who takes my air fryer???)

So Tuesday night I locked the deadbolt. Seventy-five dollars down the drain, but it was a price to pay. And I placed my old phone, the one with the cracked screen, on the bookshelf in the living room, and pointed it at the front door, recording. I tried to stay up. The last time I checked, it was 2:47 AM.

Wednesday morning, there was an email from Corwin Residential. A $75 charge. Deadbolt engaged, 3:11 AM. Twenty-four minutes after I fell asleep.

I listened to seven hours of my living room on the old phone. It's mostly fridge hum. At 3:11, there were four beeps. The keypad. Then the little chime. Then the click of the lock. Then the handle going down, and the door moving maybe an inch before the deadbolt caught it. The door eased back into the frame. Then nothing, for almost a minute.

I turned the volume all the way up and listened to that minute over and over. The fridge. The hallway light buzzing. And under all of it, so faint I almost talked myself out of it, somebody breathing through their nose. Then, right up against the door, so quiet the phone barely got it:

"Seventy-five dollars, Julia."

Here's the thing about Drift. It saves everything. I only ever listened to the excerpts from the night before. I'd never once gone back through its entire history.

I called out of work, and I sat on my kitchen floor with headphones on and went through six weeks of clips, one at a time. He's on nineteen nights. Twice the first week. Four times the week after that. This past week, every single night. The first one is the night after the locks went in. It's always around 3. It always starts the same way. The breathing. The bed. "Hey. You awake?" I always say "mm," and he always says, "That's okay. Stay asleep."

Then he asks me things.

"What's your name?" Julia. "Where'd Eli go?" Denver. "Is he coming back?" No. "His loss," he says. "Where do you work?" The pharmacy on Route 9. "What time do you leave in the morning?" Seven-fifteen. "Who's Tessa?" Tessa is Tessa. (I giggle in my sleep when I say it. I don't know why.) "Where does Tessa live?" Ninth Street. "Do you lock your deadbolt?" No.

"Good girl." I don't know how else to describe it, but you can hear him smile when he says it.

Some nights he doesn't ask anything. He just talks to me. About how I sleep with my hand underneath my cheek like a little kid. About how much easier I am to talk to like this.

"You'd never tell me any of this awake," he says on one of them.

On a clip from about two weeks ago, after the questions, he leans in close enough that the phone crackles.

"Tell Tessa I gave 'not a real dog' a ten too."

The worst one is from the Friday before that.

"What's the code on your phone, Julia?"

And I tell him. All six numbers. Mumbled, but you can hear every one.

"Perfect," he says.

And then there's last Thursday. He doesn't ask me anything on that one. There's somebody else in the room with him, farther from the phone, and they talk over me like I'm furniture.

"How much longer?" the other one asks.

He answers, but he's turned away from the phone. I've listened to it probably thirty times. It's either "after the friend's" or "after the week ends." I decided it was "week ends." It was only Wednesday. 

You can see what hours you used your phone in Screen Time. On every night he's in my clips, there's a little bar at 3 AM. Ten minutes. Fourteen. Twenty-two. Messages. Photos. Settings.

I didn't know what a stranger could do in Settings, so I googled it, and I ended up on an Apple support page that says, and I'm quoting, "If you think someone may have added an alternate appearance on your device without your permission." Face ID lets you add one extra face. It's for people who wear glasses sometimes, or have a beard sometimes, or whatever it is they do. If you've never set one up, there's a button in your Face ID settings that says Set Up an Alternate Appearance. I went and looked.

The button wasn't there.

I reset Face ID. I changed my passcode. Then I sat on the kitchen floor holding my phone in both hands like it was a dead bird.

I called Corwin Residential and got a guy named Brent. Brent listened to the whole thing. Then he asked if I'd considered that it might be my ex.

"Breakups can make girls a little..." He didn't finish the sentence.

He said their logs showed no unauthorized entries to my unit. He said the 3:11 AM attempt was a scheduled system check. He said it was really important that I stop using my deadbolt, for my own safety. Then he asked if I had somewhere I could stay for a few nights. I said my friend's.

"Where's your friend at?"

I told him the Calloway, on Ninth. "Oh, nice," Brent said. "We manage that one too."

Tessa texted: *brent sounds like he's the kind of guy that refers to women as "females"*

The police took a report. The officer asked if I had a boyfriend or a dad who could stay with me. When I played him the clips, he said, "Huh," and asked if I'd ever been evaluated for a sleep disorder.

On the bus to Tessa's, I googled the Marwick. It closed last year. The articles all say the same thing: that police found "evidence of unauthorized access to multiple units." One of them mentions a resident on the sixth floor. 6C. None of them say what happened to her.

Tessa's building has the same keypad locks as mine. Hers doesn't even have a separate deadbolt. When I pointed that out, she told me her lock guy was a sixty-year-old named Phil who showed her pictures of his grandkids and a fish he caught.

"Phil is not out here doing crimes, Julia."

I know, I know. Corwin manages her building too. But a hotel is $160 a night, my mom lives four hours away, and Tessa said if I didn't come to her, she was coming to sleep on my floor instead. I was not letting her anywhere near my apartment.

She made a frozen pizza, placed blankets on her couch, and swore she'd sleep with her bedroom door open, and I promised I'd stay awake. I opened Drift anyway, put my phone on vibrate, and tucked it under my pillow, because if something happened, I wanted proof.

Before Tessa went to bed, I texted my little sister Gabby, who is nineteen and doesn't believe in sleep.

*if i don't text you the word* "ok" *at 3:15, call 911 and give them this address.* I sent her Tessa's address. I picked 3:15 on purpose. If he came at 3:11, four minutes was enough time to know.

Tessa fell asleep around midnight. She snores a little, and I could hear her through her open door. Then I waited.

Here's what you hear in an apartment at night when you're listening for one specific thing. The fridge. The radiator ticking. A car alarm two streets over. Tessa snoring. Your own heartbeat, which gets louder the more you notice it.

At 2:36, footsteps came down the hallway outside and stopped at Tessa's door.

I sat straight up with my phone in my hand, already dialing.

Keys. A jingle, then a drop, then a guy slurring "come on" to his keys, and then the door across the hall opening and shutting. Tessa's neighbor, coming home from somewhere with a lot of beer in it. I put the phone down and let out a nervous sigh.

At 3:04, I put my phone back under my pillow so the screen wouldn't light up the room. I lay down, pulled the blanket up to my chin, and closed my eyes. I'd already decided. Whatever happened next was going to happen to the version of me that's asleep, as she was the only version he was expecting. So I breathed the way I breathe on the recordings. Slow. A little whistly.

3:11 came and went in my head maybe four times before it actually came.

At 3:11, the keypad beeped four times. I'd listened to those beeps maybe forty times that day. I knew them like a song. Then, the chime. The lock clicked. The door opened, and then closed, really gently, the way you close a door when there's a baby asleep.

One set of footsteps. I listened for a second set. 

The footsteps didn't go to the kitchen or turn on a light. They came straight to the couch, like they knew where the couch was. The cushion by my knees sank. He smelled like cold air and tobacco.

"Hey, Julia," he whispered. "You awake?"

"Mm," I said.

"That's okay. Stay asleep."

He put his hand on the blanket. Not on me. On the blanket, over my hip. Just resting there. I could feel how hot it was.

"You moved," he said.

I didn't say anything.

My mouth was filling up with saliva, and I couldn't swallow because I didn't know if sleeping people swallow. A piece of my hair had fallen across my face, and it tickled every time he breathed out. I've read that your eyes move under your eyelids when you dream. I didn't know if mine were moving too much, or not enough.

"Brent says you called him."

"Mm."

"Does Tessa live alone?"

I thought really fast for someone who was supposed to be asleep. I mumbled, "Boyfriend."

He laughed. Very softly, right next to my ear.

"She lives alone," he said. "She told me herself."

Then he didn't say anything for a long time. I could feel him looking at me. I kept breathing. Slow. Whistly.

And then my phone buzzed under my pillow. It was right under my ear. It buzzed, and buzzed, and kept buzzing, because it wasn't a text. Gabby was calling.

It was 3:15.

He went very still.

I felt his hand slide under the pillow, right next to my cheek, slow, like he was reaching under a sleeping dog. The phone left. Light came through my eyelids, pink and then white. The buzzing stopped. Then I heard typing. I have the keyboard clicks turned on. I've always thought they were cozy. Tick-tick. Then the little whoosh of a text going out. The phone buzzed one more time in his hand. He breathed out loudly through his nose. It was almost a laugh.

He slid it back under my pillow. Right where it was. "Your sister says she loves you," he whispered, and kissed me on the forehead. Wet and sloppy.

That's when my breathing went wrong. I could hear it going wrong. Short and high, through my nose, nothing like the recordings.

"You breathe different when you're awake, Julia. I've listened to you sleep forty-one nights. I know what you sound like." Forty-one. That's every night I've had the app. He patted the blanket twice, the way you'd pat a pet. "Keep your eyes closed."

I kept my eyes closed.

The cushion came back up. The footsteps went away from me, toward Tessa's room. I heard her door, which squeaks, swing all the way open.

"Hey, Tessa," he whispered. "You awake?"

"Mm," said Tessa.

"That's okay. Stay asleep."

I could have screamed. I've thought about that every minute since. I could have screamed and woken her up, and then she'd be awake in the dark with him standing over her bed, and I'd be on a couch eight steps away with my eyes shut. So I decided against it.

I count pills by fives. It was the only thing I could think to do. Five, ten, fifteen. I lay there with my eyes shut and my hand around my phone under the pillow, and I counted. Drift recorded all of it.

"Do you live alone?" he asked her.

And Tessa said, in her sleep voice, "...not tonight."

"I know," he said.

I got to four hundred and five before the footsteps came back. They stopped next to the couch. I felt him lean down.

"Girls always run to a friend's," he whispered. "They did at the Marwick too."

Then: "Night, Julia."

The keypad beeped once. The door opened and shut.

I counted to four hundred and five again before I opened my eyes.
