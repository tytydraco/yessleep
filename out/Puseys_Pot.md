Innsmouth Cape, just north of Innsmouth, was always raining. It wasn't the kind of rain that fell from the sky; rather, it felt like a gray, thick mist steaming up from the sea, heavy with salt and the stench of dead fish, weighing down on every roof tile in the small town.

My name is Elliot Vance. To others, I was probably a hopeless eccentric—a destitute scholar specializing in the bizarre folklore of New England, with pockets as empty as my reputation. If it hadn't been for the bone-chilling downpour, I probably would never have pushed open the door to that secondhand curiosity shop.

The shop sat in a dark alley near the edge of the harbor, its rotting display windows coated in a layer of grayish-white salt grime. Opening the door, I was hit by a wave of foul air—a mix of musty paper, mothballs, and the fermented smell of the sea at low tide.

Behind the counter stood old Hutchins, who possessed that peculiar look typical of the coastal town's residents: deathly gray skin, slightly bulging eyes, and a blinking reflex so slow it made your skin crawl. He stared fixedly at me and asked what I wanted in a voice as raspy as sandpaper.

I rummaged through the clutter of blackened silverware and moldy old books. Then, my gaze was drawn to a heavy shadow in the corner.

It was a cast-iron pot.

It was unusually large, measuring a full fourteen inches in diameter and heavy as a headstone dug up from a graveyard. The body was a deep, near-black forest green, covered in uneven patches of rust, which in some places took on a dark crimson hue like dried blood. The moment my palm touched the raw iron, an uncontrollable shiver ran down my spine.

It wasn't ordinary coldness. The chill carried a bizarre sucking sensation, crawling up from my fingertips, through my wrists, and straight into my marrow.

"Oh, you noticed that one," the old man chuckled strangely, revealing sparse, yellowed teeth. "That's Pusey's Pot."

According to Hutchins, forty years ago, an eccentric named Thomas Pusey lived in a cliffside shack at the edge of town. The man didn't fish, nor did he speak to anyone, but every night purple-black smoke would rise from his chimney. The smoke smelled like boiling rotten seaweed, yet it was so intensely fragrant it made you want to bite off your own tongue. Later, Pusey mysteriously vanished. Nothing was missing from his house except this pot sitting on the kitchen stove, its inside as clean as if it had been licked by a tongue.

As a folklorist, this kind of urban legend held a fatal attraction for me. I turned the heavy iron pot over and looked at its bottom.

There were no manufacturer's marks. Instead, a series of complex geometric lines were etched deep into the black iron surface, intersecting at distorted sharp angles and curves. As my eyes followed the lines, a wave of intense vertigo struck me—the geometric patterns were logically impossible, appearing to sink inward and bulge outward at the same time, leaving a warped afterimage on my retinas as if extending into some invisible dimension.

Possessed, I pulled out my last five dollars and bought it. Carrying it back to my rented room, I felt as though the pot’s weight was constantly shifting—sometimes light as a paper lantern, sometimes so heavy it nearly crushed my ribs.

Back in my second-floor apartment in an old wooden building, the storm outside beat furiously against the windowpanes.

I placed the pot on the gas stove, poured in two cups of filtered water, and prepared to boil some macaroni for dinner. I lit the burner, and crimson flames began licking the bottom of the pot.

Normally, a thick cast-iron pot like this takes a while to heat up. Yet, when I turned to grab the noodles and turned back, I froze in my tracks.

Barely ten seconds had passed.

The water was already boiling, but it wasn't a normal boil.

No white steam rose from the surface, and no bubbles rushed upward. The water had turned into an extremely viscous texture, like transparent pine resin. Reflected by the black pot bottom and the firelight, the liquid was slowly spinning along those bizarre geometric grooves.

It was a perfect, deep, and utterly silent vortex.

I bent down and stared into the pot. As the water spun, the lines etched into the iron bottom seemed to come alive, squirming beneath the surface like black worms. What terrified me most was that the center of the vortex was aligned perfectly with my eyes.

That wasn't a pot of water. It was a pupil. A massive, cold, and hungry eye made of liquid, staring back at me across an endless void.

At the same time, an indescribable odor wafted from the pot.

It was entirely unlike pure water. It was a deeply contradictory olfactory experience: on one hand, it carried the stench of the deepest ocean floor, full of millennia-old mud and decaying marine life; on the other hand, beneath that stench lay an incredibly rich, exotic, and sweet aroma of spices. The scent rushed straight to my brain like a cold silver needle, instantly piercing through my self-control.

Saliva pooled in my mouth, dripping from the corner of my lips. I didn't even realize my body was trembling slightly.  
"What... what on earth is this?" I muttered to myself, my eyes locked on the bottomless vortex of liquid.

Outside, a flash of lightning ripped through the dark sky. In that brief illumination, I thought I saw a ring of tiny, human-like white teeth emerge along the rim of the black iron pot. But when the thunder crashed a moment later, everything returned to normal.  
Only that soul-shaking fragrance grew thicker and thicker in the cramped kitchen.

The macaroni that night was the most incredible thing I had ever tasted.  
I had only used cheap dried noodles and half a can of nearly expired meat sauce, but the moment I scooped them out of that black pot, the texture of the food was entirely transformed. The noodles were a near-translucent dark red, and the thick sauce squirmed slightly on the plate, giving off that scent of deep-sea mud mixed with exotic sweetness.

When the first bite slid down my esophagus, my brain felt as if it had been hit with electricity. It wasn't simple deliciousness; it was a violent, almost aggressive ecstasy. Every single one of my taste buds screamed and trembled. The flavor tugged at my consciousness, flashing visions of massive tentacles waving slowly in freezing waters within eternal darkness. My brain screamed that this was poison, a curse, but my hand moved with a mind of its own, reaching toward Pusey's pot once more.

I didn't even bother to chew, frantically shoving the food into my mouth until my tongue blistered from the heat and the plate was scraped white, making a piercing screeching sound.

From that day on, I fell from grace.

I stopped using any other cookware. Whether frying eggs, boiling soup, or searing meat, I used only "Pusey's Pot." And I soon discovered the pot had its own rules. It required no salt, no pepper; any ordinary seasoning put into it would be broken down into a dead, stagnant bitterness. It wanted meat—massive amounts of meat, rich in moisture and fat.

As time passed, my body began to undergo transformations that terrified even me.

The first thing to change was my skin. My face, originally pale from a lack of sunlight, gradually turned a sickly, deathly gray. It felt damp and cold to the touch, like the skin of a dead fish fresh from the ocean. I developed a fierce aversion to sunlight; whenever I stepped near the window, my skin would burn with a searing pain. I drew every curtain in the house, letting darkness breed in the rooms.

Ordinary food became a lethal poison to me. Once, I tried to eat a bite of my old favorite, toasted bread, but the moment it hit my stomach, it triggered violent spasms. I knelt before the toilet and vomited severely, but what came out was nothing but black, seaweed-scented slime.

My stomach had been completely domesticated by the pot. I could only eat what was cooked inside it.  
Meanwhile, dreams began to invade my sleep.

Whenever I closed my eyes, my consciousness sank into a sunless, deep-sea abyss. In those dreams, I walked among black stone structures that defied Euclidean geometry.

Towering pillars and warped walls intersected at completely wrong, sharp angles, stretching toward a frozen sky. In the shadows of those buildings, countless lidless, massive eyes stared down at me. I heard a sound, like thousands of damp claws crawling across a beach, or a primeval, viscous chanting.

Every time I woke up in terror, I found myself lying naked on the cold kitchen floor, my cheek pressed against the black iron pot. And the pot, without the stove being lit, would radiate a faint, subtle warmth.

Even more horrifyingly, the geometric patterns at the bottom of the pot changed.

One evening, I bought a few pounds of fresh beef liver to toss into the pot. The moment I lit the burner and the flames shot up, I was shocked to find that the geometric lines etched into the black iron bottom were bulging slightly, like human blood vessels.

They were pulsing.

As the firelight flickered, those twisted lines seemed to expand and contract beneath the surface, prying open tiny fissures in the black iron. Those gaps contained no iron; instead, they revealed a dark red, throbbing flesh texture. The pot was breathing. While enduring the searing flames, it let out a purring sound similar to a satisfied feline, but to my ears, it sounded more like the muffled shrieks of countless tiny souls being fried in hot oil.

Ordinary beef and pork could no longer satisfy it. The food it produced began to taste bland, and that brain-rushing, supreme deliciousness was fading. The pot was resisting; it was using those pulsing patterns to send me a message: it was hungry. It needed better, higher-grade, more spiritually resonant "ingredients."

I looked at my own emaciated, clammy hands, my fingernails turning blue and brittle from prolonged malnutrition. My sanity told me to throw this godforsaken pot into the ocean, but my stomach convulsed in a frantic spasm, urging me, forcing me to find a sacrifice worthy of filling this divine trough.

I had to know what Thomas Pusey had put inside it back then. I had to uncover the secret of this pot.

To soothe the razor-sharp spasms in my stomach, I began a frantic search for clues about Thomas Pusey. Like a massive, light-shunning insect, I slipped into the town’s musty public library late at night. I poured over local newspapers from the past fifty years, church baptismal records, and yellowed police archives.

As my investigation went deeper, a bizarre jigsaw puzzle slowly unfolded before me.

In a corner of an 1898 issue of the *Innsmouth Chronicle*, I found a joint petition filed by Pusey’s neighbors. The neighbors of that era complained that Pusey’s cabin produced unbearable noises late at night. The sound was described as "like dozens of madmen chanting completely out of tune, mixed with the harsh crackle of large amounts of meat frying in boiling oil." Some even claimed to have seen Pusey at low tide during the dead of night, walking alone to the slimy, seaweed-covered reefs to trade with dark, half-man, half-fish silhouettes emerging from the waves.  
But the most critical piece of evidence before Pusey’s disappearance appeared in the diary of the local priest at St. Jude’s Church.

The entry read: *"November 12th. Pusey came to see me. His appearance is no longer human; his eyes bulge out, and there are slits behind his ears. He fanatically claimed to have found a holy relic—The Trough of Stars. He said physical pain is a feast for the void, and through proper geometric resonance, that pot can transform flesh and blood into pure spiritual pleasure. But the price is hunger, an endless hunger. I saw beneath his cuffs; the flesh on his wrists had been mostly hacked away..."*  
Reading this, my brain ground to a halt. The flesh on his wrists?

Trembling, I shut the diary and practically stumbled out of the library. The town was currently shrouded in the dead silence that precedes a storm. I didn't return to my room; instead, I followed the steep coastal mountain path toward the abandoned, half-cliff-hanging shack that was Pusey’s old home.

The wooden cabin had long since rotted, its walls covered in black mold and barnacles. I used a crowbar to break the rusted lock and stepped onto a floor that oozed thick seawater with every stride, heading straight for the basement.

The basement was thick with a smell so pungent it made me gag—it was the smell of "Pusey's Pot" burning, magnified a hundred times over.

Beneath a pile of rotting fishing nets in the corner, I found a heavy iron box wrapped in oilcloth. Opening it revealed a journal bound in a coarse leather that felt slightly warm, like human skin.

The handwriting was done in a dark red ink that had blackened over time. It was Pusey’s notebook.

The pages were filled with mad ravings intertwined with numerous unnamable geometric symbols, even mentioning ancient names like Dagon and Cthulhu. 

Pusey wrote in the journal that this pot was not a product of Earth; it was a "trough" left behind by those ancient entities when they crossed the freezing starry skies.

Those twisted geometric lines etched into the bottom of the pot were a cross-dimensional transformation matrix. It could distill the basest matter and the most painful agonies of the surface world into a supreme delicacy that moved the star-born gods.

I flipped to the very last page. The handwriting there had become incredibly erratic, massive, and wild, with some parts of the paper even torn through by clawing fingers:  
*"They are watching. They are waiting. Ordinary livestock can no longer appease them, nor can it appease my stomach. This pot is alive; it possesses an unseeable appetite. I understand now... I finally understand. To fully complete this matrix, to obtain that ultimate flavor that transcends the boundaries of the universe, the final ingredient required is nothing wordly."*  
*"It requires the cook himself. Only by offering one's own agony, flesh, and blood can the soul merge with the starry skies in the most absolute, ultimate state of deliciousness."*

The journal ended abruptly there.

Standing in the pitch-black basement, a bolt of pale lightning suddenly cracked outside, stretching my shadow into a grotesque shape. I felt no fear. On the contrary, a wave of indescribable ecstasy and liberation washed over me instantly.

At that exact moment, the spasms in my stomach ceased, replaced by a novel, holy state of starvation.

"So that's how it is..." I murmured, touching my cheek. By now, my skin had completely transformed into a dead-fish greenish-gray, and my finger joints were thick and warped.

Turning around, I sprinted through the pouring rain back toward my apartment with maddened steps. The pot was waiting for me.

I ran all the way back to my second-floor kitchen. The storm had drenched me completely, but inside me burned an unquenchable, morbid fever.

"Pusey's Pot" sat quietly on the stove. Without me even lighting a fire, the pot itself radiated a terrifying heat, warping the surrounding air.

I looked into the empty pot. The geometric lines had now completely transformed into translucent, throbbing, dark-red flesh vessels. As if alive, they spread and intertwined frantically across the black iron walls. To my ears, those lines were no longer dead things; they were broadcasting a call from the void—a viscous, cold psalm that smelled of deep-sea reefs.  
In my eyes, the pot was no longer a container. It was a gateway, a massive pupil looking out into the hungry stars and the eternal troughs of the old gods.  
I knew I might be mad. No, in the eyes of those self-proclaimed sane mortals, I was undoubtedly a hopeless lunatic. A madman willingly walking toward destruction, willingly consumed by the dark.

But what did it matter?

For the past thirty years, I had lived like a shadow in a dark corner, enduring the cold eyes of the world and bitter poverty. Now, I held the supreme secret of the cosmos. That ultimate pleasure transcending the limits of human perception was beckoning to me across a thin layer of skin. Compared to this soul-shaking ecstasy, the "sanity" prized by the world was nothing but worthless dust.

I was happy. It was an absolute happiness that transcended the mortal coil, standing shoulder to shoulder with ancient gods.

A manic smile spread across my face. My hands trembled, not from fear, but from sheer impatience. I bypassed the refrigerator; the cheap dead meat inside was only fit to feed numb mortals.

Turning around, I slowly drew a heavy, sharp cleaver from the knife rack.  
Flames licked frantically beneath the pot. The empty iron began to hiss and sizzle, as if demanding its first ingredient.

I took a deep breath. That scent of rotten seaweed mixed with holy sweetness made every cell in my body sing. I laid my left arm flat against the grease-stained cutting board.

"For the final feast," I whispered softly.  
The moment the blade fell, a surge of intense pleasure flooded my nerves like a tsunami, and I couldn't help but laugh out loud. My laughter echoed through the cramped kitchen, blending with the thunder outside. With a trembling right hand, I threw a steaming, fresh chunk of my own flesh—thick with my own genetic code—heavy into that black abyss.

The instant the flesh hit the searing iron walls, it let out a sharp screech like thousands of people screaming at once. A mother-of-pearl sheen, shimmering with a bizarre iridescent glow, rippled through the rendering fat at the bottom of the pot.

That indescribable aroma of meat, bearing a destructive temptation, completely submerged me in that final moment.

Smiling, I raised the blade once more.

**One week later.**

The rain at Innsmouth Cape still hadn't stopped. In the air, besides the perennial stench of dead fish, there was now a bizarre, foul odor that drew every stray dog on the street to gather downstairs, barking frantically.

Two local detectives used their batons to smash open the door of the second-floor apartment.

"Damn it, what is that smell..." the younger detective choked, covering his mouth and nose, nearly vomiting right in the entryway.

When they cautiously stepped onto the creaking floorboards and into the kitchen, the scene before them left their minds completely blank.

The room was entirely empty. There were no signs of a struggle, nor any clues suggesting an intruder. There was only a heavy, pitch-black antique cast-iron pot sitting quietly on the gas stove, which had warped slightly from the extreme heat.

The pot was unnaturally clean.

The walls and bottom shone like a mirror, as if it had just been meticulously and fanatically licked clean thousands of times by some tongue. In the center of the black iron bottom sat nothing but a pool of thick grease roughly the size of a palm.

Under the dim emergency lighting, that grease actually refracted a strange color, shimmering like the northern lights with a pearlescent, mother-of-pearl glow. It was absolutely not a fat that could be produced by humans, or any known earthly creature.

The two detectives stared fixedly at the pot. They failed to notice that, in the reflection of their own retinas, the black lines at the bottom of the pot seemed to pulse with a tiny, imperceptible movement, matching their breathing like throbbing blood vessels.

"Where's Mr. Vance? Where did he go?" The younger detective's voice carried a tremor he didn't even notice himself.  
The older detective didn't answer. He just stood dazed by the stove. The moment he was enveloped by that strange aroma—a mix of decay and ultimate sweetness—this veteran cop, who had seen his fair share of corpses, uncontrollably began to salivate.

It was a flavor he had never smelled in his entire life, and one he would never be able to forget. In every deep night to come, no matter how sumptuous the food before him, the scent of meat that lingered in this kitchen would haunt his stomach like a cancer, tormenting him until the day he died.
