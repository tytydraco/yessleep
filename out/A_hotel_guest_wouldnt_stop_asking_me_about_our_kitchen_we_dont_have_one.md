I work as a clerk at a hotel. Worked, I should say after what happened. I shouldn’t be upset about it, not like it was my forever job or I particularly enjoyed it. Definitely not like I want to ever set foot near that place again. But...I don’t know. I’d worked there for four years. It had started to feel like a second home, a second family. 



You watch scary movies and it always begins with some ominous event and it’s obvious that something bad is going to happen. You just want to shake those idiots onscreen and tell them to stop being so dumb. Maybe I was dumb too. My therapist says thinking like that isn’t beneficial, but it’s hard not to replay events. And when you replay events you think about what you did wrong.



It started with a check-in. I was on an 5-3 that day. 5pm to 3am. It wasn’t a big hotel, but management wanted the desk manned around the clock. Shifts would overlap an hour each side to allow for issues. Check-in opened at 3pm, so by the time I started at 5pm most of our expected guests for the day were already there. You’d always get a couple of Johnny-come-latelies though. He was one of them. 



He was a normal looking guy, average height, clean shaven. Kind of guy you’d struggle to pick out of a lineup. Not handsome, not ugly, not remarkable at all. He wore cargo pants and a white shirt buttoned all the way to the top. 



“I would like to check in.” He said, placing his driver’s license and credit card on the desk. He smiled at me blandly and I spoke to him in my best customer service voice as I got him checked in. 



“OK, so you’ll be in room 212, elevators are just behind me to the right, let me know if you need anything and we hope you enjoy your stay.” I removed his key card from the machine and tried to hand it to him. He frowned.



“What time do you stop serving breakfast?”



“We have a coffee machine and a vending machine stocked with pastries daily. They’re located just to the left of the entryway there.” I said, gesturing behind him to where the two machine sat humming quietly. 



“Thank you so much, you’re a peach.” He said, that bland smile on his face. He took the keycard, finally.



“Happy to help.” I tried to sound chipper still and hoped that my cringing at being called peach had been entirely internal. With that Bill headed to the elevator and presumably to his room. That was it.  No thunder and lightning, no double entendres that hinted at malicious intent, no sense of foreboding. So, while now I know I should have got up from my desk and ran, I didn’t do that. I stayed there and drew pictures of frogs in funny hats in between responding to queries from guests. 



It was around 7:30pm and I had just finished one of a frog in a wimple, which I called mother Toad-resa, when the phone rang. It was an internal call, from room 213. I picked it up. 



“Babbington front desk.” 



“Oh, hello, sorry to bother you.” Bill said, his voice as dull as his smile had been. “Is the kitchen still open?” 



It took me a little by surprise, but guests had called before with similar questions.



“I’m sorry sir, I’m afraid we don’t have a kitchen here at the Babbington.” I said. There were several restaurants that offered takeout but I wasn’t supposed to say that. We wouldn’t stop a guest from ordering, but we weren’t supposed to encourage it either. The line was silent for a moment and I thought he may have hung up, but then I heard a rustling noise before he hung up.



“Goodnight to you too, ass hat.” I said into the receiver before dropping it back into its cradle. Mother Toad-resa stared at me with judgemental eyes. “What? I didn’t say it to him?” I said to her. She still didn’t seem impressed so I decided to draw her a boyfriend to improve her mood. I drew a guy with a skateboard and a helmet, Toady Hawk, maybe that would chill her out. 



The night shift dragged on, as it always did. My attention would be drawn to the entrance doors every time a zip of yellow, then red, light went by as a car travelled along the quiet road. Mother Toad-resa and Toady Hawk had gotten married and had a multiple children. Frog Astair, with his top hat and cane; Ribbit the Bruce, with a painted face and crown; Sitting Bullfrog, with the feather on his head; and Rosie the Ribbiter, with her blue overalls and polkadot bandana. I was in the process of giving them a newborn, Ribbit Tadpole, when the phone rang again. I glanced at the time on the computer. 10:15pm. 



“Babbington front desk.” External calls didn’t come through after 9, so I didn’t have to look to know it was a guest. The line was silent for a moment, then he spoke. 



“Is the kitchen still open?” He said. Bill again, sounding groggy as if he’d just woken up. 



“I'm sorry, sir. We don’t have a kitchen here at the Babbington.” I told him again. Silence, again, and I was about to put the phone down when I heard him speaking.



“-sure?” Was all I caught of the sentence as I quickly moved the phone back up. 



“Sorry, I didn’t quite catch that.”



“I said: are you sure?” Bill said. Now I couldn’t tell if he was half asleep or all drunk. 



“Quite sure, sir, have a good evening.” I said. He made a sound, a scoff or maybe a growl, and the line went dead. I hung up and returned to my sketching. What was supposed to be a tadpole looked more like a turd. I crossed it out and drew a little gravestone instead. R.I.P. Ribbit Tadpole, taken too soon. 



I finally got around to sorting through the booking requests to make sure our system hadn’t double booked a room again by the time midnight rolled around. It was almost invariably pointless, but the system had made that mistake enough times that we were expected to do it every evening. It was the sort of task that causes your mind to glaze over and go on autopilot. Comparing emails to a spreadsheet and a spreadsheet to a calendar. By the time I was done the place behind my eyes hurt. I glanced at the time: 12:34am. How had it only been 30 minutes? It was still two and a half hours until I was done, and an hour and a half until Jeremy’s shift started. It was the sweet spot of a shift alone that dragged on and on. Each second on a clock taking ten minutes to pass.



The phone rang again and I groaned; why was a guest calling now? I looked at the phone and saw it was room 213 again. I picked up.



“I’m sorry, sir. We don’t have a kitchen at the Babbington.” I said.



“What?” Bill asked. He sounded lucid, if confused. 



“Sorry, I-" I stammered, cringing at my own stupidity. “Sorry, this is, this is the Front Desk. Babbington front desk. How can I help?”



“Uh, ok. Yeah. I was just wondering if the kitchen was still open?” Bill said. I could have screamed. I no longer felt stupid, I felt insane. Insane because I had to deal with Mr ‘Is The Kitchen Open’. 



“Sir, as I explained to you when you called earlier, I’m afraid we don’t have a kitchen at the Babbington.” 



“You don’t?” 



“No, sir.” 



“You’re sure?” 



“Quite sure.” I was grinding my teeth at having to maintain a pleasant tone with him. 



“There used to be a kitchen here.” He sounded certain.



“I can assure you there has never been a kitchen here at the Babbington.” Not something I knew for definite, truth be told, but I did feel confident about it. For one thing there was nowhere a kitchen would fit, unless you stuck a tiny stove in the manager's office in the corner by the filing cabinet. Bill made that noise again and ended the call. I dropped my head on the desk; 2am couldn’t come soon enough. Jeremy could deal with him. 



I guess I drifted off because suddenly the phone was ringing again and now it was past 1. I looked at the number and my heart sank until I realised it didn’t say 213, but 214. I sighed gratefully and picked up the receiver. 



“Babbington front desk.” 



“You said the kitchen was closed.” Bill's voice snaked down the line and I recoiled. His dead tones now had a hint of malice. I swallowed in a petty attempt to compose myself. 



“Sir, please return to your own room or I will be forced to call the police.” I told him, as if I wasn’t calling them no matter what. 



“Why didn’t you open the kitchen for me? The chef had to let me in himslef. His meat was still raw.” 



I hung up. I hung up and dialled 911 immediately. I told them I thought  one of the guests had attacked someone and they said they’d send someone as soon as possible. I didn’t know how long that would be. I didn’t like the thought of just sitting out in the open anymore. My first thought was to hide in one of the hotel rooms but that was stupid. I didn’t know where Bill was, he could have been waiting in the corridor for me. 



I remembered the manager’s office behind me. It was kept locked, but there was a key at reception for emergencies. I scrambled around searching the drawers and desks for the key. I had just grabbed it when I heard the ping of an elevator. My hand was shaking so violently I couldn’t get it in the lock. I risked a glance at the elevator; the doors rumbled as they slid open. I managed to get the key in and open the door just as I saw a figure that could only be Bill step out. I darted inside and quickly twisted the lock. My heart was going too fast and I thought I would pass out any moment from the strain of it. I slid down, resting against the door, trying to get my breathing under control.



Footsteps approached. I held my breath. Next to my head the door handle turned so slowly that it squealed in pain. When the door didn’t open, the handle returned to normal and the footsteps faded. I allowed myself to breathe again, falling into great heaving sobs. I lay like that against the door crying until I heard the sound of the front doors opening. 



“Hello, police!” Came the voice from outside. My crying turned to laughter I was so relieved. The officer must have heard me because he rushed over to the door and began knocking on it. “Ma'am? Ma'am can you hear me? Are you alright?”



“Yes, yes I’m ok.” I said, sniffing back the tears. 



“OK,” The officer said, sounding relieved. “I need you to unlock the door for me now, ma'am, and let me into the kitchen.” I was hovering over the lock when that last word sent an arc from it causing me to jerk my hand away. Then there was a slapping, slobbering sound, as if he had started voraciously licking the door. 



“Come on.” The man on the other side of the door said. His voice now flat and lifeless and recognisably Bill. “I know the kitchen’s open, just unlock the door.” 



Every muscle, every instinct screamed at me to run, but I couldn’t. I was trapped in the tiny office with nothing but an inch and a half of fibreboard protecting me from whoever, or whatever that thing was. I pushed the desk, the chair, the filing cabinet, anything that looked like it had any weight to it, in front of the door. Then I curled up in a corner away from the door and I wept. 



Exhaustion drove me to unconsciousness at some point and I awoke to bangs and crashes as the door splintered open. I screamed so hard my vision started to go black and I desperately reached out for anything I could use as a weapon.



“It's ok, you’re ok!” A man called from the doorway. He was dressed like a fireman and was trying to clear a path to me. I swung at him with a keyboard, the only thing I’d managed to grab, and he backed away from me, palms out. “Ma'am you’re ok. We’re here to help.” From behind him a police officer popped his head in, followed by a very grey looking Jeremy. 



“Oh god, I thought you were dead!” Jeremy pushed past the officer and fireman and pulled me into a hug. He was bawling and smelled faintly of sick. I greedily accepted the hug, thankful to be out of whatever nightmare I had found myself in. 



Apparently Jeremy had arrived at 2am to find bloody handprints everywhere. He’d called the cops, who escalated the dispatch since no one had been assigned following my call. Three guests were dead. I don’t know the details, I couldn’t bear to find out. The doors to their rooms had all been opened without a keycard; they’d been opened from the inside. 



I couldn’t work at the hotel after that. I moved back in with my parents. I just couldn’t face being alone after dark. I still can’t. It’s such a stupid phrase but I hear it every night as I’m drifting off to sleep.



“Is the kitchen still open?”
