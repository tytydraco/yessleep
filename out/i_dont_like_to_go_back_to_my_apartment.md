I did everything I could to avoid going home. Eating out, begging my friends to stay over at their place, taking long walks in the park—I’d used any and every excuse in the book just to feel marginally safer than I did in that horrid apartment building. No matter how unpredictable I made my schedule, or at what time of day I would walk through those cold metal doors and onto the linoleum floor of the elevator, I never had to look to know that he would soon follow. I would feel the weight of the elevator floor shift and plunge right before the doors slid shut, my heart quickly following. I’d pick the 23^(rd) floor, and he the 25^(th). The whirrs of the machinery would impregnate the silence, and I would watch those orange numbers increase with every. Agonizing. *Tick*.

He was always watching; I didn’t have to glance at him to know.

From the way his face tilted—ever so slightly—towards me, lips curled in a smile, to his stifled breathing, to the way the tips of my fingers would prick and tingle from the waves of goosebumps that ran down my spine—I always knew.

Sometimes, he would try to make light conversation. He’d ask things like if I remembered to bring an umbrella on rainy days, or what job I worked, or why I would always come home at different times of the day.

I never knew how to reply to his questions, but forcing myself to not automatically smile was a close contender.

They teach you that—the adults—you know? That you should be pleasant and friendly and easy to be around. That’s how you get people to like you. That’s how people can tell you’re a good girl.

I hated myself the most when I’d feel my lips begin to pull apart with a tremble in my chin. When the edges of my mouth would have pushed my cheeks stiff, showing my bared teeth before I’d let my expression fall. Sometimes, when I was too lightheaded from the fumes of his strong cologne to control myself, a hysterical half-giggle would escape my chest. My breaths would come in short wheezes afterward, trying to pretend I had actually coughed. I hated that I had so little control over my own body. I hated that he would always read my expressions and behaviors the way he wanted them to mean.

When the doors would finally open on the 23^(rd) floor, I made sure to never look back. I never tried to give him any doubt as I walked towards the right. I’d round the corner and wait, listening for footsteps over the pounding of my own heartbeat. I would stay rooted to the spot before I would leave a small sigh of relief at hearing the elevators close with a soft creak.

And then I’d take a peek.

It would only be after I see an empty corridor and the illuminated number 24 would I mechanically walk two floors down to my apartment floor. At home, I stripped bare and furiously washed all of my clothing by hand until my skin flaked and my hands became red and tender to the touch. Dumping month’s worth of fabric scents on my clothing in a matter of weeks was still never enough to purge his scent from my home.

My friends and family thought I was crazy for doing that every day since I knew of his existence. They said I was being paranoid—they still do. Maybe I am. Maybe I’m not. Their words never dissuaded me from my ritual. It kept me sane.

There was something different about him that day. His hair was gelled and combed back, his cologne even more nauseatingly strong than usual, and in his hands was a bouquet of red roses. He looked nervous. I felt all the blood drain out of my face before he had even said anything.

I felt the same relief of a sea-sick sailor on dry land when I felt the solid tiles of the corridor under my heels. My legs were weak and shaking, and I could not see where I was stepping as I backed out of the elevator, but I didn’t dare look away. I didn’t dare turn my back on him.

I saw him slowly lower his hands to his side, the bouquet of roses falling limp as he stood under the glare of the luminescent white light. I saw the unreadable—and terrifying—neutral expression on his face as the doors slowly closed shut.

I couldn’t sleep for weeks after that. He haunted me wherever I was, and when I’d toss and turn, I’d feel the vestige of his presence lingering. I couldn’t decide if it was a blessing when I didn’t see him for several weeks after my rejection. For once, things went back to how they always should’ve. I was relieved.

And then it happened.

I had just gotten off the metro and was walking home when I saw the lights in the distance. They flashed—oranges and blues and reds and white. As I walked closer, the sounds of what I had initially thought to be the chattering of a party became the wailing of people and sirens. I don’t remember walking the rest of the path down the street before the yellow tape stopped me from getting any closer. There was a police officer—or maybe a firefighter; it doesn’t matter—with a kind face and an even kinder voice who relayed the news in the same slow and gentle cadence a person would use to talk to a frightened child. Their words came to me in fragments; it all sounded so distant over the static in my ears.

"—a terrible tragedy."

"No survivors—"

"—floor covered in blood—"

"—stay—hotel—"

"—return—clean up—"

"23rd floor."

I was inconsolable. No one could understand why. They couldn't understand the crushing weight of my guilt when they finally took off the tape that blocked everyone from the 23rd floor. They didn't see what I saw. They didn't see how the crevices between tiles were stained just a little more reddish-brown than before. They didn't see how every hinge sparkled new because every door had to be replaced. Nor would they ever have been able to imagine how the air was permeated with stench of bleach and cheap air fresheners—not because the cheap clean-up crew management had hired had been through, but rather, to hide the stench of stale viscera.

Men, women, children, and pets…

I suppose I shouldn’t have been surprised when I opened my door to find a bouquet of wilting roses at my doorstep when I did.

He was anything if not persistent.
