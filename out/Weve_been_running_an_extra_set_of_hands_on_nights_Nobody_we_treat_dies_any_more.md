Control doesn't know about him. Our station manager doesn't know, the hospitals don't know, and if the people who decide whether I'm fit to remain registered ever find out, I will probably never work on an ambulance again. There are fourteen people alive because Jo and I kept that secret.

I've been clean for fourteen months. Opioids, mostly tablets by the end, although eventually I stopped caring what was printed on the packet as long as something in it softened the day. I got into them after a back injury, got very good at explaining why what I was doing wasn't technically addiction, and admitted the truth shortly before somebody else was going to do it for me.

I didn't steal drugs from patients. I came back to work under supervision, drug testing and enough restrictions that for a while I felt less like a paramedic than somebody being allowed to dress up as one. Most of those restrictions have gone now, but people knowing is its own restriction. If an ampoule rolls under a cupboard when I'm on station, everybody becomes very interested in finding it quickly.

My regular crewmate is Jo Mercer. She's fifty-one, has worked ambulance longer than I've been an adult and eats an apple at approximately three every morning even if we're parked outside a nightclub with somebody vomiting into a police officer's hat. She volunteered to take me when I returned, although she later admitted our team leader had asked because Jo was one of the few people trusted to supervise someone without either mothering them or making them want to resign.

On my first shift back she put her coffee on the dashboard and told me there would be no inspirational speeches.

"If you fuck up, I'll tell you. If you don't fuck up, I'll tell everyone else."

That was exactly what I needed. Fourteen months later, I still wanted the second part more than I like admitting.

The first night we picked him up was a Thursday near the end of November. It had been raining since dark, proper cold rain that made the A-road shine under headlights, and we'd already done a care-home fall, abdominal pain in student accommodation and a drunk man who called 999 because he had lost one shoe. At 02:43 we were sent to a collision on the A417, pedestrian versus car, caller reporting one person in the carriageway.

Jo drove while I ate half a cereal bar that tasted of cardboard and found the other half stuck inside my trouser pocket two days later.

Police hadn't arrived when we got there. A hatchback had stopped diagonally across the near lane with its bonnet folded upward and the windscreen punched inward on the passenger side. The driver was walking in circles beside it with both hands over his mouth, repeating the same sentence.

"He just stepped out. He just stepped out."

The pedestrian was about twenty metres behind the car. His left leg was folded underneath him in a direction that put the sole of his shoe beside his hip, most of the front of his coat had been torn away and something pale showed through the centre of his chest. From where I stood, he looked dead enough that the clinical part of my brain had already moved on to the driver.

Jo still went to him first. She checked while I cut what remained of his coat, and the rain diluted the blood on the road into dark threads that ran through the grit around my knees. The blood actually coming out of him was almost black, with the thickness and surface shine of used engine oil, and individual drops remained rounded against his skin for several seconds before sliding away.

"That's not right," I said.

"Nothing about him's right."

No pulse. No breathing. Pupils fixed. One side of the chest was open enough that I could see something beneath the ribs moving with a slow turning motion that wasn't a heartbeat and didn't match anything I knew how to name.

We decided to move him off the carriageway while we waited for police and the second crew. Traffic had already stopped in both directions, so there wasn't actually much reason to hurry, but I didn't like kneeling beside him and neither did Jo. We got the scoop under him, and when I lifted my end something shifted inside his torso with the heavy slosh of a half-full hot-water bottle.

We put him on our trolley and covered him.

The thing beneath the blanket sat up.

Jo dropped the monitor. I stepped backwards hard enough to strike my shoulder against the open ambulance door, and the blanket slid into the man's lap while he turned his ruined face slowly between us. The left leg was still wrong, the chest was still open and a strip of scalp hung over one eye, but his expression was calm.

"Bag."

His lips completed the word before the sound reached me. It was less than half a second, just enough that I saw his mouth close while the vowel was still coming out.

Jo didn't move. He pointed past her toward the car.

"Bag."

The driver collapsed.

One second he was upright and the next he folded beside the driver's door. I ran because whatever had just sat up behind me, there was a living patient on the road and training is useful to fall back on when your brain has stopped offering sensible suggestions. By the time Jo joined me his skin was grey, his pulse was disappearing and there was a diagonal steering-wheel injury across his abdomen that I should have noticed earlier.

"He's bleeding," Jo said.

I knew. His pressure was falling fast enough that the monitor looked as if it was trying different numbers to see whether any of them would help.

We hadn't heard the trolley move. The pedestrian was suddenly kneeling beside us, broken leg dragging behind him, one hand resting lightly on the wet road. Up close he smelled of rain, hot metal and something like the inside of a garage after somebody has been grinding steel.

"Don't," I said.

His mouth formed the word before mine finished.

"Don't."

Then he hooked two fingers into the torn place beneath his ribs and pulled.

The flesh came apart with a sticky peeling sound. He reached into himself and removed something about the length of my hand, black and glossy with red vessels running over it, flexing around his fingers in small contractions. It looked less like an organ than a biological thing built to do one job.

Jo caught his wrist.

He looked at her.

"Help him."

The driver's pulse was almost gone. Jo let go.

I could have tried to stop him. I keep thinking about that, mostly when I want to make the later choices seem as though they began with one moment of helplessness. The truth is I moved aside because the man on the road was dying and whatever was kneeling beside me seemed to know it too.

The pedestrian pressed the black piece against the driver's upper abdomen. He didn't make an incision. The skin dimpled around the object, separated into a neat oval and received it.

I watched his fingers push it inside until only the tips remained visible. The opening closed after them without stitches, blood or any sign that skin had been broken. Twenty seconds later, the driver's blood pressure began climbing.

His colour came back. His pulse slowed. Jo stared at the monitor as though it had personally betrayed her.

"What the fuck did you do?"

The pedestrian's mouth moved.

"Fixed."

The second crew arrived a few minutes later and gave us the kind of practical chaos that saved us from immediately having to decide what any of this meant. They took the driver, police blocked the road and one of the officers saw us moving the pedestrian back onto our trolley. I remember that because later he would insist there had been three of us beside the ambulance.

We loaded the pedestrian. For the first five minutes neither Jo nor I said anything, and I sat opposite him watching the edges of the wound in his chest. They were drawing together so slowly that I could only tell by looking away and back.

I asked his name. His lips made a shape, but nothing came out.

"Do you know your name?"

"No."

The sound arrived after his mouth had already settled.

At the hospital Jo reversed toward the ambulance entrance. A healthcare assistant standing by the doors looked through the rear window, counted us with her finger, frowned, then held up three fingers as if asking why there were three crew in the back.

I turned to look at the trolley.

It was empty.

The blanket remained folded where his legs had been. Black residue coated the mattress in greasy streaks that disinfectant only spread around. The rear doors had been locked until Jo opened them.

We told the hospital the pedestrian had left the scene before transport. The lie lasted until the next afternoon, when police asked why two officers remembered watching us load him and one remembered a third crew member standing beside us. Jo handled the call because my mouth had gone dry just listening to her side of it.

At lunch she sat opposite me in the station kitchen and peeled the label from a bottle of water in one continuous strip.

"We report it," I said.

"And say what?"

"What happened."

"Right. Go on, then. I'm management."

I tried. I got as far as a dead pedestrian removing something from his abdomen and putting it into another patient before I could hear how it would sound to anyone who had read my occupational health file.

Jo looked down at the strip of plastic in her hand.

"You know what they'll do to me."

"I know."

That was worse than if she'd argued. We agreed to wait until we knew what happened to the driver, although neither of us said what outcome would make any of this easier.

He didn't die.

He went home five days later. The surgeons never found the source of the internal bleeding they had expected from his presentation, although imaging showed a dark vascular structure beneath his liver that wasn't on any previous scan and didn't resemble a normal anatomical variant. It went into the notes as an indeterminate post-traumatic finding with outpatient follow-up.

I added his name to a note in my phone. I don't know why I started doing that. It just seemed like the sort of thing I might need later.

The pedestrian came back nine nights after that.

We were parked behind a twenty-four-hour petrol station because Jo wanted coffee and I had developed an unreasonable hatred of their vending machine. I was in the passenger seat finishing paperwork when I felt the rear suspension dip. He was sitting behind me wearing the same ruined clothes.

Most of the visible damage had gone. His leg faced the right direction, the scalp had closed and the hole in his chest was now a narrow vertical seam. Somebody or something had cleaned the grit from him.

Jo opened the side door.

"No."

He looked at her.

"No."

His mouth closed before the word sounded.

We should have taken him straight to a police station. Instead we brought him back to ours, because it was nearly five in the morning and taking a dead man who wasn't dead into our own station felt easier than explaining him to anybody else. We put him in the training room and spent three hours asking him questions he mostly couldn't answer.

A technician called Gav came in looking for extension leads, stopped when he saw him and asked whether we'd picked up a student. Jo looked at me for half a second before saying he was observing nights. Gav complained about trainees stealing his pens, found the leads and left.

That was the first time we understood it wasn't only us.

The man wouldn't give us a name. He didn't know where he lived, didn't appear hungry or thirsty and held a paper cup of water for nearly an hour without drinking it. When Jo asked why his speech was wrong, he touched his throat.

"Slow."

The sound followed after his hand had dropped.

At 05:12 we were sent to a cardiac arrest. Jo told him to stay where he was, but when we reached the ambulance he was already in the rear seat wearing a spare station fleece. Control repeated the details over the radio: male, sixty-eight, collapsed in bathroom, wife performing CPR.

"We're wasting time," I said.

Jo swore and drove.

The patient had been down for about six minutes when we arrived. His wife was doing poor compressions, but at least she hadn’t stopped, and I took over while Jo attached pads. The extra man stood in the doorway until a firefighter coming in behind him said, "Mate, either get in or get out of the way," and squeezed past.

The first shock did nothing. Neither did the second. We gave the drugs and kept working him.

I was on compressions when I heard fabric tear behind me. By the time I looked, he had opened the inside of his forearm and was holding something pale grey and thumb-sized in his palm. Fine fibres still connected it to him until he pinched them apart.

Jo said, "No."

He ignored her and crouched beside the patient.

"Mark."

I stopped compressions for half a beat.

"What?"

He pointed at the man's upper chest. There was an old pacemaker scar there.

"Mark."

The sound came after his mouth closed.

He pressed the grey piece over the scar. The skin softened beneath it and the object sank through without bleeding.

The patient's heart started beneath my hands.

His wife screamed once, then started sobbing when she saw the monitor. The man in our fleece moved away from the patient and sat on the bathroom floor beside her.

"It's okay."

His mouth gave her the sentence first.

"We've got him."

She grabbed his hand. He let her hold it while we moved her husband onto the carry chair, and when she asked three times whether he was going to die, he answered all three times without sounding impatient. I would have said the same words once and then started thinking about the stairs.

At hospital, a nurse looked at the three of us and asked whether he was a university student. I heard myself say yes.

"What's his name?"

I looked at him and thought.

"Tom."

The nurse told Tom to carry the oxygen cylinder through to resus. He did.

The patient went home neurologically intact.

I added his name to the note.

After that, Third became easier.

Jo called him Third because continuing to call him Tom after I'd invented the name for a nurse made my skin crawl. Third never objected. On ordinary calls he carried bags, held doors and handed us equipment before we asked for it.

Other people saw him constantly. Police called him mate, nurses assumed he was a trainee and one registrar spent ten minutes explaining ECG changes to him while Third nodded with complete attention. Nobody ever seemed to wonder why they'd never seen him before because ambulance crews are full of students, observers, specialists, people on placements and staff moved between stations.

He was also better with patients than I was.

A nineteen-year-old went through a windscreen and spent the first ten minutes after regaining consciousness asking for his phone. I kept telling him we'd deal with the phone later while I tried to assess him. Third found it under the passenger seat, wiped blood off the case with his sleeve and put it into the boy's hand.

"Don't call Mum yet," the boy said.

Third nodded.

"Okay."

He waited until the boy asked him to call her.

A woman with a ruptured ectopic pregnancy kept apologising because there was blood on her bathroom floor. I told her twice that nobody cared about the floor. Third got a towel from the airing cupboard, put it over the worst of the blood and told her husband where the spare house key was after hearing her mention it once.

He wasn't cleverer clinically. That would have been easier to resent. He was simply present in a way I had spent years learning not to be.

By the time I added the fifth name to my phone, I had begun waiting for him to intervene. I never said that to Jo. I told myself we were observing a pattern, which sounded clinical enough to disguise what it actually was.

Number six is the one I did.

The patient went home after surgeries the consultants could not explain going as well as they did. Her imaging showed a dark structure braided around damaged pelvic vessels. One report called it organised haematoma, another called it probable postoperative material even though the scan had been done before her operation.

There were ten names on the list before Christmas.

I stopped pretending I was merely watching what Third did. If he wasn't already in the ambulance, I found him before we left for calls that sounded bad. Once I lied to Jo and said he'd climbed in himself because I didn't want another conversation like the warehouse.

Third had changed around me too. He would still hand Jo equipment without being asked, but before he opened himself he increasingly looked toward me.

I don't know whether I taught him permission or whether he had decided I was the person most likely to give it.

At first I told myself this was temporary. I told myself we needed to understand what was happening before reporting it, then that reporting it would get Third removed before anyone could study the people he'd saved, then that exposing him might cause fourteen kinds of medical panic we had no right to start. I'd done the same thing with tablets: move the line one inch at a time, give every new version of the behaviour a better name than the last one, and make sure tomorrow was always the day when I planned to stop.

The truth was simpler.

Our outcomes were extraordinary.

A twenty-four-year-old with a head injury that should have left him severely disabled walked into rehabilitation. A scaffolder who fell four storeys sent a photograph to the station three weeks later with both thumbs up. The woman from the warehouse recorded a voice message thanking "the quiet bloke with your crew" for staying with her when she thought she was going to die.

People started noticing.

Our team leader called me into the office after a shift and told me I'd been doing excellent work. He said Jo had given good feedback on my return, my paperwork was solid and Occupational Health had recommended removing another restriction. Then he put a set of controlled-drug keys into my hand.

"You're ready."

I nearly cried in his office.

That is the part I am most ashamed of.

I had spent more than a year telling people I wanted my life back. Standing there with those keys biting into my palm, I understood that what I'd really wanted was to become somebody other people didn't have to watch.

Third had helped me get there.

Number eleven was a motorcyclist on a bypass outside Cirencester. He had chest injuries bad enough that I knew before we'd loaded him that we were losing him, and Third knew before I said anything. In the back of the ambulance, he put his fingers against his own sternum and pulled.

There were no lungs behind it.

But instead there were compartments.

He reached into one of them and removed something wet and pale. I looked away long enough to hear Jo retch into a clinical-waste bag.

The motorcyclist survived.

Number fourteen was the child.

Her name is Maisie. She was six.

I know I shouldn't use her name, but turning her into "the paediatric patient" lets me hide inside the job, and I have already done enough of that.

Her mother hit black ice on a bend just after midnight. The car spun sideways into a tree, driver's side taking most of it, and Maisie was in the rear passenger seat in a proper child seat with the straps fitted properly. Sometimes you do everything right and physics doesn't care.

Mum was trapped but conscious. Jo went to her while I climbed into the back from the opposite side, and Third remained outside because Jo had already told him to stay by the ambulance. I remember being grateful for that for perhaps thirty seconds.

"No," I said.

"No."

"Aaron."

Third didn't move. The important part is that I knew what he was waiting for. I'd taught him that at the warehouse, or during one of the calls after it, every time I'd looked at a dying person and then at him.

He held the severed thumb in his palm while the bone divided lengthwise without snapping, the nail rolled underneath and the flesh opened into branching dark fibres around something white that pulsed in the centre.

Jo caught my sleeve.

"Don't let him."

I didn't move.

"Easy."

"Rabbit."
