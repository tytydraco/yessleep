I don’t expect you to believe me. If I saw this posted on a forum three years ago, I would’ve called it bad creepypasta and moved on. But I still can’t sleep with the lights off, and every time I smell woodsmoke or salted meat, I throw up. So I’m writing this down because I need it out of my head.

In the autumn of 2021, I was working as a field technician for an environmental consulting firm in northern Maine. My job was straightforward: walk timber plots, take soil samples, and map old drainage basins near the Canadian border. It was isolated work, but I liked the quiet.

On October 14th, I was tracking a dried-up creek bed about nine miles off the logging roads, well past where the cell towers drop off. The map marked the area as uninhabited crown land, logged out in the 1950s and abandoned. But around 3:00 PM, I stumbled across a double-strand barbed wire fence—not rusted, but clean, grey steel, strung tight between old hemlock trees.

I followed the wire downhill for twenty minutes until I saw the roof.

It was a church, but not like any rural parish I’d seen. It was built from hand-hewn fieldstone and massive black pine timbers. The bell tower had collapsed into itself years ago, choking the front entrance with stone, but the rest of the structure was intact. The weirdest part wasn't the building; it was the ground around it. Every tree within fifty yards had been stripped of its bark from the root up to about six feet high, leaving the trunks white, bare, and weeping sap.

I should have turned back right then. That was my first mistake. But I was curious, cold, and my truck was a two-hour hike through dense brush. I thought it was just an old historical site.
I found a side door near the back of the building—a heavy oak panel hanging off one wrought-iron hinge. I stepped inside, using my handheld work flashlight to cut through the dust.

The air inside was thick, warm, and smelled intensely of sulfur, wet tallow, and dried iron. It didn't feel abandoned. The pews had been unbolted from the floor and dragged into a massive, jagged semicircle facing the altar. But the altar wasn't there. In its place was a square hole cut through the pine floorboards, roughly six feet wide, plunging down into the stone crawlspace beneath the foundation.

Hovering over the hole was a tripod made of welded iron rebar. Hanging from the center hook was a heavy, blackened iron bucket filled with cold grease and pale, smooth river stones.

I shone my light into the pit. The bottom was lined with flat flagstones, completely dry, and covered in a thick layer of fine grey ash. Laid out along the edges were dozens of tin plates, each one holding small, charred fragments of bone—none larger than a coin—organized neatly by size.

Then I heard the truck.

It wasn't a running engine; it was the heavy thud of a tailgate slamming shut somewhere outside the front of the church, followed by the crunch of tires on pine needles.

My stomach dropped into my shoes. I killed my flashlight instantly and ducked into the alcove behind the ruined organ bellows. The wood was tight, forcing me to pull my knees up to my chest.

Footsteps came through the front wall—not through a door, but down a hidden staircase that led from a cellar hatch on the exterior side. There were at least six of them. They weren't wearing hoods or robes or anything you see in movies. They were wearing standard work clothes: Carhartt jackets, rubber hunting boots, high-vis vests. They looked like the guys you see buying black coffee and diesel at the Irving station on Highway 11.
They didn't talk. That was the most terrifying part. They moved with a terrifying, rehearsed efficiency.
One man—tall, balding, with grease-stained sleeves rolled up to his elbows—walked straight to the edge of the pit. He pulled a kerosene lantern from a crate, lit it, and hung it from the rebar tripod. 

The amber light flooded the center of the room.
From the shadows, two other men carried a heavy wooden crate between them and set it down near the hole. Inside were three mason jars filled with a dense, yellow-white liquid that looked like lard.

The tall man knelt by the pit, dipped his fingers into one of the jars, and smeared the yellow grease across his forehead, cheeks, and throat. He passed the jar to the next person. They all did it, in total, suffocating silence. The only sound was the wet, sliding noise of their fingers against skin and the heavy, synchronized sound of their breathing.

Then the tall man stood up and looked directly at the organ where I was hiding.

He didn't yell. He didn't look surprised. He just raised one finger and pointed at the wooden frame hiding me.
"Step out into the light, son," he said. His voice was flat, raspy, and completely devoid of emotion. It sounded like an uncle asking you to pass the salt at Thanksgiving.

My heart was beating so hard in my ears I could barely hear him. I didn't move. I couldn't move.
Two of the men in flannel walked over. They didn't grab me roughly; they just reached into the alcove, took me by the elbows, and hoisted me out onto the floor. Their grip was like iron, but their hands were ice-cold and coated in that slick, sweet-smelling grease.
They didn't strip me. They didn't tie me up. They just led me to the middle bench and forced me to sit down.

The tall man knelt in front of me. Close up, I could see his eyes. The whites were completely covered in tiny broken blood vessels, making them look solid pink, and his skin had a strange, translucent quality—like wax that had been melted and reshaped. He smelled overpowering of that yellow grease.

"You're not supposed to be on the lease," he whispered. He didn't sound angry; he sounded tired. "The state doesn't come up here until November."

"I was just... I was taking soil samples," I stammered, my chest heaving. "I'm leaving. I won't say anything. I swear to God I won't say anything."

The man leaned in closer. His breath smelled of stale salt and copper.

"God doesn't come past the wire, son," he said softly.

He reached down into his jacket pocket and pulled out a small, flat wooden box. He opened it. Inside were five dark, dried strips of jerky resting on a bed of salt. He picked up one piece, held it out to me between two greasy fingers, and pressed it against my lower lip.
"Take it," he said.

"No," I choked out, pulling my head back.

The two men standing behind my bench stepped forward. One of them reached around my forehead, pulled my head back against his chest, and pinched my nose shut. The other grabbed my jaw, forcing my mouth open.

"We don't leave witnesses because nobody leaves the table," the tall man said, his voice remaining dead and calm. "You eat with us, or you go into the pit to cure for the winter. That's the only two ways out of this valley."

I fought. I kicked my legs, thrashing against the wood, but the men holding me didn't even sway. The tall man slid the dried meat onto my tongue and held my mouth forced shut, his eyes fixed on mine.

It was salty. Terribly salty, with a bitter, metallic undertone that made my stomach instantly heave. But the pressure on my throat was unbearable. I couldn't breathe. My lungs burned, my eyes watered, and finally, my throat reflexively swallowed.

The moment the meat went down, the men instantly released me. I fell forward onto the dusty floorboards, coughing, gagging, shoving my fingers down my throat to throw it up, but nothing came up except dry bile.

The tall man looked down at me, wiping his fingers on his trousers.

"The truck is parked at the bottom of the fire road," he said, turning his back on me and walking toward the pit. "Get your gear and go home. If you tell a doctor, or a cop, or your wife, it doesn't matter. You're in the book now. You'll come back when the winter gets hard."
I didn't wait. I scrambled to my feet, threw myself through the broken rear door, and ran.

I ran through the thorns, through the dark, tripping over roots and tearing my clothes to shreds on the wire fence. I didn't stop until I hit my work truck, locked the doors, and drove seventy miles an hour down the dirt roads until I reached the asphalt.
I quit my job the next morning. I moved two states south three weeks later.

I tried to tell myself it was a prank, or a weird survivalist group, or a localized drug cult. But it’s been three years now, and the winters are getting harder. Every December, when the first heavy snow hits and the air turns bitter cold, I get a craving—a deep, gnawing, ravenous ache in the back of my throat for something salty and dry.

And last Tuesday, when I opened my mailbox, there was no letter inside. Just a plain brown envelope with no return address, containing a single mason jar filled with yellow grease.
