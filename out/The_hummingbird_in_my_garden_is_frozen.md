The 8th wonder of the world is in rural Oregon, 65 feet from my patio door. Nearly 150 unique plant species, a beautiful, self-sufficient pond with its own ecosystem that keeps the water clean, and a wall of large Evergreen trees forming a crescent around the moat. 

The pond was surprisingly deep, approximately 6 feet at its lowest and about an eighth of a mile long. Three stone bridges allowed access to our “island”, all of which were designed and custom-built by my mother. The only machinery we used around here was to gently stir the pond water, making it slightly harder for any mosquitoes to lay eggs.

I took off my sandals and stripped down to my boxers. My feet sank into the muddy bottom, with the occasional large stone supporting my steps. 

Instantly, I felt all the heat in my body dissipate into the surroundings, a feeling which made me want to lie back and float alongside the lilies. Like a paper boat in the gutter after a mild rainfall, I calmly floated around my botanical lazy river. I made it roughly halfway through the loop when I floated into the shade provided by the wall of Evergreens. I opened my eyes, finally out of the sun. The green algae, red orchids, purple lavender, and a vibrant blue embraced my senses. I recognized the other colors as a common palette for the garden, but the blue was something new. 

The blue-headed hummingbird. I’ve seen hundreds in my life, but this one was so detailed. I doggy-paddled closer to the bird, careful not to cause too much disturbance in the pond. 

Cool optical illusion, I thought; it looked like it was completely still, just floating with its wings extended out. I wonder what kind of plant had a stem small enough to evade my vision yet sturdy enough to support the small bird. 

The bird didn’t fly away. Huh, usually they’d do an evasive maneuver if you even looked at it. My face was only about 2 feet away from it; I could almost count the feathers on its underbelly. 

Strange, the bird wasn’t in a perched position; it looked like a picture had been taken with a high shutter speed camera. It seemed to be in mid-flight? 

Okay, so it was not perched on any nearby plants. Perhaps my parents put in a new garden feature while I was away. I thought this must have been the same system as one of the tricks magicians do when their assistants are “floating”. I waved my hands in an orbiting fashion around the hummingbird, expecting to hit a nearly invisible wire. Nothing. 

I reached my pointer finger out, carefully churning the water with my feet to keep me at the proper height. As I pressed into the underbelly of the bird, I felt the silky feathers followed by its impossibly fragile ribcage. It was in the exact position as it was when I initially pressed it, however, it was now one inch higher, and there was an indent where my finger made contact.

“MOM!” I yelled across the backyard.

“Yeah, honey?” she responded behind the screen door. 

“Can you come here, please?” I yelled once more. 

She walked over the first stone bridge, into the garden, and finally to the end of the bank where the water line sat. 

“Wow, this is really cool. Where did you get this?” She asked, looking at the bird.

“Did you use a fishing line? That’s a really pretty addition. I'm sure the bees will be a little worried about their new competition,” she said with a grin across her face and a small chuckle. 

“I didn’t put this here; I thought you or Dad did,” I said, climbing onto the bank of the pond.

“No, the last time we added anything was before you left for college. Is this some sort of trick? How are you doing this?” My mom asked me.

“No, I swear I just came out here, and it was just stuck in midair.” I could tell she did not believe me. 

“Seriously, how did you do this? Are there wires or something?” Her voice changed from impressed to annoyed that I couldn’t give an answer. In a nearly identical system to what I had just done, she walked around, waved her hands around it, and finally touched it. 

“What the fuck?” She said under her breath. 

She proceeded to interrogate me for a few minutes after, with the same mannerisms a child would have asking a street magician how they made a dove appear.

“Call your father. I am not really sure what to do here or what I am looking at.” My mother said, staring at the bird.

Understandably, my father was confused and almost annoyed at the phone call, 

“So a hummingbird is at a standstill? Not moving or anything? Listen, if this is a practical joke, I am going to be very upset with you.”

“Look, talk to Mom she saw it too.” I handed the phone to her. 

“Mhm, yeah, no, I know… but I saw it too. I promise you. … Okay, okay, sounds good,” she said, pacing around. 

“Your father said he will come home a little early, but he needs to finish some work up. Let's head inside for now.” 

3:30 p.m., I heard a knock at our front door. My dad was holding his usual briefcase and stood in front of his professor buddies.  

“Okay, please show us what you are referring to, too.” He said. I could sense that this was a ‘better not waste my time’ sort of tone. 

I walked them to the backyard, my father in close pursuit, followed by his colleague, who I had met before. Lewis, a professor of botany, helped set up a majority of our garden. Nice guy, quiet, but he was an artist who used plants as his paintbrush.  

“Is this a trick?” My dad looked at me, raising his eyebrow. 

“I thought the same thing,” my mother interjected. 

Like mother and son, my dad waded into the pond, followed by waving his hands to try and find a wire. Lewis walked around looking for something that might give them a clue. My dad grabbed the bird and began to move it around like he was looking at every face on a Rubik's Cube. Unlike the Rubik’s Cube, however, I’m not sure he could solve this. 

He stated, “If this is a trick, I have to say I am thoroughly impressed.” At least his annoyed tone had subsided; it was now replaced with curiosity. 

“Over here,” Lewis said as he pointed to our patch of sunflowers. 

We walked over the stone bridge to the far part of our garden. We gathered in between a break in the shade provided by the Evergreen trees. I hadn’t thought about inspecting the rest of the garden; I was far too infatuated by the center of the pond.

This section of the garden was dedicated to our sunflowers; my mother adored the yellow and insisted we plant them early on. As the familiar yellow flowers appeared, instantly I knew why Lewis had called us over. 

“The Hell?” My father said softly.

It looked almost like they were having a discussion, each sunflower paired up with another, petals only inches apart. Even sunflowers that were a couple of feet away from each other had leaned into each other. A few of the more mature sunflowers that were leaning had small breaks in their stems, allowing them to reach farther than normal. We gathered around, inspecting them, my father placing his hand on his chin and shooting Lewis a look of confusion. 

I asked, “Has this happened before? Have you ever heard of something like this?”

Lewis turned to me,

“Normally, fully mature sunflowers like these would be facing directly east; the younger ones can follow the sun. Occasionally, one may have a genetic defect which makes it unable to tilt, but those tend to die off pretty young. I have never seen an entire group of sunflowers arrange themselves in this way. As for the ones that are bent, my only logical guess would be a strong wind?” 

That didn’t make much sense; the strongest wind in the past few days was nothing greater than a calm gust that could maybe take a leaf off a tree. 

My father spoke up, “Lewis, grab the hummingbird, bring it to the table by the patio.” 

Lewis did as he was told. He used a Kleenex he had in his pocket to carefully cradle the bird and placed it on the fake wood table. My father walked back towards the house. 5 minutes later, he emerged with a black case no bigger than a notebook. I recognized this; it was his pinning kit he would use for his entomology collection.

He took out a scalpel, removed the plastic cover, and walked over. After putting on a pair of nitrile gloves, he began to work. Lewis held the bird down by the armpits, trying to stabilize the fragile abdomen. My dad used the scalpel to gently shave the feathers off; the sharpness of the new blade was able to easily cut through the barbs. Once there were a few square inches of flesh, he took out a second, smaller scalpel. 

I winced as it cut into the flesh; I never really got used to seeing dissections, no matter how many times I’d watched my dad lecture. He was intensely attentive to his work, careful not to cut too deep into the small bird.  After one vertical cut, followed by two horizontal ones at each end, he peeled the flesh back with a pair of forceps. 

Lewis and my dad's heads were only inches apart, both staring into the opened flap. I walked around the table to get a better look. Leaning in, I saw exactly what I imagined the innards of a hummingbird looked like. A tiny rib cage which looked about as fragile as a half-cut toothpick, some pinkish fibers which I assumed were the muscles that pumped the wings. No blood was rushing out; I suppose he could have precisely cut away from any veins. I am not an expert on hummingbird anatomy; perhaps I am missing something. 



“This makes no sense,” he said, turning to Lewis. 

Lewis responded, “I have seen it present itself in other ways, but this is definitely a unique case.”

My mother asked, “What do you guys see? What’s wrong with it?”

My dad pulled away from the bird and wiped some sweat off his forehead, 

“Situs inversus, pretty uncommon in humans, far less common in animals. It’s when the organs are flipped along a vertical axis. For instance, a person's heart is on the right side instead of the left; pretty much all the organs are just mirrored.” 

He paused for a moment, 

“This is different; the hummingbird's organs are flipped top to bottom. The lungs are connected to the anus and the intestines are directly connected to the mouth.”

Lewis added, “Not only that, but it appears that the ribcage had been sloppily duplicated and moved slightly behind the first. Could be bone cancer, but there is absolutely zero chance a hummingbird could develop this much extra bone and live.” 

There was a silence amongst us. Gathered around the table, I could sense that everyone had the same unrest inside of them. While it’s possible that a genetic disorder may have rearranged the hummingbird’s innards, it still couldn’t explain how it was stationary in the air. I knew my dad had hoped that the autopsy would somehow explain what had caused this lapse in physics. 

“Lewis, we’ll preserve the bird for now. I can adjust the temperature of the garage fridge to keep it ideal for further dissection tomorrow. I am going to call some friends at the school, see if we can gather a small team to do a full inspection of the garden. I’ll try and get them to come over this evening, but I imagine they’ll be more inclined for the morning.”

“Can we keep checking around the garden? We’ve only briefly checked like a third of it,” I asked.

My father contemplated my question. “Look, I’d feel a lot better if we did the inspection with a group of people who could identify if there was any underlying danger here.” 

I wanted to know if there were any other anomalies, but my dad was absolutely correct. I was just going to have to wait. 

*Crrrrrrrrrrack*

We all moved our heads in unison to the location of the sound. A massive chunk of bark on one of the Evergreens had peeled off and began falling to the ground. My mother looked at me, then at my father. Right before a word had left her mouth, we heard the sound once more. *Crrrrrrack.* This time, before the bark had even touched the ground, another tree began to produce the sound. Then another, then another, then another; soon every Evergreen tree began to peel. Every single 150-foot giant as far as I could see, shedding its bark like a cicada shedding its exoskeleton. 

The delayed wind of the first falling bark hit me, a breeze which carried only enough force to gently disturb my hair. The second wind was nowhere near as kind. A wall of wind, dirt, twigs, and leaves resembling a Saharan dust storm in the middle of Oregon. It finally hit us. Dust immediately covered my open eyes, and the feeling of twigs and smaller debris scratching my face made me instantly fall into a fetal protective position. 

The wind dissipated in a matter of seconds, but the sounds of falling branches and twigs landing in the water remained even after I had begun to get up. The last twig hit the water, followed by a silence. No birds, no insects, no wind brushing the leaves of the trees, a silence that would have produced a ringing in my ears if it weren't for the gurgling sound I heard behind me. A swift yell, like a passenger in a car screaming from a window, dissipated quickly. I began to uncurl my protected body,

“IS EVERYONE OKAY?” My dad yelled as he ran to my mother. She appeared to be covered in a fine layer of dirt, with a few scratches on her face. I imagine I didn’t look much better. After grabbing her arms and face, looking at every inch of my mother as quickly as he could, he ran over to me.

“I'm fine, a couple of cuts, but I think I am okay,” I said in urgent response. I felt his hands jerk my head around, looking for any cuts that may be deep enough to cause any real issues. I heard him stand up and yell, “LEWIS?” 

I had trouble seeing; the dirt had hit faster than I was able to blink. I crawled over to the pond, able to slightly make out my surroundings to assist my pathetic crawl. Like bobbing for apples, I dipped my face in, blinking underwater to rinse them out. I could still hear my father's muffled voice while I was submerged, continuously calling out to me. 

“LEWIS, WHERE ARE YOU?” 

He wasn’t there; 30 seconds ago he was standing no more than 5 feet from me. The gust of wind was strong but not strong enough to push a human being out of sight; had he run away?   
My father ran around the house, continuing to scream his name, no response. My mother ran to me, planting a firm kiss on my forehead and crushing my torso with her arms wrapped. 

“Thank god, honey, are you hurt? You have a few cuts; is anything else wrong?” She said with a tremble in her voice. 

“No, no, I am okay, Mom. I am really scared,” I said as I felt the first hint of a cry let out. I wasn’t embarrassed at all; at that moment, crying to my mom was the only reflex I had. 

“I know I am too. Listen, we are going to get out of here; let's grab your father and go right now,” she said. 

I whimpered into her shoulder, the tears somewhat helping clean the remaining dirt that had caked my eyelids. My father, panting, rejoined us. 

“Listen, you two, I want you to look towards the house and run inside. Please trust me when I tell you to just look forward. I will be right next to you.”

“What's going on? Where's Lewis?” I begged. At this point, the small whimper I had let out earlier had turned into a full sob. 

“JUST GO INSIDE NOW, DO NOT LOOK BACK, GO,” My father said with the most certainty and authority he has ever had.

I stood up, grabbing my mom's arm to assist her. With my dad standing directly behind us, I could see he was looking backwards. Once my mom was on her feet, she pulled me towards the direction of the house, only about 125 feet towards the patio door. I was so full of adrenaline that I could physically feel that my body had stopped communicating with any pain I had. My entire being at that moment wanted to sprint into the house as fast as humanly possible. To get away from whatever was happening in this garden. This urge to run was so deep-rooted in my system, thousands of years of evolution wired to protect me from any dangers I could not beat. Thousands of years of evolution, yet it was only the second strongest feeling in my body. 

Curiosity rested above the fear like a kayak atop a landslide. It shouldn't have been there, and yet it floated above the churning powers that were trying to capsize it. I looked back, only for a moment, only long enough before my dad grabbed my skull and forcefully turned it in the other direction. No more than one second had I looked, yet the images I saw have played for days, months, weeks inside of my head. God, I wish I had listened to my father.

Lewis was suspended in the trees. A conglomerate of branches resembling a thick nest woven by something not grown from the ground in which I stand. One hundred feet in the air, grabbed by the Evergreens, held by branches, pierced by the wood. The only parts of the tree that were straight enough to resemble a natural shape were pierced through Lewis like an organic depiction of Vlad the Impaler. I couldn’t pinpoint the exact number; they entered through his abdomen and came out from his mouth, armpits, legs ... any flesh he had seemed to be disturbed by the trees. 

In the last milliseconds of my glance, I saw what had appeared behind him. The dark weave of trees blocked out all sunlight, allowing for holes in the canopy to appear as dozens of symbols. It would not have been legible at any other angle; no, this was directed. If we were ten feet to our left, we wouldn't even notice that they appeared.   
The longest second of my life followed by the quickest my legs have ever moved. I didn’t even bother opening the patio door; I just ran through the screen, instantly taking the fragile door off its hinges. Through my living room, down the cellar, into the storage room, into a closet. I hear my parents in close pursuit. My mom joined me in the cellar, sprinting down the stairs at inhuman speed. My dad appeared at the doorway, quickly slamming it shut and locking it. He frantically went through his pockets and picked up his phone. 

Lawyers said that the state wasn’t going to press charges, a relief for my father. They said that the scene that was left in the garden was unexplainable, that there was virtually no way my mother or father could have possibly gotten Lewis up there and impaled him with dozens of branches. Lewis’s family was less happy, albeit Lewis's wife insisted she needed to confirm that the body was his. Didn’t help; blood tests and a fingerprint were all that remained of him. I think she knew that this was beyond the scope of manslaughter. 

The police interrogated all of us after we made the call. Listening back to the phone call, I could hear the terror in my father's voice. His voice quaking with adrenaline, the kind of fear that is so raw and true it would be admissible in court. I could hear the cries in the background of my mother and me as we held each other, sobbing.

Towards the end of the interrogations, roughly 2 weeks after the day we found the frozen hummingbird, they placed a manilla folder on the metal table. The detective took out a photo, a photo I recognized from my glances backwards. A full picture of the canopy; the detective graciously covered Lewis's mangled corpse with his hand. I think he knew I’d remember that part in detail. 

“Believe it or not, the department hired an arborist to inspect the treetops.” The detective said, sliding the image closer. 

“He said that the branches had grown that way, that there was no human intervention possible to make those shapes naturally. We had to pay a pretty penny for this man after he initially saw the crime scene.” 

“Do you recognize these symbols?” He asked.

“Um, I recognize this one and this other one. I have seen them somewhere in a math or science class; I can't really remember which.” I responded. Having a clear image of the canopy didn’t help understand the symbols any better. 

“It’s Greek. Old Greek. Apparently a pretty common saying back then.” He attempted to pronounce it; attempted is a generous word. 

Τῆς ἐμῆς εὐφορίας ἀπέλαυσας, χάριν δ᾽ οὐκ ἀπέδωκας



He pulled out a second paper from the folder, removing the hand covering Lewis’s remains. 

He cleared his throat. “Translates to: you have enjoyed my fruitfulness yet give no thanks in return.” 



I wrote this story as a warning to anyone who plants or creates, or performs, or does anything with pride. I am not sure who or what did this, but I ask you, I beg you, please give thanks to something you may not understand.
