I'm not sure anyone is going to believe this. Hell, I'm not sure I believe it myself.

I don't know what day it is anymore. According to my watch, it's March 23, 2011. According to the clock on the dashboard, it's 0217.

It's been 0217 for a very long time.

I'm writing this from the driver's seat of a military vehicle somewhere in the Mojave Desert. I have no idea how I'm getting an internet connection, considering I haven't seen a cell tower, a building, or another living person in what feels like weeks.

If anyone reading this has served at Twentynine Palms, particularly around March 2011, I need you to tell me something.

Was there ever a report of a missing Marine or vehicle following a pre-deployment training exercise that month?

Because I think that Marine might be me.

I should probably start at the beginning.

In March 2011, my unit was conducting a month-long pre-deployment training exercise at the Marine Corps Air Ground Combat Center in Twentynine Palms, California.

Anyone who's spent time at 29 Palms knows what kind of place it is. Mountains, sand, rocks, and miles upon miles of absolutely nothing. During the day, the desert stretches out forever. At night, away from mainside, it gets so dark you can barely see your hand in front of your face.

The entire exercise was a shit show from day one.

It started with PFC Lopez.

Lopez joined our platoon as we were boarding the buses to head out to 29 Palms. I don't know where he'd come from, and I honestly don't remember much about him before that exercise.

What I do remember is that the guy couldn't seem to do anything right.

Every day, without fail, something happened that resulted in the entire platoon getting thrashed.

Sometimes it was missing gear. Sometimes he screwed up an instruction. Sometimes it was something so stupid that I can't even remember what he'd done.

But the result was always the same.

Everyone paid for it.

And because everyone paid for it, everyone took their frustration out on Lopez.

I'm not proud of that.

By the end of the month, the guy had become the platoon's punching bag, figuratively speaking. Nobody wanted anything to do with him. Every time something went wrong, people immediately assumed it was his fault.

On the final night of the exercise, Lopez disappeared.

He'd apparently decided he was going to walk out of the training area and make his way to the nearest town.

On foot.

In the middle of the night.

The town was at least fifty miles away, but if you've never been in the desert at night, it's hard to explain how deceptive distances can be.

You see lights on the horizon, and they look close enough to walk to.

They aren't.

We found Lopez the following morning, barely alive.

His rifle was missing.

That alone was enough to turn an already miserable exercise into an absolute nightmare.

We were sent back out to search for it.

The rifle was recovered fairly quickly, but the RCO was gone.

For anyone unfamiliar with military equipment, the RCO is the optic mounted on the rifle. It's serialized gear, which means you don't just shrug your shoulders when one disappears.

So there we were.

Two sections. Six vehicles. Approximately twenty Marines.

All stuck out in the desert looking for a fucking scope.

For five days.

And when I say stuck, I mean stuck.

Vehicles buried in sand. Recovery attempts that accomplished nothing. Hours of digging, pulling, repositioning, and watching another vehicle get stuck trying to recover the first one.

I'd managed to get my vehicle out on the first night, but there was no way I was getting the others out by myself.

By the fifth day, we were beyond exhausted.

The exercise had already ended. We weren't prepared to spend another five days out there. Food was practically nonexistent, water was scarce, and we were lucky to get an hour of sleep at a time.

I've been tired before.

I've pulled all-nighters, worked ridiculous hours, and gone days on very little sleep.

Nothing has ever compared to that fifth night.

Eventually, recovery vehicles arrived, and we were finally able to get everyone moving.

My vehicle was tasked with leading the convoy back to mainside so we could get everything washed down.

I remember climbing into the driver's seat and thinking that all I had to do was get us back.

That was it.

Just drive.

My section leader was sitting beside me, and another Marine was behind us.

At some point during the drive, my section leader mentioned that The Hills Have Eyes had been filmed out in the same desert.

I don't know why he brought it up.

Under normal circumstances, I wouldn't have given it a second thought.

But when you're driving through absolute darkness, running on practically no sleep, your mind starts doing some strange things.

Every rock becomes a person.

Every shadow looks like something moving.

You catch things in your peripheral vision that disappear the second you turn your head.

I kept seeing figures standing along the road.

Sometimes they looked like Marines.

Sometimes they were just shapes.

I knew they weren't real.

At least, I thought I did.

One of the vehicles in the convoy broke down, forcing us to hook it up for towing.

That slowed everything to about ten miles per hour.

Ten miles per hour.

In the middle of the night.

After five days of almost no sleep.

It was torture.

I remember rounding a bend and seeing an entire platoon of Marines running down the road in PT gear.

They were in formation.

At approximately 0200.

In the middle of the fucking desert.

I slowed down because they were blocking the road.

Then my section leader grabbed me.

"What the fuck are you doing?"

I blinked.

The road was empty.

He told me I'd been drifting off.

I tried to explain what I'd seen, but he cut me off and told me to keep my eyes on the road.

A little later, I remember pulling off to the side and parking.

In my mind, the entire platoon had stopped.

I could see the other vehicles lined up beside me.

I told my section leader I needed to take a piss.

When I climbed out, there were only two other vehicles.

I stood there for a moment, trying to understand where everyone had gone.

I remember thinking I'd somehow missed the rest of the convoy continuing down the road.

I got back in and we kept moving.

About twenty minutes later, we stopped for a vehicle check.

Standard practice.

I remember lowering my seat to get comfortable and trying to get warm.

Then my section leader slapped me across the face.

Twice.

"Wake the fuck up."

But here's the thing.

I was awake.

I could see him.

I could hear him.

I knew exactly where I was.

I just couldn't move.

I couldn't speak.

I couldn't even turn my head.

I remember staring at him, trying to tell him I was awake.

Then everything gets fuzzy.

I don't remember pulling back onto the road.

I don't remember starting the engine.

I don't remember anyone telling us to move.

But somehow, we were driving again.

And one of the vehicles was missing.

I noticed it in my mirror.

There should have been three sets of headlights behind me.

There were only two.

I waited for someone to mention it.

Nobody did.

I tried calling over the radio.

No answer.

I figured the vehicle had stopped for maintenance and would catch up.

Then another set of headlights disappeared.

Again, nobody said anything.

I kept driving.

I don't know how long.

Eventually, I realized I hadn't heard anyone speak in a while.

"Hey, you awake?"

I glanced toward my section leader.

He was looking straight ahead.

I asked him something else.

No response.

I assumed he'd fallen asleep with his eyes open.

I kept driving.

A little while later, I asked the Marine behind me to light a cigarette.

Nothing.

"Hey, man. You got a light?"

Still nothing.

I reached backward.

My hand touched an empty seat.

I turned around.

Nobody was there.

I looked at my section leader.

His seat was empty too.

I slammed on the brakes.

The vehicle stopped in the middle of the road.

I sat there staring at the empty passenger seat.

I couldn't understand it.

There was nowhere they could have gone.

We hadn't stopped.

I hadn't opened a door.

I hadn't heard anything.

I climbed out and walked around the vehicle.

The Marines in the back were gone.

The vehicle we'd been towing was gone.

Even the tow connection was missing.

I stood in the road and screamed their names.

Nothing answered.

I grabbed the radio.

"Blue 1 to any station, radio check."

Static.

"Blue 1 to any station. Does anybody copy?"

Nothing.

I tried every frequency I could remember.

No answer.

I checked my phone.

No service.

I checked my watch.

March 19.

That didn't make sense.

We'd left the recovery area on March 12.

I checked again.

March 19.

A full week.

I looked toward the eastern horizon.

Still dark.

I tried to remember the last time I'd seen the sun.

I couldn't.

I got back into the vehicle and started driving.

I figured that if I stayed on the road, eventually I'd hit mainside.

Or a paved road.

Or a checkpoint.

Something.

I drove for hours.

The road never changed.

The same hills sat on either side.

The same mountains remained in the distance.

Every now and then I'd see what looked like headlights far ahead, but they would disappear before I could reach them.

I tried turning around.

After what felt like another hour, I passed a large rock formation I'd seen earlier.

I turned around again.

Same rock formation.

I stopped and marked the road with a piece of gear.

Then I drove forward.

Twenty minutes later, I passed the same marker.

I don't know how.

The road was straight.

I hadn't made a turn.

I tried driving off-road toward the mountains.

I kept them directly in front of me.

After what felt like hours, they were no closer.

When I looked behind me, the road was still there.

Exactly the same distance away as when I'd left it.

That's when I started panicking.

I drove back to the road.

I don't remember making that decision. I just remember suddenly being on it again.

I checked my watch.

March 21.

Two more days had passed.

I wasn't hungry.

I wasn't thirsty.

I didn't need to piss.

I hadn't slept.

I should have been barely functioning, but I felt completely awake.

The fuel gauge hadn't moved.

I started paying attention to the odometer.

It wasn't moving either.

The speedometer worked.

The engine sounded normal.

The wheels were turning.

But according to the odometer, I wasn't traveling anywhere.

I turned the engine off.

The silence was immediate.

There was no wind.

No insects.

No distant aircraft.

Nothing.

I stepped outside.

The headlights were still on.

That bothered me more than it should have.

I walked to the front of the vehicle and stared into the darkness.

I could see the road for maybe a hundred feet.

Beyond that, nothing.

I looked up.

There were no stars.

I don't mean there were clouds.

There was simply nothing in the sky.

I went back inside.

I tried the radio again.

"Is anybody out there?"

Static.

I waited.

Then I heard something.

Very faintly.

A voice.

I couldn't make out the words.

I turned up the volume.

It sounded like several people talking at once.

I recognized one of them.

My section leader.

I grabbed the microphone.

"Staff Sergeant? Staff Sergeant, it's me!"

No answer.

I listened.

Someone said something about a vehicle.

Another voice asked where the driver was.

Then I heard my section leader.

"He was right here."

I screamed into the microphone.

"I'm here! I'm right fucking here!"

The voices continued.

Someone asked how long I'd been missing.

My section leader said he didn't know.

Then another Marine said something I haven't stopped thinking about.

"His vehicle's still here. Where the hell did he go?"

The transmission ended.

I sat there holding the microphone.

I looked around the cab.

My vehicle.

My seat.

My hands on the steering wheel.

I was right there.

I tried calling them again.

Nothing.

I don't know what happened after that.

I remember driving.

I remember stopping.

I remember getting out and walking until I couldn't see the headlights anymore.

But every time I turned around, the vehicle was right behind me.

Eventually, I stopped trying to leave it.

I checked my watch.

March 23.

Eleven days since we were supposed to have returned to mainside.

That's when I noticed my phone had service.

Full bars.

No carrier name.

Just bars.

I tried calling home.

The call wouldn't connect.

I tried dialing 911.

Nothing.

I opened the browser.

It worked.

I don't know how.

I could load websites, read articles, and search for things.

But every attempt to send a text or make a call failed.

Then I found Reddit.

And somehow, I was able to write this.

I don't know if anyone can actually see it.

I don't know if this is reaching the real internet or if I'm just talking to myself.

But if you can read this, please respond.

Tell me what year it is.

Tell me if there was a missing Marine at Twentynine Palms in March 2011.

Tell me if anyone ever found a military vehicle abandoned somewhere in the training area.

Because something has changed.

About an hour ago, I noticed headlights in my rearview mirror.

Just one set.

Far behind me.

I thought it was another hallucination.

But they've been getting closer.

I stopped driving twenty minutes ago.

The headlights stopped too.

They're still there.

I can hear an engine now.

And every few minutes, I hear what sounds like someone calling cadence.

I don't know if it's the same platoon I saw running down the road.

I don't know if it's my section leader.

I don't know if it's something else.

But I just checked my mirror again.

The headlights are closer.

And there are people standing in front of them.

I can barely make them out.

They're wearing PT gear.

I think one of them is waving at me.

I'm not getting out.

I'm going to start driving again.

I don't know where this road goes.

I don't know if it goes anywhere.

But I can't stay here.

Not with whatever that is getting closer.

If anyone can read this, please tell me how to get back.

I just want to go home.
