Okay, let me start of by just answering some immediate questions I usually get. Yes, the US government hires entomologists for research. No, our research isn't top secret. We mainly study how insects impact agriculture and public health.

Having said that, there are occasions where our work and data are withheld as state secrets. Think like, which areas may pose the most risk of insect-borne illness to our military. That kind of information could hold strategical value and usually isn't published until decades after it's implemented. So, usually our job is pretty low stakes. But occasionally things will get held back for mundane reasons.

We all on the same page? Good. That's where my understanding was this morning.

Our DCU has been short staffed recently so I'm not surprised to see an email come up as I'm clocking in to work and getting sat down at my desk.

"All available personnel with appropriate clearances head to DCU to help with document archival and destruction."

Okay. My day was looking pretty bare, and with my recent promotion I *do* have the proper clearance now. So I decided to head on over.

As I get there I am informed that a large batch of documents have come off of their classified holds and are set to be digitized and uploaded to the website for public access. A little different from what the email promised but no big deal. It's when I'm going through the backlog that I notice it. An aged cardboard box, seemingly forgotten beneath the steel shelves. I go to open it but find a seal preventing me from doing so. On it reads.

"For immediate destruction. Classified Station Report Transcripts."

Seeing as this box appeared older than any other I'd seen before, and since it was set for the shredder (and definitely missed that immediate deadline). I didn't see any issue with taking a little peek before sending it on its way. I carefully peeled up the seal, making sure to leave it intact so it can be put back later. And most of what I found was to be expected. Reports from field labs and other stations with some information that the higher ups deemed important enough to keep secret at the time. But nothing that would earn immediate destruction. Confused, I started flipping through the reports. I was also becoming slightly anxious of getting caught. I didn't think I would be "silenced" like what happens in the movies when the main character finds some top secret information they shouldn't be privy to. But I was still on a probational period for my promotion and snooping like this would have a good chance of getting that revoked.

It wasn't until I reached the bottom of the container that things started to make sense. Buried there amongst innocuous reports was one of the most confounding things I've read. I was able to snap a picture of the report that I can attach [here](https://imgur.com/a/hqUuZAn). If that link doesn't work, I'll transcribe it below.

  
June 23rd, 1941

Station Report: New species discovery

Station Number and location - (Classified)

  


Within the jungles of REDACTED we discovered the most curious species. Now, it took quite a bit of effort and sacrifice from our field agents to procure enough specimens for proper research and classification. Given the creature's specific tendencies.



Specific Epithet: *Hyposmocoma cerebrophagus*



Description:



The adult form of these moths appear similar to their cousins the Bone Collector moths of the same genus. With the main difference being their size. *Cerebrophagus* grows to an average 9-11 inch length with a 27-33 inch wingspan. Making them the largest insect found to date. Depending on how you measure. The adult form is completely docile; with thin silky cremello hair covering most of their body, they have made quite the pet among the researchers. However, it is their juvenile form that has given us cause to send out this notice with haste.



The caterpillar of *cerebrophagus* is unlike anything we have seen. Averaging 7 inches in length, and appearing translucent green under adequate lighting. It possesses 4 fang-like structures in place of mandibles. Its mouth is soft, save for a radula-like appendage. They are carnivorous. Choosing smaller prey until their fangs grow in completely. Once at the maximum size of this stage they will begin to search for a host suitable for metamorphosis.



In testing it appears they prefer large mammalian prey, but will make due with what is available if no preferred options are available. They begin by dangling themselves from the canopy with thin threads of silk. Waiting for a host to pass under them. They have been observed doing this most often over water sources to increase effectiveness. Once a suitable host is positioned under the *cerebrophagus* caterpillar, it will slowly lower itself onto its target. Once within range it will secrete a numbing agent onto the skin of the host. If unnoticed, it will begin to burrow into the host. Grasping with its fangs to give leverage for the radula to bore into flesh. It continues secreting the numbing agent as it does so. Subjects report a pressure-like sensation paired with mild itching at the entrance wound.



As it burrows, it gorges itself on muscle and sub-dermal tissues. This process takes anywhere from 2-3 hours up to 48 hours depending on the size of the host and entry location. Ultimately it is aiming for the brain of its host. Traveling up the back of the neck. Being careful not to damage any of the crucial organs along the way. We presume it does so to maintain the freshness of the host for as long as possible. During its journey it will begin to glow a radiant green under the skin of its host. We are unsure of why it does this. Once at the base of the cranium it will again latch using its fangs onto the base of the cranium. It will then use the radula to bore into the cranium. 

Subjects at this point report a screeching sound loud enough to hamper communication and cause immense distress. Once inside, the host finally expires as the caterpillar begins eating

brain tissue. A final, fat rich, meal before cocooning itself within the cranium. 



\- - - -



Please excuse the rushed nature of this notice as we are experiencing

difficulties culling the remaining specimens within the lab. The adults some of the researchers were keeping as pets found each other and began to mate. Laying eggs on the ceiling of the research facility. Apparently in times of distress, they will use their burrowing techniques before reaching maturity in order to hide within their hosts. We are currently examining the entire staff before we allow anyone off premises.



\- END REPORT-

  


After snapping the photos, I quickly put it back into the box and resealed it. I took it over to the shredding crew and feigned ignorance of its contents. It should be obvious to them I would've never peeked.. Right? Regardless, if that report was real then there seems like bigger fish to fry. 

  
I'm posting this now as I'm getting home and I'll do my best to research where this research station could've been. If there something like this exists out there then it seems the best we can do is avoid its endemic region.
