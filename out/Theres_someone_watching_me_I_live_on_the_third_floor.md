I think back now on what I’d seen one night with resignation. I’d clumsily wheeled my old rolling chair to the window while eating a bowl of something vaguely edible. That’s all it had been, really. Wilting mushrooms and beans and sludgy rice. I’d burned the mushrooms in the pan, so I’d gone to open the window to let some fresh air inside. The next apartment complex over across from mine had a few units lit up, so I did what I tended to do on late nights like these- check off an imaginary checklist of all the things that I noticed.

I should’ve been ashamed of my less-than-notable ‘hobby’, if you could even call it that, but I had little else to do. The lights on in each room almost appeared scattered, like checkered marks on a board. I could see a group of women dancing feverishly in one. A lowly drunk occupied another unit; lips fastened to a bottle like it was his lifeline. It was only when I peered lower that I saw, in the lowest set of apartments units, a set of eyes staring back at me.

I’d paused. I did so dumbly, mouth full of meaningless textures and flavors that did little to ground me back to reality. Yet, this *was* my reality. Glasses sliding down my face, I now watched a man from below stare back at me with unnerving resoluteness. How long had he been there for? Had I just conveniently occupied his line of sight when he looked out of his own living room, much like what I was doing?

Rolling myself back and turning off my lights to make it seem as if I’d left, I set my forgotten bowl down on my desk nearby to crawl right back to the window and peek down. Those eyes met mine, and I sunk back down to hide from the weight of his gaze. He’d been an old thing, hair clinging to his roots like stubborn weeds in a garden. His eyes were like an old car flashing its headlights, blinking offbeat but nevertheless determined to alert you to its incoming presence.

Up until that point, things in my life had been the same. I only deviated on my schedule for emergencies or to pull incessant overtime to meet a quota that I wished I could care more about. Generally, I would wake up, drink a mug of bitter black coffee, read the news online, and then work. My nights were no different either. After getting up, I’d stretch, eat whatever was in my cupboard or fridge, and then shower before bed. I didn’t have many hobbies outside of reading or observing the things in my life from a distance. Perhaps that’s why I’d done so well in my line of work. All I had to do was analyze, read, and write. Analyze, read, and write. Analyze, read, and write.

At the time, I’d worked from home because I didn’t much like the idea of being in an active environment. I needed silence to get things done. When I was a child, I’d always thought I’d have made more of myself. They tell you to aim for greatness when you’re young, then when you try, they pile you high with debt. I realized that quickly after sinking all my personal funds and borrowed loans into medical school; just to fail hard. Greatness, it turns out, can mean anything when you’re better suited to just doing what you’re told. After all, why clog the machine when helping it run is so much more rewarding than the alternative?

Still, I was a tired thing. The hair that my mother had loved so much was now flat and slick with so much grease that it nearly stained my pillow each time that I woke up. My body, which had once been fit and healthy, now sat hollow in my chair, back hunched forward ever so slightly as I leaned forward to write on my too-old desktop computer. It was cracked in the corner, much like my glasses, and buzzed like a thousand angry bees each time that I needed to run a program larger than my emails or documents. I wondered, briefly, if they would calm down with a cigarette. Nicotine seemed to do wonders for curing anger, or so I’d seen with my father.

Thinking on it now, it’d been a rough couple of years after my mom had died. My father, strong man that he was, had started to decline in health. He couldn’t deal with the heartache, at least, not in a way that was healthy for him. Every time that I’d visited, he was lighting up a cigarette. Every hour on the hour, almost. Mom used to tell me how he’d chain-smoked an entire pack after having to put the family dog down when I was a child. How he couldn’t deal with coming home to see my tear-streaked face, knowing he’d been the cause of it. It was easier, she told me, to numb down your feelings until you barely felt a thing at all.

The night that I’d seen the man watching me, I remember shutting my curtains and throwing away my food before heading straight to bed. Paranoia set in and clung to my body like plastic wrap, making me practically suffocate in my tangled sheets. He’d be gone in the morning, I was sure. At least I was attempting to be sure of it in the moment. I’d woken up the next morning and gotten out of bed, ceramic mug soon in hand. Computer buzzing as I slid the bitter brew down my throat, I soon settled back into my routine. Analyze, read, and write. I analyzed, read, then sipped on my coffee. I analyzed, read, then stared at the window.

I couldn’t help myself. Was that man there? Was he staring at me like he had been last night? I’d abandoned typing to get up and go check. My feet thudded something dull and hurried as I scrambled to go see. I could hear my curtain rod scraping and screeching from the force that I pulled the blacked-out fabric apart, separating it to look below. When I ran my eyes down each unit, numbly checking off each unit of busybodies and loners, I saw him. He was dressed differently, no doubt, but he was staring at me just like he had before.

I’m not proud of the fact that I neglected to get any work done that day. Or the day after that. Any meaningful work, at least. But if you count staring back at a set of eyes, and not moving until the sun went down as being meaningful work, then I did plenty. He’d only left my line of vision when I’d felt my legs try to give out. I’d never stood in one place for that long before in years. My stiff joints ached from use as I hobbled over to my chair, sitting the moment that I was able. I didn’t move for minutes. I hadn’t needed to. I knew that if I went back, he’d be staring at me. My hunch was correct when I checked the next morning.

My routine began to change after that. I’d wake up, drink a mug of bitter black coffee, watch the man outside, and then work before checking on him every hour on the top of the hour. My nights changed, too. When I no longer had any obligation to work and my documents were all sent in, I’d sit at the window and watch those eyes until mine grew nearly too exhausted to remain open. On rougher days, I’d forego sleep in favor of keeping an eye on him. Sometimes he’d be eating. Other times he’d be drinking from a mug. Clusters of my own mugs cluttered my desk, as if mocking me. Regardless, no matter what he’d be doing, I’d always be there observing.

I knew that it likely wasn’t the most productive thing to do, but I couldn’t help myself. I’d always been one to cling to things that didn’t suit me. I wore old shirts with tears in them because the fabric still felt nice. I hadn’t changed the shoes that I’d wore for years despite them falling apart, simply because I liked how the laces looked. I could always buy a new pair, but why? I liked things how they needed to be.

That doesn’t mean I liked the things as they were, or that I didn’t suffer the consequences of my choices. People watched me on the street or stepped farther away on the sidewalk when I came by. To them, I looked unwell. To myself, I looked like the embodiment of my routine. I looked right, under my standards. I wore my shirts, I wore my shoes, and I wore them despite knowing how I looked. Despite knowing the judgment I’d receive.

My mother had suspected my affinity of disliking change early on when I couldn’t go to school without bringing my stuffed animal. I’d been bullied relentlessly back then, and that was just fuel for the fire. Things only changed when my father stepped in and put my old teddy bear up higher than I could reach. He’d told me to ‘grow up,’ and that only then could I have Mr. Brown back. Sleepless nights were what followed, not that they’d even stopped when I had my stuffed bear. I’d sleep with the blankets over my head, even when I couldn’t breathe because of the overwhelming warmth during summer. I’d sleep, back to the wall, to ensure that I couldn’t be grabbed by what I couldn’t see. I’d sleep, eyes on the door or my too-cracked open closet until I couldn’t keep them open anymore. I suppose watching things has always been a habit of mine. Never for the better.

My workflow had begun to slow down, as did the quality of everything that I turned in. It had to at some point. With the way that things were going, spending hours on mindless work was no longer something that I could do. It had been a gradual descent comprised of less documents being sent in a day before my first warning came. I straightened up briefly, intimidated, before falling back to my newfound habit. The second warning happened not even a month after the first. They’d been patient with me because, despite being a cog in their machine, I knew that their machine was old and rusted behind the scenes.

I knew that they were struggling to remain afloat, to keep their operation well lubricated and free of blemishes. Deadlines were what they stood firm by, not that I cared anymore. My third and final warning came after I’d gotten sloppy with my work, words typed in an incomprehensible rush when I’d tried to multitask for the first time, only scanning what I was meant to be analyzing before writing a half-assed report just to get my boss off my back.

 I was then fired within six months of my new, full-time occupation. Without any interruptions however, that only fueled my desire to keep an eye on him. I did so shamelessly from the comfort of my home. I did so, shamelessly, without the regard of how I was being affected by this. At the time, I’d thought that this was all I needed to do. Keep an eye on the man watching me. Bills could wait. I didn’t need lights to see. I didn’t need heat or air conditioning to sit in my chair and stay there.

I’d never realized how busy the streets below were until all I did was spend my hours watching them. It was the digital age- I had no need to go grocery shopping anymore. I had no need to step out for fresh air, not when I could sit by my window and breathe in smog and car exhaust. At least this way, I’d told myself, I wouldn’t be looked at anymore. Not strangely by normal folks, anyway. The man below looked at me with indifference.

On a good day, I’d eat it up like the meals I should’ve been consuming. On a bad day, I’d choke on it. He’d set a new standard, one that I had to adhere to. He’d told me I was good enough to be seen bare, as is, without expectation for well-maintained hair and clothes. Or perhaps, he just enjoyed seeing how he affected me, for better or for worse.

However, in the narrow line of my obsession, I had neglected one thing. Without sleep, I’d grow tired. My eyes would shut without my permission, and I’d enter fits of micro-sleep, fueled by exhaustion. And so, I’d done the only thing I could think of. Numb the urge to sleep and keep my body going. Coffee wasn’t doing it anymore. But cigarettes surely would. I made my way through a few, much faster than I’d like to admit.

My window had been cracked open at that point almost permanently due to the summer’s heat making my apartment feel like Hell rather than a home. Although maybe, that wasn’t too far off. Stale food caked the dishes in my sink while the stale odor of stained clothing sat heavily in my room’s basket.

A few more months passed by like that. I’d sit there, I’d smoke, and I’d watch. I’d sit there, I’d hold my stomach as it starved, I’d fill it with some coffee and then watch. I’d catch myself sleeping, I’d wake up, I’d smoke, then I’d watch. Sometimes now, looking back, I don’t even think I watched as much as I did sit there and stare at nothing. He wasn’t even always there anymore. He was gone often. But I stayed. I always did. After all this, I had to.

I think I should’ve used this time to self-reflect. It was what all the well-adjusted adults and online therapists were pushing out: reflecting on yourself and self-care. But what was there to reflect on? What I did with my life? What didn’t I do? My days were so simple that the only thing I could reflect on was the slowly disappearing man from my purview. And even then, I just wanted to reach out. I wanted to tell him to stay when I saw him. I wanted to hold him by his frail shoulders and squeeze desperately until he’d just look at me once more. I’d poured so much of myself into this, into him, so why should he get to stop what he’d started? It didn’t work like that. Bad things would happen if they did, I was sure.

But I was alone. At this point, I think I knew that deep down. It was rare that I’d see the man come to his window to look outside. I wasn’t even his priority anymore. He’d become hunched, staring down at the sidewalk below instead, probably more concerned with what little presents the birds above left behind as opposed to whatever I could do for him. I saw him holding a cane on the days he hobbled outside, and even that was getting rarer. I sat- no, I lingered. I managed to overstay my welcome in another man’s consciousness. I was done.

It wasn’t until the moving trucks came that I truly woke up from my trance. Furniture was pulled out of the apartment complex one at a time. Wooden wardrobes, old coatracks, bags of clothes. There was more taken out than I’d like to think was ever even inside for an old man. A new family moved in, some well-adjusted adults and their kids. Their dogs, their cats, and probably even other pets. I wouldn’t know after losing interest with the man was gone. But I had no need to worry. When I sat back and looked at my window, I saw a pair of eyes staring back at me, and I knew it hadn’t been for nothing.
