Names are a fascinating thing; people, probably old Victorians, have given groups of animals all manner of curious names; A shiver of sharks, an ambush of tigers, a parliament of owls, a court of eagles, and any number of fantastical and whimsical appelations intended, presumably, to reflect some inherent characteristic of said animal. Ravens are called an unkindness which has always seemed uncharitable to me. You are doubtlessly familiar with what a gathering of their fellow corvids crows are called.



Moons too have names; A September moon is called a Harvest moon, and an October moon a Hunters moon. These names may seem silly to us, but they reflect something real, ancient knowledge of the cycles of nature and pre- industrial life.



Names mean something; they hold a certain power. Even if you eschew any belief in the esoteric or supranormal, it is a fact of life in the digital age that ones name is not something you should easily part with. A name allows someone to find you.



I apologize dearly for digressing in such an egregious fashion; I have been in a pensieve mood as of late, as the season has caused me to recollect the events of an Halloween night some years prior, events which chills my soul to this very day. I share this partially as a way of unburdening myself, as once King Midas barber shouted the kings secrets into a hole in the ground, and partially as a warning to those willing to hear it.



My home and place of origin lies in the northern parts of the world, a vast expanse of boreal forest and swampland, of lakes, rivers and mountains. There are old things in those forests, things that have perhaps not seen daylight since Pangea and Panthalassia; fungi whose mycelium stretches for miles and is thousands of years old, flies that will ejaculate their larva straight into a mans eye where they will promptly start burrowing deeper, bears whose winter hunger drives them to brutal and violent madness, and isolated cabins whose denizens have neither interest nor ability to interact with the civilized world at any terms but their own.



It was All Hallows Eve, and there was a full moon, a hunters moon. Snow already covered the ground, its high albedo reflecting the moonlight to such a degree that one might see almost as well as in daylight. My friends and I agreed that this was an excellent night to hunt; perhaps a wiser man might have reasoned that it would be inappropriate to hunt on that sacred night, but we are modern men quite beyond superstition. One of our trail cameras had spotted a very special prey as well; A massive white bull moose with a magnificent antlered crown. It was, I am sad to say, greed that drove us out into that snowy wilderness that night, and greed that led to everything that transpired afterwards.



Old folks will tell you that one should not hunt white moose, but there is no law against it. Again, we are creatures of modernity and ancient customs do not gain legitimacy by their age alone. Sometimes, however, the reasons are quite valid though forgotten.



My friends were Johan, Petter and Fredrik, and Daniel, Viktor and Torleif. Torleif was the oldest of us, and had brough his teenaged son Johan, or little Johan, along.



We had driven out into the woods, then parted; I had found my spot, a hill overlooking an bog where moose are likely to cross. My friends had likewise found their position, save for the houndsman who would follow and track his hounds as they gave chase. The moon was bright, and the landscape an eerie shade of silver and dark blues. There was a chill in the air, and the only sound was the wind in the spruces and pines.



I sat like that for a few hours, trying to remains as still as possible. The radio was quiet; it a mountainous and hilly terrain where signals often travel poorly. I heard the occasional burst of static, but the words were almost impossible to make out.



Three hours after the sunset, there was an aurora; a faint, green and ghostly thing dancing over the horizon. So transfixed was I with it, that I failed to pay attention to the ground, and I saw a red fox slip away into the trees far to late to aim. Cursing at myself, I vowed to be more focused. The dogs were most likely far away as I had not heard any barking, but the moose are wiley and will often try to sneak away though the dogs are many miles from their location.



I did not hear her approach; suddenly there was a woman standing only a few feet away from me. It was strange, as footsteps in deep snow is usually quite audible. She smiled at me, a sweet summer thing, then spoke.



”Good evening sir. Are you having a pleasant evening?”



I was a tad spooked; she was wearing a dress rather than appropriate gear for the forest at night, and my first thought was that she was mad, perhaps dangerously so. There is a mental asylum not to far away, a century old institution home to many dangerous and disturbed individuals. Still, she looked harmless and had adressed me politely.



”Um… yes, I suppose. My friends and I are out hunting. What are you doing out here in the forest at night?”



She smiled, and stepped closer; My fingers tightened on my rifle before I realized how absurd I was being. I put the rifle to the side as she spoke.



”Oh, I am just out on a walk. Its a hunter moon tonight, and the world is bright and beautiful; May I surmise you are here for much the same reasons as I?”



I was somewhat calmed by this; people are allowed to take walks in the forest after all, even in peculiar garments.



”Ah yes- my friends and I really wanted to enjoy the moon tonight.”



My radio buzzed a little with static; an unheard signal from one of my friends. I smiled at her, and poured some coffee from my thermos. ”Would you like some?”



She nodded, and we spent a few minutes in amiable silence as she sipped the coffee and eagerly gnawed at the mixed nuts I had brought as a snack. When I brought out the chocolate bar she tried it out carefully, even sceptically, before devouring it with gusto.



”This is excellent; never have I tasted its like!” She exclaimed with a wide smile ”But tell me sir; what is your name”



I have her my name of course; we had spent a few minutes together, and to not offer it when prompted would be rude. As an armed man alone in the middle of the forest with an unarmed woman I naturally felt an inclination to make her feel as safe and comfortable as possible.



”Oh, that is an excellent name. I shall remember it, and I think you will have a lot of luck hunting in this forest sir.”



There was another burst of static on the radio. It seemed to be growing more frequent. The others must have spotted something, though it was likely far away from me.



She reached into one of her pockets and brought out some berries; I was sceptical at first, but they turned out to be a mix of blueberries and lingonberries, somewhat frosted and chilled but seemingly quite edible. I accepted and their taste was indeed excellent, if with a coppery aftertaste. Seeing the relish with which I devoured them, she laughed and offered me more.



”Did you dig in the snow for these?” I joked, and she laughed and shrugged, then pulled out some more berries and offered them up.



The radio grew more insistent, though it was still impossible to make out more than a few words.



”… blood… trail….”



It was easy to extrapolate what had happened; bull moose can easily run for 1000 feet after a lethal shot to the lung. Even after a succesful shot it is common to have to track them a bit, and blood shows up quite well on snow. Still, I should probably ask if they needed assistance.



”Miss, I am sorry but I think I have to go; you can go with me if you would like, but I believe my friends needs me.”



Her smile faded slightly, replaced by a slight pout; it only lasted a moment or two however before she shook her head.



”Well, it has been a pleasure to make your acquintance sir. I am sure we will meet again.”



I packed up my gear and made to depart, though I paused; the girl was wearing neither a cap nor mittens, and I had spares. I dug through my backpack and offered them to her, at which her face shone with delight. I pulled back the bolt on my rifle to remove the bullets, but she lifted a hand to stop me.



”Wait! Sir, I spotted a large white moose not very far from here. Keep your cartridge in its chamber, and you may walk away with an antlered crown this night.”



I hesitated; it is frowned upon to keep the cartridge in the rifle while not on ones post, but it would be a sad state of affairs to miss an opportunity at such worthy prey. I nodded and kept the rifle ready to fire as I departed.



”If I get a chance, I will owe you. Let me know if you want any of the meat; if you do it is yours.” I said with a smile. She did not reply, but smiled and put her hands to her heart.



I walked only a few hundred feet before I saw him; a huge white bull moose with perhaps 26 points on his antlered crown. I barely even remember aiming and firing, but he fell on the spot, the snow red with his blood. I approached carefully; when they fall immediately it is often a sign of spinal shock, and they may well get up again, to run or charge at you. Still, I need not have worried, as he was quite dead. I dipped my fingers in the wound and tasted the blood; an old tradition I had been taught as a boy. Next I snapped off a small bransch of pine and put it behind my ear; my crown, another old tradition. Finally, I signalled on the radio ”I shot a bull, and it is down.”



I got back another burst of static but nothing I could readily make out. After having marked the position on the map, I made to where Torleif, the friend closest to me, had been posted. Perhaps the signal would get better there.



As I approached his position, there was a flurry of sound and motion; A large murder of crows took flight. A good sign I thought; crows are carrion feeders and such a large gathering indicates that a prey had been taken down.



My good mood soon turned to horror; the scene was a nightmare of blood and gore. Torleif and his son, little Johan, were mutilated beyond reckoning; Torleifs legs had been torn or gnawed off at the knees, large spots of blood staining the snow below the useless stumps where the ends of the femur met the open air. Little Johan was more intact but perhaps in an even more gruesome state; his torso was unnaturally elongated, as if it had been pulled in two directions by wild horses. His torso was twisted at an angle. Blood stained his jacket, and both father and son lay with unnatural stillness.



I am not ashamed to admit that I vomited there and then, and cursed, and prayed to a god I do not believe in. Then I signalled on the radio to Daniel, our huntsman and the one most likely to be able to hear my signal.



”Daniel come in! Daniel come in! Something horrible has happened you need to call an ambulance! Anyone come in!”



I was again met only with a burst of static. With little else to do, I reloaded my rifle, and made for the cars that we had parked by the road. I stumbled frantically through the midnight forest, crying and panting with exertion. I fell several times, my feet catching on roots, or collapsing deeper into the snow. I crawled for a not inconsiderable distance.



As I reached the road, I found Daniel, sweaty and wild eyed and mumbling into his radio.



”Daniel! Something horrible has happened! We need to call the police, and the ambulance!”



I was crying as I shouted, snot running down my face. Viktor was in a similarly bad state.



”I have already called them. They are on their way.”



There was something flat in his voice, and something hollow in his eyes.



”The others are dead. I found Viktor with a bullet in his chest, and Johan nearby… Johan-...”



It took the police half an hour to arrive after that. The rest of the evening is a blur. I will spare you the details of the extent of my friends mutilations; I did not inquire, having not desire for such macabre details. What I had seen was quite enough. The police investigation eventually determined it to have been a bear attack; a large bear that had awakened to early from its hibernation and, mad with hunger, stalked us one by one.



I do not believe it. The police found no sign of the girl, no footprints at my post besides my own, but I remember her well. The head of the white moose on my office wall serves as a testament to the advice she gave me, as the dreams of Torleif and his sons mangled corpses haunt my dreams nightly.



I have not hunted since; bereft of all my friends it felt hollow, even pointless. But tonight it is a Hunters moon, and All Hallows Eve. I feel the pull of the forest, almost as if it is calling me, like a fire in my veins.



Tonight I will venture into the woods alone. Tomorrow I will post again to tell you of my experience. Until then, have a happy Halloween.
