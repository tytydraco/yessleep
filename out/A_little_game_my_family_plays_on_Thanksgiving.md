I’ve never really liked Thanksgiving.

My parents loved it, my Grandma *lived* for it, but to be honest it’s always been one of my least favorite days on the calendar.

Maybe I have a weird palate, because the food kind of turns me off. 

I also despise the season. In small-town Michigan, where I grew up, it’s far past the fall and well into early winter, with the pretty leaves long dead and the crisp sunny days now gray and dreary and depressingly short.

And family? Sure, some of them are OK. Many are actually great. But one bad person can ruin a whole gathering.

Growing up, that person was Uncle Tod.

Now, he never did anything untoward. We never felt in danger. 

But damn was Tod annoying.

He smacked his lips when he talked. He always had that uncomfortable smile, with one corner of his mouth pointing up. He yammered on about things only *he* found interesting. And he stood just way too close to everyone. He did it all without any self-awareness whatsoever. When he entered the room, you could feel the air suck out of it. Me and the cousins had to keep ourselves from audibly groaning.

This wasn’t just us being cruel kids, either. The adults hated him even more.

Tod would hover in the kitchen, going on and on about how he knows a guy who ran a restaurant once (seriously, he could go for 10 whole minutes about his friend’s straw purchases). Grandma, every single time, would “suggest” very firmly that he leave the cooking or the cleaning to the women. He would relocate to the living room, where my dad and some of the other guys would get tired of his running football commentary — he didn’t understand a thing about the game but still felt the need to chime in on every little detail — and boot him out of the bedroom, telling him to go spend time with us kids.

So, inevitably, he would find his way into the den where we were playing with toys or doing art or wrestling, and with that goofy grin on his face, he’d say: 

“Wanna play a game?”

To an adult, especially one who has seen a *Saw* movie, that might sound creepy. But to us, it was a bummer. Our self-guided fun was over. Tod was in charge. Get ready to play the same game, every time. And no matter how much we complained to our parents, in all those 12 years, we never had a choice.

Until Benji disappeared.

We stopped playing after that. 

We actually stopped gathering for Thanksgiving altogether. 

\*\*\*

I got the call three weeks ago.

Grandma wasn’t doing well.

This barely surprised me; she was a hard-living woman who had been trending that way for years. I hadn’t seen her much since I moved to Phoenix, but I still knew.

I also could sense what was coming next: She wanted one last Thanksgiving celebration.

I booked my flight within the hour. My opinion on Thanksgiving hadn’t changed one bit; in the desert I could almost pretend it wasn’t happening. But fulfilling her wish was the least I could do.

Also, and I hate to admit this mattered, but Uncle Tod died last year. 

It was sudden and tragic, the result of a home-invasion robbery gone wrong. None of us had wished anything like that on him. But I won’t lie — knowing he would be absent took a little bit of the edge off.

My flight landed in Detroit well after sunset. The drive took a while, I won’t say how long, but by the time we arrived at the old farmhouse she never left, Grandma was asleep and it was on us to prep for the following day.

There would be almost 40 of us across multiple generations — Grandma, four of her six kids (Tod was one of the absentees), numerous grandchildren and spouses (none for me, though), some nieces/nephews and grandnieces/grandnephews, and a gaggle of great-grandchildren.

About a third of them arrived the night before, including my mom and dad. And honestly, with the stress of my hectic, scattered adult life in the rearview for an evening, it was kind of nice.

The next day felt equally warm despite the frigid air outside, and only grew that way as family members showed up and we knew, morbid as it might sound, that Tod wouldn’t be there to put a damper on everything.

The older women — Grandma, plus my mom and aunts — were joined in the kitchen by my now-grown female cousins to cook, while my dad and living uncles and male cousins crowded around the TV and watched football (there were a couple of exceptions; cousin Jenna was a bigger Lions die-hard than any of us, while cousin Chase had no interest in any sport but knew exactly how to spice a holiday pie).

Grandma beamed the whole time.

The meal was, well, even I thought it was delicious. (It helped that Aunt Angie knew I’d gone vegetarian and she sneakily made me some mock turkey.) 

There was chatter and laughter, talking about our lives, *mostly* successful attempts at steering the conversation away from politics, and an actually thoughtful, loving tribute to Uncle Tod.

We did conspicuously avoid the subject of Benji, though.

When the meal ended, all the grandkids offered to clean things up, but the older women refused and said, “No, you kids go into the den and reconnect.” Unsure what that really meant, we followed the men into the living room for the final football game of the day, but even they froze us out and told us our generation was too used to looking at screens and we needed to actually talk to each other for a change.

So we did. It was even nice, for a while. I learned a lot about my cousins’ kids — Aiden, Lucy’s son, is a star in Math Olympics; Ryleigh, Clay’s daughter, got the lead in their church’s Christmas play. I even shared a little bit about myself, although it was so different from most people that I could tell their eyes were glazing over, and it certainly didn’t interest any of the children in the room, so I cut it short.

Just as I was about to pivot the conversation over to my cousin Bella’s upcoming wedding, the back door opened.

We looked over and saw a man — age unknown — standing there, bundled up, but not really visible in the low light.

“Um…hello?” I said, mostly because nobody else piped up.

He just stood there. Looming. Something was off and uncomfortable about him.

I got up to go get the “adults,” even though many of us in that room were adults ourselves, but before I could make any sort of move, he said it:

“Wanna play a game?”

He stepped into the light. For a split-second I expected it to be Uncle Tod, somehow still alive or maybe even back from the dead.

But it wasn’t. It was a face none of us recognized.

Then Grandma walked in. She knew who it was immediately:

“Benji?”

Uncle Tod called his game “Stuffing.”

It went like this:  
  
One person would start out as “it.” Or, as this was called in the game, “the stuffing.”

That “stuffing” would go hide. Inside, outside, in a car, a garage, a barn — the only rule was it had to be on Grandma’s property.

The rest of the kids were the “eaters.” After five minutes, they would begin searching high and low, seeking “the stuffing.” When the “eaters” found “the stuffing,” the game wasn’t over. It was only the beginning. Each “eater” would then become “stuffing” themselves — they would hide in that same spot, too, one by one, until all but a single “eater” was left. That last, unlucky soul would be the loser. Their punishment: They would be “the stuffing” in the next game.

(If this sounds familiar, it’s because it’s actually a very common game called Sardines. But since we never heard of it before Tod introduced us, his game name won out.)

Here’s how it went the night Benji disappeared:

Tod was “the stuffing.” He always started the game that way.

He hid, we counted, and when it was time to go look, everyone dispersed.

Except me. By that point, I was tired of his games and old enough to conduct a silent protest. So I didn’t even try.

I think Derrick found Tod first. Shelby and Shanda followed; they were just a year younger than me, but always so earnest. Kelvin, too, was so competitive that he couldn’t help himself. The others disappeared one by one. I wandered into the kitchen, pretending I thought he had hidden himself in the pantry, but the women saw right through my trick and kicked me out.

Slowly, the number of kids in my age group — there were 14 total — had shrunk to the point where I didn’t see any of them. I went into the living room to watch the last of the football game, but my dad threatened to withhold dessert for the next week if I didn’t find Tod in the next five minutes.

I hustled through all the bedrooms, including the one my mom and Aunt Mary had shared; it still had their self-drawn Beatles posters up. I searched high and low inside, even in the little closet with the water heater, despite knowing 14 people couldn’t possibly fit in there.

Annoyed that Uncle Tod wasn’t inside, I threw on a coat and shuffled out into the cold — I hated freezing temps then and still hate them now; why do you think I moved to Arizona? Through biting wind and flurries I scoured for hiding spots, looking at both sides of Grandma’s detached garage/shed and even peeking in the crawlspace before hearing a faint voice, then a snicker.

It was coming from Uncle Mike’s minivan.

I trudged over and threw open the door.

The whole crew was inside.

“Good one, Uncle Tod,” I said, but they quickly all shushed me.

I was confused; surely I had lost and was about to be “the stuffing.”

“Benji’s still out there,” Tod whispered with a grin.

Frustrated beyond belief, I piled into the back of the van and slammed the door loudly, fully intending it to catch Benji’s attention, wherever he was.

It didn't work.

I don’t know how long we sat back there in the freezing vehicle.

Some of the younger ones started whining. Molly even cried.

Benji never arrived.

\*\*\*

Benji was always a jolly sort of kid. He giggled when he played and actually laughed at Uncle Tod’s eyeroll-inducing jokes. He said please and thank you and sir and ma’am. He annoyed most of us, but not on purpose — he was no calculating suck-up, he was just Benji, but we were kids and kids are cruel and I can say we were a little unfair to him.

Benji did like to play Stuffing, though.

That’s why it was so weird when he never showed up.

The search for Benji was far from what you see on TV or in movies.

First it was just a flood of overfed family members wandering Grandma’s property, calling out,  yelling, shouting, even trying some encouraging words because maybe soft, sweet Benji would respond to those better. We kids watched it all unfold from the inside. Uncle Tod tried to entertain us. It did not work.

After an hour Benji’s mom, Aunt Linda, finally called 9-1-1. The sheriff sent out two deputies, they did the same search as our family, and found the exact same thing that we did: Nothing.

State police came next, and their slow-moving search through the wilderness around Grandma’s turned up a single, solitary glove that Linda swore was Benji’s, although it did seem a bit too dusty and old to have been lost that day.

By then night had fully fallen and the temperatures were just too cold to continue. Aunt Linda screamed for them to do more and Uncle Doug, Benji’s dad, kept repeating “why can’t you call the FBI?”

Eventually an agent from the field office did interview Linda and Doug. At least that’s what someone told me years later. The two of them stopped communicating with the family after that day. 

At least, officially.

Once a year, around Thanksgiving, me and the other 12 cousins would receive anonymous, threatening messages on various social media platforms. Cryptic notes, like “I know what you did,” and “I’m watching you.” We assumed it was Linda or Doug.

We tried to ignore them. 

\*\*\*

Nothing about Benji’s return that day made sense — he disappeared when he was 8, and now he was back, a fully grown adult?

But no one said a thing about it as we stood there watching him hug Grandma. You know how when there’s a large group of people you’re less likely to speak up, to be the voice of reason? This was one of those times.

Grandma then made us all regather in the dining room. She even called Linda and Doug, but got no answer on any of their phones. Still no one protested; I tried to whisper something to Mom, but she shushed me as we all took our original seats.

Benji ate voraciously, like he hadn’t consumed food in years.

Meanwhile we all stared. Finally, some of the bolder adults tried to get him to answer questions.

“What happened?”

“Where have you been?”

“Are you OK?”

“What. Happened.”

“How old are you?”

“Should we take you to a hospital?”

“No, seriously, what the hell happened?!”

Benji just smiled and said to Grandma, “Ma’am, thank you for this feast.”

About 11 of the longest, most awkward minutes later, after it was clear that Benji wasn’t interested in or capable of engaging with complex questions, Chase meekly asked if Benji wanted any of his pie.

“No thank you,” he said with a smile.

“What about cranberries?” Aunt Mary offered (she was the one who brought them and was always so proud).

“That’s quite all right, ma’am,” he replied, his face not changing an ounce.

Grandma jumped in with “Well how about—” but before she could finish, Benji cut her off.

“I am fuller than a stuffed pig!” he said, standing. "I would like something though, if you wouldn't mind."

“What do you want?” Grandma responded.

Benji, with that smile still plastered on his face and seeming to look directly at me, said the following words:

“I wanna play a game.”

His smile grew.

“I’ll be the stuffing.”

\*\*\*

When Benji left, we finally protested. But Grandma wouldn't have it.

“You always made Benji feel unwelcome,” she said. “This miracle is your chance to fix that.”

That took the air out of our objections. Shelby and Shanda were the first to get on board, saying simultaneously, “It’s just one silly game.” Derrick agreed and seemed eager to get going. Kelvin got back his competitive spirit and told Derrick, “You’re going down bro.” Grandma’s smile then lit up the room so much that we seemed to forget just how unusual and impossible this all was.

We entered the den to see Benji grinning at us. “You ready?” he asked.

We looked at each other and offered tepid nods.

Benji instantly closed his eyes.

“What are you doing?” I asked.

“Visualizing,” he said, never breaking that grin for one second.

We stared at him, silently, until a sound broke our trance — it was Grandma, arriving in the doorway, offering her own big smile.

Then, without any warning, Benji’s eyes flew open and he shot off the couch and said “start counting!” and then he was gone.

We started to do as we were told per the rules of the game.

Until Jenna chimed in with, “I have a bad feeling about this.” Grandma shushed her, but Jenna was undeterred: “Are we seriously just going to act like this is normal?! What if he wants to hurt us??”

“But, like…he’s hiding,” Shelby replied, her voice barely above a whisper.

“So?” Jenna shot back.

“So…how can he hurt us if he’s the stuffing?” Shanda said.

“*Children*.” That was Grandma, looking downright angry that we were disrupting this moment. We didn’t correct her about our ages. We just finished the count, opened our eyes, and all took deep breaths.

The game had officially begun.

\*\*\*

I’m writing this down from our local sheriff’s substation. One of the deputies who helped search for Benji, Dept. De Ligt, is in charge of the county now. So he knows the history. I’ve already given my statement.

Here’s the abbreviated version:

We all fanned out once the contest started. Derrick and Kelvin went with gusto, Shelby and Shanda with mild enthusiasm, Jenna and I with extreme reluctance, and the rest just going through the motions. 

Thankfully, all of my cousins’ children were being watched over by their grandparents. Otherwise…I don’t even want to think about it.

Part worried but also part irritated, I tried my old sandbagging routine from years earlier — first I visited the kitchen, where I was quickly dispatched, then the living room, where Grandma was sitting, staring at me with grave disappointment.

I realized that the longer I delayed this the longer it would last, so I started searching for real.

I went upstairs — again, to stay out of the cold — and locked my eyes on an old antique wardrobe Grandma had inherited from her mom. Thinking it couldn’t be that easy but wanting to try something, I went over and opened the double doors. A waft of dust and must and ancient cigarette smoke smacked me in the face. I should have been grateful that it was a mere annoyance, and not what was about to come next.

My head was buried amid gowns and dresses and trench coats when I heard something coming from outside. I pulled my head back and looked around, but nothing ensued for a few seconds so I returned to looking.

The next time I heard it, though, I knew what it was: 

A scream.

But the thing about screams is you don’t always know whether they’re from terror or delight. Some people yelp when they’re laughing or having fun. I barely knew my cousins, so I had little idea what it was.

The third one left no doubt, though.

This was a shriek of pure terror.

“Did you hear that?” I asked as I rushed into the living room, but the game's volume was up so loud that half couldn’t comprehend me and the others told me to quit stalling and get to looking for my long-lost cousin.

Finally more scared than exasperated, I approached the front door with apprehension, almost wondering if this was some sort of dream. Before I could grab the doorknob, though, it flew open, jumpscaring me worse than I’d ever been startled before.

On the other side: Shanda. Covered in blood. Unable to speak.

She just screamed bloody murder.

Before I knew it, I was being pulled toward the detached garage, joined by about half of my cousins, and a number of our elders, all on a mission. Shanda still hadn’t told us what we were about to see. But we knew it wasn’t good.

When we rounded the corner, what we saw we simply couldn’t comprehend.

It took what felt like minutes, but was surely just a few seconds, before anyone could move.

Shelby was hanging there.

Suspended from a rope. Eyes bulging. Pine cone shoved in her mouth. Stomach cut open and stuffed with leaves and twigs and dirt and anything that could be scrounged from the outdoors.

I don’t even know how we reacted.

As I sit here, writing this, Derrick and Kelvin have been found. I won’t even describe what happened to them. Bella and Andi are still missing. 

We also no longer believe Tod’s death was a random home invasion.

All of this, we think — no, we *know* — is Benji.

The rest of us, well, we're shaken, hiding out in this substation, hoping and praying that the security of a small-town sheriff’s department is enough to at least see us through to the morning. When we do, surely we’ll have to get back to our lives, some of us nearby, some in Grand Rapids or Detroit, and a couple including me well out of state.

But something tells me the game isn’t over.

It might never be.

Until they can track down our crazed, murderous cousin, we’ll be hiding, one way or another, forever.

Like he was that fateful first night, Benji is the last “eater.”

Once again, we’re all “the stuffing.”
