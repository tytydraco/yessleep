I'm writing this in the hope that putting everything that happened to me at that hospital into words will finally give me some peace. Ever since I left, I've been paranoid, barely sleeping, and constantly looking over my shoulder. I can't remember the last time I slept through an entire night without waking up convinced that someone was standing in my room.  
  
Before any of this happened, I had absolutely no interest in horror or the paranormal. I was never the type to watch scary movies, look up ghost stories, or go exploring abandoned buildings in the hope of finding something supernatural. I genuinely couldn't understand why people enjoyed frightening themselves.  
  
My mentality was always pretty simple: just look around you. Reality is already scary enough. There are terrible things happening in the world every single day, things we have actual proof of, and yet people would rather spend their time chasing ghosts and getting excited over the possibility of something moving in the dark. I never understood that obsession with fear, that need for an adrenaline rush from something that probably wasn't even there.  
  
Anyway, that's what I used to think. Until I experienced things I still can't explain.  
  
I found a job advertisement on a French website called Leboncoin. I remember thinking it was a strange place to advertise a security position, but by that point, I was desperate enough to look just about anywhere. I'd been going through job listings for weeks, searching for something physical that didn't involve construction work or spending another eight hours sitting behind a desk.  
  
Ideally, I wanted something at night. Somewhere quiet, where I wouldn't have to deal with too many people. After spending all day surrounded by colleagues, meetings, and conversations I had absolutely no interest in having, the idea of working alone sounded almost relaxing. A few hours of walking around, checking doors, making sure everything was where it should be, and then going home. That was what I imagined, anyway.  
  
Looking back, I find it almost funny that I was specifically searching for night shifts. These days, I couldn't imagine willingly spending an entire night alone, no matter how much someone offered to pay me.  
  
There's something about the night that I never really appreciated before. The feeling you get when you're walking down an empty street after midnight, passing through a deserted park, or being alone in a house. That strange instinct that makes you look over your shoulder, even when you know there's nobody behind you.  
  
I used to think it was just our imagination playing tricks on us. Our brains trying to find danger where there wasn't any.  
  
Now I understand why people have such a hard time believing they're completely alone.  
  
Because sometimes, they aren't.  
  
So back to the story. I don't want to take much of your time, but in order for you to believe me, I need to tell you everything.  
  
Now, I won't pretend there weren't any red flags, as the younger generation likes to call them. Looking back, there were quite a few, actually. The first one was the job advertisement itself. Like I said, finding a night security position on Leboncoin seemed a little strange to me, but I wasn't exactly in a position to be picky. The second red flag should have been the location.  
  
The hospital was in Le Kremlin-Bicêtre, just outside Paris, and I remember thinking that the whole area looked pretty rough. Not necessarily dangerous, just depressing. The kind of place where you wouldn't particularly want to find yourself wandering around in the middle of the night.  
  
The hospital itself didn't exactly inspire confidence either. Parts of it looked modern enough, but other sections seemed like they belonged to an entirely different century. There were these enormous old buildings, worn stone walls, and courtyards that looked like they hadn't changed much in decades. I remember standing outside the entrance, wondering whether I'd somehow ended up at the wrong address.  
  
I later found out that Bicêtre had originally been established in the seventeenth century and had served as everything from a hospital to a prison and an asylum. Apparently, it had housed psychiatric patients and political prisoners, and some pretty horrific things had happened there over the centuries. There were stories about brutal treatments, experiments, and people who had spent most of their lives locked away inside those walls.  
  
Of course, I didn't know any of that when I first arrived. If I had, maybe I would have thought twice about accepting the job. Then again, knowing myself back then, I probably would have dismissed it as another one of those exaggerated ghost stories people love telling about old buildings.  
  
And besides, what choice did I really have? Hobson's choice, as they say. I needed the money, someone was offering me a job, and I wasn't about to turn it down because the building looked a little creepy.  
  
So I went inside.  
  
I was asked to come in around 7 p.m. so someone could show me around, explain my responsibilities, and basically teach me how to do the job. I wasn't completely new to security work, though. A few years earlier, I'd worked as a security guard in a corporate building. Now, I should probably mention that I didn't exactly leave my previous security job on the best of terms. I got fired after they caught me sleeping on my shift, and they didn’t appreciate my nonchalant attitude.  
  
I needed this job so I arrived a little before seven, hoping that being early would at least make a decent first impression.  
  
A man was waiting for me on the first floor. After a quick introduction, I was told that I'd be responsible for the west wing of the hospital. Apparently, it was mostly occupied by elderly patients, many of whom suffered from dementia, confusion, or other psychiatric conditions. The man showing me around warned me that some of them could become agitated during the night, but he assured me that it was nothing I should worry about. Most of them were harmless, and the nursing staff would be there if anything serious happened.  
  
My instructions were surprisingly simple. I was supposed to patrol the main corridor, starting from the rooms on the right-hand side and gradually making my way toward the left. Each room had a small window built into its door, allowing me to check on the patients without disturbing them. All I had to do was look through the glass, make sure everyone was in their assigned bed and appeared to be lying in a normal position, and report anything unusual to the nurses. I wasn't supposed to enter the rooms unless a member of the medical staff specifically asked me to.  
  
I remember thinking it sounded almost too easy. I was essentially being paid to walk down a hallway and look through a few windows every hour. Compared to dealing with office workers, delivery drivers, and the occasional drunk visitor at my previous job, this seemed like a pretty good arrangement. The only thing that struck me as a little odd was how particular the man was about the order of the patrol. He repeated twice that I should always start on the right and finish on the left. I didn't ask why. I assumed it was simply the way they organised their rounds, and I wasn't about to start questioning instructions on my first evening.  
  
While he was showing me around the wing, I noticed that one of the rooms was empty. It wasn't particularly unusual for a hospital, I suppose, but something about it caught my attention. Every other room we'd passed had some sign of life, whether it was a patient sleeping, a television playing quietly, or personal belongings scattered around the bed. This one had none of that. The bed was perfectly made, the curtains were drawn, and there wasn't a single personal item in sight. What bothered me most was that the light above the bed was still on, illuminating an otherwise completely empty room.  
  
I remember slowing down to get a better look through the little window, but the man showing me around continued walking without so much as glancing inside. For a moment, I considered asking him why the room was empty, especially since he'd been so particular about checking every single patient. But I was tired, my feet were already hurting, and, frankly, I just wanted him to finish the damn tour so I could get on with my shift. So I kept my mouth shut and followed him down the corridor.  
  
Looking back, I wish I'd asked him about that room. Not because I think he would have told me the truth, but because I sometimes wonder whether he would have reacted differently if he'd known I'd noticed it.  
  
As we continued down the corridor, I felt a headache coming on. I've suffered from chronic migraines for as long as I can remember, so I recognised the feeling almost immediately. Thankfully, things got a little better as I grew older, and over the years, I learned how to manage them.  
  
The medication I take is pretty strong, so I try to save it for when the pain becomes unbearable. Thankfully, it hadn't reached that point yet, although I could already feel the pressure building behind my eyes.  
  
We eventually reached the nurses' station, where a woman in her late forties was sitting behind a desk, typing something into a computer. Her ginger hair was tied back tightly and looked like she hadn't had a particularly good evening. The man accompanying me approached her and explained that I was the new security guard assigned to the west wing.  
  
"Another one?" she asked without looking up.  
  
"He's starting tonight. I just need you to go over the emergency procedures with him."  
  
She finally looked away from her screen, clearly irritated, and looked straight into me. What caught my attention, though, were her eyes. They were a striking shade of green, although the whites were so bloodshot that she looked as if she hadn't slept properly in days, her lips were dry and slightly cracked, as though she'd spent the entire day without drinking a drop of water.  
  
"Seriously? I've got three patients waiting for medication, and you want me to train security?"  
  
"It'll take five minutes," he replied.  
  
She sighed and pushed her chair away from the desk. I stood there feeling slightly embarrassed, as if I'd somehow caused an argument just by showing up.  
  
"Fine. Come here," she said, waving me over.  
  
As she stood up, I noticed her adjusting the collar of her uniform. She pulled it higher on one side, covering what looked like a dark mark on her neck. I didn't get a particularly good look at it, but it seemed too large to be an ordinary bruise.  
  
She caught me looking and immediately dropped her hand.  
  
"Something wrong?"  
  
"No, sorry. Just a bit of a headache."  
  
"Well, you're going to have to get used to those around here," she replied.  
  
I wasn't entirely sure whether she was joking. Judging by her expression, probably not.  
  
She went through the emergency procedures rather quickly, explaining which number to call if a patient became aggressive, where the nearest exits were, and what I was supposed to do if someone tried to leave the ward without permission. Every so often, she'd stop to check the time or glance toward the corridor, clearly eager to get back to whatever she'd been doing before we arrived.  
  
At one point, she asked whether I'd understood everything.  
  
"Yeah, I think so. Just check the rooms, report anything unusual, and don't go inside unless someone asks me to."  
  
"Good," she said. "And don't try to be a hero. If something happens, you call us."  
  
She said that last part with a seriousness that caught me slightly off guard. Before I could respond, she adjusted her collar again, pressing her fingers against the side of her neck.  
  
Then she turned back toward her desk.  
  
"Anything else?"  
  
I shook my head. I still had a few questions, but between my headache and her obvious lack of enthusiasm, I decided they could wait.  
  
\*\*The First Night\*\*  
  
My first round started a little after ten. By then, most of the patients had settled down for the night, and the west wing was surprisingly quiet. There was the occasional cough coming from behind a door, the distant sound of a television, and the constant humming of those horrible fluorescent lights that seem to exist in every hospital in the world. I remember thinking that, apart from the headache, the evening was actually going pretty well.  
  
I started on the right-hand side, just as I'd been instructed, looking through the little windows in each door. Most of the patients were asleep, although a few were still awake, staring at the ceiling or watching television. One elderly man noticed me looking in, he gave me a sad smile and waved. I waved back, feeling a little ridiculous, and continued down the corridor.  
  
It was around the fourth or fifth room that I noticed something that made the hairs on my back stand straight up. As I was passing next to another room, I looked through the window and there was an old woman lying in bed, facing the wall, and behind her, stretching across the pale wallpaper, was what looked like the shadow of someone standing beside her. I leaned closer to the glass, expecting to see a nurse or perhaps a visitor just outside my field of vision, but the room was empty. The shadow didn't seem to move either, even when the woman turned over in her sleep.  
  
I figured it was probably something to do with the lighting. Hospitals are full of equipment, and the rooms had all sorts of strange lamps and monitors that could easily cast unusual shapes on the walls. Besides, my head was beginning to hurt again, and I wasn't particularly interested in standing there trying to solve the mystery of a shadow.  
  
The next few rooms were perfectly normal, and after a few hours of walking around I was convinced that what I saw earlier was a fluke.  
  
Then, as I approached one of the doors on the left, I saw something that made me stop. There was a man standing on a chair beside the window. He looked quite old, perhaps in his seventies, and he was leaning forward with both hands pressed against the glass. At first, I thought he was trying to open it, but then he shifted his weight, and I realised how dangerously close he was to falling. I couldn't tell whether he was confused or deliberately trying to hurt himself.  
  
"Sir?" I called through the door, tapping on the little window. "Are you alright?"  
  
He didn't react. I knocked again, harder this time, and reached for the handle, completely forgetting the nurse's instructions about not entering patients' rooms.  
  
Then I looked through the glass again.  
  
The man was lying in bed.  
  
I stood there with my hand on the door handle, trying to understand what I'd just seen. The chair was tucked neatly beneath a small table on the opposite side of the room, nowhere near the window. The man was lying on his back with his eyes closed, and from what I could tell, he was fast asleep.  
  
I must have stared at him for a good thirty seconds. I even checked the room number to make sure I hadn't somehow confused two doors, although I knew perfectly well that I hadn't moved.  
  
Eventually, I stepped away. My migraine had become considerably worse by then, and I was starting to feel that familiar nausea that usually meant I needed to sit down. I told myself I'd probably caught the man moving around and misinterpreted what he was doing. Maybe he'd already climbed down and returned to bed while I was looking at the handle. It didn't make much sense, but neither did the idea that he'd somehow teleported across the room.  
  
I continued my patrol, although I was paying considerably more attention to the windows now.  
  
That's when I noticed someone walking across the far end of the corridor.  
  
I only saw them for a second, just long enough to make out a dark figure disappearing around the corner. I assumed it was a member of staff, perhaps the nurse I'd met earlier, although something about the way the person moved seemed strange. They weren't walking particularly fast, but I hadn't heard any footsteps.  
  
"Excuse me?" I called.  
  
Nobody answered.  
  
I waited for a moment, expecting someone to come back around the corner, but the corridor remained empty. I considered following them, then remembered that I was supposed to stay within my assigned section of the wing.  
  
By that point, my head was pounding so badly that I could barely concentrate. I decided to finish the round and return to the security office. Whatever was happening in that corridor could wait until I'd had a chance to sit down. Once in the office, I opened my bag and started looking for my medication. I was sure I'd brought it with me. In fact, I distinctly remembered putting the little packet in the front pocket that morning, precisely because I knew I'd be working a double shift.  
  
But it wasn't there.  
  
I emptied the entire bag onto the desk, checked my jacket, my trouser pockets, and even looked underneath the chair in case it had fallen out. Nothing. I remember getting irrationally angry about it, which usually happens when my migraines get this bad. I was exhausted, I felt sick, and the thought of having to spend another six hours in that place without anything to relieve the pain was enough to make me want to cry.  
  
I was still searching through my things when the telephone on the desk rang.  
  
I nearly jumped out of my chair. It was an old-fashioned office phone, the kind with a horrible, sharp ringing sound that seems unnecessarily loud in an otherwise quiet room. I stared at it for a moment before picking up.  
  
"Security, hello?"  
  
There was silence on the other end, followed by a faint crackling noise.  
  
"Hello? Can you hear me?"  
  
"Please..."  
  
The voice was so quiet that I could barely make it out. It sounded like an elderly man, although there was something strange about the way he spoke, as if he were struggling to breathe.  
  
"Hello, sir. Are you alright?"  
  
"Please, I need help."  
  
"Of course. Can you tell me the number of your room?"  
  
There was a pause, and then I heard what sounded like someone moving the telephone away from their mouth. In the background, several people appeared to be talking at once. I couldn't understand what they were saying, but their voices were getting louder.  
  
"Sir? Which room are you calling from?"  
  
"Please don't leave me here."  
  
I was taken aback by that sentence, as it was the same thing my mother told me before I left her at the old people's home; I immediately felt the guilt.  
  
"I'm not going to leave you. I just need to know where you are."  
  
Another breathless pause.  
  
"Fourteen."  
  
I frowned. I remembered that number because it belonged to the empty room I'd noticed during the tour. I was almost certain of it.  
  
"Room fourteen?"  
  
"He's coming back."  
  
"Who's coming back?"  
  
The man didn't answer. Instead, I heard a sudden burst of noise, like several people shouting over one another. Then, through all that noise, I heard a woman scream. I pulled the receiver away from my ear, startled by the volume. Then, just as abruptly as it had started, everything went quiet.  
  
For a few seconds, I heard nothing but a faint crackling sound. Then a woman's voice came through, so quiet that I almost missed it.  
  
"Don't leave me here."  
  
"Hello? Ma'am, can you hear me?"  
  
"Don't leave me here," she repeated, barely above a whisper.  
  
I pressed the receiver closer to my ear, struggling to make out her words. There was something strangely familiar about her voice, but she was speaking so softly that I couldn't place it.  
  
"Please, speak a little louder. I can't hear you."  
  
"Don't... leave... me... here..."  
  
I brought the receiver even closer, concentrating on every word.  
  
And then she screamed.  
  
"JULIEN! DON'T LEAVE ME HERE!"  
  
The sound was so sudden and deafening that I cried out and dropped the receiver. It hit the desk with a loud clatter, but I could still hear her screaming through it.  
  
I knew that voice.  
  
It was my mother.  
  
My mother, who had been dead for almost eight years.  
  
For a few seconds, I just stood there staring at the phone. I could still hear the voices coming through the speaker, although they were quieter now. I wanted to believe I'd imagined it, that my headache had somehow made me confuse a stranger's voice with my mother's, but there was something about the way she'd pronounced my name that I couldn't dismiss. She used to put a particular emphasis on the last syllable whenever she was frightened or angry with me. I'd forgotten about that until I heard it again.  
  
I don't know why I decided to go to Room 14. Looking back, it was probably the stupidest decision I made that night. But I wasn't thinking clearly anymore. Part of me wanted to prove that there was nothing there, that someone was playing a particularly cruel joke on the new security guard. Another part of me, one I'm not particularly proud of, wanted to hear my mother's voice again.  
  
I grabbed my phone and hurried back into the corridor.  
  
The west wing was quiet when I reached it. I passed the first few rooms without looking inside, keeping my eyes fixed on the numbers above the doors. I remember noticing that the light in Room 14 was still on, just as it had been during the tour.  
  
I stopped outside and looked through the little window.  
  
The room was empty.  
  
The bed was perfectly made, the curtains were drawn, and there wasn't a single personal belonging in sight. I stood there for a moment, listening, but couldn't hear anything coming from inside.  
  
"Hello?" I called, feeling rather stupid. "Is someone in there?"  
  
No answer.  
  
I tried the handle. To my surprise, the door opened.  
  
I stepped inside, intending to check whether there was a telephone somewhere near the bed. I thought perhaps someone had been using the room earlier and left a phone connected, or that the call had somehow been transferred incorrectly. I realise none of this explains what I'd heard, but I was desperately looking for something that made sense.  
  
There was a telephone on the bedside table, but the receiver was resting neatly in its cradle.  
  
I approached it, and that was when the door slammed shut behind me.  
  
The sound was so violent that I flinched. I turned around immediately and tried the handle, but it wouldn't move. I pulled harder, then pushed against the door with my shoulder, convinced that the lock had somehow caught.  
  
"Hello? Can somebody open this?"  
  
I knocked several times, then started banging with my fist.  
  
"HEY! I'M LOCKED IN HERE!"  
  
Nobody answered.  
  
I took out my mobile and tried calling the nurses' station. The screen showed that it was dialling, but there was no ringing tone. After a few seconds, the call ended by itself. I tried again, then attempted another number, but the same thing happened. The phone would display the number, begin dialling, and then return to the call screen as though nothing had happened.  
  
I still had reception. I could see the signal bars at the top of the screen, which somehow made the whole thing even more frustrating.  
  
"Come on, for fuck's sake," I muttered, trying again.  
  
Then the light went out.  
  
I don't mean it flickered or gradually dimmed. One second, the room was illuminated by the lamp above the bed, and the next, everything was completely dark.  
  
I froze.  
  
For a moment, I couldn't even remember where I was standing. The darkness seemed so complete that I couldn't distinguish the bed from the walls, and I had the horrible sensation that the room was much larger than it had been a few seconds earlier. I switched on my phone's flashlight, but my hands were shaking so badly that I nearly dropped it.  
  
My headache suddenly became excruciating. It felt as though someone had driven something sharp through the back of my skull and was slowly pushing it toward my eyes. The pain made me nauseous, and when I tried to take a step toward the door, the entire room seemed to tilt beneath my feet.  
  
I couldn't stand it anymore.  
  
I slid down against the wall until I was sitting on the floor, pulling my knees toward my chest and burying my head in my hands. I pressed my palms against my temples as hard as I could, hoping the pressure might somehow ease the pain. My breathing had become shallow and irregular, and I could feel sweat running down my back despite how cold the room seemed.  
  
I remember thinking that I was going to pass out. It had happened when I was younger, during the worst of my migraines, but I hadn't experienced pain like this in years. I was terrified of losing consciousness in that room, with the door locked and nobody knowing where I was.  
  
I tried calling for help again, but my voice came out weak and unsteady.  
  
And then, somewhere in the darkness, I heard my mother say my name.  
  
At first, I thought I'd imagined it. I could barely keep my eyes open, and I was beginning to lose track of how long I'd been sitting there. But then I heard it again.  
  
"Julien..."  
  
It was my mother's voice, coming from somewhere in front of me. I lifted my head, trying to see through the darkness, but there was nothing. I wanted to answer her, although I couldn't bring myself to speak. My mother had been dead for eight years. I knew that. I'd attended her funeral, for God's sake. Yet I could hear her breathing, and when she said my name again, it sounded so much like her that I felt my stomach turn.  
  
Then something moved beside me.  
  
I heard what sounded like a chair being dragged across the floor, followed by a series of footsteps. At first, I thought someone had finally come to help me, but the footsteps didn't seem to be coming from the door. They were moving around the room, slowly, as though someone were pacing back and forth. Then I heard more footsteps, this time behind me, and the unmistakable sound of people whispering to one another.  
  
Within seconds, it felt as though the entire room had filled with people. I could hear them moving around the bed, brushing against the furniture, breathing heavily just a few feet away from me. Something knocked against the bedside table, and I heard an object fall to the floor. I tried to turn on my phone's flashlight again, but the screen was so bright against my migraine that I immediately shut my eyes.  
  
That's when I felt something touch my shoulder.  
  
It was unmistakably a hand. I could feel the pressure of fingers pressing through the fabric of my uniform, and I jerked away so violently that the back of my head struck the wall. Once, I felt a hand close around my ankle, and I pulled my leg toward my chest with all the strength I had.  
  
"GET AWAY FROM ME!" I shouted.  
  
The whispering grew louder. I couldn't understand most of it, but there were so many voices that I genuinely believed I was surrounded by people. Some sounded elderly, others much younger. I could hear someone crying near the bed, a man laughing somewhere behind me, and a woman repeating the same few words over and over again.  
  
Then my mother's voice rose above them.  
  
"You betrayed me, Julien."  
  
I stopped moving.  
  
"You betrayed me."  
  
"No..." I whispered.  
  
"You left me there."  
  
I covered my ears, pressing my hands so hard against the sides of my head that it made the pain even worse. I didn't want to hear her. I couldn't bear hearing her say those things, especially in that voice. The same voice that used to call me downstairs for dinner, that used to comfort me when I was sick. Except now she sounded furious.  
  
"You knew what you were doing."  
  
"Please, Mum..."  
  
"You betrayed me."  
  
I kept trying to make myself smaller, pressing my knees against my chest, but there was nowhere left to go.  
  
"YOU BETRAYED ME!"  
  
"I'M SORRY!" I screamed.  
  
I was crying by then, properly crying, the way I hadn't cried since I was a child. I just wanted it to stop.  
  
"I'M FUCKING SORRY! I DIDN'T KNOW WHAT ELSE TO DO!"  
  
I screamed it again, even louder, my throat hurting from the effort.  
  
"I DIDN'T KNOW WHAT ELSE TO DO! PLEASE!"  
  
And then the lights came on.  
  
I flinched and threw my arms over my face, completely blinded by the sudden brightness. For a few seconds, I couldn't see anything but white spots dancing in front of my eyes. The whispering had stopped. So had the footsteps. I could no longer feel anything touching me.  
  
"Jesus Christ! What the hell are you doing?"  
  
I slowly lowered my hands.  
  
Standing in the doorway was the nurse I'd met earlier. She was staring at me with her mouth slightly open, one hand still resting on the light switch.  
  
I looked around the room.  
  
There was nobody there.  
  
The bed was perfectly made, the bedside table was in its place, and the chair hadn't moved. Nothing was overturned, nothing was broken. The room looked exactly as it had when I'd first entered it.  
  
I was still sitting on the floor with my knees pulled against my chest, my face wet with tears and my uniform soaked in sweat. My hands were trembling so badly that I couldn't even push myself up.  
  
"Are you alright?" she asked, although the expression on her face suggested she already had an answer.  
  
I opened my mouth, but nothing came out.  
  
She looked from me to the empty bed and then back again. I could see her trying to understand what she'd walked in on, and frankly, I couldn't blame her. From where she was standing, I must have looked completely insane.  
  
"Who were you talking to?" she asked.  
  
I stared at her.  
  
I wanted to tell her about the telephone call, the voices, the hands touching me, and my mother screaming in my ear. But as I looked around that perfectly ordinary hospital room, I suddenly became aware of how ridiculous it would all sound.  
  
So I just shook my head.  
  
She didn't look convinced.  
  
"Can you stand up?"  
  
I nodded, although I wasn't entirely sure I could. The nurse approached me cautiously and offered her hand. I took it and managed to pull myself to my feet, using the wall for support. My legs felt weak, and the headache was still there, although the worst of the pain seemed to have passed.  
  
"What happened?" she asked.  
  
"I got locked in."  
  
"Locked in?"  
  
"Yes. I came in here because someone called the security office. A patient. He said he needed help."  
  
She frowned.  
  
"From this room?"  
  
"Yes."  
  
She stared at me for a moment before looking toward the bedside table.  
  
"There's nobody assigned to this room."  
  
"I know. That's why I came to check."  
  
She walked over to the telephone and picked up the receiver. She listened for a moment, then put it back down.  
  
"This phone isn't connected."  
  
I didn't answer.  
  
She looked at me again, and I could see that her expression had changed. She wasn't irritated anymore. If anything, she looked concerned.  
  
"Have you taken anything tonight?"  
  
"What?"  
  
"Medication. Anything?"  
  
I almost laughed.  
  
"No. Actually, I've been looking for my migraine medication all evening. I couldn't find it."  
  
She glanced at my face.  
  
"You're very pale."  
  
"I get migraines. Sometimes they're pretty bad."  
  
"Well, you shouldn't be working in this condition."  
  
I wanted to argue, but I was too exhausted. Besides, what was I supposed to tell her? That I'd heard my dead mother screaming through a disconnected telephone? That I'd been attacked by people who apparently didn't exist?  
  
She helped me back to the security office and brought me a glass of water. I sat there for a while, trying to calm down, while she made a few calls. Eventually, she told me that someone would take over my shift and that I should go home.  
  
I didn't protest.  
  
Before leaving, I asked her one last question.  
  
"Did you hear anything when you came to get me?"  
  
She looked confused.  
  
"What do you mean?"  
  
"From the room. Before you opened the door."  
  
She hesitated.  
  
"I heard you screaming."  
  
"Nothing else?"  
  
"No."  
  
I nodded.  
  
She stood there for another moment, looking at me in a way that made me feel terribly embarrassed.  
  
"Get some rest," she said.  
  
I left the hospital shortly afterward.  
  
I remember the feeling of stepping outside into the cold morning air. The sky was beginning to lighten, and there were already cars passing along the road. I stood there for a few minutes, breathing deeply, watching people going about their mornings as though nothing had happened.  
  
I don't think I've ever been so relieved to see ordinary people in my life.  
  
On the way home, I kept going over everything that had happened. The shadows in the corridors, the telephone call, the voices, my mother. I knew migraines could cause visual disturbances, and I'd heard that severe ones could sometimes produce strange sensory symptoms. I kept telling myself that I'd been exhausted, frightened, and in terrible pain. Perhaps my mind had simply filled in the gaps.  
  
By the time I reached my apartment, I'd almost convinced myself.  
  
I went straight to bed and slept for nearly fourteen hours.  
  
When I woke up, my headache was gone. I felt weak, but otherwise normal. I took a shower, made something to eat, and tried not to think about the hospital.  
  
It was while getting dressed that I noticed the bruise.  
  
There was a dark bruise around my ankle, exactly where I'd felt something grab me in that room.  
  
I sat on the edge of my bed and stared at it for a long time.  
  
I tried to remember whether I'd knocked my leg against anything during the night. Maybe I'd hurt myself while panicking. Maybe I'd caught my ankle against the bed frame without noticing.  
  
It was possible.  
  
That's what I kept telling myself.  
  
I never went back to the hospital. I called the agency the following day and told them I wouldn't be returning. They weren't particularly happy about it, but I didn't care. I didn't even ask to be paid for the hours I'd worked.  
  
That was several weeks ago.  
  
The bruise has faded, and my migraines have returned to their usual frequency. I've gone back to my regular job, and from the outside, my life probably looks exactly the same.  
  
But I haven't been sleeping well.  
  
Sometimes, just as I'm about to fall asleep, I hear people whispering. It's never anything clear enough to understand, and the moment I sit up and turn on the light, it stops. I know that hearing things while falling asleep isn't necessarily unusual. I've looked it up more times than I care to admit.  
  
Ever since that night, I haven't been able to stop thinking about my mother. About everything that happened before she died, the decisions I made, and the things I wish I'd done differently. I keep going over it all in my head, wondering whether I really did the right thing.  
  
I just hope that, wherever she is, she knows I did what I thought was best.  
  
And I hope she forgives me.  
  
But what I know is that I have to forgive myself.  
  
I have to forgive myself.  
  
I have to forgive myself.
