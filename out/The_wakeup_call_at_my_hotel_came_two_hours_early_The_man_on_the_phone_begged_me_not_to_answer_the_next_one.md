The first wake-up call came at 3:17 in the morning.

The man on the other end was crying, and he knew I had asked to be woken at 5:30.

“Listen to me,” he whispered. “When the phone rings again, do not answer it.”

I sat up and reached for the lamp. For a few seconds, I had no idea where I was. The room was dark except for the green digits on the alarm clock and a narrow strip of orange light beneath the door.

“Who is this?”

“You’re in room 505.”

It was not a question.

I switched on the lamp. The hotel room came back around me. Beige walls. Brown carpet. Suitcase on the luggage rack. My shirt hanging from the back of the desk chair.

The room phone was warm against my ear.

“How do you know what room I’m in?”

The man breathed unsteadily. Somewhere behind him, I could hear a phone ringing.

It sounded exactly like mine.

“You asked for a wake-up call at 5:30,” he said. “You have an early flight. You ordered the taxi for six. You put your passport inside the zipped pocket of your laptop bag because you’re worried you’ll leave it on the desk.”

I looked at the laptop bag beside the television.

Nobody had seen me put the passport there.

I had done it twenty minutes before going to sleep.

“Who are you?”

“My name is Owen. Owen Kessler. I’m staying in this room tomorrow night.”

I said nothing.

“I know how that sounds,” he continued. “I know you think this is a prank. I thought the same thing when someone called me. I didn’t listen.”

The phone ringing behind him stopped.

Owen started crying harder.

“They know I’m awake now.”

Then the line went dead.

I remained sitting on the edge of the bed, holding the receiver to my ear.

The dial tone returned.

I looked at the clock.

3:19 a.m.

I had checked into the hotel less than six hours earlier.

It was called the Parkland Airport Hotel, though there was no parkland anywhere near it. It stood between an eight-storey car park and a dual carriageway, ten minutes from the airport if traffic was good.

I was flying to Denver for a conference. My company had booked the cheapest room within shuttle distance of the terminal. The photographs online had made the hotel look brighter than it was.

The lobby had smelled of lemon cleaner and old cooking oil. Half the ceiling lights were off, and the restaurant chairs had been stacked on the tables even though it was only nine.

The woman at reception was in her late twenties, with blonde hair tied into a tight bun. Her name badge said CLAIRE.

“Checking in?” she had asked.

I gave her my name.

She slid a form across the desk. “Adam Cooper. One night. Room 505.”

“That’s me.”

“Will you need a shuttle in the morning?”

“I booked a taxi for six.”

“Would you like a wake-up call?”

I almost said no. I had three alarms set on my phone, but I had missed an early flight the previous year and still heard about it whenever expenses were discussed.

“Five thirty, please.”

Claire typed something into the computer.

“Five thirty,” she repeated. “Make sure you answer.”

I thought she meant so I would not fall back asleep.

Before giving me the key, she asked to see my identification. I handed over my driving licence. She looked at the photograph, then at me.

For a moment, she seemed confused.

“Something wrong?”

“No.” She passed it back. “You just look different.”

“Bad picture.”

“It usually is.”

Her smile appeared a second too late.

I had forgotten the exchange by the time I reached the fifth floor.

Now, sitting beside the bed with the receiver in my hand, I remembered every word.

Make sure you answer.

I placed the phone back in its cradle and picked up my mobile.

I had full signal.

The first thing I did was search Owen Kessler’s name. There were dozens of results, but none immediately stood out. A dentist in Ohio. A high school wrestler. Several social media accounts set to private.

I searched the number that had appeared on the hotel phone.

The display had only shown ROOM 505.

I called reception.

The phone rang eight times before someone answered.

“Front desk.”

It was a man’s voice.

“Hi. I’m in room 505.”

There was a pause.

I heard typing.

“Can I have your name?”

“Adam Cooper.”

More typing.

“What room did you say?”

“Five-oh-five.”

Another pause.

“Sir, there’s nobody checked into 505.”

I looked around the room.

My laptop was on the desk. My shoes were beside the bed. The television welcome screen displayed GOOD EVENING, ADAM.

“I’m standing in it.”

The man gave a quiet laugh, though nothing about it sounded amused.

“Room 505 is out of service.”

“I checked in tonight. Claire gave me the key.”

“Claire?”

“The woman at reception.”

“There’s no Claire working tonight.”

“She checked me in around nine.”

“I’ve been here since seven.”

I moved the receiver away from my mouth and stared at it.

“Is this some kind of joke?”

“No, sir.”

“You have my wake-up call scheduled for 5:30.”

The typing stopped.

When the man spoke again, his voice was quieter.

“Did somebody call you?”

I tightened my grip on the phone.

“What?”

“Before you called me. Did the room phone ring?”

I did not answer.

“Sir?”

“Who is this?”

The line clicked.

A new voice spoke through the receiver.

“Adam?”

It was Claire.

I stood up so quickly that my knee struck the bedside table.

“Is everything all right?” she asked.

“You checked me in.”

“Yes.”

“There was a man on the phone who said this room was empty.”

“You must have reached the night office.”

“He said there was nobody called Claire working here.”

She laughed lightly. “He’s new. He gets confused.”

“He knew somebody had called me.”

The laughter stopped.

“What did they say?”

I walked to the door and checked the deadbolt. It was still turned. The security chain hung beneath it, unused. I slid it into place.

“Nothing,” I said.

“Adam, what did the caller say?”

Her voice had changed.

She no longer sounded tired or polite. She sounded alert.

Interested.

I ended the call.

The room went completely silent.

Then someone knocked on the door.

Three firm knocks.

I stepped backwards.

“Mr Cooper?” Claire called from the corridor. “It’s reception.”

I looked through the peephole.

The hallway was empty.

The patterned carpet stretched towards the elevators in one direction and the fire door in the other. The ceiling lights buzzed softly. Every door was closed.

“Mr Cooper?”

Her voice came from directly outside.

There was nobody there.

I kept my eye pressed to the glass.

“I just want to make sure you’re all right,” she said. “You sounded distressed on the phone.”

I took my mobile from the bed and called reception again.

The ringing came through the receiver and from the other side of the door.

Claire answered both at once.

“Front desk.”

I watched the empty hallway.

“Where are you?”

“At the desk.”

Her voice sounded identical through my phone and through the door.

I whispered, “Who’s outside my room?”

The handle moved.

Slowly down.

Then back up.

“Mr Cooper,” Claire said, “please open the door.”

I ended the call and backed away.

The handle moved again. Whoever was outside pressed harder this time, rattling the door in its frame.

The electronic lock beeped.

A green light appeared beneath the handle.

The deadbolt held.

I grabbed the desk chair and wedged it beneath the handle. My hands were shaking badly enough that one of the chair legs knocked against the door.

The person in the corridor stopped trying the lock.

For almost a minute, there was no sound.

Then Claire whispered through the door.

“You were asked to answer.”

Footsteps moved away.

I waited until I heard the elevator arrive.

The doors opened with a soft chime. After several seconds, they closed again.

The display above the elevator remained on five.

I checked the time.

3:34.

The room phone rang.

I almost ripped the cord from the wall.

Then I remembered what Owen had said.

The call he had warned me about was supposed to come at 5:30. This was too early.

The phone rang six times before I answered.

“Owen?”

For several seconds, there was only heavy breathing.

Then he said, “You called reception.”

“How are you doing this?”

“You called them and gave them your name.”

“They already knew my name.”

“You confirmed it.”

“What difference does that make?”

Behind Owen, something struck a door. The impact was loud enough to distort the call.

He made a small frightened sound.

“Listen,” he said. “I don’t have long. The man who called me said the room connects to the previous guest at 3:17. He told me not to answer the wake-up call. I thought he was trying to scare me.”

“Who was he?”

“I don’t know. He wouldn’t tell me.”

Another impact shook Owen’s door.

“Where are you right now?”

“Room 505.”

“What date is it?”

He told me.

Friday, October 16.

I looked at my phone.

Thursday, October 15.

My mouth went dry.

“That’s tomorrow.”

“I know.”

“This is impossible.”

“I know.”

Something scraped slowly down his door.

Owen lowered his voice.

“The call came at 5:30. A woman said good morning and used my full name. I answered before I remembered the warning. Then somebody knocked.”

“Claire?”

“I don’t know her name. She said she worked at the front desk. When I looked through the peephole, the corridor was empty.”

I stared at my door.

The chair remained wedged beneath the handle.

“What happened?”

“I called reception. They said they hadn’t sent anyone. Then I saw myself on the television.”

I turned towards the screen.

It still displayed the hotel welcome page.

“What do you mean?”

“There’s a camera in the room. I don’t know where. The television switched on and showed me sleeping in the bed. I was standing right in front of it, but I could see myself under the covers.”

A muffled voice called Owen’s name from his side of the connection.

He stopped speaking.

The voice came again.

This time, I recognised it.

It was Owen.

The person outside his door sounded exactly like the man on the phone.

“Please,” Owen whispered. “Please go away.”

His own voice replied from the corridor.

“You asked to be woken.”

I could hear the handle moving.

“What do I do?” I asked.

“Don’t answer the call. Don’t open the door. Don’t look at the television.”

The line crackled.

“Owen?”

“If you see yourself, it can see you.”

His door burst open.

There was a rush of movement, a strangled cry, then the receiver struck something hard.

For a few seconds, the call remained connected.

I heard a man breathing.

Then Owen’s voice spoke calmly.

“Sorry about that.”

The line went dead.

I pulled the cable from the back of the hotel phone.

The display went dark.

I checked every part of the room.

There were no obvious cameras. I examined the smoke detector, the television, the alarm clock and the vents. I used my phone’s camera to search for infrared lights. Nothing.

I opened the wardrobe and checked behind the clothes rail. I looked under the bed.

There was a shoe beneath it.

A black leather dress shoe, lying on its side near the wall.

It was identical to the pair I had placed beside the bed.

I looked behind me.

Both of my shoes were still there.

I crouched and reached underneath.

My fingers touched cold leather.

The shoe was attached to a foot.

I stumbled backwards and hit the wall.

For a moment, I could not breathe.

Then I forced myself to look again.

There was nobody beneath the bed.

Only the shoe.

I hooked it with the luggage rack and dragged it into the light.

Something had been pushed inside.

A driving licence.

The photograph showed my face.

The name beneath it was LUCAS BENNETT.

The date of birth was mine. The address belonged to a town I had never visited.

The licence looked real. The edges were worn, and there was a pale scratch across the photograph.

On the back, someone had written in blue ink.

YOU ANSWERED LAST TIME.

I dropped it.

My own driving licence was still inside my wallet. I took it out and placed the two cards next to each other.

Same photograph.

Same face.

Different names.

I called the police.

The dispatcher asked for the hotel’s address, my room number and a description of what had happened. I left out the part about tomorrow’s guest. I told her someone was impersonating hotel staff and trying to enter my room.

“Officers are on their way,” she said. “Stay inside and keep the door secured.”

“How long?”

“Approximately ten minutes.”

I checked the time.

4:02.

I had ninety minutes before the wake-up call.

I told myself the police would arrive and this would become explainable. A criminal scam. Hidden cameras. Stolen identification. Somebody inside the hotel targeting lone travellers.

That explanation lasted four minutes.

Then the dispatcher called back.

“Mr Bennett?”

I almost answered automatically.

“My name is Adam Cooper.”

There was a pause.

“I apologise. Mr Cooper, officers are in the hotel lobby. Staff are saying there is no fifth floor.”

I looked towards the door.

“What?”

“They’ve checked the elevators and stairwell. The building has four guest floors.”

“I came up in the elevator.”

“What room are you in?”

“505.”

She spoke to someone away from the phone.

I heard a man say, “There’s no five.”

The dispatcher returned.

“Can you leave the room and go to the stairwell?”

“No.”

“Are you physically able to?”

“There’s someone outside.”

The television switched on.

The dispatcher continued talking, but I stopped hearing her.

The screen showed my hotel room.

The image came from a high corner near the ceiling, angled towards the bed.

I was asleep beneath the covers.

The lamp was off. The room was dark.

Someone stood beside the bed.

He had his back to the camera. He wore dark trousers and a white shirt.

The same clothes I had laid out for the morning.

“Mr Cooper?” the dispatcher said.

I watched the man on the television.

He leaned over the sleeping version of me and placed one hand on his shoulder.

The man in the bed opened his eyes.

Both of them turned towards the camera.

They had my face.

I switched off the television.

The screen went black for half a second.

Then the image returned.

The room on the screen was empty now.

The bed was unmade.

The door stood open.

Someone moved past the camera, too close for me to see anything except a white shirt and the side of a face.

I pulled the television plug from the wall.

The picture stayed on.

“Can you see the television?” I asked the dispatcher.

“What television?”

“I’m going to video call you.”

I opened the camera on my phone and switched to video. The dispatcher sent a link by text. When the call connected, I pointed my phone at the screen.

“What do you see?”

Silence.

“Can you see it?”

“I can see the television,” she said carefully.

“What’s on it?”

“A hotel information page.”

I looked at the screen.

My room was still there.

The door in the recording slowly swung shut.

Then someone stepped into view.

Me.

He walked towards the camera until his face filled the television.

He raised one finger to his lips.

The screen went black.

The room phone rang.

It was still unplugged.

The dark display lit up.

5:30 WAKE-UP CALL.

I looked at the clock beside the bed.

4:11.

The dispatcher said my name.

The phone continued ringing.

I took the receiver from its cradle and placed it face down on the desk without answering. The ringing came from the speaker anyway.

“Do you hear that?” I asked.

“Hear what?”

The ringtone grew louder.

It was no longer coming only from the phone.

It came from the walls. From the bathroom. From beneath the bed.

Everywhere at once.

Then it stopped.

The clock changed.

4:12 became 5:29.

My mobile showed the same time.

The dispatcher’s voice cut out.

A message appeared on the screen.

CALL ENDED.

The room lights dimmed.

The green digits on the clock changed to 5:30.

The hotel phone rang once.

Twice.

Three times.

I sat on the floor between the bed and the wall with both hands clamped over my mouth.

At the sixth ring, someone knocked.

“Mr Cooper?” Claire called. “This is your wake-up call.”

The phone kept ringing.

“Mr Cooper, please answer the telephone.”

I did not move.

The knock came again.

Then my own voice spoke from the hallway.

“Adam, open the door.”

My skin tightened across my entire body.

“I know you’re in there,” it said. “I know you can hear me.”

The handle pressed down.

The chair creaked but held.

“Look through the peephole.”

I stared at the door.

“Go on,” my voice said. “You need to see this.”

I remembered Owen’s warning.

If you see yourself, it can see you.

I lowered my head.

Something scratched the other side of the door. Slow, deliberate lines, one beneath another.

“Your taxi is downstairs.”

The hotel phone rang again.

“You’ll miss your flight.”

I closed my eyes.

“You’ll lose your job.”

Another knock.

“Leah will be worried.”

My eyes opened.

Leah was my wife.

Her name had not appeared on the booking. I had not spoken to her since entering the hotel.

The voice outside softened.

“Come on, Adam. She’s waiting for you.”

My mobile rang.

LEAH CALLING.

I watched it vibrate across the carpet.

I did not answer.

A voicemail notification appeared immediately.

Then another.

Then six more.

The person outside began humming.

I recognised the tune. Leah used to sing it to our daughter when she was a baby.

My phone displayed a text from her.

Why won’t you open the door?

I switched it off.

The scratching stopped.

Silence settled over the room.

The clock still showed 5:30.

I do not know how long I remained on the floor.

It might have been ten minutes. It might have been an hour.

Eventually, pale light appeared around the curtains.

The clock changed to 6:01.

The chair beneath the handle fell over.

Nobody had touched it.

I waited another five minutes before approaching the door. I listened with my ear against the wood.

Nothing.

I removed the chain and turned the deadbolt.

The hallway was empty.

Daylight shone through a window beside the elevators. The carpet looked different in the morning, faded and stained rather than dark.

I took the stairs.

There were five flights.

When I reached the lobby, several guests were checking out. People drank coffee from paper cups. A family argued beside a pile of suitcases. A shuttle driver stood near the entrance holding a sign.

Everything looked ordinary.

Claire was behind the desk.

She smiled when she saw me.

“Good morning.”

I walked towards her.

A man stood at the counter with his back to me.

Dark trousers. White shirt.

My suitcase beside his feet.

Claire handed him a receipt.

“Thank you, Mr Cooper. I hope the wake-up call was helpful.”

The man turned.

He had my face.

He looked rested. His hair was combed. He wore the watch Leah had given me for our anniversary.

He looked at me for less than a second.

There was no surprise in his expression.

He leaned towards Claire.

“That’s him,” he said quietly.

Claire’s eyes moved to me.

“Sir, can I help you?”

“That’s my suitcase.”

The man smiled apologetically.

“He was wandering around the fifth floor last night.”

Claire pressed a button beneath the desk.

“There is no fifth floor,” she said.

Two security guards approached from the lobby entrance.

I reached for my wallet and pulled out my driving licence.

“This is me. My name is Adam Cooper.”

The photograph showed my face.

The name beneath it said LUCAS BENNETT.

I dropped it on the counter.

The other man picked it up.

“You should be more careful with your identification,” he said.

His voice was mine, but there was something wrong with the rhythm. He paused before certain words, as though choosing them from a list.

One of the guards took my arm.

I pulled away and ran.

Nobody followed me outside.

The taxi I had booked was waiting at the kerb. The driver looked at me, then at the hotel entrance.

“You Adam?”

I stopped.

Before I could answer, the other version of me walked through the automatic doors.

The driver waved to him.

My replacement placed my suitcase in the boot and climbed into the back seat. Through the window, he looked directly at me.

Then he smiled.

The taxi drove away.

I walked for nearly an hour before finding a petrol station. I bought a charger and turned on my phone.

There were no missed calls from Leah.

Her number was no longer in my contacts.

I dialled it from memory.

A man answered.

“Hello?”

It was me.

I hung up.

I have spent the last three days trying to prove who I am.

My email password has been changed. My bank says my security answers are incorrect. My employer told me Adam Cooper attended the conference in Denver and gave his presentation yesterday morning.

Leah answered once more.

She listened while I tried to explain. I could hear our daughter in the background.

Then Leah said, “I don’t know who you are, but please stop calling.”

I went to the police. The licence identifying me as Lucas Bennett is genuine. According to their system, Lucas Bennett has existed for thirty-eight years. He has tax records, employment history and an apartment outside Columbus.

I went there.

The key from room 505 opened the front door.

Inside, I found clothes in my size. Photographs of people I do not know. Birthday cards addressed to Lucas.

There was a suitcase on the bed.

Inside were dozens of hotel key sleeves.

Different hotels. Different cities. Different dates.

Every room number ended in 05.

On the back of each sleeve, someone had written a name.

Most had been crossed out.

Adam Cooperwas the newest.

Last night, the telephone in the apartment rang at 3:17.

I answered because it was not a hotel phone. Because I was exhausted. Because I still had some part of me that believed ordinary rules applied outside that room.

A frightened woman was on the other end.

She said she had just checked into an airport hotel.

Room 505.

She said the hotel had offered her a wake-up call for 5:30.

I told her not to answer it.

She asked who I was.

Before I could respond, somebody knocked on her door.

A woman’s voice said, “Reception.”

The caller looked through the peephole.

I heard her breathing change.

“There’s nobody there,” she whispered.

Then the line went dead.

The phone rang again this morning.

The display showed my old mobile number.

I did not answer.

A voicemail appeared anyway.

It was Leah’s voice.

“Good morning, Lucas,” she said. “This is your requested wake-up call.”

There was a pause.

Then my daughter spoke.

“Daddy says it’s time to get up.”

Someone is standing outside the apartment now.

He has been there for twenty minutes.

I cannot see him through the peephole, but I can hear him breathing.

Every few minutes, he knocks three times.

Then he says my name.

Not Adam.

Lucas.
