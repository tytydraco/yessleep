Every year, one person in my apartment building disappears.

I used to think they just moved out quietly.

That’s what everyone said.

Rent got too high. Family emergency. Better opportunity. Fresh start.

It was always something clean, something boring, something that made you feel stupid for asking more than one question.

But I’ve lived in Marrow House for almost six years now, and after a while, patterns start to itch.

People didn’t just leave this building.  
They vanished in the same way.

Always at night.  
Always near the end of November.  
Always after the boiler started making that sound.

Not the usual banging-pipe noise old buildings make.  
I mean a deep, wet knocking from somewhere below the floorboards, like something with fists the size of cinder blocks was asking to be let out.

The first person I remember was Mrs. Ortega in 2B. She was old, mean as broken glass, and fed every stray cat on the block. One day her door was covered in family photos, grocery coupons, and those little church candles with saints on them. The next morning, the door was open, the apartment was empty, and Mr. Barlow from 1A was carrying her mattress out to the curb like he’d been waiting for the chance.

Nobody said a word.

A year later it was Devin from 4C, the nursing student with the lazy eye and the drum set. He went downstairs to complain about the heat and never came back up. Super said he broke lease. His mother came by three days later screaming in the hallway because his phone, wallet, and keys were still on the kitchen counter.

Last year it was Nadia in 3F.  
She told me two nights before she disappeared that she kept hearing someone whispering through her radiator.

Not voices exactly.  
Just one voice.  
A raspy one.  
Asking the same question every night.

\*\*Who should go next?\*\*

I told her to call the police if she was that scared.  
She laughed and said, “And tell them what? My heater’s making threats?”

Then she grabbed my wrist hard enough to hurt and said something I didn’t understand until yesterday.

“Don’t ever go to the basement if they ask nicely.”

Yesterday, I found the note taped to the inside of my apartment door.

No envelope. No signature.  
Just a rectangle of yellowed paper that looked older than the building itself.

\*\*TENANT MEETING\*\*  
\*\*BASEMENT - 11:45 P.M.\*\*  
\*\*MANDATORY ATTENDANCE\*\*

And beneath that, in a different handwriting—jagged, shaky, pressed so hard the letters tore the paper:

\*\*YOU ARE ON THE BALLOT.\*\*

I laughed when I read it.  
Not because it was funny, but because sometimes your body does the wrong thing when it gets scared.

I took the note to the super, Mr. Klein, who looked at it for maybe half a second before handing it back.

“Co-op business,” he said.

“This isn’t a co-op.”

He shrugged. “Building business, then.”

“What ballot?”

He stared at me a little too long. “You should dress warm.”

Then he closed the office door in my face.

I spent the rest of the evening trying to convince myself it was a prank.  
A sick one, sure, but still just a prank.

By eleven-thirty, I had every light in my apartment on and a kitchen knife in my hoodie pocket.  
The boiler started knocking at exactly eleven-forty.

Three slow thuds.  
A pause.  
Then three more.

The pipes in my walls shivered.  
Something hot and sour blew through the radiator vents, carrying a smell like pennies, mildew, and burned hair.

Then someone knocked on my front door.

I looked through the peephole.  
Every tenant on my floor was standing in the hallway.

Not talking.  
Not moving.  
Just waiting.

Mr. Barlow in his yellow cardigan.  
The twins from 5A with their matching migraines and matching silver crosses.  
Mrs. Lang from 3C clutching her oxygen tank.  
Even Mr. Klein, standing at the back with his hands folded like a funeral director.

“Meeting time,” he said.

I should have run.  
Maybe I could have made it to the fire escape.  
Maybe I could have called somebody.  
Maybe I could have done a hundred things smarter than what I did.

Instead, I opened the door.

Because when twenty people are staring at you like they’re trying not to look guilty, your brain starts begging for explanations.  
Even bad ones.

They walked me downstairs in silence.  
Past the laundry room.  
Past the storage cages.  
Past the old door marked BOILER ACCESS that I’d seen a thousand times and never once noticed had scratch marks all around the frame.

The basement beyond it was bigger than it should have been.  
That’s the first thing that made my stomach drop.

Our building sits on a normal city block. I’ve seen the footprint. I know how space works.  
But past that door, the basement stretched out like a church under the earth—low concrete ceilings, rows of rusted folding chairs, naked bulbs swinging on long cords, and a boiler at the far end so huge it looked less like machinery and more like an iron building growing inside another building.

Its door was round, black, and ribbed like a pupil.

And it was breathing.

Not metaphorically.  
Not in a poetic, sleep-deprived way.  
I mean the metal door swelled outward and sank back in with slow, damp inhalations.  
Every exhale came with that knocking sound, like something inside was shifting its weight.

I stopped walking.  
“No.”

Nobody answered.

The tenants took their seats.  
Mr. Klein stood beside a table with a wooden ballot box on top of it. The box was dark with age and stained black around the slot.

“Welcome,” he said.  
His voice echoed strangely, as if the walls were listening too. “As required by the leasehold covenant of Marrow House, all adult tenants must attend the annual vote. One unit is selected for Offering. One resident is chosen by majority rule. In exchange, the building remains standing, fertile, and protected for another year.”

I actually laughed again.  
A short, cracked sound.

“You people are insane.”

No one disagreed.

Mrs. Lang lifted her oxygen mask and whispered, “We were insane the first year. After that, we were only practical.”

Mr. Klein opened a ledger on the table.  
The pages were thick and warped, each line filled with names written in different inks, different hands, different decades.  
Some of the entries had apartment numbers next to them.  
Some just said things like \*\*widower\*\*, \*\*new mother\*\*, \*\*boarder\*\*, \*\*child in hall closet\*\*.

My mouth went dry.

“This building was raised in 1907,” Klein said, “over a burn pit used by the slaughterhouses before the city rezoned the district. The men who built Marrow House discovered something in the ash below the foundation. Something hungry. Something useful.”

The boiler let out a long metallic groan.

“In winter of 1908, the first collapse was prevented after an Offering. In 1931, the fire stopped at the third floor. In 1967, the floodwater receded from this block alone. In 1989, the gas main ruptured under three neighboring buildings. They fell. We did not.”

He placed one hand on the ballot box.

“We endure because it eats elsewhere.”

I backed toward the stairs.  
Two of the tenants stood before I’d taken a second step.  
Not threatening.  
Just blocking.

“You can’t do this,” I said.

Mr. Barlow looked embarrassed, which somehow made it worse.  
“Everyone says that.”

Klein cleared his throat. “This year’s nominees are selected by need, debt, solitude, and structural intuition.”

“What the hell does that mean?”

“It means the building knows who will be missed least.”

Then he started reading names.

Mine first.

Then Mrs. Lang.  
Then one of the twins from 5A.  
Then a guy from 2D I’d only spoken to once in the mailroom.

Four names.  
Four slips of paper.  
Twenty-two tenants.  
A simple majority decides who goes into the boiler room.

I wish I could tell you I fought harder.  
I wish I overturned chairs and smashed bulbs and slit Klein’s throat with the knife in my pocket.

What I actually did was beg.

I begged like an animal.  
I told them I had a sister in Ohio. I told them I paid my rent early. I told them I fixed Mrs. Lang’s groceries when the elevator broke. I told them Nadia had warned me, that Devin’s mother still called his phone, that whatever they thought they were feeding down there would never stop.

That got a few of them looking at the floor.  
But not enough.

Then the boiler spoke.

The whole room vibrated before the voice came, deep and raw and wet with heat.

“COUNT.”

Several tenants flinched.  
Like they hated hearing it directly.

Klein nodded and passed around the slips.  
No booths. No privacy. Just paper squares and shaking hands.

I watched my neighbors write.  
People I’d lent batteries to.  
People whose packages I brought upstairs.  
People whose children I’d smiled at in the lobby.

One by one, they folded my death into neat little squares.

When Klein tallied the slips, he did it calmly.  
Like reading raffle results.

“Eighteen votes for Daniel Voss. Three for Evelyn Lang. One abstention.”

Eighteen.

For a second, nobody moved.  
Then the boiler door unlocked.

I heard the bolts slide back from the inside.

The round black hatch eased open an inch.  
A ribbon of heat spilled out, carrying a smell so foul and sweet my eyes watered instantly. Rot, wet rust, sewage, cooked meat, and something medicinal underneath, like old bandages sealed in steam.

And from the darkness beyond the hatch, something shifted.

I only saw parts.  
That was enough.

A knuckle the size of a brick dragging over iron.  
A cluster of pale shapes packed together like teeth, except they opened and closed sideways.  
Something slick unfurling across the threshold and then retreating when the light hit it.

The room made a sound then—every tenant inhaling at once.  
Not fear.  
Recognition.

This had happened before.  
Many times.

Klein looked at me. “Please proceed.”

I pulled the knife from my hoodie and rushed him.

I actually got close enough to cut him. The blade opened his cheek from the corner of his mouth to his ear. He screamed and stumbled back into the ballot table, knocking the ledger and box onto the floor.

The room exploded.  
People shouting, chairs scraping, Mrs. Lang coughing, someone grabbing my arm.  
I slashed backward and felt the knife sink into soft flesh.  
A man howled.

And then the thing in the boiler room moved fast.

Faster than anything that big should move.

A black, jointed limb shot through the doorway and wrapped around the nearest tenant—the guy from 2D—and folded him in half before anyone could even process what they were seeing. There was a crack like a snapped table leg, then another, wetter one.

Blood hit the concrete in a fan.

The twins started screaming.  
The thing lunged again.  
This time it hooked Mr. Barlow around the waist and dragged him across the floor so hard his cardigan rolled up over his face. His nails left ten red grooves in the concrete before he vanished into the hatch.

The sounds from inside were unbearable.  
Chewing isn’t the right word.  
Neither is tearing.  
It sounded like someone trying to breathe through a mouth full of drowning people.

The tenants scattered.  
Some ran for the stairs. Some dropped to their knees, sobbing apologies toward the boiler like prayer could reset the rules.  
Mr. Klein was on the floor clutching his face, blood spilling between his fingers.

That’s when I saw the ledger lying open.

Every Offering had a mark beside it.  
A name, a unit, a year.  
And sometimes a note.

\*\*refused to vote\*\*  
\*\*attempted escape\*\*  
\*\*pregnant\*\*  
\*\*outsider\*\*

Next to Nadia’s name was a sentence written in fresh ink.

\*\*warned the next one\*\*

Under that, on the final page, was tonight’s entry.  
My name was already there.  
Not in the nominee list.  
In the Offering line.

Beneath it, in a handwriting that looked burned into the page rather than written:

\*\*If the chosen one is not given freely, the building will take a floor.\*\*

Then the lights went out.

Complete dark.

The boiler began laughing.

I know how stupid that sounds. Boilers do not laugh. Metal does not find you funny.  
But in that darkness, with people shoving and falling and sobbing around me, I heard a low, bubbling rhythm rise from the chamber—pleased, patient, amused.

Then came the first sound from upstairs.

A deep crack.  
Not from the basement.  
From above us.

Then another.

Concrete screaming.  
Pipes bursting.  
The whole building groaning like a ship torn open underwater.

Everyone froze.

The voice came again, louder this time, from every vent and pipe in the room.

“PAY.”

Something collapsed overhead.  
Dust blasted down the stairwell.  
A woman started shrieking that her son was asleep on the sixth floor.  
One of the twins ran for the stairs and was crushed instantly when a section of ceiling sheared off and dropped like a guillotine.

Panic finally broke whatever ritual politeness had been keeping these people civilized.  
They turned on each other.

Not on me.  
On each other.

Mrs. Lang pointed at the guy from 2D’s blood smear and yelled that two bodies should count for this year. Someone else shouted that abstention voided the ballot. Mr. Klein, half-blind and bleeding, kept saying, “It has to be voluntary. It prefers voluntary.”

That line cut through the room.

Voluntary.

They all looked at me.  
Then at the stairs collapsing one slab at a time.  
Then back at me.

I understood what Nadia had meant.  
Don’t go to the basement if they ask nicely.  
Because nice means they still need your feet to carry you there.

After that, they stop asking.

I ran.  
Not to the stairs. To the boiler.

I know that makes no sense, but neither did anything else. The hatch was still open. Heat poured out in waves thick enough to blur the edges of the room. Behind me I heard people shouting my name, not with concern but urgency—the way you’d shout after a dog slipping its leash near traffic.

I reached the threshold and looked inside.

There are things I could describe better if my hands would stop shaking when I think about them.  
But the closest I can get is this:

The inside of the boiler room was not a room.  
It was a throat.

Brick walls coated in black mucus pulsed inward and outward around a shaft that went down farther than light could reach. Iron ladders had been bolted into living tissue. Old bones were embedded in the walls like seeds in fruit. Steam rose from below carrying scraps of voices—pleading, bargaining, praying—some fresh, some ancient.

And clinging to the inner curve of that impossible space was the thing itself.

Not one creature.  
A colony.  
A knot of limbs, mouths, and human faces pressed halfway out of a tar-black mass, all fused together like they were still trying to crawl free of each other. Eyes opened in places eyes should never be. Teeth moved independently beneath skin. It had hands growing from it the way mold grows from bread.

And several of those faces were still alive.

Mrs. Ortega was one of them.

Her mouth opened when she saw me.  
At first I thought she was trying to scream.  
Then I realized she was whispering.

“Choose,” she mouthed.

The tenants were almost on me when I saw the chain.  
A thick iron one disappearing down into the creature’s body, anchored to an old valve wheel mounted beside the hatch.  
On the wheel someone had scratched a single word:

\*\*FEED\*\*

And beneath it, much older, nearly worn away:

\*\*STARVE\*\*

I don’t know why no one ever tried it before.  
Maybe they had.  
Maybe they failed.  
Maybe fear makes people obedient long before it makes them brave.

I grabbed the wheel and turned.

The chain tightened.

The thing screamed.

Every face in its body screamed with it.  
The sound punched the air out of my lungs and burst every bulb in the basement. Sparks rained from the ceiling. Behind me the tenants fell back, covering their ears.

I kept turning.

The wheel fought me like I was cranking against a car wrapped in concrete. My palms split. Something hot ran over my hands. The chain drew tighter and tighter, pulling the creature downward into the shaft inch by shrieking inch.

It thrashed. Limbs slammed the hatch frame. Teeth shattered against iron. One of the fused faces bit through another face trying to claw upward. Steam blasted around me so hot I thought my eyes would melt.

But the building stopped shaking.

That’s what made the tenants rush me.  
Not because they wanted to save me.  
Because they realized it was working.  
And if the thing could be starved, then every year they’d done this for nothing.

Mr. Klein reached me first.  
He hit me in the back of the head with something heavy. I dropped to one knee but didn’t let go. He grabbed my shoulders and tried to wrench me off the wheel.

“You idiot,” he screamed. “Do you know what happens when it wakes hungry?”

I looked at his torn face, at the blood in his teeth, at the terror behind his anger.

And I understood.

This wasn’t a ritual anymore.  
It was addiction.  
The building had trained them.  
Fed them safety in exchange for obedience until they couldn’t imagine surviving any other way.

So I turned the wheel harder.

Klein slipped on blood. I elbowed him in the throat and he crashed backward. The twins grabbed my arms next, then Mrs. Lang, then two more tenants piling on, clawing, sobbing, begging me to stop.

The thing in the shaft made a sound like a hundred people inhaling through broken teeth.  
Then, suddenly, the wheel gave.

One final turn.  
A loud internal clank.  
And the chain locked.

The hatch slammed shut on its own.

Everything stopped.

No shaking.  
No steam.  
No knocking.

Just twenty seconds of perfect silence.

Then the building exhaled.

All the radiators on the property screamed at once.  
A blast of black water burst from every pipe in the basement, coating the walls, the chairs, the tenants, me. It smelled like grave dirt and old pennies. People fell and slid in it, crying, retching, scrambling for the stairs.

Some made it.  
Some didn’t.

I don’t remember getting outside.  
I remember cold air. Sirens. Half the block cordoned off by sunrise. The top two floors of Marrow House had partially collapsed inward. Officials called it a gas explosion combined with structural failure.

They found eleven survivors.  
Three bodies.  
No boiler room.

That part is important.

When the city tore through the basement, there was no chamber under the building. No rows of chairs. No giant hatch. No shaft.  
Just a shallow concrete utility space with one rusted residential boiler and a cracked foundation wall.

Mr. Klein told them I attacked people during the evacuation.  
The surviving tenants backed him up.  
Said panic made me hallucinate.  
Said I was always unstable.  
Said the missing tenants had moved out over the years and I’d become obsessed with conspiracy theories.

Maybe that would have worked.  
Except for one thing.

Yesterday, while I was being discharged from the hospital, a nurse asked if I had family history forms on file. I said no. She handed me a clipboard anyway.  
Tucked beneath the papers was a small square of yellowed note paper.

The same kind that had been taped to my door.

On it, in jagged handwriting, were seven words.

\*\*MARROW HOUSE HAS BEEN CONDEMNED. YOU HAVE NOT.\*\*

And from the boiler room three floors below the hospital, I heard three slow knocks.

A pause.

Then three more.
