“Heaven is closed…”

were the first words she said to me. January 1st, 2000. A hard date to forget, I guess. There's quite a bit that's hard to forget about her, though. I think it might be a bit harder to figure out where to begin. Before anything, I should say that my experiences may seem normal. Trust me, there is a lot to explain. I hope someone out there will read this, my admittance of guilt, and understand that my situation is anything but normal.

  


Well, whatever, I'll just start with that New Year's Eve. With everybody “panicking” about Y2K, I figured the end of the world would be a great time to aimlessly party. I spent the night on a bar crawl with some guys from school. Nothing of note really came of that mission, other than a makeout that was pretty rudely interrupted by a very upset boyfriend.

  


I was heading home sometime past two in the morning with a splitting headache (honestly, it was more from that fight-hungry douche than the drinks, but neither did me any good). To correct my moral missteps, I dropped a dollar near a homeless girl. She was virtually non-existant–the beams of a flickering streetlamp were barely enough to show me the soft, gentle look on her resting face. I was moved, unaware as I was of this fact, by a strand of her hair that had settled along the bridge of her nose. It curled so slightly away, gently reaching up to tickle her eyelid. My hand stretched hesitantly, moving to comfort this distant stranger. The hair remained, though, and my hand returned to my pocket.

  


This was one of those countless moments where your life \*could\* change if things just happened the right way, but most of the time nothing ever happens and you go on your way, none the wiser. That night, and specifically, my brief moment looking at Julia was one of these by-chance nothing moments that occur at invisible crossroads throughout your life. Take the left turn, take the right turn, whatever--I'm sure no matter what I would have done, it would have been the same thing. It's kinda like those people that were supposed to be on the Titanic but canceled at the last minute, you know? Sometimes we just skid by, and usually we never even notice.

  


So, as I stood under a streetlamp, noting the sour air that hangs around the poorer neighborhoods in NYC, I saw Julia's eyes snap open. And then she said it:

  


 "Heaven is closed... Hell is too... can't go paintin' zebra stripes on a kangaroo!" 

  


I probably would have laughed if it weren't for the headache, or for the feeling that a presence was looming over me. At that time, I would always feel that sort of thing when I was wandering the city late at night. I don’t really seem to notice that stuff nowadays, even when I’m looking for it.

  


And Julia, after spewing that nonsense, went straight back to bed. Like any other drunk New Year's celebrant would do, I just kept on moving. It wasn’t until I was walking into my apartment that I realized I had spent the entire time thinking about her. In the middle of the winter, swaddled in horse blankets, she looked so calm–honestly, she didn’t look like a homeless girl at all. Her expression was so peaceful. I felt it home in that brief interaction in a way that I still don’t really understand.

  


For a long time that was that. Actually, it was well over a year until I saw her again, not that I knew it at the time. 

  


There's too much to say about Julia, and I find the more detail I get into, the less willing I become to tell the story. Forgive me for what I exclude here, but know that I haven’t forgotten anything. As clear as day, I see her chestnut hair, the smile she had as she slept, the way her eyes looked unwaveringly into mine as she spoke those words--not a thing about her has escaped my memory and I truly doubt it ever will.

  


I can now effortlessly recall when I saw her next. In 2001, as the penultimate days of a summer spent in Michigan brought my time at home to a close. The final note of an already drab, humid summer, was the loss of my uncle. Uncomfortable in my hand-me-down suit, I patiently thanked unfamiliar faces for their condolences. I didn’t notice until she was already in front of me. My eyes panned up from the ground, grabbing every detail I could–her dress fell above her knee, half hiding the twisting scar that wrapped around her thigh. Pensively, she looked into my eyes and whispered her apologies. Her long, thin arms wrapped around me. She squeezed me and then pulled back, looking down and leaving her hand to linger on my chest for just a moment.

  


The young women spoke next to my sister, and while she was just as sincere, their interaction lacked the affection of my own.

  


In my state of confusion, all I could manage was a questioning glance towards my sister, who shrugged it off and said "probably Danny's friend." I laughed but quickly hid it, afraid to seem too cheery; especially to not upset my Mom. And really, I was upset, too, I just had an easier time coping by ignoring the problem in front of me--I still do. 

  


Admittedly, the thought of Danny, who I found to be the most repulsive man I had ever met, being "friends" with a girl like her cracked me up. Over dinner I asked him–as insufferably as possible–where the “dime-piece” had gone.

  


"Man, you talking about Tiff? She's no dime, maybe a few pennies..." As odd as it was, I found it sweet that Danny’s ex-girlfriend showed up. I was not, however, asking about Tiff. I realized the girl who hugged me hadn't been there with him. Nor was she with any one else that I asked, actually. I might have pursued further, but I was only back for a few more days before heading to the city for school. There was so much going on, I spent very little time thinking about her, back then at least. I can remember those moments more than much of the funeral, twisted as that may be. The whole summer was a haze except for those few clear moments. Julia had this way of crystallizing memories for me--our times together are as clear as day in my mind.

  


Then came New Year's Eve going into '04. For my final year of college, I decided to try the bar crawl again, much like I did the year before, and the year before that. It wasn't the most luxurious route, but we were able to hit cheap bars that always had young crowds. Not that you can get a pint for $2.50 today, but we were lucky back then. I can’t help thinking about how different the world felt at that time. Rose-tinted glasses, maybe, but I’ll tell you this much–if I could go back I wouldn’t hesitate for a moment.

  


For my final "Berklee Ball Busting Bar Crawl," as the group had taken to calling it (pretty dorky, in hindsight), to celebrate both graduation and the fact that I no longer needed a fake, I went all out. All out.

  


I left my mark all over Tony C's, The Basement, on Sharv's shoes. I mean it, seriously, all out.

  


By the time I was heading home, I could tell it was going to be morning soon--one of those rare moments where you're awake at the right time to catch the sun before it rises and the moon after it sets. An empty sky, ever so slightly illuminated by the sun... it's something I've always really enjoyed, yet I always forget about it in times between those moments.

  


As I passed the corner where I had seen Julia years prior, the sun was already coming up; I missed my train at some point and had the bright idea to walk. I never really paid any mind to how close Julia had been to my place when I saw her for the first time, not until this very moment. Maybe if I had seen her there again, but she wasn't in her old spot. She wasn't on the street at all, she was in my kitchen, cooking my food.

  


I didn't have a lot of money back then, so judge me if you will, but my first instinct wasn't to kick Julia out, it was to lay into her for throwing out a waffle that fell on the stovetop. She was funny that way, willing to sleep on the street but afraid of the germs on my (relatively) clean stove. Clearly, I was pretty "funny" too because that was my first concern: it took longer to notice than I'd like to admit that a strange girl was in my kitchen.

  


Not that it mattered so much, because the moment I recognized the situation, she turned around, smiled, and said that the food was almost ready. I could smell it, it was the first time I smelled her cooking ... I haven't forgotten the scent, nor have I forgotten how much it comforted me. Even without the horse blankets, all it took was one look at her eyes to immediately tell it was her. She was radiant, just as she had been at the funeral. It was the same beautiful face that latched itself to my subconscious that first night.

  


I have to imagine that the feeling I had at that moment is what sailors would experience listening to a siren song. My ship, settling after a storm, was ushered forth along a calm sea by her presence. It would be simple for a sailor to recall the maiden’s tales about sirens. A virtual pre-requisite to the journey; the warning to not allow yourself to be beckoned into the dark. Yet, how can one turn away from something like that; so welcoming and desirable it can blind you without lifting a finger. I’m sure the countless missing crews understood, just as I do, how easy it is to ignore your instincts–how easy it is to fall for a trap when it is designed for you personally. Julia--her whole presence just felt like that--a call which I could not refuse, the words of a friend from the lips of a stranger.

  


Neither could my friends, who seemed to forget that she was my girlfriend anytime she was around. It didn't concern me though, I knew that they were just being dicks, and I was confident Julia wasn't gonna leave me for some other guy, no matter what. I was 100% sure that I wouldn't lose her, if I could help it.

  


But I was still sober enough to question why she was there, and how she had broken in, to which she retorted, while laughing; "Are you really scared of little ol’ me, you dork?"

  


It's hard to explain how, but it just felt... alright; like I didn't have to worry about anything at that moment. I ate as she spoke about a coworker that was “still” flirting with her, I looked up from my plate to see her eyes dart away nervously. Her confidence and humor evaporated–I listened as she punctuated her story with awkward laughter. I sheltered her tensed hands with my own and watched the discomfort wash away. While she seemed more relaxed, my heart-rate began soaring. I wasn't new to girls, but she was different, and that little touch felt like it was the only thing I'd ever want again.

  


I noticed her smiling a lot for the rest of breakfast, which I thought was peculiar given that I wasn't really doing anything but quietly eating. Then, I began to feel it--my cheeks were burning; I had been grinning like a fool without realizing. The moment I tried to correct my face, she giggled, clearly having noticed my self-awareness. She just went on, telling a story about a call from her uncle. Really, I should have paid more attention to what she was saying, rather than just the fact that she was talking to me. I didn't ask any questions when we first met, and I didn't realize how weird that was for quite some time.

  


My roommate came back as I was picking up our dishes, and he quickly followed me into the kitchen. He damn near bumped into me as we walked in, he couldn't seem to stop staring in awe at Julia. Neither could I, so there was no judgment. He quietly closed the kitchen door behind him, its draft pulling the front door shut.

  


Without hesitation, he shot the elephant in the room, asking faster than he could remember to whisper, "How did you get her? What kinda tool are you carrying, man?" and I, with even less hesitation, punched him in the shoulder. Maybe just a little harder than I intended, honestly, but it shut him up. He must have just thought I didn't want her to hear him, but that hadn't crossed my mind. I was preoccupied and didn't care to hear Mark's drivel at that moment, as much as I loved the guy.

  


My focus, standing there with a mixture of dish soap, powdered sugar, and maple syrup (if you can call the cheap kind anything but sugar water) coating my fingertips, I was trying to remember how I knew her. Not consciously, it was as if my whole body knew her, every sense was familiar--but my mind was lagging behind. So far behind, in fact, that when Mark asked why I punched him, all I could respond with was a deeply confused shrug. My mind continued to grapple with the obvious truth that I did not know her--that she shouldn't be here--and the fact that, for some reason, I wasn't worried about it.

  


Thinking back, it was like being half awake--I could comprehend the world around me, I could act as if I was in control, but I was just a witness. I still don't really know what it was, what Julia was doing, but I didn't ask those questions. I just kept going, for nearly a year of my life.

  


On occasion, a store clerk or server might ask how "the lovely couple" met or if it was our anniversary, to which she would just giggle or kick me under the table. I'd usually come up with some far-fetched story to crack her up even more, but I didn't ever truly answer the question and neither did she.

  


You know, it would have been odd to tell someone about the "homeless" girl who wandered into my apartment and acted as if she owned the place, but that wasn't the reason I kept from telling that story. Really, it was because that wasn't the story. I knew her, it was just this undeniable truth hanging over my head, so clear that I allowed myself to ignore the glaringly obvious piece of it all; I didn't know her, or who she was, we hadn't met before I first saw her on the street, and yet we both acted like there was some long history between us. That sort of cognitive dissonance--my conflict between her familiarity and the lack of any substance to back it up--lingered for our whole relationship.

  


And then came her birthday...

  


She hadn't ever mentioned her birthday, but at the time that fact failed to register; I still set a reservation at Perry's with all of our friends. All of whom, by the way, brought gifts that she loved. They knew her pretty well, I suppose.

  


She got hammered. This was pretty rare, but several rounds of shots and a little birthday magic were enough to bring her past her usual limit.

  


For once, I stayed relatively sober--for that reason, Tina and Sharv asked if we wanted to hang out at my apartment. Sharv wanted to see me get plastered and Tina was always happy for a double date.

  


We got comfortable on the couch, put in the Die Hard VHS (a tradition for our friend group anytime after thanksgiving) and poured the wine that we picked up at the bodega off the green line. Tina grabbed my Monopoly board, to which I groaned but grudgingly agreed to play. Every time they came over, she'd try to start a game and every time I'd wonder why I hadn't donated it to Goodwill. It wasn't Monopoly's fault; I was so competitive that Julia and I had once gotten into a massive argument over an accusation that she was sneaking extra hotels onto the board.

  


She hadn't been of course, and I felt like the sorriest fuck in the world for making her cry over it. It's moments like these that make me not want to tell her story--moments that make me hate myself more than I can handle.

  


Julia seemed different during that game, she was so lively. It wasn't drunkenness, though it probably looked that way to Tina and Sharv. No, she seemed... absent. It was as though the joy was a performance, there was something bothering her.

  


Our friends left around midnight, thanking us for a good time and promising to call the next day (an allowance made for Julia, who was concerned with making sure they got home safe).

  


The second the door shut, I turned to Julia and asked what was wrong.

  


"What? That was so much fun! Thank you for making today great."

  


"No, I don't mean it like that. You seem different-"

  


"Baby, I'm probably just wiped out and a little too fucked up, let's just go to bed."

  


Despite my every instinct, I couldn't help but believe her. The look in her eyes was so opposite the words she said, but her voice comforted me, muting my concerns.

  


That night, I slept soundly--like I always did with her next to me. The sun broke through my busted blinds, and I woke up. The haziness in my first moments of consciousness faded as soon as I reached for Julia, only to find an empty space beside me.

  


I sat up, able to smell her cooking, and smiled to myself. As much as I spent this time in a state of semi-consciousness, it was still pretty great. I messed up plenty, but Julia meant more to me than anyone or anything I had ever found before. She was special, and everybody knew it from the moment they met her. Her calming tone, a laugh that seemed to bring up even the darkest room, her thoughtful, patient way of supporting those around her. More than anything, her witty humor that I could never match.

  


So I sat there, grinning like a fool--which I often did when she was making breakfast. I laid my head down on the pillow, hoping to catch a little more sleep before the food was ready, hoping to wake up to her smiling face telling me to come eat.

  


Then I realized how badly I needed to pee. I slowly opened my eyes, and saw it. Maybe it was because of the crust in my eyes or my drowsiness, or the aroma of fruit and syrup and coffee, or the general peace that I felt in her presence--whatever it was, I had missed the words on the ceiling.

  


In what looked like black chalk, the words sprawled across the room, from corner to corner of the stucco ceiling. They read;

  


"Heaven is closed. Hell is too. Can't go painting zebra stripes on a kangaroo."

  


I felt my breathing become unsteady. My eyes darted across each line of the text... this was Julia's handwriting. It was, as much as I had blocked it out, the first thing she had ever said to me.

  


"Julia? Julia?!" I tried to speak, but something was wrong. As scary as this would be to anyone, my dread felt disproportionately large. Entirely overwhelmed by my state of panic, I struggled to even get the words out.

  


I stumbled out of bed, following the smell of breakfast to the kitchen. She wasn't there, but her food was. Neatly set on a plate--the plate she always gave me because it could hold more food--was a pile of french toast, blueberries and cantaloupe made into a smiley face, and a note.

  


Her final message to me. Before I even began to read it, Mark came to check on me. He heard my stumbling, panicked movement as I passed his door, he later told me.

  


I sat there, clutching the note as my tears drenched the page. Mark pulled me up and moved me to the couch, but I hardly noticed his presence. After some time, Mark took the note from me and read it, only disrupting his quiet study of it with confused expressions and the occasional glance at me. After finishing, he set it down and asked me: 

  


"What did you do?"

  


And then, she was gone. Totally gone.
