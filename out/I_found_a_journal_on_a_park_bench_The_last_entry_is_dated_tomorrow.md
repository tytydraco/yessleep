I jog the loop at Verona Park most mornings. Last Tuesday there was a composition notebook sealed in a freezer bag on the bench by the boathouse. I waited twenty minutes for someone to come back for it. Nobody did, so I opened it, which I know was wrong.

I'm posting it because I can't stop reading it. I've only fixed the spelling.

**ENTRY 1**

I got out in March after sixteen years. I fold sheets at a laundry and study for my massage exam at night. My girlfriend Kim quizzes me with flash cards and does a terrible accent on the Latin. I'm writing that first so it's on the record that for a few months I was someone I liked.

My boss Gary pushed me today. For one second a little cold door opened under my ribs, and through it I could see exactly how long it would take to put his head in the industrial dryer. Eleven seconds. I closed the door and took out the trash and felt proud of myself.

There was a black bag on top of the dumpster, double knotted, no smell, like someone had set it there. Pills, and a brick of cash. Twelve grand, about.

I knew what the right thing was. It took me four seconds to know it and five seconds to put the bag in my car.

**ENTRY 2**

Kim looked at the money for a long time. "Dealers," she said. "They ditched it. It's nobody's, Jer."

I said we should give it back. I want that on the record.

"To who?" she said, laughing, and fanned the bills out like a hand of cards. She already had a folder with listings for a storefront on Bloomfield Ave. She'd been looking since March and hadn't wanted to say.

We signed the lease Monday. The money covered the deposit and two months. Exactly.

**ENTRY 3**

We came up short on the massage tables. "What about your cousin?" Kim said. "Family looks out for each other, Jer. You said so yourself."

I did say that, in April, about my mother. I never told her what my cousin is. I never told her I took a fall for him, or that nobody asked me to.

I called him from the empty storefront while she was in the bathroom. He answered on the first ring. "I've been waiting for you to ask for something," he said.

**ENTRY 4**

I went to a garage today. I'm not writing what happened there. I'll write what came after.

The door in my chest swung all the way open, and what came through wasn't rage. It was relief, like putting down a bag I'd carried for years.

I washed my hands at the laundromat until the skin went white. Kim had a cake waiting. She said she was proud of me. I held her, and my hands were perfectly steady, and that's what scared me.

**ENTRY 5**

I went back to the dumpster tonight. I don't know why. The bag was there again, on top, double knotted, no smell.

I didn't open it. I know how much is in it. It's exactly what we're short for the next thing, whatever the next thing is.

I told Kim. She was washing dishes and she didn't turn around. "Lucky us," she said.

I've been trying to remember whether I ever told her which dumpster.

**ENTRY 6**

I need to write this while I'm sure of it.

Kim keeps her flash cards in a recipe box. I took it down at 3 a.m. The muscles are in front, Latin with little smiley faces. Behind a rubber band there's a second stack. Each card has a name and a date, and in her handwriting: *Door?* with a Y or an N.

Mine is in there. *Jeremy. Door: Y. Found at 19.* I was nineteen the first time I hurt someone badly enough to feel it open. I have never told her that.

Gary has a card. *Door: Y (small). Let him push. Thursday.*

The man from the garage has a card. *Door: Y (opened).* I read his name out loud. I don't know why.

The last card had no name. Just tomorrow's date, and one word: *Ready.*

**ENTRY 7**

I'm writing this in the car. Kim has never lied to me. She's never once asked me to hurt anyone. Every time, I walked through it myself, and she was happy for me when I came home. "It's a learning curve," she says.

At dinner I asked her why she wanted me.

"I like finding people who've been holding their breath," she said. "You'd be amazed how many just need someone to tell them it's okay to let go."

I don't know what she is. I know nothing she does is provable. She just stands where the door opens.

**ENTRY 8**

I'm leaving this on the bench by the boathouse. If you're reading it, she'll know you did.

She'll come in the morning. She'll be so nice. She'll bring banana bread and say it's just her husband's silly diary and could she please have it back.

Don't give it back. Don't be kind to her. Whatever you do, don't let her tell you it's okay.

It's already open a little in everyone. That's what I figured out too late.

It's 8:40 a.m. Wednesday and I'm in my car outside the park.

A woman was waiting at the bench when I got there. Early fifties, cardigan, a foil-covered plate in her hands. She smiled before I was close enough to see her face.

"Oh, I think you found my husband's notebook," she said. "He gets so embarrassed. I'm Kim."

I told her I hadn't seen anything. She nodded like I'd said something sweet. She said she was sure I was a good person, and she held out the banana bread, and I took it.

I don't know why I took it.

The notebook is in my glovebox. Last night, right after I first read Entry 5, I took my trash out to the dumpster behind my building. There was a black bag on top. Double knotted. No smell.

I haven't opened it. I'm telling you that so somebody knows.
