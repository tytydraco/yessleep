My husband, Dan, is not only a mumbler, but frustratingly apologetic. He's the kind of guy who says sorry when someone else bumps into him. He even says sorry to the dog when he steps over her. He says it so often, and so quietly, that after eight years of marriage I barely hear it. It's like the hum of the fridge these days. Background noise.

So I can't tell you when the weirdness began. I can only tell you about the first time I really noticed it. It was a Tuesday morning. I was leaning against the counter with my coffee when he shuffled past, kissed the top of my head, and mumbled, "Sorry, babe. I shrank your favorite shirt."

I looked down. Most of my shirts were Lululemon, except for my beloved vintage Cher t-shirt, which I happened to be wearing.

"You didn't," I said, pointing to my chest.

But he was already halfway down the hall. All I got back was a distracted "Hm?" and then the sound of the shower turning on.

Dan is not a morning person. Half of what he says before nine is gibberish, so I let it go. Then on Saturday, he poured a pile of clean laundry onto the bed for folding when I heard him whisper, "Shit." He held up my shirt. "Sorry, babe. I shrank your favorite shirt."

I snatched it from him and nearly screamed. Cher had never looked so tiny.

"If this is a joke, it's a shitty one."

Dan looked confused.

"You already apologized for it earlier this week."

He blinked twice.

"Forget it," I said.

He apologized again as I stormed out of the room. He told me he'd buy an even better one, the one with Cher holding a boa constrictor I'd been eyeing.

I told the t-shirt story at work the following week, and Ebonie, who sat across from me and I'm pretty sure had the loudest laugh on the planet, decided Dan wasn't pulling a cruel prank. No, he was psychic. For days, she'd lean over the divider and ask me to have him predict the lottery numbers or find out whether her date that weekend was going to be as cheap as the last one.

And I had another one for her. A few nights after the shirt incident, Dan had mumbled through his toothbrush, "Sorry I missed your birthday."

My birthday wasn't until August, and he'd never missed one.

Ebonie said that meant, at the very least, that I was going to get a really good present. We laughed, and then I forgot all about it as the emails piled up in my inbox.

About two weeks later, I was looking over receipts at the kitchen table when my husband started up again while washing dishes. Distracted, I only caught the tail end of it, low and soft, the way he says everything.

"...so sorry you had to find her."

I turned to him, and he was drying his hands with a dishtowel while gazing out the window. I asked who he was talking about, and he looked as if I'd snapped him out of a daydream. "Hm? I didn't say anything. —Should we watch a movie tonight?"

I figured he was referring to something on the podcast I was playing in the background, so I shrugged it off. It wasn't really an apology anyway. More like condolences.

Four days later, I understood what he meant.

I went to the office early that Friday to finish a report I'd been putting off. I thought I was the first one in, but there was Ebonie's head peeking over the divider with her fuzzy pink headphones on and her monitor glowing. I wasn't surprised. She was always catching up on work because she was, in her words, cursed with the gift for gab.

"Hey, Dan told me your winning numbers," I joked, dropping my bag on my desk.

Ebonie didn't answer. I knew she was blasting music to keep herself awake. I came around the divider, and her fogged eyes looked straight through me while her gray tongue sat dry in her open mouth. On her screen was an email she'd started at 11:42 the night before and never finished. I could smell the piss that had already dried into her pants.

I don't remember calling 911. All I remember was Dan's mumbled phrase in the kitchen while gazing out the window.

They said it was Ebonie's heart, a genetic defect she was born with that nobody had ever caught. She was only thirty-four. Dan left work to pick me up, and when I got into the car, he leaned across the console, held my face in both hands, and said, "I'm so sorry you had to find her."

I pulled away from him so fast I hit my head on the window.

In bed that night, I told him exactly what he'd said in the kitchen before Ebonie died, word for word. He looked so sad and appalled. He took my hands and said, "Babe, how on Earth could I possibly know that about her? I swear to you, I never said that."

The worst part was that it wasn't Ebonie's death that kept me awake that night. It was Dan. Whether or not he knew it, he was lying. Somehow, he knew things he shouldn't be able to know.

I lay there in the dark, trying to remember any strange things he had said over the years that I hadn't really been paying attention to. It was like turning over rocks, looking for signs of life underneath.

Last winter, he'd mumbled from the hallway, "Sorry I dented your car," even though the car was fine. A month later, he backed into it in the driveway, dinging the bumper, and repeated those very words.

In the spring, he'd come in from the garage and said, "Sorry about your sister," which I'd assumed was about the fight she'd had with her fiancé after dinner at our place. A week later though, the engagement was off.

There were a handful of examples like that now that I thought of it, most of them minor. But every time, he would say sorry twice: once before and once when it happened. I just hadn't been listening.

After Ebonie's death though, I did. I listened the way you'd listen for a stranger's footsteps in your house at night.

For almost two weeks, everything Dan mumbled was ordinary again. He said sorry to the dog for stepping over her, sorry for leaving the cupboard open, sorry for hogging the blankets, and so on. I started to think I'd concocted the whole thing out of grief and coincidence. Then on a Sunday afternoon, I was on the couch while he was talking to his brother on the phone in the other room. I could hear the gist of the conversation. Football, their parents' wedding anniversary, and then "Okay, love you, man." And then, as if to no one:

"I'm so sorry you fell."

The words seemed to ring in my head, louder than he'd spoken them. Something wet then touched my lips. I wiped my mouth, and my fingers were smeared with blood. I grabbed a tissue and held my head back. I couldn't remember the last time I had a nosebleed.

Later that day, I texted his brother, Kevin, and asked if Dan had seemed okay on the phone. Kevin joked that he'd sounded no more or less stupid than usual. When I asked, as casually as I could, whether Dan had apologized for anything, he sent back a question mark. I told him to forget it. I then typed that he should watch his step. But it kind of sounded like a threat, so I deleted it.

I didn't tell Dan. I didn't know how to tell him that his own mouth was warning people about things to come. Also, after how he'd looked at me in bed, I didn't want to see that worry on his face again. So I just started being extra careful. After all, maybe I was the one who was going to fall.

I held on to the railing in the shower. I salted the driveway twice a day, even though it had barely snowed. I stopped wearing socks on the hardwood. I even stopped going upstairs, which was laughable. I told Dan my back was acting up and the couch was easier on it, and he believed me, because he always believes me. He brought down my pillows, my phone charger, and the Knausgaard book I was halfway through. And he apologized for the couch being so lumpy. When I snapped at him to stop fucking apologizing for everything, he apologized for that too. For a week, I slept downstairs and listened to him through the ceiling. Nothing happened.

Then, not long later, he told me his brother tripped over a rock at the edge of his property and broke his big toe. I sighed with relief. I was safe, and his brother had gotten off easy.

The following week, Dan went upstairs to take a nap, and soon his mumbly voice reached my ear in the living room. I couldn't make out a single word, but I knew he was talking in his sleep. It happened every so often. I feared it might be his next premonition or whatever the hell it was, so I ran upstairs to catch it in time.

The next thing I knew Dan was kneeling over me in his boxers, his face white.

"I'm so sorry you fell," he said.

Dan had left a newspaper at the top of the stairs to remind himself to bring it down to the recycling bin in the garage. I didn't remember stepping on it, my foot sliding out from under me, or tumbling backward down the stairs and hitting my head. But now, I had a mild concussion and a wrist broken in two places. And under the ugly white lights of the ER, I understood that although his brother had tripped, Dan's terrible words were meant for me.

I moved back upstairs after that. I slept terribly, with my cast propped up on a pillow, and most nights I lay awake listening to Dan's breathing, neurotically waiting for the next apology.

It happened two weeks later. A little after three in the morning, he rolled toward me in his sleep and murmured, "I'm sorry, Ellie."

His tone was so tender, just how you'd say it to someone you loved.

They were his worst words yet. I didn't know an Ellie.

I asked him about it over breakfast, perhaps with too much acid in my voice, and he laughed uncomfortably and said he had no idea. I'd spent eight years with the man though, and despite his endless reservoir of sorrys, he had never once apologized in that oddly intimate tone. For the next few weeks, things were cold between us. I hate to admit it, but I even checked his phone while he was in the shower. I didn't find anything, and that only made it worse.

In January, my grandmother went into the hospital with pneumonia. She was ninety-one. The doctors began using phrases like "minimize the pain." A few nights later, I was dozing in the passenger seat on the way home from visiting her when Dan's murmuring woke me:

"I'm sorry for your loss."

I didn't even open my eyes. I didn't ask him about it. I knew he wouldn't remember, and I knew what it meant.

I hated him at that moment, but it prepared me. I went back to the hospital every day that week. I held my grandmother's hand and told her I loved her. I told her about the Cher shirt, because she always liked a good Dan story. I said goodbye to her four separate times. Then, the following Monday, she sat up in bed and asked for a coffee. By the end of the month, she was home. I remember sitting in the hospital parking lot, laughing until I cried, because for the first time since Ebonie's death, one of my husband's sorrys had been wrong.

For a few weeks, things returned to normal. My grandmother's recovery cheered me up, and Dan and I were cozy again. Of course, neither of us mentioned Ellie. My cast then came off, and my grandmother called every Sunday to complain about her physical therapist. I started to let myself believe it was over, that whatever had been happening had run its course.

On a Saturday afternoon in February, Dan clipped the leash onto the dog and stood in the doorway in his big green parka. "Sorry, I'll be quick." He always apologized for leaving, even for twenty minutes. I told him, "No more sorrys," without looking up from my book.

An hour later, I heard scratching at the front door. It was the dog, alone, soaked through and shaking. Her leash trailed behind her, frozen stiff.

Police found Dan that evening in the pond at the end of our street, floating face down in a hole in the ice near the reeds. A woman walking by told them she'd seen the dog take off after some geese and then a man chasing after her onto the ice. The officer who came to the door that night stood on my porch with his hat in his hands and said:

"Ma'am, I'm sorry for your loss."

I don't remember much about the funeral. Dan's brother crushed me in a hug while I sobbed. And there were too many flowers and too many people telling me how sorry they were. I flinched every time I heard the word. My grandmother sat with me for hours and, thankfully, didn't say a thing. Lying awake that week, I finally understood that Dan's words in the car had never been about her. I'd just misunderstood it, like I had so many of his sorrys.

In March, I started throwing up in the mornings. I'd lost my appetite, stopped sleeping, and was lucky if I knew the day of the week.

I thought it was grief. Then, my sister drove me to the pharmacy and stood outside the bathroom door while I took the test. When I came out holding it, she started bawling before I could even say a word. A doctor later told me I was more than four months along. Dan had never known.

She was born in July, a week early, with a full head of dark curly hair like Dan's. I'd made a list of names during the long hours at the hospital, but none of them sounded right. When the nurse asked, I blurted, "Ellie," to my surprise.

The first few weeks were bottles, diapers, and tears. Hers and mine. Then one day, I was trying to rock her to sleep in the nursery. She looked up at me with glossy unfocused eyes and the tiniest eyelashes, and I thought about the father she would never meet. I pressed my lips to her forehead and whispered, "I'm sorry, Ellie."

I sat very still. Dan had said those exact words one night, just as tenderly, from the other side of the bed. I'd spent weeks thinking he was apologizing to someone he loved more than me.

He was. He just hadn't met her yet.

On a humid August night, just before dawn, I woke up to a ridiculously early text from my grandmother. She wished me a happy birthday. I'd nearly forgotten. Knowing I wouldn't be able to sleep again, I got up and unceremoniously began folding laundry. The baby monitor then crackled beside me. On the screen, Ellie was asleep in her crib, alone in the room, the night light casting long shadows off the bars of her crib. And then through the little speaker came a sharp whisper:

"Sorry he missed your birthday."
