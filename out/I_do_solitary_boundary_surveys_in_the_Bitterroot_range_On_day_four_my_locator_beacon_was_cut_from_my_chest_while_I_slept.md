When running solitary boundary surveys across high-elevation timber tracts, you keep your personal locator beacon tethered to your chest strap with fifty-pound utility cord. If you drop a beacon down a shale ravine out there, search and rescue ends up working off coordinates that can be three days old. On the fourth afternoon of a twenty-day cruise in the upper Bitterroot range, my tether was cut clean through while I slept, and the unit was sitting dead center on a flat slab of granite thirty yards down the trail.  
I do seasonal boundary work for private timber outfits. It means working way outside radio range with topo maps, a hand compass, and a standard blaze tool. That October, I had a four-thousand-acre parcel up against state land. The only way in was an old decommissioned logging road that ended at a creek wash three miles out. My cousin Jesse hauled me up there in his flatbed, helped me dump fifty pounds of oats, salt pork, and steel pins at an open timber lean-to, and told me he’d be back in two weeks. The shelter sat on a high bench right above a switchback, surrounded by lodgepoles so thick you couldn't see the ridge.  
The first three days went by the book. I put in eight hours a day on the line, shot my bearings, marked forty-foot intervals in red paint, and got back to camp by four to cut firewood before the freeze set in.  
Things went sideways on the fourth morning at the corner pin. That boundary had been marked back in seventy-eight with three-quarter-inch steel pipes and yellow caps. When I cleared the brush off the corner, the pipe wasn't sticking up anymore. It hadn't been pulled, either. Somebody or something had pounded four feet of solid steel straight down into the rock until the cap was flush with the granite shelf. Directly behind it, forty feet up a dead pine with no low branches, a strip of survey tape was tied in a clean square knot. There weren't any spike marks on the bark.  
I wrote down the missing pipe, marked the spot on my Garmin, and kept heading north. Right around noon, the GPS dropped out. The battery was full, but the satellite screen went completely blank, and the digital compass locked up pointing south-southeast. I shoved it in my vest and pulled out my Silva magnetic compass. The needle swung around twice, hung up against the side of the housing, and stayed there.  
I turned around and followed my paint blazes back toward the shelter. Right then, the woods went completely dead quiet. Up on those ridges, you always hear the wind pushing through the needles, or squirrels moving through the brush. There was nothing. Just total silence. The only thing making any noise for miles was my boots hitting the dirt.  
I got back to the clearing around four, and my camp had been messed with. The shelter itself was standing, but every single piece of steel gear had been moved. My splitting axe, the extra pins, and the trench shovel were gone from my pack. They were lined up side-by-side on that flat rock down the trail, spaced about three inches apart, all pointed toward the trailhead. My beacon was sitting at the end of the line. The cord was sliced flat, and the screen wouldn't turn on.  
I grabbed the gear, took the dead unit, and stacked four heavy logs across the front of the lean-to. I pulled my hatchet, set my twenty-gauge across my knees, and left the headlamp off to save the cells. The sun went behind the ridge around five-thirty, and five minutes later, it was pitch black.  
By nine, it was down below freezing. I didn't dare light a fire. From where I sat, the trail dropped straight down into the timber about thirty yards out before cutting into the brush.  
Right around midnight, something was on that trail fifty yards down. I never heard a stick snap. I never heard footsteps in the needles. There was just a tall, completely still shape standing right in the center of the path between two lodgepoles. I pulled my flashlight, aimed it straight down the trail, and clicked it on.  
The beam lit up the dirt, the trees, and the pine needles on the ground. But the light just stopped right where that shape was standing. It didn't hit fabric or skin, and it didn't throw a shadow behind it. The space between those two trees just swallowed the beam. The figure didn't flinch. It didn't put a hand up, didn't shift its boots, and didn't make a sound. I rested the shotgun on the top log, clicked off the safety, and sat there. I didn't say a word.  
We stayed like that for four hours. It got cold enough that frost started forming on the shotgun receiver. The wind never picked up once, and that shape never moved an inch. My watch ticked away in the dark, and right around five, the sky started turning gray through the timber.  
The second the real daylight hit the dirt, the shape was gone. No sound of brush moving, no steps, nothing. The trail between those two trees was just empty dirt.  
I didn't wait around for the sun to come all the way up. I left the oats, the pins, and the tarps right there in the lean-to. I threw my notes, the dead beacon, and the compass into my daypack, grabbed the gun, and started down the mountain as fast as I could walk.  
Two miles down, where the old road cuts across a mud flat by the creek, I stopped to catch my breath. The edges of the mud were frozen hard, but the middle was still soft.  
Right in the center of that mud were three bare footprints. Long, narrow, pushed about two inches down, with no heel mark at all. They started right in the middle of the wash, took three steps along the track, and just ended. There weren't any tracks coming in from the bank, none going out into the trees, and the frost on both sides wasn't touched.  
I hit the main gate two hours before noon. Jesse was already sitting there in his truck with the engine running. He told me he'd driven up early because he just had a bad feeling that my gear wasn't secure. When I threw my pack in the bed, I pulled out that mechanical compass one more time to check the road heading. The needle was still stuck hard against the south side of the casing, completely locked up.
