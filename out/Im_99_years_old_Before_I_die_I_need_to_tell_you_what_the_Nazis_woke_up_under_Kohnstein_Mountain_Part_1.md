My grandson set up this account for me. He told me there’s a place on the internet where people share scary stories, and he thought it might be a good way for me to “get things off my chest” now that the doctors have put me on hospice. He thinks I’m typing up some ghost story or a piece of fiction to pass the time.  
I’m not.

I’m 99 years old. My heart is failing, my lungs are mostly fluid, and I probably won't live to see the leaves turn this autumn. At my age, you stop being afraid of hell, because you realize you already visited it when you were twenty years old.  
I never talked about the war. Not to my wife, not to my son, and certainly not to my grandson. Whenever they asked, I just told them, “War isn’t like the movies, kid,” and changed the subject. I spent the last eighty years locking my doors, double-checking the windows, and sitting facing the exit in every diner I ever walked into.  
I stole a heavily redacted After-Action Report back in 1945. I wrapped it in oilcloth and hid it in a pine crate in my attic, along with my Bronze Star. I’m looking at it right now. The ink is fading, but I don't need it. The memories are burned into the back of my eyelids.

Before I die, someone needs to know the truth. The brass buried it. The OSS sealed it. But it's still down there, waiting in the dark.  
In April 1945, I was a tech sergeant with Company G, 414th Infantry Regiment, 104th Infantry Division—the Timberwolves.  
We were tasked with clearing out scattered resistance on the outskirts of Nordhausen in Central Europe. We had just liberated the Mittelbau-Dora concentration camp the day prior. I won't describe what we found there. We were physically exhausted and spiritually hollowed out by the mountains of emaciated corpses. We thought we had seen the absolute worst of what humanity had to offer. We   
were wrong.

Intelligence told us that a fanatical detachment of SS troops had retreated and barricaded themselves inside the Mittelwerk. It was a massive, three-kilometer network of subterranean tunnels bored directly into the solid limestone of the Kohnstein Mountain, originally used to manufacture V-2 rockets.

We expected a desperate, bloody last stand. The German war machine was in its death throes, but as we advanced through the freezing, mud-soaked pine forests toward a heavily reinforced railway adit on the mountain's north face, the atmosphere changed. It grew suffocatingly unnatural.  
An OSS tactical intelligence team attached to our unit intercepted a series of erratic, unencrypted German radio transmissions coming from deep inside the mountain. The SS weren't calling for reinforcements. They weren't coordinating a retreat.  
They were broadcasting a frantic, repeating warning on all local frequencies. Our radioman wrote down the translation, his hands shaking in the freezing rain: *"Das untere Gewölbe nicht öffnen. Befehl umkehren. Schießt auf alles, was herauskommt."* *(Do not open the lower chamber. Reverse order. Shoot anything that comes out.)*

We reached the railway entrance under a heavy, creeping April mist. The first shots crackled out from the dense tree line—vicious bursts from an MG 42 machine gun that chewed through the pine bark and sent splinters raining down on us.  
These weren't regular, battle-weary Wehrmacht kids. They were a hardened security detachment of the SS-Totenkopfverbände (Death's Head units). But they didn't fight with tactical discipline. They fought with a suicidal, erratic fury, firing wildly like cornered animals trying to keep us *away* from the door.

The skirmish lasted less than twenty minutes. We had the numbers, and we laid down punishing suppressing fire with our M1 Garands and BARs.  
Then, the Germans abruptly stopped firing.  
It wasn't a surrender. We watched from the tree line through the lingering, acrid smoke of white phosphorus. The surviving SS soldiers threw their weapons aside and pressed their bodies flat against the frozen mud. They pressed their ears to the wet earth, their eyes wide with absolute, primal terror.  
They were listening. From deep within the gaping, pitch-black maw of the limestone adit came a sound. At first, I thought it was the low, rolling thud of distant 105mm artillery. But the rhythm was too fast. Too steady. It resolved into a deep, mechanical vibration that rattled the loose gravel around the iron train tracks. I could feel it humming in the fillings of my teeth.  
It was the sound of boots. Hundreds of boots, marching in perfect, terrifyingly synchronized lockstep, echoing up from the pitch-black bowels of the mountain.

A young German second lieutenant stumbled out of the bunker, his uniform shredded by shrapnel, his left arm bleeding profusely. He paid absolutely no attention to the dozen American rifles trained on his chest. Instead, he stared back into the dark mouth of the tunnel, trembling violently.  
Our platoon interpreter translated his panicked screams in real-time over the deafening roar of the approaching footsteps: *"Seal it! Blast the entrance! Burn it! For the love of God, don't let them out!"*  
The first figures breached the fog at the tunnel entrance.

From fifty yards away, they resembled a platoon of infantrymen stepping out in flawless parade-ground formation. They wore the gray-green tunics and stark steel helmets of the SS. But as the fog shifted and the wind died down, the temperature plummeted. I could see my breath pluming in the air.  
Trailing directly behind the Germans were men in olive-drab M1943 field jackets. They wore GI helmets. Some still carried the distinct canvas webbing and Thompson submachine guns of American paratroopers from the 82nd Airborne—reconnaissance scouts who had vanished without a trace in the sector three weeks prior.  
Enemy and ally, walking shoulder-to-shoulder. Their movements were unnaturally mirrored, their bootfalls hitting the gravel at the exact same millisecond.

A corporal next to me let out a nervous, breathless laugh. He lowered his rifle an inch and muttered that they looked like scarecrows.  
That’s exactly what they were. Clothes hanging over something that had completely forgotten how to be human. Meat puppets strung up by invisible wire.  
The lead figure, an American sergeant with his jaw entirely blown away by mortar shrapnel, marched into the pale daylight. The skin of its face was an ashen grey, translucent, and stretched so violently tight over the skull that the remaining flesh had split apart, exposing teeth and stained bone in a permanent, horrific grin. The empty, ruined space where its jaw used to be pulsed in a rhythmic, mocking imitation of breathing.  
It didn't breathe. There was no vapor leaving its open throat in the freezing morning air.

A second figure emerged—an SS soldier missing half his skull. Then a third, its uniform soaked in dried, black blood. Then a flood of dozens, pouring out of the darkness in perfect, silent unison.  
We froze. Our fingers went stiff on our triggers. It’s a profound psychological shock to see your own dead brothers marching alongside the enemy.  
But the surviving SS soldiers didn't hesitate.  
The Germans instantly turned their weapons away from us and unleashed a devastating hail of gunfire into the tunnel mouth. The battlefield erupted into absolute chaos. We stood paralyzed in the trees, watching our mortal enemies desperately trying to annihilate the very things emerging from their own base. A German machine gunner poured a full belt of 7.92mm ammunition directly into the torso of the lead American corpse. The impacts tore its canvas jacket to ribbons, punching gaping, bloodless holes through its chest cavity. The kinetic force lifted the dead sergeant off his feet and threw him into the dirt.

A second later, it stood back up.  
Not with a struggle. Not with a groan. It simply corrected its posture, its limbs snapping into place with jerky, mechanical precision, and resumed its forward march. Having its chest cavity destroyed was nothing more than a minor interruption in its programming.

We finally shook off our stupor and opened fire. The clearing became a deafening meat grinder of crossfire. My squad lobbed frag grenades, turning the tunnel entrance into a roaring inferno of dirt, fire, and shrapnel. During the frantic melee, a high-explosive Mk 2 grenade detonated directly beside one of the larger grey-skinned entities—a massive corpse wearing the winter uniform of a Soviet lieutenant. The blinding blast completely severed its right arm at the shoulder, sending the limb spinning wildly into the brush.

The Soviet creature stopped. It looked down at its own smoking, violently emptying sleeve with a slow, mechanical tilt of its head. It seemed entirely unbothered by pain, but deeply, fundamentally confused by the sudden structural failure of its vessel. Then, the true horror revealed itself.  
Every single creature in the clearing—nearly eighty of them—stopped moving at the exact same millisecond.

The synchronized bootfalls ceased. The gunfire from the panicked Germans tapered off into a stunned, breathless silence.  
Slowly, smoothly, every single head in the horde—German, American, Russian—snapped toward the wounded Soviet entity in perfect unison. Every gray, milky eye locked onto the severed stump of the missing arm.

One body had suffered an injury; *all* bodies reacted to it.

They weren't communicating with each other. Communication implies a delay—a transmission of thought from one mind to another. There was no delay. There was only one mind operating eighty bodies. Up until that point, the creatures had moved like soldiers only because the rotting flesh they inhabited retained the residual, physical muscle memory of military drilling. But as the horde slowly turned their collective gaze away from the severed arm and locked their dead eyes directly on us in the tree line, that rigid, human discipline violently dissolved.

The march was over.

The things dropped their rifles into the mud. Their jaws stretched open in silent, synchronized screams, and their bodies dropped to all fours.
