I am writing this in pieces, over several nights, because I can't always trust what I remember anymore. If parts of this don't line up perfectly, that's not a mistake. That's the point. I'll explain why as I go.

I taught high school for eighteen years. I was not a good teacher, and I need to say that first, clearly, before anything else. I was arrogant. I was impatient. If a student didn't understand something, I never once thought it might be because of how I taught it. I thought it meant they were lazy. Slow. Not worth my time.

Four years ago, a boy joined my class. I'll call him Daniel.

Daniel was quiet in a way that made people uncomfortable. He rarely spoke unless he was forced to. He struggled with basic things — simple equations, simple instructions, things every other twelve-year-old in that room understood without effort. Nobody told me he had a learning difficulty. I don't know if that would have changed anything. I was not the kind of man who looked for reasons to be kinder.

I scolded him constantly. In front of everyone. I called him slow. I told him once, in front of the whole class, that he was dragging everyone else down with him. I made him stand outside the door for an entire afternoon because he couldn't answer a question I hadn't even explained properly.

He didn't cry. He didn't argue back. He just looked at me, very still, like he was memorizing something.

Three weeks later his family left town without any notice. No goodbye. I didn't think about him again for almost two years.

It started with the dreams.

Same one, every night. I'm standing in my classroom, empty except for one desk in the back corner. Daniel is sitting there, writing in a notebook. I try to walk toward him. The distance never closes, no matter how far I walk.

Then it stopped staying in the dreams.

I'd be mid-sentence, teaching a lesson, and for half a second I'd see him. Back row. Watching. Gone before I could be sure I'd seen anything at all.

Around that time, my memory started slipping. Not everything — that's the part that scared me most. I'd forget where I parked. I'd forget what I ate that morning. I'd forget the names of colleagues I'd worked beside for a decade. But I never once forgot his face. I never forgot a single word he ever said to me. Everything else in my life was going hazy at the edges, except him. He stayed sharp while the rest of my mind went soft, like something was clearing space and keeping only him in it.

My doctor ran every test there is. Brain scans. Blood work. Nothing. He looked at me and said there was no physical explanation for what I was describing.

That was the night I found the notebook on my desk at home. I live alone with my wife. Nobody else has a key.

The handwriting wasn't mine. It was small, careful, a child's handwriting. Two hundred pages, and every page said the same five words:

"You said I was slow."

I need to stop here and explain something, because I know how this sounds. I know it sounds like I'm making a story out of guilt. I wish that's all this was.

Ten days ago I finally looked up Daniel's family. I found an old neighbor of theirs and asked about them, said I used to teach him and lost touch. She went quiet for a moment before she answered.

She told me Daniel died six weeks after his family moved. A heart condition nobody at the school ever knew about. Something that could be triggered by prolonged stress. She said his parents blamed themselves for not telling anyone. She said in his last weeks he barely spoke, just carried a notebook everywhere and wrote in it constantly, and no one ever asked what.

His family didn't move away. They left to bury their son.

Which means, for two years, I told myself he was somewhere else, living some other life, forgetting about me the way I'd forgotten about him. He was never somewhere else.

He never left that classroom at all.

I'm running out of nights where my mind is clear enough to keep writing this, so I need to finish it now.

Two nights ago, in the dream, the distance between us finally closed. He was standing right beside the bed when I woke up — not in the dream, awake, in my own room, in the dark. He wasn't holding the notebook anymore.

He was holding a stick of chalk. The same chalk that keeps disappearing from my classroom, a piece a week, for longer than I can remember — which, these days, isn't saying much.

He held it out to me. Not like a threat. Like an offer. Like he was giving me one more chance to teach something correctly before whatever this is finishes.

I didn't take it.

Last night my wife asked me what year our daughter was born in, and I couldn't answer her. I stood there in the kitchen with my mouth open, and nothing came. She's been married to me for twenty-two years and I watched her face when I couldn't answer, and I understood then that whatever is happening to me isn't going to stop.

But I still remember Daniel's voice perfectly. I remember exactly what my classroom smelled like the day I made him stand outside. I remember every single word I said to him, in order, like it happened an hour ago.

I think that's the whole design of it. I think I'm not losing my memory.

I think I'm being emptied out, on purpose, until there's nothing left in me except him.
