The antique trader demanded no money for the golden Zippo. It lay inside a velvet casket behind the cash desk. Massive brass, gilded, decorated with a fine engraving that showed two into each other intertwined snakes.

"A gift," the old man said with a voice that sounded like dry leaves. "I ask you only for a small thing. When you ignite it for the first time, do it at a dark place. And look exactly there, what the flame shows to you."

I held him for a strange romantic. A massive gold lighter for free, there one asked no questions.  
Late in the night I sat in my dark flat. The display of the telephone was the only light source. The curiosity won. I switched off the smartphone. The darkness inside the room was absolute.

I pulled the heavy lighter out from the pocket. The gilded metal was ice cold, almost like freezer burn on the skin.

I flipped the lid open. A deep, metallic ringing echoed through the silent room. I stroked with the thumb over the friction wheel.

A spark jumped over. The flame blazed up.  
It was not yellow or blue. It burned in a deep, deep black violet that did not brighten the room, but condensed the darkness around me like liquid tar.  
In the weak, unnatural shine I saw my mirror image inside the windowpane opposite.

Behind me in the room stood a figure.  
It was giant, thin, and fully soundless. Its skin had the color of dead parchment, and its long, knotty fingers lay already lead heavy onto my shoulders. It bended itself over my head, the mouth wide opened, as if it wanted to drink the flame.

The ringing faded. The room was completely dark again. The coldness on my shoulders vanished abruptly.

My heart hammered against my ribs. I sat for minutes rigid inside the armchair, the sweat stood on my forehead. It had been a hallucination. An optical illusion in the dark space. It had to be so.  
I switched on the standing lamp. The bright, warm light flooded the living room. Everything was normal. No figure. Only me and the brass lighter on the table.

To quieten myself down, I reached for the gold piece. I wanted to prove to myself that it was just a completely normal lighter. A technical object made from metal and wick.

In the bright light of the standing lamp I flipped the lid open and turned the wheel. A normal, yellow spark shot off. The flame burned quiet and warm.  
I breathed deeply out and wanted to flip the lid close again.

But the lid moved itself not. The hinge spring was not broken. It was blocked. When I looked with the thumb to the top, I saw that the small flint pin was no longer sitting below.

Out from the narrow slit under the friction wheel pushed itself slowly, millimeter by millimeter, something white to the top.

It was no metal. It was the ice cold, horny end of a long, human fingernail that hooked itself firmly from the inside around the folding mechanism and locked the lid.

The yellow flame went not out. It colored itself slowly violet again. And out from the ceiling lamp above me, the light extinguished soundlessly.
