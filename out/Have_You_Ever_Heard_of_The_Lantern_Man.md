As soon as we opened the door, everyone inside went silent.

Their glares told us that suburban tourists weren’t supposed to enter the local fishing haunt. But we persisted. Our money was still green after all. Still, it was hard to see the wisdom in three young outsiders intruding on the watering hole of surly local old salts.

But this was the experience we’d paid for. An authentic fishing adventure in a desolate fishing village. Odd glances and lukewarm receptions were par for the course.

“Shall we?” I offered, nodding to the bar.

We made our way out of the whistling, chilly night air. The conversations inside resumed. The shock of seeing unknown faces wore off - or at least, abated for the time being - and we bellied up to the bar. Della, a barrel of a woman with prison-ink tattoos on her forearms and a no-bullshit look in her eye, came over and dropped three cardboard coasters in front of us.

“What’ll ya have?”

“Heard Scupper’s is famous for their Tiki drinks,” Jake joked. “How do you make your Cobra Fang around here? With or without Falernum?”

We politely chuckled. Della didn’t.

“We don’t make that shit here.”

Jake cleared his throat to remove his foot and tried to course-correct. “I was…uh, kidding.”

“What do you recommend?” Gus asked, trying to smooth things over. Since the first time we met in seventh-grade shop class, our group dynamic has always played out this way: Jake makes a weird joke, Gus repairs any unpleasantness it may have caused, and I sweep away any lingering awkwardness. It worked with Mr. Pressman and everyone else since.

“I recommend this guy make better jokes,” Della said. That scored a laugh from a few of the older anglers at the bar. Once Jake had been properly washed and folded, she nodded. “Beer and a shot of whiskey’ll work just fine.”

“Three,” I said. “And one for yourself, if you’re so inclined.”

“Hell, if you’re givin’ away booze, I’ll take some,” a man at the end of the bar said. I’d thought he was asleep or dead, but it turns out he was just resting his eyes.

“I think you’ve had enough, Earl,” Della said. “I ain’t got the strength to haul your carcass home tonight.”

“Let me sleep here,” he slurred. “Hell, I’ll be back first thing in the mornin’ anyway. Just save me the trouble.”

“You can leave him in the marsh, Della,” another man yelled out. The bar erupted into peals of laughter. Even Earl chuckled.

“Naw, Earl’s good people. Just can’t say no to a drink.”

“Aye. The liquor’s bite is mean,” he said with a smile. “But when it’s properly biting, I can ignore the constant barking in my mind.”

“Still wouldn’t leave you out there,” she said. “You keep that end of the bar busy.”

“Besides, it’s a new moon,” Earl said, swallowing the last drops of his beer. “Wouldn’t leave my worst enemy out in the marsh tonight.”

“Why’s that?” Gus asked.

“Evil lives out there, boyo,” Earl slurred.

“Christ, not the Lantern Man again,” Della said. “You bring it up so often, the fella should have an open tab here.”

The bar chuckled, but Earl didn’t join in this time. He eyed everyone in the room with venom. If he had fangs, everyone around him would have bloody wounds from his bites and be twitching on the floor.

“If the Lantern Man shows up, first drink is on me!” a fat man yelled. The room erupted in laughter. Even Della cracked a smile.

“Gonna hold you to that, Bill!”

Earl slammed his beer bottle down on the bar. The noise was as sudden as a car crash, and shards flew in every direction. A piece skipped down the bar and clinked off a jar of pickled eggs near my arm.

Everyone went silent again. No murmurs. No breathing. Just the Scupper’s sign squeaking outside in the gusty wind. It was nice to know that the entire bar being shocked into silence wasn’t just reserved for bumbling outsiders.

“Christ and his donkey, Earl,” Della spat out. “What you go and do that for?”

“Ya know goddamn well why I did,” he said, his jaw trembling. “I’ve told ya before about bringing him up.”

“Was just a joke,” Della said. “No need to go breaking glass over it.”

Jake leaned into me and whispered, “Now who needs to make better jokes?”

“If you saw what I saw, you wouldn’t be jokin’ about it!” Earl spat. “He took everything from me except my own worthless hide.”

Della had a lot to say, but kept her cool and let the tension diminish. Once Earl’s shaking hands stilled, she reached back behind the bar and grabbed a broom and dustpan.

“If you don’t want me to take away the only place you’re allowed to hang out, you’ll clean up that glass,” Della said, handing the equipment over. “Can’t have these city boys cuttin’ their delicate hands on broken glass, suing me, and taking over the bar.”

“You think they’d want this place?” someone yelled.

“I think they’d rather eat the ass end of a beached whale than go through the shit I have to go through every night,” she said.

“Ain’t that the truth!” another person yelled.

“Besides, their manicured city hands would get ruined at the first sign of hard work. I hate seein’ pretty things suffer!”

The bar laughed and returned to some sense of normalcy. We should’ve taken more offense to the jab, but we all had the same question: Who the hell is the Lantern Man? What had Earl been talking about? Why did everyone else laugh at him?

Della came over with the three beers and placed them on the coasters. “Sorry about the razzin’, boys, but have to keep the regulars happy. You’re like shootin’ fish in a barrel, ya know?”

“For the record, I wouldn’t eat the ass end of a rotting whale under any circumstances,” Jake said before giving it a second thought. “Well, maybe for a million bucks.”

“I’d kill for the idea that more money would solve all my problems,” she said, shaking her head. “If eating rotten whale shit-hole gave me peace of mind, I say hand over a blubbery chunk with extra hot sauce.”

“Della, you sure know how to turn a phrase,” Jake said.

“I’m a regular Shakespeare,” she said, turning and grabbing an unlabeled jug and three shot glasses. With a flick of her wrist, she slid the glasses across the bar one by one with sharpshooter accuracy. They stopped right in front of us. She filled the three glasses with a deftness that surprised me. Not a drop spilled on the ancient bar top.

“Get every shard, Earl,” she said, eyeing the man. Earl set to cleaning the broken bottle, muttering to himself, while normal conversation continued all around us. Within a minute, the broken-bottle incident faded from memory.

We clutched our shots and held them aloft. I swirled the bronze colored liquor in the glass and took a sniff. It was harsh - like putting my head inside a jet fuel tank. I recoiled, and my reaction drew titters from the nearby fishermen. Gus took a snoot of the drink and had the same response.

“Jesus Christ,” he said. “What is this?”

“Local brand,” Della said. “Call it the eye-opener. Men take a shot of it before heading out of the harbor. Puts the fear of God in your soul and hair on your balls.”

Jake finally took a sniff and shook his head. “I already have enough hair on my balls.”

“Come on,” I said, elbowing him. “You wanted the full experience, right? This is it.”

“If I’m willing to do it,” Gus said, trailing off. He didn’t need to say the rest for the point to sting Jake.

“Fine.” Jake raised his glass and motioned to Della, who was trying to spin away from us like we were plague rats. “Not going to join us with a shot?”

“I don’t drink,” Della said. “Makes you act like a ragin’ prick.” She didn’t mention Earl by name, but we all got the gist.

“I’m sorry. I gotta know,” Gus whispered to Della. “What’s the Lantern Man?”

She shook her head. “Old legend around here. Nothing you boys need to worry about.”

“Unless they’re heading to the marshes tonight,” a voice from the peanut gallery called out.

Hell, the cat was out of the bag now. Might as well go for it, Earl’s fragile emotions be damned. “Seriously,” I asked. “What’s the legend?”

Della sighed. She wasn’t getting out of telling this saga to a group of curious tourists. “The Lantern Man is an old fisherman who haunts the marshes around here,” she said. “Or so they say, anyway. I’ve never seen him. Only pieced together the story from what these old salts spew in between drinks.”

“What’s his deal?” I asked.

“Legend says if you’re out in the marshes on a moonless night, there’s a good chance you’ll hear something whistling. Won’t see a person, but you’ll hear the whistling as clear as a bell. If you stay in the marshes after you hear it, the next thing you’re gonna see is the lantern light up in the distance.”

Earl piped up. “He’s tellin’ ya where the fish are bitin’,” he said, dumping the glass into the bin. “Life around here revolves around the sea. A bad haul might bring ruin to yer family. Anglers look for any advantage they can find.”

He stumbled over to us and leaned against the bar. You could smell the whiskey from a mile out. His name may have been Earl, but it could’ve just as easily been Jack Daniels. Still, considering he was as pickled as the eggs in the jar near me, the man moved like someone sober…ish. Obviously, being this hammered was his norm.

“Did you meet him?” Gus asked.

“Aye. Dumbest goddamn thing I’ve ever done, but Anna was sick, and I needed to pay the doctor,” he said, shaking his head. “I couldn’t afford another trip that ended with an empty hull, not when my last three had been disasters. My men were leavin’ for other captains. I was out of options.”

Earl plopped down on the empty stool next to me and put his head in his hands. He hunched his shoulders and took a deep breath. He spoke through his hands, it muffled his voice, but it was impossible not to hear his heartache. It oozed out from him.

“I brought it up to the remaining crew, but most of them were of the superstitious type. They drifted away, started tellin’ other crews I’d lost it. Alls I had were me and two mates willing to stick it out. Petey and Hank - good boys with nowhere to turn, so’s they stuck by me. Their captain. What fools we were.”

“Don’t go sad-sackin’ in the pub, Earl. These boys’ll leave if it gets too dreary,” Della said.

“Life is dreary!” he spat, sitting bolt upright. “A horror show, it is.”

She softened her tone, came over to him and patted his shoulder. “If you want to sleep it off, I got the cot in the back for ya.”

“Aye, but they still gotta know the truth,” he said, leveling an unsteady gaze at us. “You gotta know there are things out there that will kill ya where you stand and not think twice about it. Skin ya and leave you a bleeding mess for the animals to devour.”

“Earl,” Della said.

“What happened?” Jake said, interrupting Della’s attempt to stop the story. She gave him a stink-eye, but backed away and threw up her arms. There was no stopping a speeding train.

“The three of us went out into the marshes not far from here. It’d been no more than twenty minutes before we heard the whistlin’. Old sea shanties, something to let you know it weren’t no bird callin’ for ya. It was the Lantern Man.”

“You saw him?”

“Not at first, no. But we knew we’d found his lair, so we pushed deeper into the marsh. The water was freezin’, but we kept movin’. Came up to our knees, but we kept going. Had to,” he said, shaking his head. “The worst was the smell. Ye gads, man, the whole marsh smelled like rot. Death warmed over. The stench got into my clothes so deep I had to burn ‘em.”

A few old sailors, having heard the tale before, stood, placed some cash on the bar and nodded at Della. They passed by Earl, clapped him on the shoulders and chuckled as they left. We watched them go, but Earl kept his eyes on the bar.

“We walked deeper into the marsh. Followin’ the whistlin’. Finally, Petey spied the orange flicker of a lantern off in the distance. He’d shown up. I didn’t believe my eyes. Even after hearing the whistling, I wasn’t sure this wasn’t some dumb joke. But when that lantern lit up…” He trailed off.

“Did you talk to him?” Gus asked. None of us had touched our beers. We were so rapt by Earl’s tale that we leaned on our elbows to get as close as we could. We didn’t want to miss a single word.

“He’s a man of few words, boyos, and he was mum. He beckoned, and we followed. Deeper into the salt marsh than I’d ever gone. Alien. Unnatural. The sky was so dark. No moon. No stars. Even if I wanted to head back, we’d never find the way home. We were lost. A sailor’s nightmare. Our only guiding light was being swung by some ghoul, and we aimed to follow him until the end.”

Earl paused and caught his breath. His eyes were glassy with tears. He bit his hand to keep the sobs at bay.

“You don’t have to finish the story, Earl,” Della said softly.

“I owe it to them to finish it,” Earl responded. “That way, their deaths at least function as a warning if nothing else.”

“Deaths?” I said.

Earl ignored my shock and kept moving. “We marched forward, but never gettin’ any closer to the light. The deeper we went into the marsh, the more nervous Hank got. Started doubting the wisdom of the trip. Lookin’ back now, I agreed, but I was more afraid of losing my boat than seeing this thing. I should’ve listened or cut him loose. But I was stubborn.”

Della sighed and grabbed a different unlabeled bottle from behind the counter. She poured out a shot of a rust-colored liquor and slid it over to Earl. The man didn’t miss a beat and grabbed the glass before it stopped sliding. He raised it high. The yellow bar lights shone through the glass, reflecting amber shadows across the bar top.

“The Lantern Man stopped and held his light aloft like this. He’d come to the spot he wanted to show us. Suddenly, we were gaining on him. The smell of rotting fish was thicker now. We had to wave away dozens of bugs that swarmed the stink. As we drew closer, the only thing outlined in the faint light was the bone-white flesh of the arm that held the lantern. The rest of him was shrouded in darkness. His face hidden from us.”

He downed the shot. The alcohol burned his throat, and he winced as it went down. Still, he placed the glass back on the bar top, and Della dutifully refilled it. It was obvious that Earl’s soul-rending story time was a regular occurrence at Scupper’s.

“Has anyone seen his face?”

“Aye,” Earl said, swallowing the shot. Sweat beaded on his forehead, and he made no effort to wipe it away. It rolled down his nose and fell into his mustache. While he faced us, his gaze wasn’t on us. It was right through the wall. Into the past. Into the marsh. “But those that do survive get to live out their worthless lives as fall down drunks.”

A few more sailors, not wanting to listen to Earl ramble, got up and wished Della goodbye. She nodded at them, but never spoke or left the drunk man’s side. She might hate this entire ordeal, but she had some honor-bound need to stick with him as he traveled through the tempest. The woman supported her regulars.

“When we got within spitting distance of the ghoul, we stopped. The Lantern Man pointed out to the stones at Cadaver Point and nodded.”

“Cadaver Point?” Jake said.

“Aye, the rocky shoals just outside the bay.”

“Might be time for new branding. You’d probably attract more tourists.”

“Plenty of good men have met their demise at Cadaver Point. It’s earned the name,” Della said. “More tourists aren’t worth shamin’ the men who died there.”

I kicked Jake under the bar. Now was not the time to suggest travel tips to attract new tourists. For God’s sake, some old mariner was pouring out his heart to us. Before Jake could respond, Earl continued.

“These are rich waters. Here be a good place to cast your nets,” he said, his voice taking on a deeper timbre. “That’s what it said to us. When it spoke, my whole body tingled like I was having an allergic response to something. My head started hurting, and a trickle of blood came out my nose. My body was sick of my brain ignorin’ the warnings and started sending out pain to get my attention. I should’ve listened.”

“What happened?” I whispered. I was afraid that if I spoke any louder, I’d break the man’s already fragile mind. But I was in too deep, now. I needed to know. Had to know.

“Ever been in the water with a shark?” he said, turning to us. “No, can’t imagine you city folk have. A shark’s not an evil thing. It’s designed for killin’. It don’t think about what it kills, it just does. No anger. A shark kills because sharks kill.”

His tremulous hand knocked the shot glass against the bar. Della dutifully refilled it. Without glancing at the stuff, Earl threw it back. It burned, and he coughed. I went to pat his back, but he begged me off.

“Boyos, when you’re in the drink with one, you won’t even know it until it’s right on top of you. Slices through the water, its maw of pearly daggers cluttered with fish guts comin’ for ya,” he said, shivering. “Like I said, it kills because it does. But when those black eyes roll white, and you’re counting your remaining life in seconds, you’d be forgiven to think the fish had it out for ya.”

“Did The Lantern Man get Petey and Hank?” Jake asked.

Earl nodded. “The marsh claimed them for its own.”

“How did you get away?”

“I almost didn’t. I ran, but you can’t outrun evil. I tried, but these things moved at speeds unnatural to man. They nearly caught me, but I tripped and landed face-first in the mess. I got a mouthful of mud and marsh water. In my panic, I swallowed it down by accident. Everything stopped. No Lantern Man. No chase. I was free.”

Off in the distance, a low rumbling filled the void. The lights in the bar flickered. Bill, the drunk who promised the Lantern Man a drink, walked over and cracked the front door. A gust of wind snapped it all the way open and damn near took the drunk with it. He slammed it shut and hollered out, “Hell’s bells, the storm is damn near on top of us!”

Choruses of boos and damnations followed the news. Gus, Jake, and I all shared a glance. We all wanted the real fisherman experience, and we were getting it in spades.

“Christ,” Della said. “You boys better hunker down. The storms off the coast can get bad quick. Might be bunking here for the night.”

“Our rental is just down the street.”

“You ain’t gonna walk in that rain, trust me,” Della said with a small chuckle. “Storms around here drag men to their graves.” Our faces went pale. She rolled her eyes and nodded. “It’ll blow over soon, but why risk it?”

“Listen to her, boyos,” Earl said. “She knows more than any man in this place.”

The door whipped open again. The wind outside was howling now. Della pointed to Bill, who was still loitering near the door. “Bill, batten down the hatches, eh? If we don’t keep that closed, the goddamn wind is gonna take the door clean off.”

Bill nodded, waddled to the corner of the bar and grabbed a large piece of wood. He carefully walked it to the door and slid it through two metal latches that barred the door and made it impossible to open. We were locked in until the storm passed.

Jake kicked my leg, leaned into my ear, and excitedly said, “Bro, she legit said batten down the hatches! If we’re lucky, maybe a pirate will offer us some grog!”

“Aren’t you worried?”

Jake shrugged. “Just a storm. We tracked it the whole drive up. We knew it was coming this way.”

“It’ll pass,” Gus added, holding out his phone. “I can show you the radar…Huh. No signal.”

“Not gonna get one now, either,” Della said. “Spotty out here in the best of times. With a squall hitting, you’d have better luck porkin’ a pig with Bugs Bunny’s dick.”

Jake laughed. “Della, again, the way you paint with words is an art. A true poet.”

The lights in the bar flickered again, only this time they didn’t immediately kick back on. With no moonlight to help, it was as black as squid ink inside. I couldn’t see my hand in front of my face.

“A shark does what it does, eh,” Earl said. He laughed. It was far from comforting.

The lights came back on. Everyone was still, but no one was nervous. I had to imagine that these guys were used to everything happening now. Blustery storms, a fickle electrical grid, and haunted Lantern men - really, the whole haunted fishing town vibe - were no match for the steely nerves of the old mariners. If they didn’t panic, I’d do my best to stay sane.

Outside, fat raindrops pelted the pebbled glass windows and roof. The downpour was so severe, it momentarily overtook every conversation in the bar. The wind whipped so hard against the building, I half expected it to come off its foundation and take to the air like Dorothy in The Wizard of Oz.

“Ill omen,” Della said to no one’s comfort. “Thunderstorm on the first night of a new moon.”

Bill, the fisherman who barricaded the door, went over to the window. “Streets takin’ on water. Drains must be clogged.”

“How bad?” Della asked.

“Get the sump ready.”

“Ah hell. Just had these floors repaired!”

On cue, water started streaming in from the street. It cascaded down the stairs, creating little waterfalls before pooling on the main floor. We all raised our feet on the stools, careful not to get our shoes wet.

“It’s really coming down out there,” I said. “Our inn might not even be there by the time this passes.”

“That’s why I bought travel insurance,” Gus said, holding his head high.

“If we had to make a boat out of something here, what do you think would work? I’m calling dibs on one of the longer tables,” Jake said. “We can float to….”

He stopped speaking and started retching. We all did. The water washed in some kind of mind-melting funk from the street. It settled around us and made sucking in the air a challenge.

“Christ, not a sewer backup, too,” Della said with a sigh.

I glanced over at Earl, who kept his eye trained on the front door like he was waiting for something to come bursting through. A thunderclap directly over the bar tore the air in two. The vibrations went into your internal organs. If you’ve never had your lungs shake from an explosion, it’s an odd sensation, to say the least.

“He’s out there,” Earl mumbled.

“Earl, don’t start scaring….” Della never finished her sentence. A bright shock of white light hit the building, and a bone-displacing thunderclap erupted over us. The power inside the place blinked out again. Men groaned, and some laughed at the string of bad luck. Their conversations ceased, though. The rushing floodwater and the lashing of the hard rain were louder now.

Della lit a flashlight and shone it on her face. It gave me a jump, and I was embarrassed for being afraid of a stupid campfire trick. Really living up to the tourist vibe. “Nobody do anything dumb. I’m gonna go flip the breakers.” She headed off to the back of the bar, muttering about wanting to sell this place and move to Tahiti.

“This sewer reeks,” Jake said. “Christ on the cross. It smells like fresh shit!”

“Maybe we should’ve skipped the bar, huh?” Gus unhelpfully offered. “At least it wouldn’t smell like a port-a-potty at the inn.”

“I dunno, man. Our last meal was gas station food. That’s gotta be doing a number on our guts. A few blasts would turn our room into a funk factory.”

“Boyos, that ain’t sewer you’re smelling. It’s the Lantern Man. He’s nearby.”

“We’re safe inside, though, right?” I said, taking Gus’s insane proclamation as gospel.

“You ain’t ever safe from him,” he said.

The lights came back on, and some men clapped and gave mock cheers. The storm continued the rage outside. More water streamed under the door, the pool growing below our feet. The cold sent a wave of pain up my body. My skin prickled as if a thousand needles were pressing into me at the same time. I glanced at Gus, and he was examining his goose-pimpled flesh, too.

He caught me looking and shrugged. “Maybe it’s static electricity from the lightning strike?”

“That should do it,” Della said, reappearing from the back room. “Lightning damn near blew every breaker. Still, don’t think there’s any permanent damage.”

“Dude,” Jake said, pointing at me. “Your nose is bleeding.”

The lights went out again. Half the room protested, and the other half laughed at the bad luck. Gus absentmindedly tapped his shot glass against the bar top.

Someone started whistling a sea shanty toward the back of the bar.

At first, there were some nervous chuckles, but when no one owned up to the mean joke, everyone went quiet. The lights flickered, staying on just long enough for me to glance around. It was empty.

Earl was gone. Della too. Hell, everyone was gone except for us.

“What the fuck, man,” Jake whispered.

In the far corner of the room, a lantern illuminated. The soft orange glow barely lit anything, but it was enough to see someone’s arm holding it aloft. The candle’s wick danced behind the glass, making it hard to tell who was holding the damn thing.

“The Lantern Man.”

The power flickered back on. The Lantern Man was gone, but the place was still empty. Except for Della. She’d returned and now stood behind the bar, staring toward the back room she’d just come from. She was still.

“Della,” I whispered. “What’s going on?”

She turned to face us. We all stood and pushed away from the bar. Her eyes had gone completely white, like a shark’s third eyelid sliding out before the bite. She wheezed. Not breathing so much as squeezing air from her lungs.

I wasn’t afraid she’d attack. That’s not why we stumbled away from the bar. Della’s face had changed. Was changing. All of her facial features moved around like they were detached from the underlying skin. Her eyes, nose, and mouth had become flotsam on the tawny, placid waters of her head.

The crooked, upside-down mouth spoke from where her forehead should be. “He sees everything in the dark.”

The lights went out again.

The Lantern light glowed in the corner. The water rushing into the building was ankle-deep and rising. Rot permeated everything and clung to my skin. I kicked my chair back, and it splashed in the puddle.

The wind blew at my back and sent a wave of air billowing up my shirt. There shouldn’t be wind inside the….

“Where’s the fucking bar?” Jake said.

It was gone. Like it had never existed in the first place. We were standing in the cold, rising waters of the salt marshes.

“These are rich waters,” The Lantern Man said, raising his lamp high. “Here be a good place to cast your nets.”

The small light grew impossibly brighter, casting an eerie rusty glow over the surrounding marshland. Rings on the placid marsh water formed as hundreds of hands poked through the surface. All around us, bodies rose from the muck. Some were all bones. Others were half decomposed, their skin dripping off them as they pulled themselves free from the water. A recently dead and still mostly intact body rose near me, and the face was familiar. It was Bill. It clicked.

These were all the people from the bar.

Bill grabbed my ankle. His eyes were white. His skin was waterlogged and soggy. He grinned, and his broken teeth reflected the orange glow.

“A shark does what it does, boyos,” the Lantern Man said, before descending into laughter.

I smashed my foot into Bill’s face and stomped it back under the water. “Run!” I screamed, and we all took off like Hell was chasing after us.

We stayed close together at first, but the marsh was damn near impossible to run through. Gus was the first one of us to fall. His shoe caught in the mud, and he crashed into the cold water. He struggled to stand and screamed for help.

We stopped and turned to run back, but it was already too late. Some ghouls had reached him and were gnawing at his legs and arms. Dozens of other hands shot up from the water and wrapped around his writhing body. With a sickening crack, his body broke, and they pulled him into the muck.

Jake was spellbound. He tried (and failed) to make sense of what was happening when Della came rip-roaring out of the marsh grass and tackled him to the ground. She bit his neck with her upside-down face. Blood spewed from his throat. His screams became gargles before he stopped moving altogether.

I turned to run, but a hand shot up and caught my foot. I yanked free, but I stumbled and fell into the water. Half my face was in the muck. I was about to pull it out when I stopped.

Earl had eaten the mud. Drank the water. Lived to tell the tale.

“Fuck it,” I said, opening my mouth and filling it with the marsh. The earthy, salty mixture coated my tongue and nearly made me gag. I chewed and crunched the small rocks and shells against my teeth. I swallowed. The sandpaper scrape burned my throat until the mud dropped into my stomach. I closed my eyes and screamed.

When I opened them again, the marsh was gone. I’d passed out on the steps of Scupper’s at some point last night. The sky had started to purple. Morning was fast approaching.

I got on all fours and threw up. Yellow bile mixed with black chunks of earth spilled on the sidewalk in front of me. I stared at it, my eyes wide. I coughed, tried to chase the bitterness of the spew out of my sinuses, and failed. It nested in my head, and I dry heaved.

When I was done, I sat back up, rested my head against the outside wall of Scupper’s, and sighed. Maybe it had all been a dream? Della had given us that harsh booze. Maybe we’d overdone it, and I just blacked out. Maybe Gus and Jake were waiting for me at the inn, ready to tell me about our wild night.

The first rays of the sun came over the bay. I shut my eyes and let the light warm my face. Too much to drink. That had to be what happened. Boy, what a story this was. We’d talk about this until the day we died. When I opened my eyes again, my mouth went dry.

The lantern was at my feet. The burning flame danced on the last bit of the candle’s wick. From somewhere inside my brain, a familiar voice laughed. Earl.

*It’s your turn, now, boyo.*

The candle burned out.

I blinked, and a brand-new candle sat inside the lantern.

Fuck.
