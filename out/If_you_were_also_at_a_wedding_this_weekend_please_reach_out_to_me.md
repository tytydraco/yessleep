I need to know if the same thing happened to you. And if you live anywhere in Pruitt County, I would like to hear from you too.

My sister Dana got married today. She’d been firm about it being adults only, no kids, for months, mostly because of her cousin’s toddler. It’s the one decision of hers I will never complain about again.

I almost didn’t go. Last night, my boyfriend, of 1 year and eleven months, told me, over Chinese takeout, that he “just didn’t see us growing old together.” He ate the rest of our noodles before he left. I also want that on record.

So I spent Saturday morning in front of a mirror in a green bridesmaid-adjacent dress I’d bought for this exact day, looking like a boiled vegetable. But it was for my sister so I did my face, drove out, and I made the best plan I could think of. I would sit through the ceremony, say something nice, then fake a migraine before the reception and go home to my couch.

The chapel was small and white, with a steeple that looks like it was added as an afterthought. Inside, it’s one long room, cheap drywall painted cream, narrow windows, two iron candelabras by the altar. About a hundred and twenty people. And couples everywhere. Holding hands, heads tilted together, a woman fixing her husband’s tie without looking at it. I hated every one of them.

The Reverend was forty minutes late. Dana’s maid of honor whispered that he’d been marrying people all over the county since sunrise. It’s a small county but I guess this is his busy season. When he finally showed up, Reverend Ambrose was a tall man with a crewcut and a smile that went a little too far across his face. He shook hands like he was closing on a house.

Then he took Tyler into the side room for a “final prayer.” Another twenty minutes. I sat there with my jaw tight, checking my phone, rehearsing my migraine. When they came out, Tyler had a red thread tied around his left wrist. His lips looked stained, like he’d had wine.

Then the ceremony started. I was annoyed seeing the groomsmen and bridesmaids enter arm in arm. They all were fake laughing as if they were real couples. Looking into each other’s faces as if they really cared at all.   
  
But then Dana came out and I remembered why I was here in the first place. She was really beautiful. I noticed that she also had a red string tied around her left wrist. She held it up in front of me and mouthed that it was, “Good luck.” I remember thinking it was sweet. I remember thinking a lot of things that day.

Then the Reverend did the usual wedding speech. My attention drifted in and out as he rattled off bible verses and talked about marriage like he was trying to sell it to me. “The best decision you’ll ever make.” “Zero regrets, folks.” “A lifetime warranty.” People laughed but I didn’t find anything funny.   
  
Then he looked out at the pews and said, “And to those still sitting alone out there, don’t be shy. The Lord has a plan for all of you.” I remember being offended when his eyes stayed on me a little too long.

Then Dana and Tyler said their vows into the same microphone, sharing it, giggling, being sickeningly cute. Then Ambrose asked each of them, slowly, almost tenderly, if they would. Dana said, “I do.” Tyler said, “I do.”

“Wonderful,” said Reverend Ambrose. "Just wonderful."  
  


Then, in the third row, a man named Phil Gaines went pop.  
  
  
It was not a loud sound. It was a wet little pop, like a water balloon bursting. Phil Gaines was a heavy man in a gray suit, sitting at the end of the pew, and then he wasn’t. In his place was a cloud of pink mist, fine as flour, that spread out across the pews and settled on people’s shoulders and hair. No bits or pieces, just pink everywhere.

Nobody moved. Someone might have laughed, I heard their one short ugly bark. Then someone else screamed and then it sounded like everyone was screaming. I might have been too.

Some people stood up and rushed over. The people who were near Phil stood up and rushed away. There was nothing anyone could do. I wanted to call someone but we had all put our phones in a lockbox outside the chapel.  
  
I got up and made my way to that exit. I wasn’t the only one with this plan because a woman was already trying to open that door when she went pop too.  
  
It was exactly like Phil. Someone yelled for everyone to get down. It made sense to me at the time, so I followed everyone else and we laid prone while trying to crane our necks and see if any of the windows had been shot out.  
  
Then a man in the middle of our group also went pop. There had been no glass breaking. No one from the outside was doing this to us. So, we all collectively got up and ran panicked to different exits. I went straight to the double doors at the back.  
  
They wouldn’t open. The handles turned all the way, smooth, no resistance, and yet absolutely nothing happened. No latch, no lock, nothing to pick. A groomsman put his shoulder into it, then his whole body, then several men helped him carry a pew like it was a battering ram. The wood rang like it was hitting a bell.   
  
Someone threw a chair at a window and it bounced off while breaking the chair’s leg. The windows stayed whole. Through them you could see the Church’s backyard, set up with an Ivy wall, fountain, and other photo op locations. 

People started to get crushed into the doors. I saw my uncle split another man’s scalp with a fire extinguisher he’d ripped off the wall, while trying to smash the glass, and hit the wrong head. The pops kept coming at regular intervals and every time there was more screaming.

Someone got the microphone from the altar. It was Dana, in her white dress, shaking, Tyler beside her. “Reverend,” she said, her voice hitting every speaker in the room. “What is happening?”

Ambrose had not moved from the altar. He was standing with his hands folded, looking pale but easily the calmest man there. “It appears,” he said, “to be God’s will.”

Nobody said anything to that. I’ve thought about it a lot since then. I think nobody said anything because everybody else believed he said the Truth.

With nothing else to do, I started counting. I could at least learn more about what was happening. A woman who’d been Dana’s coworker popped. One, two, three… Sixty-one, sixty-two, then Dana’s college friend Corbin popped. I kept counting.  
  
Next was Tyler’s cousin, a man whom I had never met but had been warned about repeatedly. He also popped about 60 seconds from Corbin. At the time, I felt like this wasn't very useful information. It just told me when to expect my own inevitable pop.  
  
I thought harder, did any of them have something in common? As more people popped, I struggled to remember who they were or even what they looked like. I didn’t know most of the people here. I couldn’t recall them doing anything that attracted my attention earlier.  
  
I looked at the other survivors and saw a lot of faces I recognized, even if I didn’t know their names. I slowly realized all the couples, the ones I’d hated an hour ago, all of them, were still there. Most were still holding each other with their now white-knuckled hands.

I yelled it out loud, “It’s the single people! They are all single!”

It took a second to spread but then it was like fire to dry grass. It was just a guess but all the couples did the math too and realized I might be right, then they instinctively flattened to the walls and away from the single people. I found myself standing in a rough circle full of people who kept popping every minute.

The next few minutes are the part I think about most, because that was when people showed who they were. They grabbed each other. Hands on arms, faces, collars. A man seized a woman he’d never met and shouted in her face, ““You and me. We’re together right? You’re my girlfriend! Right? Say it. SAY IT.” while she cried yes yes yes.   
  
Some of them were gentle. A lot of them were not. Two best friends from the back, Mike and John, who’d come together as friends, grabbed each other by the shoulders and said they were together too, laughing and sobbing at once, and held on.

A man in a rented suit that didn’t fit him was standing next to me, eyes huge, looking at me like he was waiting for a verdict. I didn’t know his name. I don’t think I ever will. “Boyfriend?” I said. “For now,” he said. “For now,” I said. We held hands. His palm was soaking wet.

There was a woman in a red dress who was grabbed by a short guy with glasses. “Ew, no,” she told him, loud enough for the room to hear. She shoved him off and ran down the aisle toward a tall handsome guy in a navy suit, arm out, mouth open to say it. She was three steps from him. She was smiling.

She went Pop.

The handsome man looked at his shoes, which were speckled pink, and then at the short guy in glasses, who had not been touched at all. The short guy in glasses had already found another woman to be his temporary girlfriend. She was holding him around the neck like a life preserver.

Others did nothing. I can’t blame them. It was a long shot but at least we could hope. One by one, the ones who did nothing popped. Then there was nobody “single” left. Everyone in that room had a hand in somebody else’s hand. We stood there, almost a hundred people in a chapel, staring at the ceiling.

I told them all it was about a minute between the explosions. Everyone counted silently. A minute passed and there was no popping. Then another minute. And another with nothing happening.

Someone started crying with relief and then everyone was crying. I didn’t even know most of the people who were gone and I was crying hard. The people who actually loved them, who actually had been excited to see them there were inconsolable.  
  
I looked at the man in the rented suit, whose name I still didn’t know, and he gave me a thumbs-up like we had just finished a group project. I’ll admit it. A little of me was thrilled. I was alive. I was alive in a green dress I hated and it was now soiled with pink that I couldn’t wait to throw it away but at least I was alive.

People went back to working the doors. A few switched partners, quietly, unhappy with the first person they’d grabbed. A divorced uncle of Tyler’s swapped a younger woman for an older one who’d turned out to be, he said, “less scared.” I watched Dana and Tyler walk up to Ambrose at the altar, still in their wedding clothes, and speak to him low and hard. Dana had her finger in his chest. Tyler wasn’t smiling. Ambrose just kept nodding, hands out, soothing. I remember wondering how he’d remained so calm. Whether he’d known beforehand that this would happen.

Then somebody found out they could damage the drywall.

It wasn’t much, a few scratches from the fire extinguisher, a chunk that came away in a man’s hands. But if the walls could be damaged, they weren’t part of whatever sealed and protected the doors and windows.   
  
Within a minute, seven men were tearing at the wall by the east window with keys and candlesticks and their bare hands. Dust everywhere. A hole the size of a dinner plate revealed a strip of studs.

That’s when Mike and John went pop.

The best friends. They’d been pressed into the corner, watching the wall, still holding hands. Mike’s mouth was open to say something. Then they were both gone. Just pink remained.

Nobody screamed this time. We all just went quiet, feeling sick to our stomaches. It wasn’t over.  
  
Another couple went next, one of the ones I had hated on earlier in the day. Then the short man and his new girlfriend popped too. My “boyfriend” quickly cornered me, “What’s going on, what do all of these ones have in common?”  
  
I blurted out the first thing I thought of, “They weren’t married couples!” 

It was Dana’s Blonde of Honor who moved first. She grabbed Dana’s elbow and tore her aside, almost knocking the bouquet out of her hand. Then she turned to Ambrose. “Marry us,” she said. “Marry me and Daniel. Right now.”

Ambrose’s smile broke open like a seam. It was the first time I’d seen it look real. “Of course,” he said. “Of course.”  
  
He married them even as another couple popped in the back. Everyone started pushing to form a line in front of the Reverend.

He went through them like a man at a drive-through. “Do you? You do? I do, I do, you’re married.” He didn’t even finish the words. There was a pop in the line and the next couple didn’t hesitate, they just stepped forward and stood in the pink aftermath.  
  
I tried to pull the man in the rented suit with me but he was busy scanning the room. I asked what he was looking for and, in response, he let go of my hand.

“Look,” he said, kind of embarrassed, but not very,  “I think I can do better.”

I didn’t get a chance to answer. He had already turned to a tall redhead in a silver dress, maybe twenty-two, standing next to a guy with a nice watch. He took her by the wrist and said something, pulled her away from the man with the watch, and marched her to the line. She didn’t fight it. She looked scared.   
  
The man with the watch looked angry for a moment but he didn’t chase after them. Instead, he cut further up the line, butting in to take another woman. This time, she pushed him back and her partner lined up a punch but only hit pink as the man popped.

I was alone again. I could feel it in my chest, like standing on the edge of a roof. I was going to pop soon.   
  
Someone touched my sleeve. A guy my age, brown hair, dust on his nose from the wall. I’d seen him earlier with his date, who’d left him fast to stand with her cousin. He looked at me, and then he said, slow and clear like he’d practiced it, “I would feel lucky on any day to marry you.”

I didn’t stop to think. I just said, “Okay, let’s do it.”

We got in line but here is where it got bad.

People had stopped being people. Some were using whatever was close: fists, heels, a hymnal, a shard of the chair. They weren’t trying to kill each other, I don’t think. They were trying to move each other. A woman clawed a man out of a spot near the front. A man threw a pew cushion over another man’s face and hung on.

One of the groomsmen grabbed an iron candelabra. His date had been walking toward the front with a different man, and he swung and hit that man in the head, once. A single sound I will hear for the rest of my life. The man dropped.

The groomsman stood over him, breathing hard, candelabra dripping. His date, the girl he’d been protecting, was staring at him. She was horrified. Then she backed away, found the nearest unmarried man, and said, “You. Come with me.”

The groomsman wasn’t done. He started running down the aisle, candelabra still in his hands, pleading with everyone and no one. “I’m not a bad guy! I’m not! You saw, he was taking her, I just, I’m not a bad guy!”

Pop.

The candelabra rang as it fell on the floor.

All the already married couples were still lining the walls, not wanting any part of it. I saw Dana and Tyler there. Somehow they had found a bottle of sacramental wine and were passing it between them. I thought at least they’ll be safe.  
  
But as more people went pop and the others fought, I saw them crying. I wished so hard that I could just make everything better for them. Instead, Dana revealed the microphone in her other hand and turned it on. I noticed they had ripped off the red string from their wrists when she brought the microphone to her lips.

“He told us,” she said, to the room drunkenly. Her mascara had run down her cheeks. “He said there was a ritual to bless our marriage, it was supposed to be good luck. You do it before. We did it before.” She waved her wrist. “He said it would protect us. We thought it was cute. We’re so sorry. We’re so sorry.”

The room went very quiet. In the line, in the aisle, on the floor, almost seventy pairs of eyes turned to Reverend Ambrose.

Ambrose didn’t deny anything. He stood at the altar, and just beckoned the next couple.

The married couples, including the ones he’d just joined, the ones who’d made it, flew at him with their hands and their shoes and the candlesticks. The couples still waiting in line tried to hold them back, shouting, “We need him! We need him! He has to marry us!”   
  
It turned into a pile. I couldn’t see the Reverend in it. I could hear him, though. I could hear him  laughing until he didn’t.

Then it was over. He was dead on the altar steps, and the people who’d done it stepped back with blood to their elbows, staring at each other. They’d killed a man. They’d done it together, and I’m sure they felt like they had won.

Pop.

A couple at the edge of the crowd. They’d been the next ones in line, to be married by Ambrose. Pink mist while his body was still warm. Nothing had been stopped.

I looked around at the people who were left and at the man beside me. There were maybe fifty of us. Half of them were in the line, and the line had just lost the man who ran it.

Someone shouted, “Is anyone else ordained? Anywhere? Any religion?” Nobody spoke up.

I had been watching Ambrose all day. I remembered how he’d said it. “I do.” Every time, he put a little weight on those words. He said them like a salesman says sign here. He’d said it to Dana, and he’d said it to Tyler, and he’d said it to every couple in the line before he skipped the rest of his spiel, and I realized he’d never stopped saying it.

I turned to the man next to me. “Trust me,” I said.

“Okay,” he said.

“Do you?” I said.

He looked at me. He had very dark eyes, and I could see him deciding. “I do,” he said.

“I do,” I said.

Nothing. No flash, no chime, no change in the room.

I shouted it to the others. “It’s the words! You don’t need him! Just say it! Say I do!” Some of them heard. A few couples near me grabbed hands and said it, scattered, trembling. The rest were too far away, too frightened, still shoving toward a dead man’s altar.

The ones who didn’t say it went pop until everyone had said it to everyone else. The pink was so thick in the air that I could taste it. It was sweet. I don’t know why that’s the part that still gets me.

And then it was quiet again. And again we thought it was over.

We waited. A hand squeezed mine until my fingers went numb. One minute. Two.

Pop. A couple by the wall. Married, the both of them.

The thing I hated most about this whole ordeal is that as much as I tried to figure out, I could never tell when I was right. I could only tell when I was wrong.

Everyone started guessing. Maybe it’s a quota. Maybe it’s a number. Maybe it only wants so many left. My grandparents, Ruth and Walt, who had been married fifty-eight years and who were sitting in the front pew because nobody could convince them to leave, listened to all of it with their hands knotted together.

“Maybe there's a quota,” Grandpa Walt said and Grandma Ruth nodded. They looked at each other. I watched them figure it out. I started to run toward them and Dana started to scream.

“We divorce,” Grandpa said, loud and clear. “I divorce you, Ruth.”

“And I divorce you, Walter,” said Grandma. She gave a little shrug, that’s that. “Go on, then.”

They didn’t let go of each other’s hands. They didn’t have to. Maybe six seconds later, they were gone. And somehow, I knew while they’d done exactly what they meant to do, that it hadn’t helped anybody.

Dana and I grabbed each other and made sounds I could only describe as a heart breaking.

We slumped to the floor but I never stopped trying to think as more pops sounded. I didn’t bother counting the seconds anymore, but I was sure it was speeding up. Instead, I focused again on who was going.

Uncle Rick, who’d slept with half the county. Gone. A man who’d been rumored to have two families. Gone. A woman from the groom’s side who had been seen kissing someone else’s husband in the parking lot. Gone. Couples who had been married by Ambrose, Gone, Gone, Gone.

The man in the rented suit, who I had held hands with an hour ago, was across the aisle with his silver-dress wife. I heard him say, to her, flat and cold, “I told you to stand there.” She said something back, and he said, “Don’t talk to me like that. I picked you. You should be grateful.” She was crying.

Pop. Both of them.

I saw his partner’s expression, just before. I think she was actually relieved to feel like it was about to happen.  
  
They’d said the right words, the ones popping now. They’d either gotten the ring, or the “I do” but now they were going and I couldn’t believe it was at random.

I said it to the man next to me, while shaking, “It’s not the words. Not only. It’s whether you mean them.”

He didn’t answer for a while. Then he said, very quietly, “I don’t know anything about you.”

“I know,” I said. I laughed, once, in a way that hurt. “I don’t know you either. I’m not going to lie to you. I don’t love you. I’ve known you for forty minutes.”

“Jamie,” he said. “It’s Jamie. In case that matters.”

“Okay. Jamie. I’m Dawn.” I had to say something true, and it came out stupid and slow. “I think what it, whatever it is, what it wants is, a good marriage.”  
  
“How can two strangers show that to each other? Especially under these conditions.”  
  
“I don’t know. I honestly don’t. But I want to try. I’ve never been married but even so, I think a good marriage just means that both people are trying.”

“I hear you,” said Jamie. His voice cracked a little. “I can try. I can promise to try and be the best husband for you.”

“I’ll try to be the best wife too,” I said.

We held hands, and I won’t pretend it wasn’t terrifying. Then he kissed me, or I kissed him, I honestly don’t know. It was a real kiss. It was awkward and salty and it went on a while, and beside us the couple who’d been kneeling there, an older man and his wife, burst into pink.

The mist hit us in the face. We didn’t let go.

After that there was nothing. We counted. We counted to five minutes, then ten. A groomsman said, “Is it…” and a woman said “Shh.” Nobody breathed.

But yeah, that was the end of it. The only people left in that church were the ones who actually meant it.

The doors could open again.

I mean they were just doors. Jamie put his hand on the handle, and it clicked, and it swung out into plain evening and the smell of cut grass. Someone behind us started to sob. There were twenty of us left. We all stood in the doorway not wanting to go first.

Between the drinking and crying, Dana couldn’t walk. Tyler could also barely stand. So Jamie took one of Tyler’s arms and I took Dana’s, and we walked the two of them down the church steps like people leaving a funeral. Dana’s head lay on my shoulder. “I’m sorry,” she kept saying. “I’m so sorry, I’m so sorry.” I said it wasn’t her fault and I think I still believe it.

The street in front of us was full. Too full. Many of the cars were open. The ones that weren’t had pink film on their windshields. A man’s bicycle lying on the sidewalk, covered with pink. A little farther, the light at Main and Fifth was green, but nothing moved through it.

It hadn’t been just the chapel. Whatever Ambrose had been doing, he’d been doing it all over the county, all day. Marrying people. “Blessing” them. Tying red thread over who knows how many wrists. I don’t know how many weddings that was so I don’t know how much of the county is left.

We drove into town in Jamie’s truck, with Dana and Tyler asleep in the back. We’ve found messages that survivors are to meet up at the local grocery store so we’re headed there now. I’m in the passenger seat typing this on my phone with the heat on and the radio off. I don’t have an explanation for the radio. It just doesn’t play.  
  
There is pink mist everywhere.

I like Jamie. I like him more than I’ve ever allowed myself to like anyone before. He’s looking half dead in the driver’s seat, he’s got drywall in his hair, and every few minutes I find myself looking at him and bracing. I can’t help it. I keep waiting for the pop. I keep asking myself, do I mean it. Do I mean it now. And now. And what about now.

I’m scared that someday the answer will be no, and I’ll find out the way everyone else did.

One more thing. Dana showed me her wrist an hour ago. The red thread is gone. But there’s a line where it was, thin and red, like a scratch, like it’s tied under the skin. It feels warm to the touch. I don't think it's truly over yet.

So, if you live in Pruitt County, say something. If you live anywhere else please tell me this didn’t travel any further than here. I don’t know how many people would be left if something like this went global.

And if you’re married or going to be, and you’re reading this, and you feel something tighten when you think about the vows you took or are writing, I don’t know what to tell you. Just be honest. Please, be honest.

And please, reach out. I’ll be checking my messages
