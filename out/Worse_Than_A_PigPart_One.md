There is a witch that lives in the woods. No one knows how long she’s been there, watching us from the darkness. All that is known is if you need something done, something no one can help you with, something you can’t bear to do yourself; you take a visit to the witch in the woods.

Despite this, it isn’t easy to find her. The witch likes her privacy. You could walk through the forest for days, and see no sign of her. You could camp out there for weeks only to be rewarded with a flash of silver hair, an animal carcass picked clean, or a cruel laugh that carries on the wind.

The ones that claim to have seen her say you need to offer her something. Not money, something else, something they refuse to say. The ones that actually met her disprove that; the witch chooses who to help. She has to want to help you. You can bring her all the riches you want, money, wine, deeds to land, but no matter what, she refused it all.

The couple struggling for a baby, she refused to help.

The man with a failing liver, she refused to help.

The woman losing her sight, refused.

The witch just slinked back into the shadows, wielding them like a cloak.

It wasn’t bitterness talking, each one had described the witch perfectly.

A small, elderly woman, barefoot, dressed in rags, with long silver hair that shone like silk in the moonlight.

The witch was a strange, fickle being. The few she did help were tight-lipped about it. No matter how many begged them, they refused to talk about their encounters. The man who regained his hearing ignores the pleas of the desperate.

It started a rumour, as all things do, that they were cursed. A rumour that the prize for help from a witch was far higher that anyone is willing to pay.

Soon, any mention of the word witch got you ostracised. A glance towards the forest earned you a disapproving scowl, any child caught playing close to the trees were clapped around the ear.

Time passed, the witch fell out of fashion, but not forgotten. I saw proof in the footprints in the snow, barefoot, and tinged red. No, the witch was still out there, and I was going to find her. It might be pointless, I know, but I had to try. I had no other choice.

I was alone for the weekend. There would be no better time for it.

And yet, as I stood at the mouth of the forest, staring into the endless black maw of shadows, I hesitated. If I went missing, things would get worse. If I did nothing, things would definitely get worse. I took a deep breath; the night air tinged with wet earth. Hitching up my backpack, I swallowed my fear, and took a step forwards, closer into the mouth of the beast.

The moment I entered the forest, I felt heavier, like a weighted blanket was thrown over me. The silence was dense, nothing, not even a bird was singing. It was strangely comforting. Twigs snapped like brittle bones beneath my boot. Guilt at breaking the tranquillity of the forest rose within me like bile. The apology already on my lips before I remembered I was alone; there was no one to apologise to. My weak, little ‘sorry’ fell to the floor like a dead bird.

I walked in a steady line, every crackle and snap that rang out had me flinching, irritating my already aching body. The wind picked up, rustling the trees in a low murmur, the new sound dogged my steps. My fingers went numb; my face had long since gone numb. The straps of my bag dug into my shoulders, I already wanted to sit down and rest, eyeing plenty of logs that would make comfortable seats. I refused to slow, even when the ache travelled down my legs, sharp, shooting pains spreading from my hips. I pushed on.

Despite the layers I was wearing, I couldn’t help shivering. It wasn’t just the chill of the wind; it was the eyes. I could feel eyes watching me from the dark, following my every move. I tried to ignore the feeling, when a thought struck me. My breath quickened, I stopped and turned to the darkness.

“E-Excuse me,” my whispered words were like a crack of thunder in the silent night,

“I-I’m sorry to intrude, I won’t stay long, I just need to speak with the witch, please.”

In the middle of the rustling bushes, something blinked. A pair of golden eyes stared out at me, inspecting me before silently retreating back into the darkness. Leaving me alone.

The black trees towered over me; the spindly branches curled towards the sky like claws. Through the few gaps, I saw a gloomy, overcast sky. It darkened with every pulse of my heart. Would I have to stay overnight? John wouldn’t like it, but I was prepared for it. The bulky tent I packed poked my back as I walked. Should I stop to set it up while there’s still light?

As I dithered, my steps slowing, I caught a glimpse of those golden eyes again, following me from the brush. I stopped, the eyes stopped too.

My mouth went dry, “H-Hello?”

The golden eyes blinked in response, before I could say anything else, the bush shivered and something jumped in front of my path. I jumped back with a yelp, hissing when it pulled on my injuries. On instinct, I covered my face. When nothing happened, I peered through my fingers.

A fox stood in front of me, its dark fur blending into the darkness around it. It blinked up at me, head cocked like a curious dog.

“Oh, um, hello?” I said, stupidly, I reached out my hand, perhaps to pet it but stopped when it flashed its fangs. I curled back into a ball, a stream of apologies spilling from my lips. The fangs flashed in my mind, sinking into my exposed flesh and ripping it apart. At this rate, I was going to run out of unblemished skin.

Instead, the fox gave a small yip, sharp enough to grab my attention. I peeked through my fingers again like a child. The fox yipped again, shifting from paw to paw. I think it wanted me to follow it.

Seeing no other option, that’s what I did. Its steps were quick and sure, weaving in and out of the brush, huffing when I struggled to follow. Despite all this, childish glee bubbled up inside me. I was following a forest animal just like a fairy tale. As I stumbled after it, tripping over roots, and branches striking me in the face. One struck me in the cheek, right over a fresh bruise blossoming over my eye. The impact brought a fresh wave of pain, but I swallowed it down. Sensing the golden eyes on me once again, I gave a nod to show I was still following. The fox hopped atop a log, whining when I struggled to do the same.

The further I ventured in; the smell of the wet earth grew overpowering. The scent clung to me like smoke. It had me coughing. Eventually, we reached a clearing, a perfect circle with a single mossy log. The fox bounded and hopped atop the log with a flourish. His bushy tail swishing to and fro like a gentle pendulum. I limped forward, confused, scanning the treeline for a sign of the witch.

Nothing.

Just trees, grass and the lone fox, his orange fur burning like fire. Wait, that wasn’t right.

Before I could dwell on it any further, it was gone. An orange flame swallowed up by the shadows. Was I supposed to follow him? No, the thorn bushes were far too thick. Too thick for any creature to pass through. I looked around, waiting for a sign or another yip, but there was just silence. A tense kind of silence that came with holding your breath. I sat on the log, the moss soft as a cushion. My muscles finally loosened. I hunched forward, resting my arms on my thighs. I looked down at my hands, the bitter wind had my fingers curling like that of an old woman. They twitched like one too. I sighed; the straps dug into my shoulders. I sighed again.

What was I doing here?

Visiting a witch to solve my problems, this wasn’t a fairy tale, no matter how much I wanted it to be.

I was going to shiver overnight in a dark forest, scuttle back before the sun rose, and wait for my boys to come home. And, nothing would change. I would have to deal with him. His gnashing teeth, and hands curled into fists.

I needed to find that witch.

A yip pulled me from my spiral, my head snapped round, expecting the fox again.

It wasn’t the fox.

Peering at me from around the log, inches from my knee, was a long, pale face. its eyes and mouth were black, stretching wide in a toothless smile. It stared up at me with curiosity. I shot up with a cry.

The pale face just watched me, a single slow blink of his eyes its only sign of life. Then, in a motion I can’t describe, the face floated up, bobbing like a balloon. Shadows curled around the face, falling from it like a cloak. I was expecting the thing to tower over me but the head stopped just below my chest.

“Alright, pet?” came a crackling voice. I blinked, in that brief moment, the face changed. It was an old woman, squat, wizened, her face swam with wrinkles, with black eyes almost hidden under thick folds of skin. She blinked up at me with marble-like eyes. The question didn’t register until a cold hand touched my shoulder. It was paper white, thin, spindly fingers curled around my shoulder, not gripping, just resting.

“Um, hello…,” I answered back, mouth dry, “Sorry to bother you.”

I had a feeling I knew who this was but didn’t want to assume lest I insult a random grandmother.

“Are you out here alone?” The voice asked me. The fingers on my shoulder were stained dark, the dirt under her nails like black semicircles. They gave off a strange odour.

The urge to lie was fleeting, “Yes, I’m alone.”

The old woman’s expression didn’t change. Her mouth looked jagged, like a crude cut made into a potato. It stretched wide, her few teeth yellow.

“Good, good,” her crooked smile didn’t falter, “I don’t like much company.”

A lump of fear rose in my throat,

“You’re…I mean, are you…?” I couldn’t bring myself to say the word. The old woman made a sound like broken glass.

“Yes, I am the witch.”

A surge of relief washed over me, but the fear remained. I passed the first hurdle, but would she help me?

I followed the elderly woman. She kept her hands outstretched, fingers trailing through rustling leaves. She took the time to touch each tree trunk, lips moving in a quiet prayer. I couldn’t be sure, it might have been the darkness, but the plants seemed to perk up as she passed. It had to be my imagination. With the moon right above us, the light glinted off the witch’s hair made it look like spun silver. We walked in silence; the crunching of our steps was the only sound. I don’t know how long we walked, nothing changed, every tree looked the same.

Then, rising out of the darkness was another tree. A behemoth of a tree. It was a yew tree; towering above the rest. The black branches spread out across the dark sky like black veins. The trunk was thick, thicker than an elephant’s leg. It was bare, dead, the base was a mass of knobbled roots. Like a bundle of ribcages woven together. Resembling the corpse of a decaying eldritch creature.

I assumed we would walk past it, instead, I collided with the old witch,

“Oh, sorry,” I steadied my hands, ready to catch a flailing woman, but it was like walking into a wall. She didn’t even budge. The witch stared at the monstrous tree, a hard look in her eyes. The fox was nowhere to be seen.

“Wait here,” she croaked.

Her bare feet crunched on the brittle roots, the sound brought tears to my eyes. The old woman didn’t even flinch. With each step forward, she got lower, I don’t mean her legs bent, she was descending, like going down a flight of stairs. Each step was a crackling crescendo, the thunder of a thousand, tiny bones. I watched quietly as she turned from a torso, into a floating, bobbing silver head that sank below the roots. After a moment, a pale hand poked back up, beckoning me to follow. I sighed, in for a penny, in for a pound.

Clenching my eyes shut, I stepped forward, I could feel the roots pulling at my clothes, tugging at the legs of my jeans. Descending down into a cavern of roots was, for lack of a better word, painful. It scratched every available patch of skin. hot drops of blood dripped down my face, one poked at my closed eye, but I didn’t care. This had to be a test to see if I was worthy or something.

My temple collided with a particularly sharp root, almost knocking me back on my ass. Hissing through the pain, I pushed on. Pain was something I was becoming used to.

Finally, after what seemed like a long, agonising hour of struggling through the tunnel of pain, it vanished. The roots released me and I was falling forward, getting a face full of dirt. The first thing I was aware of was the heat. Not the heat of a tree ablaze, this was the comforting warmth from a lit hearth. I looked up and was amazed at what I saw. It wasn’t a hovel, or a cave, far from it. It was like the inside of a homely cottage. It was warm and cosy, with a cauldron bubbling over a fire. The crackling flames cast everything in an orange glow, the shadows danced around her. Dried flowers hung from the dirt walls, their sweet scent mixing with the burning wood.  

The fire sat in a space carved into the dirt, a makeshift chimney. It had a strange smell, a strong, heady smell that made my head swim. The only pieces of furniture within were a table and two chairs, one occupied by the witch. The wood was dark and knobbly, like it had been buried underground for a thousand years. There were no windows, the only light came from the flickering hearth. The shadows twitched against the witch’s face; her wrinkles twitched like worms. She sat still as a statue, staring at nothing, then, her head slowly turned to me, eyes bright as burning coals.

“Come, child,” she poured something into two clay cups I hadn’t noticed before,

“Sit and tell me your story.”

She pushed a cup towards me, filled with a hot, amber liquid. Hesitantly, I reached out with trembling fingers, aching for the heat. It smelled malty yet sweet. I took a quick sip, burning my tongue,

“My story,” I gulped, “It’s a long one.”

The old witch made a clucking sound,

“Not as long as mine I imagine.”

The hand she used to pull at her shawl was covered in scars, white, silvery scars that no doubt each told a story. The urge to ask reared its head, but a flash of the witch’s dark eyes told me to get on with it. No changing the subject.

“Well,” I swallowed the lump in my throat, my throat dry despite the tangy tea, “The reason I’m here is because of my husband.”

The old witch hummed, “The doctor.”

It wasn’t a question. I shouldn’t have been surprised. Watching us from the dark, she must know every one of us.

“Yes. My husband, John, has been the town’s doctor for about 12 years now. He’s well respected, well liked, a pillar of the community.” The words felt hollow, like I was reading off a script.

The witch scoffed, “I didn’t let you in to brag, stop beating around the bush and tell me.”

She picked a dry berry off the table, popping it into her mouth. She chewed with her mouth open, teeth and tongue turning purple. I watched, the wet slobbery sound of her tongue running over her lips sickened me. That, along with the pain that refused to fade, sparked something within me.

“Well, how do I know I can trust you?” I practically spat,

“How do I know you can even help?”

The witch looked at me, dark eyes glinting like a knife. I wanted to cower but the heat curling in my belly grew into a flickering flame.

“You didn’t help the Martins with their infertility!” My voice rose as I spoke.

“You didn’t help old Joseph with his bad liver!” It was the loudest I had ever spoken to someone.

“You didn’t help Kerri-Ann fix her eyes! You’re probably just some crazy woman living in the woods!”

The witch just looked at me, unfazed. A hand went to my mouth, as if I could take back the words I said. That was it. I blew my chance. I set the cup down with a clatter and got ready to push back through the undergrowth, but something stopped me. A little angry ball of fur. The fox who led me here, dark again, its red lips pulled back into a snarl. I cowered back, hiding behind the chair. The fox advanced, the witch clicked her tongue, and it stopped. The witch sipped from her tea, gesturing me to do the same.

“I have my reasons for not helping them,” her voice had lowered to a rasp,

“W-Which is?” I swallowed another burning mouthful,

“They didn’t deserve my help.”

“Why?” I raised my voice, ignoring the yip of the fox. “Tell me why?!”

The witch set her cup down with a clatter, hard enough to crack the clay. She looked me up and down, a strange vibration of power radiating from her small frame. I sat back heavily in my chair, skin crawling under the witch’s scrutinous gaze.

“The Martins were unfit to be parents.”

“And how could you know that?”

“I saw it,” she leaned forward, bones creaking like an old tree, “With my eyes.”

I scoffed, but the witch continued.

“If I had given them a child, they would have sent it to an early grave.”

“The couple would have treated it like a toy, a toy to suit their own needs, to show them off to friends. A child is supposed to be an individual; to grow into its own person, the Martins merely wanted a copy of themselves.”

“The child would revolt, bend away from their parents’ impossible standards. They would wear the ‘wrong’ thing, say the ‘wrong’ thing, love the ‘wrong’ person. The Martins would ruin their child, grind them into nothing with their unrealistic expectations. They child will take their life, and the Martins would just milk their death for sympathy.”

She said it all as a matter of fact, gesturing to her old eyes,

“I have seen it. Every possibility, every outcome of a potential Martin child, and they would destroy it. So, yes, I refused to help. They do not deserve a child. They can cry all they want; it is only crocodile tears.”

I look down at my hands, I had no idea. The Martins seemed like a fine couple, always fawning over my son when he was baby. Then again, as he grew older, I saw disinterest festering in their eyes.

“Well, what about old Joseph?”

“He’s a drunk.”

“What?” I finally looked back up at the witch. “He was a teacher; he never stepped foot inside a pub.”

The witch looked at me over the rim of her cup,  

“He drinks at home,” she said with the tone of a disappointed parent “He forces his children to buy his whiskey, too much of a coward to show how far he’s fallen.”

She tutted, the sound like a clucking hen,

“He drank until his liver rotted black. He has no intent on fixing himself, even after his children cried for him to stop, he gulps down his poison like a gasping fish. I will not waste my powers on him.”

I hadn’t seen him in a while. No one had.

“And-And, Kerri-Ann?” I was scared to ask, but I had to know. I had to get rid of any doubt.

The witch’s face soured, puckering like a rotten apple,

“She witnessed a terrible crime. She watched a woman being attacked from her bedroom window. She was the only witness, and said nothing.”

More shadows flickered around the witch, like the crackling fire,

“She had plenty of opportunities to do so, and didn’t. Her fading vision is retribution. She will never get her sight back.”

Something in the witch’s expression irked me, she almost looked smug. I was sick of people looking smug.

“And, what gives you the right to judge?”

In the span of a heartbeat, the witch was out of her chair, towering over me. She was changing before my eyes. Her limbs grew longer, her pale face shrouded in darkness, with a new red glint in her eyes. I turned away, her face was becoming hard to look at.

“You come into my domain,” her voice now a low growl, “And doubt my actions?”

I kept my face turned, breathing hard. Then, bony fingers grabbed my face, forcing me to look into her ghoulish eyes. Her once matronly face twisted into that of cruel hag. The perfect image of a fairy tale witch.

“Who I choose to help is none of your concern. If you’re here to judge, get out.”

She never raised her voice; she didn’t have to. It carried like the promise of thunder in the wind. The witch stepped back, still unnaturally tall, her ghoulish face blurred as tears burned my eyes. I fell to my knees, this was my last chance, if I left now, I would never find the witch again. Sharp stones dug into my skin; I leaned into the pain.

“No, please,” the words spilled from my mouth like my tears,

“I’m sorry, please forgive me. I just-,” my voice hitched without warning,

“I just-I don’t know what to do.”

Even with my head bowed, I could feel the old woman’s gaze boring into me.

“I need help, my husband, he-,”my cheek throbbed as a reminder, like my body was trained to expect pain whenever I thought of him.

“He’s-He isn’t a good man.”

It felt wrong to admit it. My trembling hand instinctively went to my mouth. John was a doctor, a good one. He treated his patients with respect, treated the children like his own, always ready to help no matter the time.

His dimpled smile was what first attracted me to him, that and his blond hair, though it had receded in the years I knew him, it hadn’t dulled my love for him. He was tall, gentle, everything a woman could ask for.

So, why was I here?

I tried to explain myself. I kept breathing, trying to force the words out. All that came out was heaving sobs, sobs that shook my body. The witch tutted, I heard her rustling fabric, I tensed, thinking she was going to grab me by the scruff of the neck, or curse me.

Instead, I looked up, vision still blurry, to see her hunched over the cauldron. As it bubbled over, the witch added nettles, and with a quick nip of her teeth, a drop of blood. Whatever was in the cauldron, it turned a sickening shade of murky brown. She scooped out a bowl, bringing it up to breathe in the steam. It smelled like a wet spoon in a stagnant pond. As the lumpy mixture cooled, the witch dipped her wrinkled fingers, and smeared it over her bare eyes. Thick tears streamed from her painted eyes, filling her deep wrinkles like rivers. My own eyes stung with sympathy. The witch refused to blink, her eyes bulged out of her head as she strained to see what she needed. Through the glassy sheen of tears, her eyes focused,

“I see you.”

Before I could ask, a blinding pain struck me behind the eyes.
