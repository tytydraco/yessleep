When I saw the spider on my hand, I nearly ran my car off the road. Thank God that I didn’t, it’s a company vehicle. I hadn’t even felt it, I just noticed it out of the corner of my eye while I was driving. Without thinking, I immediately swiped it off my hand, slammed on the brakes, threw the car into park and jumped out into the road. Again, I’m thankful that it was a rural backroad with no traffic — I hadn’t even looked behind me before stopping the car. I looked at the palm of my right hand, hoping to see bug juice leftover from a successful squishing. Unfortunately, there was only sweat, which could mean only one thing: the spider was still alive. I threw off my sweatshirt and examined my pants, but couldn’t find it. The spider must have still been in the car.

I carefully opened the driver’s side door and looked around. If it was still in the car, it wouldn’t be hard to find. It had been as large as my hand and had a magnificently blue abdomen with horribly hairy legs. I looked high and low in the car but couldn’t find where it was hiding. After ten minutes of this, I concluded that I must have imagined it. After all, I had only really seen it for a single moment out of the corner of my eye, and I reacted to it so quickly and recklessly that I couldn’t really be sure that I had seen anything at all. I convinced myself that it must have just been shadows from the trees around me, and maybe a reflection of the sky through the windshield, that had created the illusion of a spider. I got back into the driver’s seat and continued on to my destination, trying to ignore the near constant sensation that something was still crawling on me.

I work for an electric company as a “Collections Agent”. Basically, I deliver notices on past-due bills and shut off the power for people who refuse to pay. It’s a thankless job, and uncomfortable at times, but the pay was decent enough. “Our number one concern is safety”, I was told. If there was a mean looking dog on the porch or something just didn’t feel right, I was supposed to just back off and go on to the next house. After what’s happened to me, I wish that I had taken my training more seriously.

On my way to the next house after my encounter with the hallucinatory spider, the GPS in my phone started going haywire. This wasn’t unusual when I was in the more rural parts of my route, so I started paying closer attention to the road, looking for signs to guide me. I realized that I had no idea where I was, this must have been some area of my territory that I had never been in before. Trees loomed above me on every side, and dark clouds drifted in front of the sun. Eventually, after flying blind for a while in the gloom, I found a mailbox with the house number I was looking for. Beside it was the entrance to the driveway, which was totally destroyed. Huge chunks of asphalt toppled over on top of each other. My car could handle most things, but not this.

I felt that I should maybe just skip this one and move on to the next, but my stubbornness got the better of me. I parked my car on the side of the road and ascended the driveway on foot. I walked up a big hill surrounded by trees and foliage, passing several broken down cars along the way. I thought to myself that there was no way that this place would be inhabited when I got there, the only reason that the power was still on was because my coworkers didn’t want to come up to the place on foot to deliver the shutoff paperwork.

When I finally made it to the house, I was out of breath, and could barely believe my eyes. The place was horribly dilapidated and literally sinking into the ground, tilted slightly sideways. The driveway stopped abruptly and revealed that I was at the edge of a cliff, where once a wooden deck connected it to the second floor of the house. I could still jump across a small gap to get to the deck, but that was way outside of my job description. Instead of knocking on the door, I cupped my hands around my mouth and hollered “hello” several times.

As expected, I got no response. I chuckled to myself about what a waste of time it had been to walk up there, and threw the shutoff paperwork onto the wood in front of me. A moment after turning around to head back to the car, I heard it: the soft voice of an elderly woman saying “help”.

At first, I thought that I had imagined that as well. I yelled “hello” toward the house again, and to my surprise I heard the voice again as well: “help.”

With a loud voice, I asked where she was and what she needed help with.

“I’m here, in the house, I can’t move! Please, help me!”

I should have just called 911 and waited for trained professionals to come and help her. Instead, I let my foolish ego and desire to be some kind of hero get the better of me. I told the woman that I was coming to help, and leaped onto the deck.

This was an idiotic thing to do, and I realized that quickly  when I heard the wood creak and groan beneath me as I landed. Thankfully, the boards didn’t give out on me, and I was able to make it in the door.

The house looked exactly as I imagined it upon my first impression: totally abandoned. Dusty furniture and appliances from decades ago. A musty smell of mold in the air. Droppings from some rodent dotting the floor and counters. Cobwebs in every corner. I called out to the woman again after I had inspected the second floor and found no trace of her. Later, I would consider how odd it was that she hadn’t made a sound that whole time.

“I’m in the basement! Please, come quickly!”

I descended the carpeted stairs to the first floor. It was more of the same, but I began to notice the distinct smell of decay. Like roadkill that’s been laying outside for weeks. I didn’t mean to look around that floor much, but I was having trouble finding the door to the basement steps. By chance, I opened a bedroom door and found a horrific scene: the huddled and emaciated corpse of a man lying on shag carpet, covered in cobwebs from what I assumed must have been maybe a year of rotting. I gasped, shut the door, and remembered why I was there: to help the old woman. As I continued to search for the entrance to the basement steps, I began to piece together in my mind what must have happened: the man in the bedroom was this lady’s husband, son, or some other caretaker, who had dropped dead suddenly and left her abandoned in the basement. How long had she been down there? I finally found the door, but more questions pressed on my mind: why did the entire house look so decrepit and uninhabited, and how long could that elderly woman possibly have been stuck down there?

I descended the creaky wooden basement steps, brushing spiderwebs out of my face the whole time, as I approached complete darkness at the bottom. How horrible must it have been for this woman to get stuck down here in the dark, I thought. I reached for my phone and turned the flashlight on when I finally reached the concrete floor. There was nothing much to see, some empty shelves and some more dusty furniture. The amount of cobwebs was really something, it looked like a spider habitat. My skin crawled as the woman called out to me from my right: “over here!”

I turned and saw an incredibly old woman, hunched over in a wheelchair, with a blanket covering her legs and feet. One of the wheels was flat, which I assumed was why she said that she couldn’t move. I asked her how long she had been stuck down there.

“I’ve been down here a long, long time. Thank you so much for coming. Can you please help me?”

I wanted to ask how she had gotten into the basement in the first place if she was in a wheelchair, but I felt that it may not have been appropriate to ask. Instead, I asked how I could help.

“I have a spare wheel somewhere around here. Could you pick me up and set me on the ground so that you can replace the wheel? I’m very light, I haven’t eaten in so long. Please help me.”

The idea of picking up the old woman made me uncomfortable, but I pushed through that feeling and agreed. I set my phone down on the ground, approached the woman, and picked her up.

The first thing that I noticed was that she was, like she said, very light. The second thing that I noticed was another dead man, nearly mummified, encased in spiderwebs behind the wheelchair. In the moment that I was processing this, I noticed a third thing: the woman had no legs. No legs? She had something dangling beneath her torso, something moving and squirming now, several of them, but they weren’t human legs.

“I’m so hungry,” she whispered into my ear.

I screamed and threw the woman to the ground. She stood up on eight spindly spider legs. The blanket was gone now, revealing her blue abdomen.

I ran toward the steps as she crawled onto the wall and dashed toward me. A thick web caught my foot and I smashed my head off of the wood. I felt her teeth sink into my leg as I scrambled to get away. I must have given myself a concussion, or maybe it was blood loss, because I can barely even remember how I managed to escape. I think I found the exit on the first floor, ran through the woods, and drove my car to the hospital. At least, that’s what makes the most sense. They say that I drove myself.

I woke up at some point the next day in a hospital bed. My leg was killing me. The doctor came by and told me that I had been bitten by what he thought was a dog. In his opinion, it appeared to be more like a very large bat bite than any dog bite he had ever seen, but there were no bats large enough to do that to me. He asked me where I had been when it happened, and I told him the address. He wanted to call animal control about the dog, or whatever it was that had bitten me, but he couldn’t find the address anywhere. It didn’t exist.

Later, a friendly young nurse told me that she liked my tattoo. I thought that she was playing some sort of joke on me. I told her that I don’t have any tattoos.

“What do you mean? You have one on your left hand. It looks like you’ve had it for a long time.”

I turned my head to my left and saw, on the back of my left hand, the faded image of a spider with a magnificently blue abdomen and horribly hairy legs.
