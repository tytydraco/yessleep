My husband and I moved to a remote village on the edge of the forest for a single reason that sounded deeply romantic on paper: we wanted to slow down time. After five years of living in a cramped apartment where the windows rattled every time a subway train passed underneath, Ethan—my husband—suggested we buy an old house in Alderwood. He is an antique furniture restorer, and that two-story wooden cabin at the very end of the village road offered a spacious workshop for a price so absurdly cheap it felt unreal. For me, as a freelance book illustrator who only needed a drawing table and good morning light, moving anywhere was never a problem.

The first day we arrived in October, the air felt as though it seeped straight into our bones. Alderwood sat at the bottom of a mist-shrouded valley hemmed in by towering pine trees, while along its eastern border stretched Blackwater Lake—a freshwater lake whose surface was so dark and still it looked like a massive slab of black glass. Our cell service died completely the moment we crossed the stone bridge at the entrance of town, leaving us reliant on a sluggish satellite dish mounted to the cabin roof just so I could send out my illustration files for work. Ethan simply laughed happily and tossed his phone into the glove compartment. He said this was the exact kind of quiet that allowed a person to hear their own thoughts again.

However, it took less than twenty-four hours for me to realize that the quiet in Alderwood felt slightly... off. Not because the place was desolate, but because all of our neighbors shared habits far too uniform to be dismissed as mere coincidence. That morning, as I stood on the front porch sipping hot coffee and watching the mist roll down from the treeline, I saw Old Martha—our neighbor to the left—kneeling on the wooden planks of her own porch. She wasn't gardening or scrubbing moss, she was holding a small bucket filled with thick white paste and a wooden ruler, furiously packing a hairline crack between the floorboards with stiff, hurried movements, as if her life depended on sealing a gap no wider than a single strand of hair.

The moment she realized I was watching her, Old Martha's hands froze. She didn't smile or wave a greeting like a normal neighbor would. Her pale eyes swept straight past my shoulder—staring intently toward the corner of our wooden siding before dropping down to the gap beneath the back door frame visible from the side of the house.

In a raspy voice nearly swallowed by the cold breeze blowing off the lake, she muttered quietly without ever meeting my eyes, "You haven't sealed the gap beneath your back door frame yet, miss. And that split in the side porch planks... don't leave it open."

I blinked, caught slightly off guard. "Oh, thank you for the heads-up, ma'am. My husband actually plans on fixing some of the weathered wood around here this week."

Old Martha finally turned her head, looking at me with an expression that wasn't anger, but rather a unsettling mix of anxiety and pity. "Not this week. Do it before four-thirty this afternoon. Before the shadows of the pines touch your walls."

Without waiting for my reply, she dropped back to her knees, scooped up more of that sharp-smelling white paste—something like a mixture of lime and wood ash—and resumed her work at a frantic pace, like someone racing against a ticking clock.

The front door creaked open behind me, followed by the heavy sound of Ethan stepping out with two fresh mugs of coffee. He watched Old Martha still meticulously leveling the surface of her porch boards with her wooden ruler, then looked over at me with a raised eyebrow.

"Everything okay, Clara? You look pretty serious," Ethan asked softly.

I let out a small breath, trying to shake off the strange chill that had crept up the back of my neck. "Nothing really. Old Martha just warned me about the gap under the back door and the split in the side porch. She said we need to seal them before four-thirty this afternoon."

Ethan paused for a moment, watching the old woman's nimble hands smear the chalky paste into every wooden seam. Instead of finding it bizarre, my husband chuckled knowingly as he handed one of the mugs to me.

"Makes sense, honestly," Ethan said lightly. "We're sitting in a valley boxed in by a lake and dense pines, Clara. The second the sun drops behind that ridge, the temperature is going to plummet near freezing. Old folks out in remote places like this get obsessive about sealing drafts so their heating bills don't skyrocket. I'll grab some wood putty from the workshop later this afternoon and take care of it."

Ethan's explanation sounded so grounded and logical that the unease I had felt moments earlier evaporated completely. I smiled, took a sip of my coffee, and decided not to give it another thought.

By midday, the sun finally cleared the tops of the pine ridge, burning away the lingering morning fog from Alderwood's main road. Ethan and I spent the next few hours unpacking moving boxes and inspecting every corner of our new home. Yet the closer I examined this two-story building, the more peculiar details I uncovered—details that had never shown up in the online auction photos we'd browsed last month.

The house had previously belonged to a middle-aged couple named Thomas and Evelyn Callahan. According to the real estate liquidator back in the city, they had vanished four years ago without filing any forwarding paperwork, leading the county to seize the property for unpaid back taxes and auction it off for a sum far too low to pass up. Still, the interior didn't look like a place that had simply been abandoned in passing.

While wiping down the kitchen and living room, I noticed that every single mirror in the house had been removed. On the upstairs bathroom wall, in the main hallway, even on the inside of the master bedroom closet door, all that remained were empty nail holes and rectangular patches of paint slightly brighter than the surrounding walls. It looked as though the previous owners had hastily pried every reflective surface off the walls before leaving.

And that wasn't all. When I tried to push open the window in my downstairs study to let some fresh air in, my fingers brushed against a layer of yellowish wax hardened along the frame's groove. Beeswax. Someone had been periodically sealing the edges of these windows with soft wax. Meanwhile, the ventilation grates in the bathroom and basement walls were fitted with triple-layered copper wire mesh woven so tightly the holes were barely the size of a pinprick, their frames crusted over with a rough white residue like dried salt.

"The previous owners must have been overly sensitive to bugs and cold drafts," I murmured to myself as I wiped down the windowsill.

At two in the afternoon, our stomachs began to growl, and the groceries we'd brought from the city were running low. Since we also needed a new mirror for the bathroom along with some cleaning supplies, Ethan and I decided to walk down to the center of town to visit Alderwood's sole general store: Miller's General Store.

Along the gravel footpath leading into the village center, the atmosphere was quiet yet orderly. The wooden houses sat far apart without high fences, and across every backyard bordering the pine forest, I noticed neat rows of river stones painted stark white, stacked to an exact, uniform height. In front of one house, a middle-aged man in a flannel jacket was crouched down with a wooden yardstick in hand, measuring the height of his white stone pile with a dead-serious expression.

Arthur Miller's general store occupied an aging timber building with peeling paint across its wooden sign. The moment we pushed the front door open and the small bell above the frame chimed, the scent of aged wood, dried herbs, and chalk greeted us.

The interior of the store was dim, lit only by a few low-wattage hanging bulbs. Standing behind a long wooden counter was a man in his sixties with thinning gray hair and reading glasses perched on the tip of his nose. He had been writing in a leather-bound ledger when we walked in.

"Good afternoon," I greeted warmly as I stepped toward the dry goods aisle. "We're the new folks living at the east end of the road."

The man looked up slowly. His gaze settled on me before shifting over to Ethan beside me. There was none of the warm hospitality you'd usually expect in a small town. His expression was flat, bordering on exhausted.

"Arthur Miller," he said curtly. "You bought the old Callahan place."

"That's right," Ethan replied, setting a few cans of soup, a loaf of bread, and a box of candles onto the counter. "It's an unbelievably peaceful spot. By the way, Mr. Miller, do you happen to sell wall mirrors here? The previous owners took every mirror in the house with them."

At the mention of the word mirror, Arthur's hand froze mid-motion while tallying our items. He set his pencil down on the wooden counter with a soft tap that echoed sharply in the quiet room. For the first time, I took a closer look around the store. Tucked into the corners were several household items draped tightly in thick, dark burlap—rectangular frames shaped like wall mirrors, polished metal pots, and even the glass display case beside the counter had its front pane covered with opaque brown paper.

"We don't sell reflective glass in Alderwood, Mr. Vance," Arthur said, his voice dropping half an octave. "And if I might offer some advice to newcomers, you shouldn't bother ordering any from the city, either."

Ethan frowned, caught off guard by the older man's grave tone. "Why is that? We just need one small mirror for shaving and getting ready in the bathroom."

Arthur didn't answer right away. His eyes flicked up to the round wall clock behind the counter. The hands read 2:48 PM.

"Out here, glass doesn't just reflect whatever stands in front of it," Arthur murmured quietly, almost as if speaking to himself. He packed our groceries into a brown paper bag with brisk, practiced movements and slid it toward Ethan. "You ought to head home now. It's almost three o'clock. If you're truly set on staying in that house at the east end, make sure every crack in your walls is sealed before the shadows of the pines reach your porch."

Before Ethan could ask what he meant, the bell above the shop door chimed again. Two villagers hurried inside, heading straight toward a shelf stacked with heavy bags of gray salt and blocks of beeswax without sparing us a single glance.

Sensing the sudden, suffocating tension in the room, I lightly touched Ethan's arm, signaling that we should leave.

The moment we stepped back out onto the main road at exactly three in the afternoon, the sight waiting for us made my steps slow down. The street, which had seen a few pedestrians earlier, was now completely deserted. From the wooden houses lining the road, the sound of shutters being pulled tight echoed almost in unison, followed by the metallic click of deadbolts sliding shut from the inside. Heavy dark curtains were drawn across windowpanes one by one, transforming what had looked like a quaint village into a row of silent wooden boxes sealing themselves off from the outside world.

"They're certainly strict about their afternoon quiet hours," Ethan muttered softly, though this time his voice carried a faint note of hesitation.

We arrived back home around half past three. The sun was dipping low behind the pine ridge, stretching the shadows of the trees until they crept right up to the edge of our backyard—directly beside the neat row of white river stones belonging to the Bennetts, our neighbors to the right.

Since the air inside felt stale after being shut up all day, I headed into my downstairs study to unpack my drawing supplies and parted the curtains slightly to let the remaining afternoon light fall across my desk. Through the study window, I could see the wide expanse of our backyard bordering the dark wall of pine trees, and beyond that, the surface of Blackwater Lake already beginning to gather a thin veil of gray mist.

"I'm going to take a quick walk down to the lakeshore before it gets dark, Clara!" Ethan called out from the kitchen. "Want to check out the timber along the water's edge. Won't be long!"

"Don't stay out too late, Ethan! It's already getting freezing out there!" I called back without looking up from my stack of sketchbooks.

The faint sound of the back door opening and shutting echoed from the kitchen. I stayed absorbed in organizing my brushes, ink bottles, and drawing paper, completely losing track of how quickly the light outside the window was fading. In less than forty minutes, the sky turned a heavy, bruised gray, and the mist rising off the lake began creeping past the pine treeline like a cold curtain of white smoke.

I glanced at the wall clock in my study. 4:25 PM.

Remembering Old Martha's and Arthur Miller's warnings about four-thirty, I walked into the living room to switch on the standing lamp. Just as the warm light flickered on, footsteps sounded on the back porch, followed by the creak of the kitchen door opening.

Ethan stepped into the living room, the cuffs of his jeans damp from the grass and his cheeks flushed red from the cold air. Yet he wore a wide grin—the satisfied smile of a craftsman who had just stumbled upon buried treasure. Cradled in his arm was a piece of aged driftwood nearly two feet long, twisted into a striking natural curve with smooth, silvery-gray grain, while his left hand held three remarkably smooth white river stones.

"Look what I found right on the shore of Blackwater Lake," Ethan said enthusiastically, walking over to the wooden coffee table in the center of the room. He set the driftwood and the three lake stones directly onto the tabletop. "This wood has been submerged so long the grain has hardened practically like stone. Once I dry it out for a few days and polish it up, it's going to make the best mantelpiece for our fireplace."

I stared down at the damp piece of driftwood. For some reason, the cold, faintly metallic scent of lake water immediately drifted through the living room the moment the wood touched the table.

"It really has a great shape," I said, offering a faint smile as I tried to match his excitement. "By the way, you didn't forget to seal the gap under the back door like Old Martha mentioned this morning, did you?"

Ethan tapped his forehead lightly with a small laugh. "Ah, shoot, I forgot to dig the putty out of my toolbox in the workshop. It's already too dark now—I'll take care of it first thing tomorrow morning. Besides, the back door is locked tight; no draft is getting through."

I nodded, choosing to trust him.

Yet as I walked into the kitchen to pour myself a glass of water before heading upstairs, my steps came to an abrupt halt in front of the kitchen window, whose curtains I hadn't gotten around to drawing shut. Outside, darkness had swallowed the entire backyard. The white boundary stones marking the edge of our property were no longer visible beneath the thick wall of mist that had crept forward—stopping just a few yards from the wooden walls of our house, as if something inside the fog had just found its way home.
