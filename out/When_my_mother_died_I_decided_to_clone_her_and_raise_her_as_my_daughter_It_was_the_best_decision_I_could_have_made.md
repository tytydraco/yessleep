I knew my mother was dying. I’d known for a long time. Countless nights were spent crying, reminiscing, and trying to spend as much time as possible with her. But I truly realized that her time was coming to an end on a September night as I sat next to her, clutching her frail hands. They used to be so strong. Those were the hands that would pick me up after I fell as a toddler; the hands that would let me lick the batter off the spoon when she baked; the hands that would press against my forehead to check for a fever when I didn’t feel well. And now, her fragile hands were in mine as she stared at me with cloudy eyes. 

It took everything in me to bite back the tears and return her gaze with a smile. I couldn’t lose her. She was my everything, my world. But even in those desperate moments, I knew there was nothing I could do to get my mom’s youth back. So I did the next best thing. I cloned her. 

I wanted to pay her back and give her the life she deserved. My mother rarely talked about her childhood, but through bits and pieces, I could tell that it had been hard for her. She had done so much to raise me with the proper values and wisdom, and I felt that I owed everything to her. So to pay her back, I would remake her childhood and give her what she truly deserved. I was determined to forge her not just into the woman she had become, but an even better version. A happier Gwen. A healthier Gwen. 

I won’t disclose the details of the company here because I don’t want them getting in trouble, but the process was seamless. All I had to do was pay in cash and send her body to the lab. Once they were done collecting DNA samples, they would cremate her and send me her ashes. After a bit of waiting, I would get my mother back, except I would be the one raising her as my own.  

When Little Gwen first arrived, I was nervously arranging the house. I wasn’t sure why, she was just a baby after all, but my mother would clean before every guest no matter what. Even as I grew older and my visits grew less frequent, she would always do a deep clean before I came home.

My heart leapt with joy when the man knocked on the door and handed me my Gwen, still swaddled in a light pink blanket. She looked so much like the faded baby photos I had seen of my mother decades ago. A tear rolled down my cheek as I looked into her eyes, the same dusty blue that I remembered before the glaucoma and the cataracts took away its vibrance. She was beautiful. No. She was more than beautiful. She was my mother.

From that day forward, I dedicated my life to raising Gwen the way she raised me. Blood, sweat, and tears were put into math homework, swim practice, chess lessons, and writing. I wanted her to achieve greatness. She would be everything my mother wanted to be in her past life. I would make sure of it. 

It wasn’t always easy, especially as Gwen grew older. She was around 12 when we began fighting more. Every day, she would look more like my mother but act less like her. She put the books away and spent more time borrowing my phone. Instead of swimming practice, she would talk about how all her friends were on the soccer team. The extra math problems Gwen would solve when she was younger were greeted with grumbles and complaints. It was so unlike her. My mother had a deep passion for math that carried her into a career as a mathematics professor. She loved swimming ever since she first learned how to swim. Even in her later years, laps in the pool offered her weak joints some much needed relief. And reading was what had turned her into such a well-spoken woman. My mother loved nonfiction. The few books Little Gwen did read were usually fantasy, filling her head with unnecessary distractions. 

She was Gwen. She had the flesh and blood of my mother. They had the same brain, the same heart, the same hands and eyes. So why were their souls so different?

The first night Gwen and I had a real fight was when she insisted on switching to soccer. I had been reading a magazine at the table when she approached me, homework in hand. She usually had me review her homework and check for errors. Especially after her chess lessons, she was prone to making simple mistakes. 

Gwen slid the paper towards me before asking, “Mom, would you consider switching me to soccer instead of swimming at school?”

“Why soccer?” I asked, puzzled. “You’ve always loved swimming.”

“All my friends are in soccer. Plus, it looks like fun.”

I set the magazine down on the table and looked at her. “Sometimes, we need to dedicate ourselves to one thing at a time. It’s important to have the discipline to do things, even when they grow hard.”

“I don’t want to quit because it’s hard. I just don’t like it.” She sounded annoyed. 

“You’re not quitting. Sticking to swimming will help you later in life.”

“I hate swimming!” Gwen cried out. “It’s boring and the people on the team are weird. Stop acting like you know more than me.”

“I do know more than you.”

“No you don’t. You don’t know me. You just expect me to do whatever you want,” she shouted. I hoped the neighbors weren’t able to hear. 

Agitated, I replied, “I’m doing this *because* I know you. You’ll thank me later.”

Tears welled in Gwen’s eyes as she glared at me before stomping off to her room and slamming the door shut. 



I scoffed at the idea of my mother playing soccer. She couldn’t care less about it. Gwen only wanted to join because her friends were there. How selfish. She might not have realized how good swimming was for her, but she knew how much it meant to me. How could she treat me like this?

As it got closer to midnight, guilt began to eat at me. Gwen might have acted differently, but she was all I had left of my mother. I didn’t want to hurt her. She just wasn’t acting like herself. I knew her. I had known her for fifty years. I knew her likes and dislikes. I knew what helped her sleep at night. I knew how talented she was. I knew how much she committed herself to a challenge. Gwen should have known that I knew her best. 

But she was still growing, just like my mother once did. Everybody went through puberty. Everybody wanted to try new things. Everybody got into arguments. It wasn’t like I didn’t expect these fights, I just never really prepared for them. She would just have to trust me. She should have known that I knew her best. Gwen just needed to stick with it. She had the potential for greatness that my mother could only dream of. Everybody said it. Coaches, teachers, even parents of her friends. She was a star in everything she did. So why was she trying to push away the very things that made her great?

The question kept repeating in my head, so I told myself that she was just being foolish, that it was the hormones talking. But I knew I’d hurt her and I didn’t want to lose her twice. They might act completely different right now, but she was all I had left of my mom. 

I crept to her bedroom and opened the door just a crack to see if she was still awake. Gwen’s chest rose and fell steadily, so I pushed it open even farther. She looked so much like my mom that it hurt. Every day, I would see my mother in her more than the day before. She looked healthier though, with fuller cheeks and twinkling eyes. It was what my mother should have looked like in her youth. I took another step closer, my eyes watering. The familiar pang in my chest had returned. 

“Mom,” I whispered. “I miss you so much.”

Gwen’s heavy breathing stopped. “I’m here, Stacey.”

I froze. Did I wake her up? 

But the heavy breathing returned and the tension in my shoulders relaxed. Weird. Gwen had never sleep-talked before. She sounded like my mother. I could almost hear that familiar depth in her adult voice.

I crouched beside her bed. Maybe I should let her try soccer. It could solidify her love for swimming if she tried a different sport and didn’t like it. 

“Stacey,” Gwen groaned. Her eyes remained closed.

I reached out and cupped her smooth face. I could feel her heartbeat where my fingers rested beneath her ears. 

“I’m here, Gwen.” I whispered. 

Her eyelids fluttered but remained closed. “Stacey baby. It’s me. Mommy”

I held back a sob. She sounded so much like my mom. Could it really be her? Was she speaking to me through Little Gwen?

“Mommy?”

“Yes, pumpkin.”

My mom had always called me pumpkin. It was something reserved between us. As much as I missed her, I never told Little Gwen about the nickname she’d once given me in her past life. I tried not to let Gwen know too much about my mother. I couldn’t let her catch on. It would be too big of a shock and I didn’t want her to question her whole humanity, her very life. I wanted her to live like a normal person. 

“I’ve missed you so much, dear,” she spoke again. 

Oh, god. It really was my mom. How was this possible? Tears kept streaming down my face as I realized that I was speaking to her again. Somehow, I was here, talking to the one woman I would’ve done anything in the world for. I felt like a little girl. It was so good to hear her old voice one last time. I’d recorded her in her final days so that I could listen to her, but it was nothing compared to this moment. Her voice sounded so much stronger now. Older, but stronger. 

“I’ve missed you too.” I couldn’t tell if what I said was discernable between my hiccups. I was crying quietly to not wake Gwen up, but it was so hard. I let my tears fall onto the blanket beside her. 

“Now honey, we don’t have a lot of time. Gwen might wake up soon.”

“What do you mean?”

“I need you to trust me on this, okay? Do you want to see me again?”

I nodded vigorously. I would give anything to spend one last day with my mother again. “Yes, of course.”

“Where your hands are, do you feel that pulsing?”

I paused. What I had mistaken for a heartbeat was actually something else entirely. There was something underneath her skin pushing from inside. Like it was trying to get out. It moved at a steady pace, but it definitely wasn’t natural. 

“Yes, mom.” It felt so good to address my mother again. I never thought I would be able to say it after she died. 

“That’s me. Can you feel me pushing?”

I nodded, my breath hitching in excitement. I could feel her. I was only millimeters away from feeling my mother. 

“This is going to sound strange, but you need to believe me. You know that I’ve never lied to you before, right?”

“Okay.”

“Gwen is a skin. They did more than just clone her, they put my soul inside of her too.”

I furrowed my brow. “Skin? What do you mean?”  
  
Gwen suddenly shifted, and I quickly drew my hand back towards my chest. My mother waited for her deep breathing to resume before she continued. 

“It was never about cloning. They targeted people who had lost loved ones for a reason. They wanted to keep the body *and* the soul alive. They did more than you asked for honey, they kept me alive too.”

I pinched myself to make sure I wasn’t dreaming. It sounded unbelievable, but my mother wouldn’t lie to me. “But Gwen is nothing like you. She strays farther from who you were every day.”

“Gwen is my shell, honey. But she is not me. I’m here, but Gwen and I are two separate beings.”

I lost track of time as my mother explained the situation. More than once, Gwen moved in her sleep and every time she did, I feared that she would wake up and I would lose the chance to speak to my mother again. But my mom went on, explaining how Gwen was simply a vessel for her. How inside, her soul had drawn enough of Little Gwen’s energy and cells for her to begin growing inside of her clone. The company had done more than it had promised. In fact, it had recreated life in a way that no other scientists were able to do. They had reversed death. They had brought my mother back from the dead. All for a couple hundred thousand dollars in cold hard cash. It was a steal, honestly. 

“It’s getting tight in here. I’m going to need to be let out soon.”

“Is there anything I can do to help, mom?” I couldn’t stop saying it. “I’ll do whatever it takes, mom.” I tasted the words on my tongue. It felt so good. 

For a few moments, my mother didn’t say anything.

“Mom?” I asked again. 

“This is going to be the hard part.”

“I’ll do it. I’ll do anything to see you again.” My heart raced with excitement.

I missed my mother so much. When I was a little girl, she always told me when I’d hurt her. She reminded me when I was being ungrateful. She’d teach me equality, to show that the pain from her blows were less than the pain she felt when I’d make a mistake. I still needed to make it up to her. I had hurt her with my mistakes, my failed tests, my petty complaints. So much of her pain and suffering had been caused by me, but I could fix it all now. My mother was so wise, but she was gone before she could teach me everything. There was still so much I could learn from her.

I needed to see her again, to hear her voice and hold her hand. Little Gwen wasn’t enough. She might have looked like her, but she was nothing like my mother. And I was so close to being back with her again.

Some might have called it a big sacrifice when I did what I did. Others might have said it was a risk. But my mother was always right, so I had to listen. I would hurt anybody just to protect her. That’s why when she told me to press a pillow against Gwen’s face, I didn’t protest. She said Gwen couldn’t actually feel pain. When she told me to start cutting at Gwen’s scalp, I listened again, slicing obediently until there was no skin left. All the struggle and blood was worth it when fingers protruded from flesh, eventually becoming arms, which became shoulders, and soon, emerged the face of my mother. 

Her bones and joints cracked as she crawled out of her shell, but her movements were distinctly hers. She was so small, but she would continue to grow now that she had more space. A smile formed on my lips as I drank up the sight of her; I’d missed the mole on her lip and the slight hunch in her posture. Little Gwen didn’t have those things. Standing before me was my mom, just as beautiful as I remembered. And that was all that I needed. 

“Thank you Stacey. You’ve made me proud.” 

My heart soared as a smile bloomed across my face. It was all I had ever wanted to hear. When I was younger, she would offer her critiques to better me. I knew she wanted me to be the best version of myself, but in my teenage years, I’d resisted. Then I went to university and moved out, and I couldn’t prove myself to her like I wanted to. Her harsh comments and painful strikes and blows all made up for this moment. I had finally done it, I’d made her proud. And all I had to do was listen. This is why I didn’t protest against her. Because my mother was always right. 

I’m posting this now for those of you that want to see your loved ones again. It’s possible. I’ve found my happiness, and maybe you can too.
