I come back to Cape May every summer. It’s a tradition of choice, really. It all started with an Appalachian family getting sick of the hills and itching to get away for a weekend. We’d pack the car up until you could no longer see out the rearview and set our sights on the undisturbed coast.

Well, at least it was undisturbed then.

I’ve been doing it for long enough that the drive down doesn’t really feel like a trip anymore. I know every stretch of road, every Wawa stop, the exact point where I can usually smell the ocean through the car windows, and, more importantly, where I’d stop for a piss.

This year, though, something felt different before I even got there.

Growing up, I developed a really good friendship with a local store owner’s son. Winnie and I grew up in pretty similar ways, despite living hundreds of miles apart. I think it was the few small differences between us that drew us together. The kind of friend you recognize almost immediately, like you’ve known them longer than you actually have.

Surfing sorta just became a natural progression of that once-a-year trip and my obsession with the ocean.

Once I had a driver’s license, the only thing holding me back from my hidden corner of paradise was gas prices. Any chance I could get a few days off work, I’d happily make the few-hour drive down, typically in the middle of the night when traffic died down and the radar guns disappeared.

Surfing was my passion. One thing any surfer will tell you, vet or beginner, is the addicting nature of catching your first real wave. It really is like being sucked into a trap, but the bait is so rewarding and fulfilling that you become numb to the burden of costly gear and years of serious commitment and hard tumbles in the waves. Another thing they’d definitely tell you about is the absolute gamble of East Coast surf conditions. Often, only after heavy storms do we truly get our fill.

Regardless of the gamble, I’d make that trip almost weekly. Because even the worst day out on the water beats the absolute best days at work, and besides, Cape May was always my true safe space.

I shot Winnie a call to let him know I’d most likely be hanging off the couch before the sun rose. Unsurprisingly, there was no answer. After inheriting the store, he really did have his hands full. However, I knew he was always truly appreciative of my help.

On arrival, I slid the car into the gravel driveway, threw my board on the porch, and snuck through the front door. I let myself in and claimed the living room like I’d done a dozen times before. I hauled my small pile of clothes for the weekend. I tossed my shoes onto the doormat and hit the couch feeling like a ton of bricks.

There’s something inherently different about mornings on the coast. The air feels fuller. The sun beats a little harder. The days feel full of promise, adventure, and opportunity. Everything just seems a little more exciting and a little less mundane.

Between all the sensory overload, the scent of coffee still found its place in the humble three-bedroom Cape Cod at the eastern end of town. Winnie tossed me my thermos, already filled with my usual espresso, two creams, and a dash of sugar. He knew how I liked it, and somehow, he always seemed to have it ready before I even had to ask.

Honestly, regardless of all his quirks and his outwardly quiet demeanor, Winnie was always a good friend to me. The kind of friend you laugh like a kid with. The type of friend that makes you feel more at home than a house ever could.

We hit the morning early.

I helped him haul in some new crewnecks that had come in the night before and threw labels on the new Cape May tourist hoodies. Admittedly, not something I’d ever thought I’d find any joy in, but I did. There was something satisfying about helping him keep the place afloat.

Once he signaled that he was all caught up, I knew it was finally my time to shine.

I checked the radar, and everything looked promising. Tide charts showed a promising low tide, no shorebreak at least. Northeast winds were pushing in a solid, punchy swell, and the forecast showed nothing but sun for the rest of the day. Just the walk down to the beach alone was already getting me amped.

Even in the height of tourist season, the early morning still had its reprieve from the loud tourists and packed beaches.

I worked my way down the winding trails through the endless sand until I finally peeked over the dunes. Without a doubt, they were the best conditions I’d seen since the winter northeasterlies.

Waves curling perfectly.

A perfectly placed low tide.

The sets breaking barely twenty feet from the shallow sandbanks and shoreline.

In other words, a surfer’s wet dream.

I almost tripped head over heels scrambling toward the untapped gold. I threw a quick coat of wax on the board, slapped what I figured was enough sunscreen across my back and nose, and practically dove into the water.

That’s when I was caught a bit off guard.

Cape May was no stranger to warm water, especially considering how warm it usually gets this time of year. But this warm? I would say it was mid to upper eighties if I really had to put a number to it. Now, I’m no weather boy, but in the beginning of July, it was anomalous. No doubt odd, but who was I to question conditions so good?

I quickly pushed the thought to the back of my mind. Days like these often didn’t go unnoticed. I knew it was only a matter of time before locals and tourists alike flooded the beach, chasing the rarity of perfect surf in my very own little cove.

For the moment, though, I had it all to myself in my very own slice of paradise.

Once word got out, it became a free-for-all.

I spent half the morning getting snaked by sponsored ten-year-olds who seemed to think the laws of physics didn’t apply to them, only to have weekend-warrior grandpas glide past on longboards like they’d been surfing since before I was even an idea.

It was chaos, but the good kind. The kind you put up with when the waves are firing.

Somewhere within all of the excitement and action, it became easy to forget how strong the undertow was.

The lifeguards didn’t forget.

They were starting to freak out about the number of little rip currents popping up along the beach. Not one big, obvious rip that you could point at and avoid. Dozens of smaller ones, appearing and disappearing in places they normally didn’t.

They started pulling the younger kids out of the water and giving the usual spiel about swimming sideways, not fighting the current, and getting someone’s attention if they got caught.

Nothing about it was unusual enough to stop anyone from surfing.

Not yet.

The waves were too good.

And despite everything happening around us—the strange currents, the unusually warm water, the lifeguards keeping a closer eye on the beach than usual—it was still shaping up to be the best day of surf I’d ever had.

Honestly, probably the best any of us had ever seen.

Unfortunately, all that fun didn’t come without its consequences. I was tired, beat up, and completely spent by the time I finally rolled out.

As the sun began to retreat, so did the crowds.

I wrapped it up when I could no longer see my toes beneath the water. Almost as if it were retreating with the sun, the ocean began to calm down too. The chop disappeared, the wind settled, and all the day’s chaos slowly crescendoed into a glassy surface.

For a moment, everything was still.

There was nothing left but the lingering smell of saltwater, the warmth of the sand beneath my feet, and that sweet, familiar feeling that comes after a day you know you’ll never truly be able to forget.

It was the kind of evening that makes you forget tomorrow exists.

“How was it out there today?” Winnie asked out of habit, not curiosity.

“It was one for the books, Winn Dixie,” I replied with a smirk.

I said it like it wasn’t already obvious from the collection of scrapes, bruises, and sunburn covering every inch of skin that I’d sworn I’d put sunscreen on.

“I trust you’ll be up bright and early tomorrow to give me a hand. One of the sponsor companies called. They want us to update the ad graphics in the window. You know those vinyl sheets come off about as easily as you’d imagine,” Winnie said, sporting a half-effort smile.

“Yeah, without a doubt. I owe ya. You don’t charge me a cent in rent and let me crash here whenever I come down. It really is the absolute least I could do. Besides, after today, I could absolutely use a break from the water,” I replied genuinely.

Winnie laughed.

“We’ll see how long that lasts,” Winnie remarked sarcastically.

I laughed too.

At the time, I thought he was just giving me shit.

“It’s the damn dredging. Opening up the shallows and screwing with the tide patterns. Shit, I don’t even eat the damn oysters. And this warm water does nothing but draw the jellies and the tourists in. Why can’t it ever be good news in this damned paper?” an old-timer shouted to his wife from the entrance of the next-door cafe.

I was scraping away at the vintage vinyl advertising the latest board shorts that had been sun-baked onto the windows for who knows how many years.

“And they clocked some shipwreck or some kind of sunken mass moving along one of the bayside fishing routes,” his wife replied, visibly agitated. “As if our waters aren’t already polluted enough,” she said, falsely smiling.

I stopped scraping for a second.

“Mass?” I asked.

The sun-aged woman glared over at me.

“That’s what they’re calling it, probably another sunken buoy or conglomerate of trash from good ole New York’s wonderful dumping habits,” she said sarcastically.

She turned back toward the shelf of discounted spring clothing she was lazily browsing.

I shrugged and went back to scraping.

Admittedly, I usually tried to stay off my phone when I was helping around the shop. But that morning, it became remarkably difficult.

It was practically going off every minute.

Eventually, I excused myself to check what was going on.

Yet again, videos across all the local surf and weather pages. Texts from the locals I’d befriended over the many years, almost as if time had rewound. Everyone was talking about the same thing.

The ocean had come alive again.

Another day of some of the best surf the East Coast had seen in years.

I watched video after video of clean sets peeling down the beach, people getting barreled, boards cutting across faces that looked almost too perfect to be real. The kind you only see in movies.

It almost made me reconsider how beat up I’d been the day before.

Almost.

FOMO had never hit me harder.

I hate to admit it, but I was conflicted. I knew I should do the right thing and stay to help finish up around the boutique, but I just couldn’t get the ocean out of my head.

It was like a song that gets stuck in your mind, playing over and over until it drives you crazy. Eventually, you give in and play the damn thing just to hear it again once more.

I wiped down the final window, grabbed the things I’d left lying around, and told Winnie the game plan for the evening.

“I’m stopping for a pie tonight. You want anything?” Winnie replied tiredly.

“I’ll just grab something on my way home. Thanks, though,” I replied, distant to the thought of even eating at this moment.

Then I hurried my happy ass on down to the beach.

It was late, but I didn’t care.

I was playing the song one more time while it still sounded fresh and sweet.

I arrived just as everyone was packing up for the night.

That didn’t bother me.

The waves had slowed, but they still rolled in with an undeniable consistency—something incredibly rare for such a historically rough and erratic stretch of ocean.

I slid my longboard out of the trunk and prepared for a relaxing night to myself.

A sunset alone with the waves. Something I’d only dream of back home.

I admired the smear of sunburnt orange and hazy blues stretched across the slick surface of the water. I took in the sounds of seabirds slowly winding down for the night.

It was quiet.

Almost unusually quiet.

Aside from the occasional crash of a wave or a distant word from the last beachgoers dissipating, there was nothing.

For once, there was no rush.

I applied fresh, deliberate strokes of warm-water wax to the board, brushed the sand from the fins, and tied up my favorite pair of board shorts.

The sun slowly sank toward the horizon, a dying victim to my utter lack of haste.

One thing you learn awfully quick is the absolute importance of all your senses while surfing in low light.

You have to be hyper-aware of everything around you. The sound of the waves breaking. The pattern of the sets. The direction of the wind. Even the temperature of the air can tell you when conditions are beginning to change.

But tonight was strangely devoid of all of those indicators.

There was nothing.

No wind.

No distant voices.

No changing patterns in the waves.

Just the unusually warm ocean.

The only remarkable detail was how much saltier the water had seemed to have gotten overnight.

One lazy wave became two, then three.

I glided along the invisible but somehow apparent lip, relying almost entirely on muscle memory and instinct. The steps, the pop-up, the shifting of my weight beneath me—it all came together without thought.

Just a flow of organic movement.

Fiberglass against the sea.

The ocean really was devoid of distractions.

Until, sometime later, the water came alive beneath my feet.

I don’t know exactly how long I’d been out there. An hour, maybe more.

My paddle stroke sent a faint blue glow spiraling beneath my hands.

I stopped.

The light disappeared.

I paddled again.

There it was.

A hazy trail of faint blue-green light followed my hand through the water like I’d dragged a trail of dying embers beneath the surface.

I moved my fingers.

Another trail.

I kicked my feet.

The water around my legs erupted with tiny pricks of light.

I had seen bioluminescence before, but never anything remotely like this, especially not here.

The entire surface seemed to respond to me.

Every paddle stroke left a glowing trail. Every movement sent another cloud of pale blue light scattering beneath the surface.

For a few minutes, I forgot everything else.

I just sat there on my board, staring down into the water.

I was floating above the stars.

I remember thinking I might have been the only person who saw it.

I wasn’t.

Upon wading back, my feet sank between littered piles of broken, empty oyster shells and the remnants of coral and reef ledges that I had not noticed the night before.

When I got back to the beach, I noticed a couple standing farther down the shoreline. They were staring out at the water too, phone cameras curiously drawn to the water.

“Did you see that?” the woman asked curiously.

I nodded.

“Yeah.”

Neither of us really knew what to say after that.

By the time I made it back to the house, I’d gotten a few texts from people I knew around town.

“You see that shit out there?”

Another:

“The water is glowing like in Hawaii.”

And another, from someone I’d known for years:

“Never seen anything like that here.”

It wasn’t everyone.

Most people hadn’t seen anything at all. Ignorant to the ocean’s gift.

But apparently, a handful of people scattered around the immediate area had.

That somehow made it feel stranger, almost foreign.

It wasn’t some massive phenomenon stretching up and down the coast.

It was here.

Just here.

A few miles of ocean around Cape May had lit up that night, and nobody seemed to know why.

As soon as I got back, I went straight for my computer instead of the couch.

I spent the better part of an hour searching.

Article after article.

Bioluminescent plankton in the Atlantic.

Bioluminescence along the New England coast.

Reports from Delaware. Maryland. New Jersey.

I found pictures. I found explanations. I found people arguing about algae blooms and water temperatures and microscopic organisms.

But nothing explained what I’d seen.

Nothing mentioned anything remotely like the amount of light I’d just watched spreading beneath my board.

I finally shut the computer. Grabbed a slice of pizza and sat silently on the kitchen chair.

I told myself I’d find an explanation in the morning.

At the time, I was still excited.

I thought I’d gotten lucky.

I didn’t understand that the ocean hadn’t rewarded me with anything.

It had simply let me see what was already there.
