**One Occupant Only**

*Part one of the Terms of Occupancy series. This story can also be read as a standalone.*

The apartment was too cheap. I knew that before I even booked the showing.

It was a one bedroom in a decent part of the city, ten minutes from the subway station and within walking distance of the supermarket where I worked. Most importantly, it was hundreds of dollars cheaper than every other place I had looked at. I assumed there had to be something wrong with it. Mold, probably. Rats if I was unlucky. Maybe a neighbor who spent every night screaming at strangers through the walls.

Instead, the place was clean.

A little old and rundown, but clean. The hardwood floors creaked beneath my feet, the pipes knocked whenever someone used the water upstairs, and the bedroom window faced the brick wall of the building next door, but none of that explained the rent. 

The landlord was waiting for me in the lobby when I arrived. She looked younger than I had expected, maybe early thirties, with a long dark coat and a ring of brass keys clipped to her belt. She showed me around without any of the forced enthusiasm I had grown used to from other showings. She pointed out the laundry room, explained which trash cans were collected on which days and told me the blue mailbox in the lobby was for mail delivered to the wrong apartment.

Then, just as I was beginning to think I had somehow found the only reasonably priced apartment left in the city, she stopped beside the bedroom door.

“There is one condition I need you to understand,” she said seriously.

I glanced back at her. “Okay.”

“The lease is for one permanent occupant.”

“I’d be living alone.”

“Visitors are fine,” she continued. “Overnight guests are fine. But no one else can live here.”

There was something about the way she said it that made me pause. It didn’t sound like a standard warning about subletting or overcrowding. It sounded personal.

“I’m not planning to move anyone else in,” I said.

Her eyes fixed on me. “That means no furniture belonging to someone else. No mail in another person’s name. No clothes left here permanently. Nothing that would make this their home too.”

I should have asked why she was being so specific. Instead, I thought about the affordable rent and how many applications I had already filled out that month.

“Fine,” I said. “Just me.”

She held my gaze for another second before nodding and handing me the application.

I moved in the following week.

For the first month, the apartment was perfect. For the first time in months, I had somewhere quiet that I could afford without spending nearly all my money on rent.

The strange things didn't start immediately. At least, I don’t think they did.

The first time I came home and found the lamp beside the couch already switched on, I assumed I had forgotten to turn it off. The same went for the mug I had left beside the sink and later found washed, dried and placed neatly in the cupboard. I had been working long shifts and getting home exhausted. It wouldn't be impossible that I had cleaned without remembering, although that explanation became harder to believe when I woke up one morning and found the shirt I had dropped on the floor folded at the end of my bed.

After that, I started noticing things more often. My keys always ended up in the dish beside the front door, even when I was certain I had left them in my coat pocket. Cupboards I forgot to close would be shut by morning. Once, after rushing out late for work, I came home to find the bed made and the blanket tucked neatly under the mattress.

Eventually, I called the landlord.

“Has anyone been inside my apartment today?”

“No.”

“Maintenance or anything?”

“No. Why?”

I looked at the bed. “Nothing. I just thought someone might have come in.”

There was a brief pause on the other end.

“You are still living alone, aren’t you?”

“Yes.”

“Then I wouldn’t worry.”

That should have worried me more than it did. Instead, I decided she was simply strange. Besides, nothing had been stolen or damaged. If anything, the apartment seemed to be helping me.  
Sometimes, while I was cooking, I would hear the floorboards creak behind me as though someone had walked into the kitchen. At night, the mattress occasionally dipped on the empty side of the bed. I told myself it was the building settling. Old floors. Pipes shifting inside the walls. That sort of thing. 

Then Hannah started staying over.

We had been together for almost a year by then, but our schedules rarely matched. I worked early shifts at the supermarket and she usually finished late at the restaurant, so most of the time we only saw each other for dinner or on whichever random day we were both off. Once I had my own place, it made sense for her to stay over more often.

The first night, she woke me just after four in the morning.

“Did you move my bag?”

I opened my eyes to see her sitting upright beside me, staring towards the bedroom door.

“What bag?”

“My overnight bag. I left it on the chair.”

It was sitting on the floor beside the door, zipped shut.

“You probably moved it before we went to sleep.”

“I didn’t.”

“You might have kicked it.”

“From the chair?”

I was too tired to argue. I told her I would look at it in the morning, pulled the blanket over my shoulder and went back to sleep.

One of her shoes was missing when we woke up.  
   
We checked under the bed, inside the wardrobe and behind the chair. Eventually, Hannah found it in the hallway outside the apartment, placed by the wall beside my front door.

She gave me a look when she brought it inside, but I laughed and said maybe she had left it there without noticing.

“I walked in wearing both shoes.”

“Then maybe I moved it.”

“You were behind me.”

I shrugged. “I don’t know, Hannah. It’s a shoe.”

She let it go, although she kept glancing towards the bedroom whenever she thought I wasn’t looking.

After that, something happened almost every time she stayed over. Her charger would be unplugged during the night. Clothes she left folded on the chair would end up stuffed back into her bag. The framed photograph of us on my dresser kept falling facedown, even after I moved it away from the edge.

The bathroom door was the worst.

It didn’t have a lock. I know that because Hannah made me check after it refused to open while she was inside. She had only been in there a few minutes when I heard her pulling at the handle.

“Can you stop messing around?”

“I’m not.”

The handle turned in my hand, but the door didn't budge. It felt like someone on the other side was pulling against me.

“Hannah, step back.”

“I am.”

I put my shoulder into it. The door opened so suddenly that I nearly fell into the bathroom.

Hannah was standing beside the sink in a towel, pale and shaking. There was nobody else in the room, and nothing had been wedged beneath the door.

“What happened?”

“I don’t know.” She looked back at the handle. “It just wouldn’t open.”

“It felt like you were pulling it shut.”

Her eyes snapped to mine. “I wasn’t touching it.”

For a second, neither of us said anything.

Then the door swung closed behind me.

Hannah stopped staying over as often after that. When we did spend time together, she insisted that we stay at her place instead.

For a while, that solved the problem.

Then the restaurant where she worked closed.

It happened without warning. Hannah had enough saved to cover one month of rent, maybe two if she stopped paying for everything else, and her landlord had already told her he would not accept late payments.

She never asked to move in with me.

I offered.

“It would only be temporary,” she said. “Just until I find another job.”

“I know.”

“What about your landlord?”

I thought of the warning she had given me during the showing and how serious she had looked when she said it.

“She won’t know.”

That answer seemed good enough at the time.

We moved Hannah’s things in the next evening. Two suitcases, a few boxes, a bag of shoes and the coffee machine she refused to leave in storage because, according to her, mine made coffee that tasted like burnt coal.

The next morning, everything was packed beside the front door.

Not thrown there. Packed.

Her clothes had been folded back into the suitcases. The boxes were sealed with the tape in my kitchen drawer. Her shoes were lined neatly beneath the coat hooks, and the coffee machine sat on top of one of the boxes with its cord wrapped around the base.

Hannah stood staring at the pile for so long that I eventually asked if she was okay.

“Did you do this?” She asked.

“No.”

“You were up before me.”

“I went to the bathroom.”

“So who packed everything?”

I tried to make a joke about the world’s most efficient burglar, but she didn’t find it funny.

We unpacked again.

That night, the wardrobe doors slammed hard enough to crack one of the mirrors.

After that, the apartment stopped being subtle.

Hannah’s clothes were repeatedly moved into trash bags. The shower ran either freezing cold or hot enough to leave red patches on her skin, but worked normally the moment I tested it. Kitchen drawers slid open as she passed by, catching her across her hips and thighs. Once, while I was at work, the apartment locked her outside wearing only slippers and a robe.

Her key worked when I tried it.

She was sitting on the floor in the hallway with her knees pulled to her chest.

“It wouldn’t let me in.”

I crouched beside her. “Maybe the lock jammed.”

“It opened for you.”

“You might not have turned the key properly.”

She looked at me, properly looked at me, and I could see how frightened she was.

“It doesn’t want me here.”

I wanted to tell her that was ridiculous. Instead, I helped her inside and found every photograph of us turned facedown.

That evening, the lights flickered whenever Hannah spoke.

Not randomly. Only when she spoke.

She would say something and the light would dim. But when I spoke it would steady again. After the fourth or fifth time, she put down her fork.

“You see that, right?”

“It’s the wiring.”

“It’s not the wiring.”

“Hannah.”

“It hates me.”

The bulb above us went out. For several seconds, we sat in darkness. Then the lamp beside my chair switched on. Only my side of the table was lit.

Hannah started looking for places the next morning.

We agreed that she would stay for one more week, just until she found somewhere affordable. Neither of us mentioned telling the landlord. By then, I was certain she would evict me if she found out, and I could not afford another deposit.

Two nights later, I woke up to Hannah screaming.

I reached across the mattress and found nothing beside me. When I opened my eyes, she was halfway across the bedroom floor.

Something had her by the ankles.

I couldn’t see any hands or arms. I could only see the way her body was pulled backwards a few inches at a time, towards the wardrobe.

“Hannah?”

She clawed at the carpet. “Help me!”

I threw myself out of bed and caught her arms. The moment I touched her, whatever was pulling seemed to tighten its grip. She screamed and kicked, but her feet had already disappeared under the wardrobe.

That should not have been possible.

The wardrobe stood flat against the floor. There was no gap beneath it large enough for even a hand, let alone both of Hannah’s legs.

I pulled harder.

Something whispered beside my ear.

“Let go.”

I froze because the voice sounded exactly like Hannah.

Hannah slipped another inch.

The carpet beneath her began to split. A dark line ripped through the fibers and spread across the floorboards, widening slowly as though the room had developed a mouth.

I wrapped both arms around her chest and leaned back with all my weight. She screamed again, and there was a sharp popping sound from her shoulder.

The bedroom door slammed shut.

The dresser scraped across the floor and lodged itself in front of it.

“Let go,” the apartment said again.

This time, the voice came from underneath.

Hannah’s fingers dug into my arms. I could feel blood beginning to run beneath my sleeves where her nails had broken the skin.

I looked around for anything I could use. The bedside lamp. The chair. The broken mirror on the wardrobe.

I kicked at the wardrobe door.

The cracked mirror shattered, spraying glass across the floor. The sound was so sudden and violent that the thing holding Hannah loosened its grip. 

I pulled hard and we crashed backwards into the side of the bed. Her pants were torn from the knee down, and deep red marks circled both ankles.

I dragged the dresser away from the door while Hannah limped behind me. Every cupboard in the kitchen opened as we ran past. Plates hit the floor. The bathroom door slammed hard enough to crack the frame.

The front door began closing before Hannah had made it through.

I threw my shoulder into it and held it open while she squeezed into the hallway. Something caught the back of my shirt and pulled. The fabric tightened around my throat until I managed to tear free.

We stumbled towards the stairs.

The landlord was already waiting in the lobby.

She wore pajamas beneath a heavy coat, with her brass keys held in one hand. She looked from Hannah’s torn pants to the blood on my arms, then at the staircase behind us.

“How long?” she asked.

I was breathing too hard to understand.

“How long has she been living there?”

Hannah stared at her. “You knew this would happen?”

The landlord ignored her and looked at me.

“Two weeks,” I said.

She closed her eyes for a second, as though I had confirmed something she had been hoping not to hear.

“I warned you.”

“You told me it was a lease condition.”

“It was.”

Something struck the inside of my apartment door upstairs.

Once.

Then again.

The landlord barely reacted.

“What is in there?” Hannah asked.

The landlord looked towards the ceiling. “It doesn’t mind visitors. It minds competition.”

“That doesn’t answer the question.”

“No,” she said. “It doesn’t.”

She unlocked the front door and told us to leave the building. When I asked if I could go back for our things, she said absolutely not.

Hannah and I stayed with her sister for the next few weeks. She found another job, and eventually we rented a place together on the other side of the city. Nothing followed us, no doors locked on their own, and our belongings stayed exactly where we left them.

I never went back to the apartment. The landlord arranged for our things to be collected and delivered to a storage unit, although most of Hannah’s belongings were missing. She said the bedroom had been almost empty when she entered.

A few days later, she called me again.

“I need to ask you something,” she said. “Before Hannah moved in, did you ever talk about leaving?”

“Leaving the apartment?”

“Yes.”

I thought about it. Hannah and I had discussed finding a place together eventually. We had talked about it in bed sometimes, usually late at night when we assumed we would not have to worry about money forever.

“I suppose so.”

“And did you say you would move out if she found work?”

“I might have.”

The landlord was quiet.

“What does that have to do with anything?”

“When I entered the bedroom, the wardrobe had been pulled away from the wall,” she said. “There was writing behind it.”

I sat up. “What writing?”

“Your name. Over and over again.”

I waited for her to continue.

“At the bottom, it had written one sentence.”

“What did it say?”

The landlord hesitated before answering.

**“He was mine before she came.”**
