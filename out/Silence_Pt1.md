There is a jar on the shelf above my bed. It’s about the size of a spice jar, the cheap kind with a cork in the top, and it’s half full of fine gray ash.   
I want to tell you whose ash it is, but if I tell you now, you’ll read the rest of this for the wrong reasons, waiting for it, the way you wait for the bad part of a movie; and that isn’t fair to her. So, I’m going to start at the beginning, the way it actually happened, and you can find out when I did.   
I was seventeen. That’s my only excuse, and it isn’t a very good one. 

\*\*\*\*\*\*\*\*\*\*  
On the morning I turned seventeen, my father gave me a person.  
Nobody in the Spire would have said it like that. What he said was, “Your allowance came through early,” and he slid his tablet across the breakfast counter without looking up from his coffee. On the screen was a Halcyon gift card with a gold ribbon turning slowly around it. Four hundred credits.  
“Everyone your age plays,” he said. “It teaches cause and effect. I had a Piece when I was your age. You remember the fisherman.”  
I remembered the fisherman. He drowned when I was nine. My father kept the jar of ash on his office shelf for a while, then I guess he threw it out, because one day it was gone and there was a new Piece glowing on his desk, a woman in Manila who sold phone cases. I never asked what happened to her. You didn’t ask about other people’s Pieces. It was like asking about their bank balance.  
We lived on the hundred-and-sixty-fourth floor. From my window you could see the whole coast, and on clear mornings you could see the Drowns: the old city, standing in the sea up to its knees. From that high up it looked almost peaceful. Laundry lines between rooftops. Little boats moving between the towers like water bugs.  
That’s where most of the Pieces came from. Everybody knew that, the same way everybody knew where meat came from. I should tell you about the knocking, too, because I didn’t think anything of it then.  
Some nights, ever since I was little, I’d wake up and hear it. A soft, patient tapping from the end of the hall, somewhere past my father’s office. Three knocks. A pause. Three knocks. Never loud. It was like someone who’d been told to be quiet and was trying very hard, but couldn’t quite stop.  
When I was seven I asked my father about it. He said it was the water in the pipes. The Spire was full of pipes, he said, miles of them, and they talked to each other at night. Then he tucked me back in and sat on the end of my bed in the dark until I fell asleep. He did that every time I asked. He never once seemed annoyed.  
After a while I stopped hearing it. You can stop hearing almost anything, if you live next to it long enough.  
If you’re reading this somewhere Life never reached, or somewhere it’s finally gone, I should explain it. I don’t know how much time has passed for you.  
Halcyon put a halo behind your ear, a thin silver implant about the size of a fingernail. In return, they paid you a monthly stipend. The halo recorded everything: what you saw, what you heard, your heartbeat, your blood...and it let Players reach in.  
A Player could send you a Nudge: a few words that showed up in your head like a thought you’d had yourself. A Player could buy you a Boon, and somewhere a small good thing would happen to you. A job. A lucky find. A clinic appointment that opened up out of nowhere. Or a Player could buy a Trial for someone else’s Piece, and a small bad thing would happen instead. When a Piece died, the feed didn’t show you anything at all. It just went black, and one word came up in the middle in soft gold letters.  
SILENCE.  
My father said it was tasteful. That was the word he used. Tasteful.  
That’s what the ads said. Small. A flat tire. A lost key.  
I believed that for about three weeks.

\*\*\*\*\*\*\*\*\*\*  
I locked my door and opened the Store on my wall. The listings scrolled up in tiles: a photo, a name, an age, a city, and a row of numbers. Health. Luck. Drama Potential. Watchers. The famous Pieces were at the top, singers and athletes and a surgeon in Seoul, split between thousands of owners like shares of stock.  
I scrolled past them, down to where the photos got blurry and every location said the same thing. Drowned District. Outer Harbor.  
That’s where I found her.  
***WREN ASHBY, 19. Outer Harbor. Desalination plant, night shift. Health 61. Luck 22. Drama Potential: Low. Watchers: 3. Price: 210 credits. Sole ownership available.***  
The photo was crooked, like the halo had grabbed it while she was turning. She was laughing at something off to the side. Not a nice laugh. A huge, ugly, honking laugh, mouth wide, eyes squeezed shut, one hand coming up too late to hide a chipped front tooth. Her hair was short and uneven, like she cut it herself in the dark.  
Behind her, out of focus, was a tree standing in water. Its bark was painted red.  
I keep trying to remember what I felt, looking at that picture. I want it to have been something good. Pity, maybe, or kindness. But I think if I’m honest, what I felt was want. She didn’t know anyone was looking. Every other Piece on the wall was smiling for a camera they knew was there. She was just alive, in the middle of her own life, and someone had stolen a second of it and put a price on it, and I wanted that second for myself.  
Three watchers. I remember thinking that was sad. I remember thinking, I’ll be the one who actually cares.  
I didn’t think about who the other three were.  
I pressed Buy.  
The terms scrolled up the wall. Nobody reads them. I read them, because I was the kind of kid who read terms, and I want you to know that, so you understand I can’t say I didn’t know.  
*Your Avatar will be bound to your Piece. Your Avatar’s condition reflects your Piece’s condition. If your Piece dies, your Avatar dies with them, and ownership ends. Influence is non-refundable. Players may not contact Pieces outside of approved channels. Pieces are aware of their participation in Life but not of the identity or number of their Players. Halcyon is not responsible for outcomes.*  
I pressed Accept.  
The room went dark. A small light bloomed above my desk and grew, and folded itself into the shape of a girl about the size of my hand.  
The Avatar was soft blue light. It didn’t look exactly like her. It was smoother, simpler, a cartoon of a person, but it had her choppy hair and the way she stood with her weight on one hip. It was flickering at the edges, very faintly, the way a candle does when a door opens somewhere in the house.  
The numbers hovered over its head.  
Heart rate: 112. Stress: High. Sleep debt: 31 hours.  
One hundred and twelve. I was sitting in a chair. I checked my own pulse on my watch, just to compare. Sixty-four.  
“What are you doing,” I said to the little light on my desk, “that your heart’s going that fast?”  
I opened the feed.  
The halo doesn’t give you video. It gives you a kind of dream of what the Piece is seeing, rebuilt by Halcyon’s software: colors a little too smooth, sounds a little too clean. It takes a second for your eyes to stop fighting it.  
Wren was in a boat, in the dark.  
It was a flat metal skiff with an outboard motor that coughed every few seconds. She was steering it down a canal between two drowned apartment blocks, no lights on either side, the water black and thick and full of floating plastic. One hand was on the tiller. The other was holding a paper bag against her chest the way you’d hold a baby.  
She was whispering to someone. “…it’s the real stuff. I got it off Doss, not off the market. Doss doesn’t cut it.”  
I turned the view and saw a kid in the front of the boat, maybe eleven, buried in a coat three sizes too big. He was coughing into his sleeve. It was a wet, heavy cough, too big for him, like a grown man’s cough had climbed into a little boy.  
“That’s your whole check,” he said.  
“It’s what it costs, Kit.”  
“You didn’t sleep again.”  
“I slept.”  
“You didn’t. You get the face.”  
“What face?”  
He let his eyes go half shut and his mouth hang open and his head roll to the side. It was a perfect impression. Wren laughed, the laugh from the photo, and the boat bumped the wall, and they both said “Shh” at the same time and then laughed harder, trying to be quiet.  
I was smiling at my wall. I remember that. It’s one of the few things from that night I don’t mind remembering.  
Then Kit stopped laughing.  
He was looking at something past her shoulder, back the way they’d come. Wren turned the boat’s little light around, and the halo turned with her, and I saw what he was looking at.  
On the footbridge behind them, maybe thirty meters back, a man was standing perfectly still. It was hard to see him. The halo’s software smoothed shadows out, and he was mostly shadow. Tall. A long grey coat. His hands at his sides. He wasn’t doing anything. He was just standing on the bridge, facing them.  
“Is that a Handler?” Kit whispered.  
“Don’t look at him.”  
“Wren, is that...”  
“Don’t look at him.”  
She turned the light away and opened the motor up as far as it would go, and the boat jerked forward into the dark. I could hear her breathing. In my room, on my desk, the little blue Avatar was flickering hard now, the light stuttering around its edges.  
Heart rate: 141.  
I didn’t know what a Handler was. I typed it into the Halcyon help menu, and it gave me a smiling cartoon of a man in a grey uniform holding a toolbox. Handlers are Halcyon’s friendly field staff, who help deliver Boons and Trials in the real world!  
They came out of the canal into a wide flooded square, and in the middle of it stood the tree from the photo.  
It was enormous. An oak, I think, or it had been once, dead for a long time, its bare branches clawing at a sky the color of a bruise. Every inch of it was painted red. Not spray paint. Real paint, thick, laid on with brushes, over and over, until it looked like the tree was bleeding out of every pore.  
Things were hanging from the lowest branches. Hundreds of them, turning in the wind. Strips of cloth. Photographs sealed in plastic bags. And small silver things that caught Wren’s light as it swept past.  
Halos.  
Someone had tied hundreds of halos to the branches with string. Little silver fingernails. Some of them still had dark stuff on them, at the ends where they would have gone into the skull.  
A gold notice slid into the corner of my view.  
*This content may not be suitable for all Players. Would you like to look away?*  
I didn’t press anything.  
Wren steered wide around the tree, keeping her head down. Kit twisted in his seat to stare at it until they were past.  
“Mrs. Abara’s on there now,” he said quietly. “From downstairs. I saw her string. She tied it with her blue one.”  
Wren didn’t answer.  
“They took her Tuesday,” Kit said. “That’s what Doss said. The grey van came Tuesday.”  
“Doss says a lot of things.”  
“Then why is her halo on the tree?”  
Wren didn’t answer that either.  
They tied up at a building that was mostly underwater, and climbed in through a window on what used to be the fourth floor, and I watched her make Kit drink something from the paper bag, tuck him into a nest of blankets on a mattress on the floor, and then sit with her back against the wall and her knees pulled up, staring at the window. Not sleeping. Watching the water.  
Sleep debt: 32 hours.  
I opened the Influence menu. A hundred and ninety credits left. The cheapest Nudge was two.  
I sat there for a long time with my finger over the button, trying to figure out what you’re supposed to say to a person when you own them.  
In the end I wrote: *You’re safe tonight. Sleep. I’ll watch.*  
I pressed Send.  
On the screen, Wren went very still. She lifted one hand slowly to the silver thing behind her ear, the way you’d touch a bruise to see if it still hurt.  
Then a line appeared in the Influence log at the bottom of my view. I want to be exact about this, because later on people told me I must have misread it.  
\[10:52:07\] Nudge delivered (You): “You’re safe tonight. Sleep. I’ll watch.”  
\[10:52:09\] Nudge delivered (Player 0): “He’s lying. Stay awake.”  
Two seconds apart.  
I didn’t understand it. I thought it was a glitch. I scrolled up through her log, and there were dozens of lines from Player 0, going back weeks, months, before I ever bought her. Always at night. Always short.  
*Stay awake.*  
*Check the window.*  
*Don’t let him sleep near the water.*  
*He’s coming back.*  
*He’s coming back.*  
*He’s coming back.*  
On the screen, Wren took her hand away from her ear. She didn’t lie down. She pulled her knees in tighter and stared at the black window, and very quietly, so she wouldn’t wake her brother, she said to the empty room:  
“Which one of you is that?”  
I didn’t answer. I couldn’t. And somewhere out there, neither did Player 0.	  
I filed a support ticket the next morning. I still have the reply. I’ve read it so many times I don’t need to look.  
*Hi Theo! Thanks for reaching out. We’ve reviewed your Piece’s Influence history and can confirm that there is no Player 0 associated with Wren Ashby. Player IDs begin at 1. What you saw may have been a display error. We’re sorry for the confusion! Enjoy Life.*  
I scrolled back through her log that afternoon. Every line from Player 0 was still there.  
I took a screenshot. When I opened the screenshot an hour later, the lines were gone from it too. Just a clean list of my own Nudges, and a blank space where the others had been, as if someone had wiped a window.  
I told myself it was a glitch. I was very good at telling myself things back then.  
You learn a person fast when you watch them every waking hour. Faster than you’d think. Faster than you should.
