Her name was Ms. Valentine.

At least that's what she told me it was when she sat down across the table from me at Mo's, the small-town diner where all the waitresses call you hun and that makes the best apple pie in the world.

At the time I wasn't right. My parents had called me crazy when I left home to go sell hunting properties in rural Missouri, and I was starting to think they were right. Not to mention my mother was battling stage 3 lung cancer and it wasn't looking good.

I was at the lowest moment in my life, hating myself and every choice I had made.

I was sulking, contemplating my lack of success, when this strange woman slid into the booth in front of me.

"You don't look like you're doing so hot, hun," she said to me after introducing herself.

She was tall. Had blond hair, and wore a suit not too dissimilar from mine but probably much more expensive.

I didn't really know what to say. "Ms. Valentine" sounds like a character in a Stephen King book.

So I told her as politely as I could muster that she was right: I wasn't doing so hot, and I'd prefer to be alone.

"You're one of the new owners of Osage Ridge Land Company, right?" The name of my brokerage perked my ears up.

My business partner, we'll call him Dillion, and I set up a billboard on the side of Highway 11 with our pictures on it, so I assumed that's how she knew who I was.

After confirming it was me, Ms. Valentine gave me a card. It was black with a little shiny gold dove emblazoned in the upper left-hand corner. All it said on the card was "Jane" and a phone number.

"Call that number," she said. "She'll put you in touch with a client who will change your life beyond your wildest dreams. But if you call that number, in five years' time you will have to pay your debt with blood and misery."

It sounded like a get-rich-quick scheme. There is no way a stranger just walked in out of the blue promising this and would actually be able to follow through. That, coupled with the name Ms. Valentine, pretty much screamed "this is a prank."

Plus as she rose from the table to leave she added, "Oh, and one more thing: no one can find Jane's card. If anyone does, you have to kill them, otherwise you will suffer the most horrific death possible, and you will relive it, over and over and over again."

I had to laugh as she walked out of the diner. This was ridiculous.

For about fifteen minutes all I could do was stare at the card and turn it over in my hand. Eventually I decided I didn't have anything to lose and called it.

A woman answered. I told her who I was and why I was calling. She asked if I had a pen and paper ready, and I told her I did (I used a napkin and a pen I had in my pocket) and she gave me a number for a man who was looking for a hunting property in rural Missouri.

We'll call him Keith and it was hard not to like him. He's the kind of person who can talk about anything to anyone. We talked for an hour on that first phone call; about ten minutes of that was what I could do for Keith as his agent, the other fifty were about hunting, sharing memories of the men who had first taken us into a tree stand. After that phone call I had a new friend.

A few days later, a $6 million property just hit the market, and within hours Keith and I were walking the property.

That tour ended with Keith putting a cash offer on the property, and the invite: "You should come out for opening weekend."

I said yes in a heartbeat.

"Dude, this sale literally saves our brokerage from going under," Dillion spewed excitement when I called him and told him about it from the coffee shop I stopped at on my way back to the office to get the paperwork ready.

My day was about to get even better.

I had just ordered my coffee, black Americano like I always get, when she walked in.

It was the beginning of archery season, and she, we'll call her Maddy, walked in wearing full camo and covered in blood.

She looked a little out of sorts, like she was looking for someone and wasn't sure who, and when they called out my order she rushed up to the counter to grab it just as I did.

I didn't think anything of it at the time; the place had online ordering, so it wasn't odd that she didn't place an order.

Maybe a little odd that this angel in full camo and covered in blood ordered a coffee black, but any doubts I had about why she was there were dispelled when she blushed as we both reached for the drink.

"Good hunt?" I asked, letting her take the drink.

"Good," she confirmed. "Got a nice ten-pointer, better now that I've got coffee."

We shared a table and talked for over an hour. Dillion was so mad that I was late to the office but I didn't care. I'd just pulled the brokerage out of the red, and while I didn't know it at the time, I'd just met my fiancée.

On my way out of the coffee shop I ripped up the card Valentine had given me. Not that I believed her, more out of spite than anything. I controlled my destiny, at least that's what I thought, and continued to think for the next five years.

A few months later, my mother's cancer went into remission. A few months after that our brokerage had become successful enough that we were able to hire another agent.

Year after year we turned a profit. My mom's cancer never returned, my brokerage was successful, and I was starting to think I'd found my better half in Maddy, so five years to the day since we'd met, I proposed.

I'd forgotten all about the woman I'd met at the diner. She hadn't forgotten about me.

That night was the first time I saw her again. I woke in bed next to Maddy, who was sound asleep, and I swear on my life I saw Ms. Valentine sitting on the edge of the bed, looking down at me and grinning like a cat.

It frightened me so much I let out a yell that woke Maddy. Valentine was gone, like the vestiges of a dream fading into reality. When she asked what was wrong, I told her nothing, just a bad dream, nothing to worry about, but I lay awake for the rest of the night remembering what she had told me about the price I would have to pay for all the good that came to me.

I couldn't believe it, but the next day I called a client only to hear Valentine answer the phone.

"Valentine?" I asked, trying to keep the panic from my voice, only for my client to reply in their normal voice asking me what I was talking about.

At first I thought I was seeing things; I was even considering going to see someone. That was when things took a hard left turn off the edge of a cliff.

Since I'd sold Keith his property, he, Dillion and I had started a tradition. Every year the three of us would go out to his property and hunt opening weekend.

Over the five years we'd created a lot of great memories there and this year I had no reason to suspect things would be different.

Opening morning found me tired and cold in a tree stand on Keith's property when a beautiful ten-point buck walked out of the tree line about 100 yards away from where I was sitting.

The animal just stood there, looking around, not a care in the world, almost as if he were waiting for me to take the shot.

So, the familiar adrenaline rush coursing through my veins, I raised my rifle, put the crosshairs just behind his shoulder and squeezed the trigger.

The bang that exploded from the gun was deafening, and the animal dropped instantly, a perfect shot.

Giddy, I hopped out of the tree stand and texted the group chat with Keith and Dillion to tell them I'd got one, but as soon as I got to where the animal had fallen, dread turned my stomach into knots.

There was no deer, and instead lying there in a pool of blood was Dillion. His eyes were empty and lifeless, looking up blankly at the sky, and there was a giant hole in his chest where the bullet had passed through.

This couldn't be happening. I knew it was a deer I'd shot at; after all, the thing stood there and gave me ample time to look at it. We were in an open field; there had been nothing to obstruct my vision and no hint of the blaze orange Missouri hunters are required to wear, but there Dillion was with a bullet hole through his bright orange jacket and what remained of his heart and his left side lung splattered all over the ground.

I don't know how long I held him, but when Keith arrived all he could say was, "What did you do?" as he screamed and dialed the police, and I swear as he did I saw her, Ms. Valentine, just beyond the tree line grinning at me like she'd won some battle I didn't even know I was fighting.

Keith doesn't believe me that it was an accident and neither do the police; they think my text is something I planted as an alibi.

Why would I have murdered my business partner? Well, it just so happens some client money went missing from the company escrow account a few weeks earlier. Dillion and I had been looking into it, and couldn't make heads or tails of it. There were no transfers out of the account, no suspicious transactions, no evidence the money even went anywhere outside of a sudden drop in the number behind the account. This is all the police needed for a motivation. They can't prove it, and it's not true, but they think Dillion found out I'd stolen the money so I killed him to eliminate the problem.

I know how bad it looks but I swear it isn't true. No one believes me.

Since then my life has been in a downward spiral. I've lost the brokerage. Every single one of the agents working for me quit. I've been calling Keith, sending him emails and messages through social media; not a single one was read.

The Valentine sightings are getting worse. I see her following me down the street or in the car behind me, matching each of my turns when I'm driving somewhere. A few days ago I had a job interview and she was the secretary in an office, sitting there and welcoming me as if she wasn't the cause of all my misery.

I'm not sleeping and have been obsessing over finding out who she is. There's nothing on the internet about her; the closest thing my research has turned up is the legend of Robert Johnson, the blues guitarist who sold his soul to the devil at a crossroads in Mississippi.

The whole time Maddy has been my rock. I didn't tell her about Valentine (how could I?), but she never doubted me when I told her why I shot Dillion or that I knew nothing about where the money in our escrow account went.

Then a few days ago my mom called to tell me her cancer was back. I was in the bedroom, trying to stay strong and keep the tears from streaming down my cheeks while she gave me the news.

She was going back to the hospital and wanted to give me her doctor's number so I rummaged around my bedside table for pen and paper to write it down.

Finding none on my side of the bed I moved over to Maddy's. When I pulled out a black card with a gold emblazoned dove in the corner with only the name "Jane" and a phone number written on the card, my blood ran cold.

I was still holding it in my hands when I hung up with my mom. I maybe sat there for an hour looking at it wondering if I was dreaming when Maddy walked into the room.

"Oh," is all she could say when she found me, her eyes wide. "I thought I threw that away."

When I asked her where it came from, I felt like I was reliving the day five years ago.

She'd met a strange woman in the same diner as me who called herself Ms. Valentine. Maddy's dad had just died and she wasn't doing so well, and Valentine promised Maddy all the happiness in the world.

All she had to do was call the number on the card, so Maddy did, and Jane had told her to meet a man at a coffee shop two days later.

When Maddy asked who, Jane just said, "You'll know when you see him."

Maddy told me she never met whoever was supposed to be there, but she'd met me. She told me this with a sad smile.

I tried to ask her more about Valentine, about what else the woman in the diner told her, but Maddy refused. She said she didn't want to speak about it, so I didn't press on it, didn't tell her about my own experiences with Valentine either.

That was that. We went about our evening, but Maddy seemed different. Not her normal self. Withdrawn, like something was eating away at her, but whenever I asked, she'd say she was fine, but I knew she wasn't.

No one can find Jane's card. I had ripped mine up but Valentine had promised a horrible death if anyone found it and I didn't kill them first.

Now I had found Maddy's card, and Maddy had met Ms. Valentine, who had made a similar promise.

Did she promise the same fate to Maddy if anyone found that card?

I feel like she did; I keep catching Maddy staring at the kitchen knives, her face pale and vacant, only to look away with a nervous smile when I ask her what she's looking at.

One of us is living on borrowed time.
