I should probably start by saying that, until a few days ago, I didn’t believe in any of this.

Ghosts, demons, shadow people, alternate dimensions, whatever. I wasn’t aggressively skeptical about it. I didn’t spend my free time arguing with strangers online about whether the blurry thing in their security-camera footage was a spirit or a moth. I just never had a reason to believe there was anything around us that couldn’t be explained by something ordinary.

I’m thirty-six years old. I have a job. I pay bills. I forget to buy groceries until there’s nothing left in the refrigerator except condiments and something in a plastic container I’m afraid to open. My life isn’t particularly exciting, and until this week, I was perfectly happy with that.

I’m telling you this because what has been happening to me doesn’t make sense without understanding the person it started happening to.

I wasn’t looking for any of this.

I didn’t perform a ritual. I didn’t visit some supposedly haunted building. I didn’t buy an antique box from a mysterious old woman who warned me never to open it. Nothing happened that I can point to and say, There. That’s where I fucked up.

I just started seeing things.

At first, they were easy to dismiss.

Something strange in the corner of my vision. A shape somewhere it shouldn’t have been. That uncomfortable feeling that I’d seen something clearly for half a second, only for my brain to immediately start explaining why I hadn’t.

I did what I think most people would do.

I ignored it.

Then ignoring it stopped being possible.

And now there’s something in my apartment that I haven’t seen since I was a child.

His name is Mr. Crooked.

At least, that’s what I used to call him.

The problem is, until he came back… I didn't remember him at all.

I saw it on a Tuesday.

That detail matters because Tuesdays meant the drive to Riverside for the quarterly meeting with Halstead's distribution team, which meant leaving at six-thirty to beat traffic, which meant I was already two coffees deep and running on five hours of sleep. So when I saw something standing in the trees beside Route 9, my first thought wasn't \*what the hell is that\*. It was \*I need to get more sleep\*.

The meeting went fine. Halstead's people wanted to renegotiate shipping rates again, which they did every quarter like clockwork, and I gave them the same speech about fuel costs and driver availability that I'd given them in July. Everybody nodded. Everybody signed the same contract with a three percent adjustment that we'd all known was coming. I was back in my car by eleven.

I stopped for gas outside Riverside. The guy at the pump across from me had a dog in his truck, some kind of retriever mix, and it was losing its mind barking at something in the field behind the station. Just barking and barking, throwing itself against the window. The owner didn't even look up from his phone.

I thought about that dog for most of the drive home.

Not because it was unusual. Dogs bark at nothing all the time. But I kept wondering what it had seen that I hadn't. Or if it had seen anything at all. Maybe it just didn't like the smell of diesel. Maybe it was an anxious dog. Maybe I was overthinking things because I'd spent two hours in a conference room with people who used the phrase "moving forward" seventeen times.

I saw the thing again on the way back.

Same stretch of Route 9, about forty minutes outside the city. There's a section where the highway cuts through state forest, and the trees come right up to the shoulder on both sides. It's the kind of road where you're supposed to watch for deer, especially at dusk, but it was barely noon and the sun was out and I wasn't watching for anything except the idiot in the Camry who kept drifting into my lane.

The thing was standing between two trees on the right side.

I noticed it the way you notice a street sign or a mailbox—peripheral, automatic, gone before your brain fully processes it. Except my brain didn't want to process it. It wanted to file it under \*probably a person\* and move on.

But it wasn't shaped like a person.

It was too thin. Not thin like a skinny person. Thin like something that shouldn't be able to stand upright. If I had to describe it—and I've spent three weeks trying not to—I'd say it looked like someone had taken a human figure and stretched it vertically until the proportions stopped making sense. Long enough that it should have fallen over. Narrow enough that I shouldn't have been able to see it from a car going sixty miles an hour.

I didn't slow down. Didn't get a better look. Didn't do anything except keep driving while my brain helpfully supplied a list of reasonable explanations:

A trick of the light through the trees.

A dead branch or a post or some kind of trail marker.

Fatigue. Definitely fatigue.

I'd been working ten-hour days for two weeks straight because Marcus was out on paternity leave and somebody had to cover his routes. I was tired. Tired people saw things that weren't there. That was normal. That was fine.

I didn't think about it again until I got home.

My apartment's in one of those converted industrial buildings that developers bought up ten years ago and turned into "luxury loft living," which means exposed brick, high ceilings, and plumbing that sounds like someone's murdering a accordion whenever anyone on the third floor takes a shower. I've been there four years. It's fine. The rent's almost reasonable and there's a decent Thai place downstairs and my neighbors are quiet except for the guy in 3F who plays bass at two in the morning sometimes.

I made dinner. Pasta with jarred sauce because I'm not a person who cooks after a six-hour round trip. I ate it standing at the counter while scrolling through my phone, which is how I eat most meals, and then I watched half of a documentary about deep-sea creatures before falling asleep on the couch.

I woke up at two-fifteen in the morning with my neck at an angle that was going to hurt for three days.

The TV was still on, playing some true-crime thing I hadn't selected. A woman's voice was explaining how investigators had found trace evidence under the victim's fingernails. I turned it off and stood there in the dark for a minute, trying to decide if I was awake enough to brush my teeth or if I was just going to collapse into bed and hate myself in the morning.

That's when I thought about the thing by the highway again.

Not thought about it, exactly. More like my brain served up the image without asking permission. The shape between the trees. The wrongness of its proportions. The way it had been standing so still.

I went to bed.

I didn't see it again until Friday.

Friday meant the return trip to Riverside because Halstead's people had "forgotten" to include the updated insurance certificates in the contract packet, which they absolutely hadn't forgotten, they just wanted me to drive out there again so they could pitch me on their new logistics software in person. I said no to the software. I took the certificates. I left.

Same route home. Same stretch of forest.

Same thing standing between the trees.

This time I was paying attention.

It was in almost the exact same spot, maybe twenty feet further up the road. Still impossibly thin. Still upright. I had maybe three seconds to look at it as I passed, and in those three seconds my brain tried very hard to turn it into something normal.

A person in dark clothing.

A scarecrow.

A fucking optical illusion.

But it wasn't any of those things, and the worst part—the part that sat in my chest like a stone for the rest of the drive—was that I couldn't actually describe what it \*was\*. Every time I tried to picture it clearly, the details slid away. I could remember the feeling of seeing it. The sense of wrongness. The way my hands had tightened on the steering wheel without me telling them to.

But the shape itself? Gone.

I thought about calling someone. My sister, maybe. Emma's good at talking me down when I'm spiraling about something. But what was I going to say? \*Hey, I keep seeing a weird shape by the highway and I can't remember what it looks like\*? She'd tell me I was working too hard. She'd tell me to take a weekend off. She'd be right.

I didn't call her.

I went home and had a beer and watched the rest of the deep-sea documentary, which turned out to be a mistake because it was all footage of things that lived in total darkness and looked like they'd been designed by a committee of nightmares. There was this one creature it was some kind of anglerfish. Mostly mouth and teeth with a little glowing lure dangling in front of its face. The narrator explained that it lived two miles below the surface where no light ever reached, and it spent its entire life waiting in the dark for something to swim close enough to eat.

I turned it off and went to bed at a reasonable hour.

I didn't sleep well.

Sunday I drove out to my parents' place for dinner, which is a thing I do twice a month because my mom will call me every day if I don't. It's an hour west, opposite direction from Riverside, and the whole drive I kept watching the trees. I didn't see anything. Just trees and road and the occasional deer carcass on the shoulder.

Dinner was fine. Pot roast. My dad asked about work. My mom asked if I was seeing anyone, which is her way of asking why I'm not married yet, and I gave her the same answer I always give, which is that I'm busy and dating is exhausting and I'll get around to it eventually. My sister called halfway through dessert to show off the baby, who's four months old and looks like a potato with my brother-in-law's nose. Everyone agreed he was beautiful. He spit up on Emma's shoulder. We all laughed.

Normal. Everything was completely normal.

I left around eight and took the back roads home because there'd been an accident on the highway. The route added twenty minutes but it was a nice night, cool and clear, and I didn't mind the drive. I had the windows down. There was a decent song on the radio. I felt better than I had in days.

Then I passed the overpass on Miller Road.

I'd driven under that overpass a hundred times. It's nothing special—just a concrete bridge carrying the freight line over a county road that nobody uses except locals. There's graffiti on the supports. Weeds growing up through the gravel on both sides. A rusted guardrail that probably hasn't been replaced since the eighties.

Something was on the underside of the bridge.

I saw it in my headlights as I approached. A dark shape pressed against the concrete, moving slowly from one side to the other. Not walking. Not crawling. Something in between.

I slowed down without meaning to.

The shape stopped moving.

I was maybe thirty feet away when my brain finally caught up and started screaming at me to speed up, get past it, don't stop, don't look, just go. I hit the gas. The car lurched forward. I went under the bridge doing fifty in a thirty-five zone and I didn't look up.

But I wanted to.

That was the worst part. Some stupid, curious part of my brain wanted to stop the car and get out and look up at the underside of that bridge to see what the hell was up there.

I didn't stop.

I drove home doing sixty the whole way, checking my mirrors every thirty seconds like something might be following me. Nothing was. Just empty road and my own headlights reflecting off the painted lines.

I parked in the garage under my building and sat in the car for five minutes with the engine off, trying to convince myself that I'd seen a raccoon or a possum or some kind of bird. Something normal. Something with a reasonable explanation.

It didn't work.

I went upstairs and poured myself a drink I didn't want and sat on the couch in the dark, thinking about the thing under the bridge and the thing by the highway and the way that dog had been barking at the field behind the gas station.

I thought about calling my doctor.

I thought about all the reasonable explanations: stress, fatigue, eye strain, migraines, a brain tumor, early-onset schizophrenia, carbon monoxide poisoning.

I thought about the fact that I was thirty-six years old and nothing strange had ever happened to me before and there was absolutely no reason why anything strange should be happening to me now.

Then I finished my drink and went to bed.

I had to drive to Riverside again on Tuesday.

I didn't want to. I thought about calling in sick, which I never do, or asking Jenkins to cover the route, which would've required explaining why, which I couldn't do. So I got in my car at six-thirty in the morning and drove east on Route 9 and told myself I wasn't going to look at the trees when I passed that section of forest.

I looked.

The thing was still there.

Same spot. Same impossible proportions. Still standing between the same two trees like it had been there for days.

Because it had been there for days.

I'd driven past it on Tuesday. I'd driven past it on Friday. And now it was Tuesday again and the thing hadn't moved.

Four days.

It had been standing there for four days.

I made it another mile before I had to pull over. There's a scenic overlook just past the forest section—one of those little gravel lots with a historical marker that nobody reads and a trash can that's always overflowing. I pulled in and put the car in park and sat there with my hands on the wheel, trying to breathe normally.

Four days.

People didn't stand in the woods for four days. Animals didn't stand still for four days. Nothing stood still for four days except things that were dead or things that weren't alive to begin with.

I looked in the rearview mirror.

The forest was behind me, maybe a quarter mile back. I couldn't see the thing from here. Too far away. Too many trees in between.

But I knew it was there.

And worse—much worse—I knew that when I drove back this afternoon, it would still be there.

I sat in that parking lot for twenty minutes before I could make myself keep driving.

The meeting with Halstead's people was a blur. I nodded in the right places. I signed something. I shook hands. I have no idea what we talked about.

I drove home at four-thirty, earlier than usual because I wanted to pass that section of forest before it got dark.

The thing was gone.

I slowed down without meaning to, scanning the trees on both sides of the road. Nothing. Just forest and shadows and the afternoon sun filtering through the branches.

I should have felt relieved.

I didn't.

Because if it was gone, that meant it had moved.

And if it had moved, that meant it could move.

I drove the rest of the way home doing seventy, and I didn't look at the trees again.

I made it home by six. Parked in my usual spot in the garage. Sat in the car with the engine off for maybe two minutes, listening to the ticking of cooling metal and the muffled sound of someone's car alarm going off three levels down.

Normal sounds. Normal evening.

I went upstairs.

My apartment looked exactly the way I'd left it that morning. Breakfast dishes in the sink. Jacket on the back of the chair. The living room still smelled faintly like the coffee I'd made at five-thirty. Everything was fine. Everything was exactly the same as it had been for four years.

I ordered Thai food because I didn't want to think about cooking. Pad see ew, spring rolls, extra peanut sauce. The same thing I ordered every Tuesday when I got back from Riverside. The delivery guy showed up forty minutes later, handed me the bag, and left without making eye contact, which was also normal. I ate standing at the counter, scrolling through my phone, reading nothing.

I kept thinking about the thing in the woods.

Not thinking about it, exactly. More like my brain kept returning the image uninvited. The proportions that couldn't be right. The same spot, four days running. And then nothing.

I tried watching something. Picked a movie I'd seen before because I didn't want to focus on anything new. Some action thing with a lot of explosions and a plot I couldn't follow even when I was paying attention. I made it about forty minutes before I gave up and turned it off.

It was eight-thirty.

I thought about calling Emma. My sister's good at talking me down when I'm spiraling, and I was definitely spiraling. But what was I going to say? \*Hey, I've been seeing weird shapes and I think I'm losing my mind\*? She'd tell me to see a doctor. She'd be right. I should see a doctor.

I didn't call her.

Instead I did something I almost never do, I went to bed early. Nine-fifteen. Brushed my teeth, changed into the T-shirt and shorts I sleep in, set an alarm I probably didn't need. The bedroom was dark except for the streetlight coming through the window, casting long shadows across the floor and the foot of the bed.

I got under the covers and closed my eyes and tried very hard not to think about things standing in forests or moving across the underside of bridges.

I must have fallen asleep eventually because I don't remember anything after that until I woke up.

The room was darker than it should have been.

That was the first thing I noticed. Not a sound, not a movement. Just the quality of the darkness. The streetlight outside my window is bright enough that I can usually see the outline of my dresser, the chair in the corner, the doorway leading to the hall. But now everything was dim, muted, like something was blocking the light.

I opened my eyes fully.

Someone was sitting at the foot of my bed.

My brain didn't process it immediately. For maybe three seconds I just lay there, staring at the dark shape, waiting for my eyes to adjust and turn it into something normal. A pile of laundry. The jacket I'd left on the chair, somehow moved. A trick of shadows.

It didn't turn into anything normal.

It was huge. Seated, and its head still nearly brushed the ceiling. My eyes kept trying to find the logic of it—where the joints were, how the limbs connected—and kept failing, sliding off the geometry of it the way a hand slides off wet glass.

It was looking at me.

Not in the direction of me. At me. I could feel it the way you feel a finger pressed against the back of your skull.

My fingers had the blanket in a death grip. My chest was heaving. Some part of my brain was cycling through sleep paralysis, stroke, aneurysm, carbon monoxide, anything, anything, anything—

The thing tilted its head.

Too far. Way too far. Past the point where a neck stops a neck from going, and then it just—stayed there. Cocked at that angle like a question mark. Like something had broken a long time ago and nobody had ever bothered to fix it.

Then the darkness of its face split open.

The smile went wider than a face should allow. I don't mean slightly wider. I mean the kind of wide that makes you understand, on some animal level below thought, that what you are looking at does not have the same rules as you.

"You got old."

The voice came out low and unhurried, rough as something dragged across asphalt. It didn't sound like a threat. That was almost worse. It sounded like someone remarking on the weather.

My mouth opened. What came out wasn't a word.

It leaned forward.

"You don't remember me."

It wasn't a question.

I shook my head. Or I think I did. Everything felt distant and slow, like I was moving underwater.

The smile widened.

"That's disappointing."

My brain was screaming at me to move, to run, to do something, but my body wouldn't cooperate. I managed to push myself up slightly, propping myself on my elbows, putting maybe six more inches between me and it.

"What—" My voice cracked. I tried again. "What are you?"

The thing considered this for a moment, head still tilted at that horrible angle.

"You used to know."

"I don't—I've never—" I couldn't finish the sentence. Couldn't make my thoughts line up into anything coherent.

It shifted its weight, and I heard the bedframe creak under it. That sound, that ordinary and physical sound, somehow made everything worse. Because it meant this wasn't a dream. Dreams didn't have weight. Dreams didn't make furniture creak.

"You're not real," I said, and even as the words left my mouth I knew how stupid they sounded.

The thing made a sound that might have been a laugh. Low and rattling.

"You always said that."

Always.

The word hung in the air between us.

"I've never seen you before in my life," I said, but even as I said it, something in my chest tightened. Not quite recognition. Not quite memory. Something older than both.

"You look afraid," it said.

I didn't answer.

A long pause. Then, almost pleasantly:

"If I was going to eat you, I would have done it when your bones were softer."

The thing watched me with what I was increasingly certain were eyes, though I still couldn't make out any features clearly in the darkness.

"Who are you?" I said.

Another pause. Longer this time.

"You called me Mr. Crooked."

The name hit me like cold water.

I didn't remember it. Not consciously. But something in my brain responded to it anyway, some deep animal part that existed below language and logic. My hands were shaking. I realized I was gripping the blanket hard enough that my knuckles hurt.

"That's not—" I couldn't finish. Couldn't explain what it wasn't.

Mr. Crooked tilted his head the other direction, just as far, just as wrong.

"You used to hide under the covers," he said. "You thought I couldn't see you."

"Stop."

"You would count. One, two, three. You thought if you counted high enough, I would leave."

"Stop."

"Sometimes I did."

I was breathing too fast. The room felt smaller. Darker. I needed to get out. Needed to move. Needed to do literally anything except sit here having a conversation with something that shouldn't exist.

"This isn't real," I said again, and this time it sounded like begging.

Mr. Crooked didn't respond immediately. He just sat there, enormous and impossible, watching me with that terrible tilted head and that smile that was somehow worse than any expression of anger or hunger would have been.

Then he said, very quietly:

"You should have stayed blind."

Before I could ask what that meant, something moved outside my window.

Not a small movement. Not a bird or a branch or anything that belonged in the normal catalog of nighttime sounds. Something large. Something that made the glass rattle slightly in its frame.

Mr. Crooked's head snapped toward the window.

The movement was so fast, so sudden, that I flinched backward. And for the first time since I'd woken up, I saw his expression change. The smile disappeared. His entire posture shifted, coiling, like a dog that had just heard something in the distance.

"Don't look," he said.

His voice was completely different now. No amusement. No conversational tone. Just flat, immediate command.

I was already looking toward the window.

"Don't—"

Too late.

My eyes had already moved. Already focused on the window, the glass, the streetlight beyond casting its familiar orange glow across the brick wall of the neighboring building.

Everything looked normal.

The window was closed. The blinds were half-drawn the way I always left them, angled down so the streetlight wouldn't shine directly into my face while I slept. Through the gaps I could see the apartment building across the alley, maybe thirty feet away. Four stories of dark windows and fire escapes. The same view I'd had for four years.

I didn't see anything wrong.

The glass wasn't broken. Nothing was pressed against it. The streetlight was still working, still casting that same orange glow across everything, turning the brick wall the color of rust.

I started to relax. Started to think maybe I'd imagined the sound, or maybe it had been something ordinary after all. A cat on the fire escape. Something falling in the alley below.

Then I realized the shadow on the neighboring building was wrong.

Not wrong like it was shaped strangely. Wrong like it shouldn't have been there at all.

The streetlight was behind my building, which meant the light should have been hitting the neighboring wall straight on. There shouldn't have been any shadows except for the fire escape, and those shadows should have been falling to the left, following the angle of the light.

But there was a shadow directly in the center of the wall.

Tall. Narrow. Perfectly still.

And it was between my window and the building across the alley.

Which meant something was outside my window.

Hanging there.

In the air.

Three stories up.

My brain tried very hard to make it make sense. A trick of the light. A reflection. Something on my side of the glass casting a shadow I was misinterpreting.

But I could see the edges of it now. The way it interrupted the light. The way the orange glow seemed to bend slightly around it, like water flowing around a stone.

It was huge.

Bigger than Mr. Crooked, though I couldn't tell exactly how much bigger because I couldn't see where it ended. The shadow it cast was at least eight feet tall, maybe more, and narrow enough that it looked almost two-dimensional. Like someone had taken a photograph of something and hung it in the air outside my window.

It wasn't moving.

That was somehow the worst part. It wasn't trying to get in. Wasn't scratching at the glass or pressing against it or doing anything that would have made sense in the context of a threat. It was just there. Hanging in the empty air three stories above the alley, casting its impossible shadow across the brick wall, completely still.

I couldn't see details. Couldn't make out a face or limbs or anything that would have helped me understand what I was looking at. Just the outline. The shape of something that shouldn't exist, suspended in space like it was the most natural thing in the world.

"Stop looking at it," Mr. Crooked said behind me.

His voice was different now. Low. Flat. No amusement in it at all.

"What is it?" I whispered.

I don't know why I whispered. The thing was outside. It couldn't hear me through the glass.

Could it?

"Doesn't matter," Mr. Crooked said. "Stop looking."

But I couldn't stop looking. Couldn't make myself turn away. Because now that I'd seen it, now that I knew it was there, the idea of turning my back to it felt impossible.

"How long has it been there?" I asked.

"Daniel—"

"How long?"

"Always," Mr. Crooked said. "Now stop looking at it."

The shadow moved.

Not much. Just a slight shift, like someone adjusting their weight. But it was enough. Enough to confirm that it wasn't a trick of the light or a reflection or anything my brain could rationalize away.

It was real.

It was alive.

And it had just noticed me noticing it.

The change was immediate. The shadow on the wall began to darken, to sharpen, like someone had turned up the contrast on a photograph. The edges became more defined. More present.

"Daniel," Mr. Crooked said, and there was something in his voice I'd never heard before. "Look at me. Right now."

I tried. I really did. Tried to turn my head, to look away, to focus on literally anything else.

But I couldn't.

Because the thing outside had started to turn.

Slowly. So slowly I almost didn't notice it at first. The shadow on the wall began to shift, rotating, changing angle. And as it turned, I started to see more of it. Not details, exactly. Just... more. The suggestion of length. The outline of something that extended much farther than the shadow had implied.

It had been facing away from me.

Now it was turning toward my window.

"Daniel." Mr. Crooked's voice was sharp now. Urgent. "Look at me."

The shadow stopped moving.

It was facing my window now. Facing me.

I still couldn't see details. Couldn't make out eyes or a mouth or anything that would have made it comprehensible. But I could feel it looking at me. The same way I'd felt Mr. Crooked looking at me earlier. That weight. That attention.

It knew.

It knew I could see it.

The temperature in my bedroom dropped.

Not gradually. All at once. Like someone had opened a freezer door directly into my room. My breath came out in a visible cloud. The sweat on my skin turned cold.

"Fuck," Mr. Crooked said quietly.

Then the thing outside moved toward the window.

It didn't fly. Didn't walk through the air. It simply moved, closing the distance between itself and the glass in a way that made no sense. One moment it was thirty feet away, hanging in the space between buildings. The next it was right there, pressed against my window, and I hadn't seen the transition.

I could see it clearly now.

I wished I couldn't.

It was thin. Impossibly thin. Like something that had been compressed, flattened, reduced to almost nothing. But it was also long. So long. Its body extended up past the top of my window and down below the bottom, and I couldn't see where either end terminated. It might have been twenty feet long. It might have been a hundred.

And it was looking at me.

I couldn't see eyes. Couldn't see a face. But I could feel its attention like a physical pressure against my chest, making it hard to breathe.

The glass began to frost over.

Not from the outside. From the inside.

Ice spread across the window in branching patterns, growing faster than ice should grow, covering the glass in seconds. But I could still see through it. Could still see the dark shape pressed against the other side.

Waiting.

"Don't move," Mr. Crooked said.

I wasn't moving. Couldn't move. Every muscle in my body had locked up.

The thing outside pressed closer.

The glass didn't break. Didn't crack. But it began to bend inward, bowing like it was made of rubber instead of glass, bulging into my bedroom in a way that glass absolutely should not be able to bulge.

"That's not possible," I whispered.

"Stop talking," Mr. Crooked said.

The glass bent further. Six inches into my room now. Then a foot. The frame around it began to creak.

And then I saw something that made my brain simply refuse to process what I was looking at.

The thing outside was reaching through the glass.

Not breaking it. Not shattering it. Reaching through it, like the glass was water, like solid matter was just a suggestion. Something thin and dark and impossibly long began to emerge from the center of the bulging window, extending into my bedroom.

It looked like a finger.

If a finger could be three feet long and have too many joints.

"Daniel," Mr. Crooked said. "When I tell you to move, you move. Understand?"

I couldn't answer. Couldn't look away from the thing coming through my window.

The finger extended further. Four feet now. Five. Reaching toward my bed. Toward me.

I could see the tip of it now. It wasn't pointed. It was flat. Circular. And in the center of that circle was something that looked almost like a mouth.

"Move," Mr. Crooked said.

I threw myself sideways off the bed.

The finger struck where I'd been lying a fraction of a second earlier. The mattress collapsed inward with a sound like a gasp, like all the air had been sucked out of it at once. The sheets turned gray. Then black. Then they simply weren't there anymore.

I hit the floor hard, scrambled backward until my back hit the wall.

The finger withdrew slightly, then began sweeping across the bed, searching. Everywhere it touched, matter disappeared. The blanket. The mattress. The bed frame. All of it just gone, consumed, erased.

I needed to get out.

I lurched to my feet and ran for the bedroom door.

"Don't open it," Mr. Crooked said.

I grabbed the handle anyway.

The door wouldn't open.

I pulled harder. The handle turned, the latch disengaged, but the door itself wouldn't move. It was like something on the other side was holding it closed.

"I said don't open it," Mr. Crooked said.

"Why not?" I shouted.

"Because it's on both sides now."

I let go of the handle like it had burned me.

Stepped back.

Looked at the door.

There was frost spreading across it. The same branching patterns that had covered the window. Growing from the bottom up, climbing toward the ceiling.

"How?" I asked.

"You looked at it," Mr. Crooked said. "So it looked back. Now it knows where you are."

The finger had stopped searching the bed. It had gone still, angled toward me. Toward the sound of my voice.

"Stop talking," Mr. Crooked said.

The finger moved.

Fast.

It whipped across the room toward me and I dove sideways, hit the floor, rolled. The finger struck the wall where I'd been standing and the drywall simply ceased to exist. I could see through into the neighboring apartment. See someone's darkened living room. See their furniture.

See another finger reaching through their wall.

"Oh god," I said.

"Shut up," Mr. Crooked said.

But I couldn't shut up. Couldn't stop the sound that was trying to climb out of my throat. Because I understood now. Understood what Mr. Crooked had meant.

It was on both sides.

It wasn't just outside my window anymore. It was outside every window. Every wall. Every surface. It had surrounded the entire room and now it was reaching through, searching, trying to find me.

A second finger emerged from the ceiling.

A third from the floor, punching up through the hardwood.

A fourth from the wall behind me.

All of them searching. Sweeping through the air. And everywhere they touched, matter disappeared.

My desk. Gone.

My dresser. Gone.

The lamp. The bookshelf. The chair. All of it erased, consumed, pulled into whatever impossible space existed on the other side of the surfaces.

I was running out of room.

"Crooked," I said. "Crooked, what do I do?"

He didn't answer.

I looked for him. Couldn't see him. He'd been standing at the foot of my bed, but the bed was gone now and so was he.

"Crooked!"

One of the fingers changed direction.

Came straight at me.

I tried to dodge but there was nowhere left to go. My back was against the wall and the finger was coming and I knew with absolute certainty that if it touched me I would disappear just like everything else.

Then Mr. Crooked was there.

Between me and the finger.

I didn't see him move. Didn't see him cross the room. He was just suddenly there, and the finger struck him instead of me.

It should have erased him.

It didn't.

The finger hit Mr. Crooked's chest and stopped. Just stopped. Like it had struck something solid. Something real in a way that nothing else in the room was real.

Mr. Crooked reached up and grabbed the finger.

His hand closed around it.

And he pulled.

The sound that came from outside was like nothing I'd ever heard. Not a scream. Not an animal sound. Something else. Something that made my teeth ache and my vision blur and my inner ear scream that the world had tilted sideways.

Mr. Crooked pulled harder.

The finger began to withdraw. Not smoothly. It fought. Thrashed. But Mr. Crooked didn't let go. He dragged it backward, pulled it toward the window, and I could see now that he was impossibly strong. Strong enough to move something that shouldn't be able to be moved.

The other fingers stopped searching.

They all turned toward Mr. Crooked.

"Bad idea," he said quietly.

They struck simultaneously.

Four fingers. All at once. All aimed at Mr. Crooked.

He let go of the first finger and moved.

I'd thought he was fast before. I was wrong. He was faster than fast. Faster than anything that size should be able to move. He caught two of the fingers, one in each hand, and slammed them together.

The sound from outside came again. Louder.  
 

The remaining fingers retreated. Pulled back through the walls, the ceiling, the floor. The one Mr. Crooked was holding tried to follow but he didn't let go.

"Leaving?" he asked.

He wasn't talking to me.

The finger went completely still.

"Good," Mr. Crooked said.

He released it.

It withdrew immediately. Pulled back through the window so fast it blurred. The glass stopped bending. The frost began to recede. And within seconds, the window looked normal again.

The shadow on the neighboring building was gone.

My bedroom was not normal.

Half my furniture had disappeared. There were holes in the walls, the floor, the ceiling. I could see into three different neighboring apartments. Could see the night sky through a gap in the roof.

And I was shaking so hard I couldn't stand.

My legs gave out. I slid down the wall until I was sitting on the floor, arms wrapped around my knees, trying to remember how to breathe.

Mr. Crooked turned to look at me.

He wasn't hurt. Wasn't even marked. The fingers that should have erased him hadn't left a trace.

"You're an idiot," he said.

I couldn't argue with that.

"What was that?" I managed.

"Something you shouldn't have looked at."

"You said that already—"

"Because it's still true."

I looked at the window. At the holes in my walls. At the space where my bed used to be.

"It tried to kill me," I said.

"No."

"What do you mean no? It—"

"It tried to process you," Mr. Crooked said. "There's a difference."

"Process me?"

"You looked at it. So it looked back. And when it looked back, it saw something that didn't fit. Something that shouldn't be able to see it. So it tried to correct the error."

"By erasing me?"

"By returning you to a state where you couldn't see it anymore."

The words took a moment to land.

"You're saying it was trying to make me stop seeing it?"

"Yes."

"By killing me."

"Death would have been a side effect," Mr. Crooked said. "It doesn't understand death. It only understands presence and absence."

I stared at him.

"That's insane."

"That's accurate."

I pressed my palms against my eyes. Tried to think. Couldn't think. My brain felt like it had been shaken loose inside my skull.

"How did you stop it?" I asked.

"I didn't stop it. I convinced it to leave."

"How?"

"I'm more trouble than you're worth."

He said it matter-of-factly. Like it was obvious.

"What does that mean?"

"It means I've been here longer than it has," Mr. Crooked said. "And it knows what happens when things try to process me."

I didn't ask what happened. Didn't want to know.

My hands were still shaking. I looked down at them. Saw blood on my palms. Must have cut them when I hit the floor. I hadn't even felt it.

"I could have died," I said quietly.

"Yes."

"Because I looked at it."

"Yes."

"That's all I did. I just looked."

"That's all you needed to do," Mr. Crooked said. "Looking creates connection. Connection creates interaction. Interaction creates consequence."

"That's not fair."

"It's not about fair. It's about rules."

"What rules? I don't know any rules—"

"You know one now," Mr. Crooked said. "Don't look at things you don't understand."

I wanted to laugh. Wanted to scream. Wanted to do anything except sit on my destroyed bedroom floor at three in the morning trying to process the fact that I'd almost been erased from existence by something that didn't even understand what killing was.

"How many of them are there?" I asked.

Mr. Crooked was quiet for a long moment.

Then he said:

"If you can see me, you can see them."

I looked up at him. At his impossible height and his wrong proportions and his head that was still tilted at that terrible angle.

"Them?" I asked. "What do you mean them? How many—"

Mr. Crooked turned his head toward the window.

Not looking at it, exactly. Looking through it. At something beyond it. Something I couldn't see.

Or maybe something I could see, if I looked hard enough.

If I made the same mistake again.
