I have to type this fast, before he remembers he hasn’t gotten to my phone. The salty smell of canned chunk chicken clogs the kitchen, and in the hall, I can hear him singing Frank Sinatra along to the crackling radio. Their voices meld together, making spiders crawl up my spine. *“In olden days a glimpse of stocking was looked on as something shocking. Now heaven knows, anything goes.”*

He was my husband, but now he’s just *him*. The man of the house. I can feel breath on my neck, the phantom smell of Chanel No. 5 lingering like a cloud behind me. The pearls around my neck tighten.

This is my warning. If you find a cookbook on your doorstep, don’t open it. Burn it.

It started Friday. It was a beautiful fall day. The sycamore tree in our front yard had just turned fire-band red and orange, and our lawn was blanketed in crackly leaves. Ralph had gotten off work early and bought a bottle of wine and a cheesecake for us to share. Friday was movie night. We’d been married for six years now, and our lives often took us in different directions. I’m a tire technician, one of the few women in my shop. I spend most of my free time repairing other people’s cars at the cost of parts. Even with my mostly solid understanding of cars (especially pre-2010s ones), it’s a lot of YouTube videos. A lot of trial and error. A lot of visits to the junkyard. And a lot of grease.

And then there’s Ralph. A deeply regretful finance major from a family of cops, fluttering through life. Painting, sculpting, podcasting, filmmaking--he’s tried them all. He’d lock himself in the office for weeks and then shred the paper trail or delete the files of whatever he made. Well. They say opposites attract.

But Friday was always movie night. We often tossed dinner aside for popcorn and cake. But this Friday, I nearly tripped on something on our front porch. A package wrapped in brown paper and twine. It had no address label, no scrawl. I lifted the package to my ear and shook it. I half-expected it to be an abandoned old electronic, like a broken cassette player or Game Boy; they sometimes found their way to our porch and filled all my waking hours searching niche forums for how to repair them.

No rattle.

Ralph shrugged, his smile big and boyish. “Maybe people like us. Maybe it’s just a present.”

“It’s too early for Christmas.”

He rubbed my shoulder. “You’ve worked on *a lot* of cars. Maybe someone’s grateful.”

I decided this was fair enough. I tore the paper away and laughed at what I saw. It was a spiral-bound cookbook with a smiling woman on the cover. She was drawn in a few simple lines, highlighting the collar of her dress and the fat string of pearls wound tight around her neck. Her bright red lips stood out against the paisley background. The title: ‘The Happy Homemaker’s Cookbook.’

Ralph looked down at the book, and then up at me, and laughed. I laughed too. I flipped open the first page and turned my voice high, did an accent I didn’t have a name for, the kind that goes ‘dahling!’ and ‘simply marvelous!’

“The war is won, and that means us girls have left the factories and returned to our homes. The men are hungrier than ever, and I fear we’ve lost our ways in the kitchen. I’ve prepared thirty-five recipes that my children love, and my husband can’t get enough of!”

Ralph threw his head back, his green eyes sparkling. This haunts me now. “I wouldn’t change you for the world. Terrible cooking and all.”

*I wouldn’t change you for the world…*

I punched him in the shoulder, maybe a little too hard. “I could cook if I tried.”

“Yeah, right. And *I* could rotate a tire.”

“Well, let’s see.”

I flipped through the book, the pages surprisingly crispy and white for a book that the copyright page said was published in 1952. I found a recipe for “Welsh Rarebit,” bread with cheese sauce. Nothing too terribly complex. No cooked rabbit. “Shit, Let’s go to the store. I can do that.”

“You sure?”

“Sure!”

I made the rarebit that night, and it was good. Surprisingly good, considering I didn’t think I could cook at all, but I guess anyone can cook if they actually bother to follow a recipe. We had the cake and wine and went to the bedroom. It was a good night, but I kept waking up thinking I heard heels clacking in the hallway. The bedroom smelled like old perfume, my grandmother’s perfume, Chanel No. 5. Anyone who’s ever smelled Chanel No. 5 can tell you it’s thick, a perfume from an entirely different era.

I thought maybe I'd stashed a bottle in one of my drawers and it cracked, but no. I didn’t keep a single bottle of perfume, hadn’t for years.

Ralph woke up clutching his head. His cheeks were red, and he looked up at me shyly. “Don’t you think sweatpants are a little casual?”

“Excuse me?”

“Shouldn’t you be wearing—never mind.” He forced a smile, but his eyes were flat. “I feel weird. I had…really weird dreams.”

“Dreams like what?” *Like a woman’s laugh? Like the smell of ancient perfume?*

“Nothing. My head hurts. I’m going back to bed.”

I spent most of the day under my brother’s 1994 Land Cruiser. Great car, falling apart. I had already replaced two doors and was now replacing the front axle. When the sun finally set, and my mind and muscles were mush, I found Ralph sitting in the loveseat, staring out the window. A martini was in his hand. I didn’t even know we had olives and tiny toothpicks, let alone martini glasses. Frown lines bit into his face. “You’re a mess.”

I snorted, but he wasn’t laughing. “You need new clothes,” he added. And then: “What’s for dinner?”

“I dunno. I figured we could get pizza or something.”

He rose slowly from his chair, his rolled sleeves revealing muscular arms. Ralph’s never been the intimidating type, but he’s got a few inches on me. His voice was much deeper than I remembered. “That’s not acceptable.”

“What?” I blinked a few times. I think I was giving him time to take it back. “What did you say?”

He handed me the Happy Homemaker’s Cookbook. He looked exasperated, like an employer who’d explained the same concept over and over to a trainee who just wasn't getting it. “That’s not acceptable. You’re supposed to make dinner. That’s your *job*.”

I tilted my head. Maybe he was getting into performance art? I didn’t like it. I flipped through the book, skimming page after page about how a woman’s duty was to put dinner on the table at five precisely. I gritted my teeth and then landed on a recipe for tuna casserole. Egg noodles, mayonnaise, onions, canned tuna. Perfect.

“Fine. Fucking sit there. I’ll be back.”

I smashed two whole onions, crushed a box of spaghetti, and poured five cans of tuna into a glass baking pan. I set the oven to 500 degrees and only pulled it out when the fire alarm went off and the kitchen was filled with gray smoke. I slopped a clump of it onto a paper plate and heaped it in pepper. “Dinner’s served!” I called.

And believe it or not, he actually ate it. But there was something in his eyes. This glint I’d never seen before as his teeth cracked the raw spaghetti. “You didn’t follow Edith’s recipe,” he said when he finished. And then he left. His back stiff, his eyes faraway, the cookbook tucked under his arm.

I stood there, numb, the smell of burnt fish stinking up the kitchen. I decided to stay the night at my brother’s house. I just couldn’t bear staying here, couldn’t possibly understand what was going on in Ralph’s head. It had to be performance art, or some strange attempt to push me away. But why?

My brother’s guest room had a tiny twin bed, and lots of stuffed animals from his two kids. I clutched a giraffe with an orange yarn mane to my chest. As soon as I closed my eyes, I heard clicking heels and a woman’s delicate laughter. I tried to wake up, but my arms wouldn’t move, something cold wrapping tighter and tighter around my neck. I woke up with my fingers curled into claws on my face. My thumb was smeared with something red, too bright and pasty to be blood.

Lipstick.

The giraffe lay at the foot of the bed, all of its stuffing dumped out.

When I got to our house, the air smelled so thickly of Chanel No. 5 that I could barely breathe. Our flat-screen TV was gone, replaced with an oversized wooden radio in front of the TV stand. I tore into the master bedroom and found the closet open. All my clothes were gone. My coveralls, my work shirts, my comfy jeans--all of them replaced with dresses in polka dots and patterns. And Ralph, sitting at the dining table, clutching his head. The cookbook was open in front of him.

I heard the clacking of heels again, and her laugh. I lunged for the cookbook, the thing that started this whole mess, and snatched it away from Ralph. He let out an inhuman howl and flung himself out of his chair. His hand caught my shoulder and tore my sleeve off at the seam.

My heart welled up. This wasn’t Ralph. This was not the man I’d shared six years of my life with. This was something else.

I ran into the kitchen, ripped the pages out of the cookbook, and crammed them into the dish-disposal. He grabbed me by the shoulders and pulled me away, my heels screaming on the tiles. He twirled me, almost a romantic gesture, and set me on the counter. His eyes shone, his mouth on my ear.

“Whatever you tell the cops, they won’t believe you.” He hummed, his arm gripping mine. “Get that cookbook out of the dish disposal and do your job.”

I sucked in a breath, something cold squeezing my throat. I brought my fingers to my neck and felt the cold touch of pearls. I tried to rip them off, but the little round stones dug deep into my skin. The harder I pulled, the tighter they squeezed.

“Okay. I’ll—I’ll make a casserole."

“And do it right this time.”

“Okay. And I’ll make you a whiskey.”

He nodded, visibly exhaling. I poured him a double shot in a tiny glass and watched him slip into the living room.

This is where I am now. His voice singing along to the radio. And *her*. Whatever came into our home with the cookbook, whatever has its cold fingers twisted in the necklace around my throat. I pull a crumpled page from the dish disposal and carefully measure out the ingredients. Two cans of chunk chicken. A cup of chopped celery. A cup of milk. A bag of frozen peas. A can of cream of chicken soup.

In my reflection on the stovetop, I think I see her. The dark-haired woman on the cookbook’s cover. *Edith.*

I smash the measuring cup against the counter. It shatters into dozens of diamond-shaped shards that I carefully stick into the wet flesh of the casserole. I slide the pan into the oven, my heart pounding. “Dinner will be ready in twenty minutes!” I call.

I don’t hear Ralph respond. Just Sinatra.

*“Anything goes.”*
