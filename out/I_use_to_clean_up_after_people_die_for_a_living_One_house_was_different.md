I worked for a Biohazard Remediation Company in Florida. Seven-and-a-half months in, and most jobs followed the same script: suit up, contain the scene, remove what the body left behind, and scrub until the next family can pretend nothing happened.

The call came in on a Tuesday. A single-story ranch. Elderly man, weeks dead in his recliner. The coroners took the remains, and the family wanted the house emptied and deodorized for probate.

Derek and I pulled up with the usual load—Tyvek, PAPR respirators, enzymatic digester, peroxide gel, negative air scrubber. The smell met us at the door: sweet rot undercut by hot pennies and something electrical, like ozone bleeding from a shorted wire.

Inside, the living room carpet had gone glossy black around the recliner. Not the usual mottled brown-black of long decomp, but this was tar-dark and wet-looking; threaded with thin branching lines that spread across the floor in every direction. Some disappeared under the baseboards. One climbed the drywall behind the chair in a jagged vein that stopped just below the light switch. Where the light hit it, the black drank the beam and left faint rainbow sheens, like oil on wet asphalt.

We taped up, ran the scrubber, and started cutting. The carpet peeled in heavy, sodden sheets. The black stuff stretched between the floor and backing like warm taffy, then snapped with a wet, fibrous tear. Underneath, the plywood was stained deep into the grain. When I pried up a section, the black lines on the underside pulsed once, slow and lazy, before settling.

I got down on my knees, utility knife in one hand, pulling a stubborn strip of carpet free with the other, when the blade caught on a hidden staple. It slipped. The point punched through my outer glove and into the pad of my left index finger. A single bead of blood welled up and fell onto the black-stained edge of the carpet.

I stopped immediately. The standard protocol for any break in skin on a biohazard scene was as followed: I peeled the compromised outer glove off inside-out without touching the wound, sealed it in a bio bag, then used the portable eyewash station to flush the puncture under running water and soap for a full fifteen minutes. Derek helped me change the outer Tyvek layer and double-glove the hand again. I logged the exposure on our tablet, applied antiseptic, and bandaged it tight. The whole process took twenty minutes. We kept working.

Around midday our project coordinator, Evan, showed up in pressed khakis and a company polo, hard hat perched like an afterthought. He stood in the doorway with a clipboard, wrinkling his nose at the smell even through his mask. “You guys are behind,” he said, voice muffled but sharp. “Client wants this turned by Friday. Whatever this black shit is, just hit it harder and move on.”

He watched us spray for a few minutes, then got impatient. “Here, let me show you.” He yanked on a pair of gloves and grabbed the peroxide sprayer himself, stepping over the plastic sheeting like he owned the place. He aimed at a thick vein near the recliner and pulled the trigger. The black stuff didn’t foam gray like it should. It pulled back in a slow, deliberate ripple, the filaments parting around the chemical like it was only a suggestion. A few droplets splashed back onto his glove. Evan jerked his hand away, swearing, and ripped the glove off so fast he almost tore the cuff of his shirt clean off.

“Jesus Christ, what the hell is wrong with this floor?” He stood there shaking his hand like he could fling the feeling off it, face red above the respirator. The black vein had already settled back into place, darker than before. Evan even glared at us like we’d done it on purpose. “This is taking too goddamn long. You two are staying on-site until it’s clean. No leaving for dinner or clocking out early. I want the DFR done before either of you go home. Understand?”

He didn’t wait for an answer. Just turned and walked out, boots leaving faint prints in the dust.

We kept trucking on.

We sprayed enzymatic cleaner and let it dwell. The black foamed gray, then darkened again. In one spot near the recliner leg, the stain actually drew back from the puddle, the filaments contracting like something flinching from heat. Derek saw it. We both kept working.

By late afternoon I was checking the kitchen HVAC when I found a notebook. It was sealed in a plastic bag and taped inside the cabinet under the sink. The pages were warped and ink-bleed, with the handwriting growing more cramped and desperate.

The final entry, dated three days before the welfare check, read:

*It’s in the walls now. I feel it moving when I stand still. My left leg doesn’t feel like mine under the skin anymore. I cut the lines out once already. They just grew back thicker and darker. The doctor thinks its nothing, but nothing don't look like this. I think it came from the well water.*  
*I tried to clean it, but it just keeps coming back. I'm sorry for whoever finds this.*

We finished the job anyway. Cut out the stained subfloor, treated the walls up four feet, ran the hydroxyl generator for thirty-six hours. When we left, the place smelled like hospital citrus and hot plastic. Evan never came back to check.

Four days later my supervisor called on my day off. The realtor had gone in for photos and found the black stuff back, and the smell worse. Apparently, the family was threatening to sue.

The other techs were out on another project, so I had to go alone.

The moment I stepped through the door the air felt thicker, like the house had been holding its breath. In the living room the black had returned across the new subfloor we’d installed. It had climbed the fresh drywall in the same branching patterns, but the lines were thicker now and had resolved into clearer shapes: the suggestion of a torso slumped where the recliner had sat, invisible arms resting along the armrests, head tilted slightly back. The pattern didn’t look drawn. It looked like the floor and walls were trying to—I don't even know what. Remember? Maybe?

I sprayed peroxide on one of the thicker lines near what would have been the chest. The gel bubbled, then the black widened around it, and the filaments parted like lips pulling away from teeth. A faint wet sound came from somewhere in the vents. Slow and rhythmic, like breathing through liquid. Barely even audible.

The bandage on my finger felt too tight.

I documented, took pictures, and left. After I stripped in the garage, I showered until the water ran cold.

The next morning a small black spot had appeared beside my shower drain. It had thin lines radiating from it. I started to feel a pit in my stomach. I scrubbed it with bleach until the grout wore away. By evening the spot was back, larger, and the lines had lengthened.

Two nights later I woke up dehydrated coughing black threads into the sink. They were fine and stringy and didn’t dissolve when I ran water over them.

I quit my job and packed what fit in my car and drove. I’m writing this from a motel two counties away. The crawling under the skin of my left arm has reached my elbow now and it follows my pulse. The bandage on my finger has started to stain dark from the inside.

Last night I woke to condensation on the bathroom mirror even though the shower was dry. In the water droplets the same branching lines were spreading slowly downward, matching the pattern on my arm.

I’m typing this right now with the lights on and the door chained. The crawling has moved into my shoulder. When I flex my fingers the black under the bandage shifts like it’s trying to match the movement.

I don’t know what to do now, and if I stay still too long, **I feel it**.

If you do this kind of work and you ever see black that pulls away from the chemicals, that seals over its own cuts, and starts forming shapes on the floor and walls—just get out. Leave. Don’t let anyone pressure you into finishing the job. Walk away and let the place rot. Burn it if you have to.

Because it’s not staying in the walls anymore. It learns. It travels in the people who try to get rid of it. And I feel it inside me.
