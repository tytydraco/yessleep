A long time ago, I was on a submarine. It was a research mission, there were fish deep down that had some sort of property we needed to learn more about. I can't say much about the research itself, but I can talk about my experiences. 

The mission was already in progress, but the day before I was supposed to go down with the rest of the crew, my wife went into labor. They gave me enough time off to get my wife and newborn home, then they flew me out to the middle of the ocean and sent me down. That first day, I descended.

I sat in a smaller, descent sub for a long time before I moved. Small waves crashed against the side, rocking me slightly and making sounds like a soft, distant banging against sheet metal. Then I was let go. A sudden drop wrenched my stomach up into my throat then threw it back down into its normal place as the water stabilized me. Then my stomach went up again and I went down faster and faster. I felt so sick in that small space, the walls so close that my wingspan surpassed the width of the whole interior, so small that I couldn't stand if I wanted to, I would have to be in a small crouch. My breath filled up the space with hot, humid air and the smell of the sea permeated everything. In that moment I wished I never went. I still do. 

With an ear splitting screech that echoed and jumped around the empty metal inside, the sub came to an abrupt stop. It came to the side of the submarine, slowly crashing into it, and once we were properly docked, I entered. There was no one in sight as I entered the sterile research station. It was dark, the bright lights doing very little to light the space up, the darkness of the metal sucking it up. Each footstep I made seemed to announce itself to the entire station, echoing forever into the maze of tunnels that weaved everywhere. I had no idea where to go, I walked on and on, ending up in circles even when I turned. No matter how I went, which hallways I walked down, I always ended up where I started. 

The steady humming of the machine served as a background noise that climbed higher and higher, the pressing silence of the place tore me to shreds as I walked, faster and faster, getting more and more lost but not finding a single room other than the one I already knew. It was a large station, I was aware of that, but it could not be so large that I could walk for what seemed miles without finding anything. The machinery rose and rose and rose until I could hear nothing else. My own thoughts were gone, replaced with that hum.

Once I heard the laughter, though, I had a spot to anchor on. I crept through, walking and walking while listening to the manic giggling, turning and weaving with the hallways until I entered into some sort of lab. A man in a lab coat was standing, doubled over looking at a table. I moved closer to the man, my colleague? And saw he was laughing at a fish. 

"What...are you okay?" I asked.

"Look at it! Look!" He responded, struggling to speak and breathe between fits of laughter. I looked closer and spotted what he was laughing at. The fish was something I had never seen before, it was lumpy and misshapen, almost hairy in patchy spots, with eyes like a spider, both in looks and number, clouded and facing in all directions. The fins seemed cut at angles that made it impossible to swim. And in its large mouth, between almost human lips, there was an eyeball. A real eyeball, one that could only belong to a human. 

"How did he get that!?" The man beside me laughed, and then he put two fingers underneath the eyeball and yanked it out, the eye came out and some strand of meat came behind it, slowly stringing its way out of the fish's throat. The man stopped laughing as he realized it was a part of the creature. He stared at the nerve, the connecting strand, and I believe we became the same in that moment, and we were horrified. Then we laughed.
