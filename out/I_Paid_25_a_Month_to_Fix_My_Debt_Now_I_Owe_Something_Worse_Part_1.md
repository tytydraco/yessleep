Nobody blames the frog. It’s impossible to notice the kind of slow escalation that boils you alive when the first half of being cooked still feels survivable. 

The actual knife in the gut is that you’re comfortable enough to justify staying for one more degree while the heat keeps rising.

Debt, interest, compound processes. The modern world’s boiling pot. A slow death that you mistake for something warm and comfortable.  

All I wanted was to get out of debt. I didn’t realize then that I’d end up owing something worse.

I see it on a YouTube ad, my neck bent, fingers wrapped around my phone, a cold beer sweating in my other hand.

The app’s name is ‘Slate™’

They pitch it to me as if it will fix all my problems.

*"We manage the stress. You settle for less."*

The app itself has a clean, minimalist design, all purple and white, easy to navigate.   
Its icon, a pair of interlocking chevrons, two-toned, purple. The tabs have these neat little animations that vibrate your phone when you swipe between them. 

It ‌looks completely legitimate and even has a trustworthy score on the Better Business Bureau. 

For $25 a month, they take control of your debt and slowly pay it off by negotiating with collection agencies. 

You link your bank account, and they scrape your email for receipts, find all your bills, set up auto-pay, and save you money with deals. 

Sounds like a million other apps I’ve already heard of. But none of those had offered to open the door to debt relief.

“You’ll never have to think about your debt again,” said my agent, Jessica.

Her voice is consistently bright and cheerful. A little creepy. Sometimes it feels  like I’m talking to a bot. She always pauses after my questions, like an automated system combing through its pre-recorded responses for the right one to match my tone.

“Think of it like insurance.” Jessica chimes. 

“You pay a small premium now. That way, you don’t have to pay the ultimate price later. Like a safety net.”

I skim through the terms and service, my thumb scrolling faster and faster until I don’t care what the fine print says and I’m just clicking agree on all the little boxes just to get it over with.

Once everything’s set up, I’m taken to the home screen where there’s a green progress bar at the top.

They each say the same thing. And each number rebrands those words into its own corporate-slop linguistics. But the message stays the same.

*“Give us your money,”* 

It feels like a miracle. My phone battery lasts all day now, and I’m not constantly swiping notifications away from missed calls and voicemails.

*Liquidation.*

There’s nothing but pavement and dead leaves fidgeting in the wind where my aloe green 2010 Toyota Camry should be.

I turn to look at the outside of my door for a notice or something. Nothing. I don’t even recall hearing a tow truck. The car I paid off eleven years ago. Just fucking gone.  

I feel my skin growing hot, the rational paths in my brain shutting down and detouring straight toward anger.

Me: “I want to cancel my subscription.”

My stomach twists tighter with every word I read in her response.

410% interest.

The world tilts. I feel the text close around my ankle like a wire snare, whipping me off my feet and dangling me in my own living room. The ceiling fan spins beneath me as I sway.

*“I reckon he ain’t much of a meal, is he?”* laughs regret.

What the hell did I just sign up for?

“Don’t worry, Arthur. You’re on the right track to taking back your life!”

Don’t worry. Hilarious.

It takes some time to adjust to walking and taking the city bus everywhere. Every time I leave the house, it’s hours sooner than I need to because I have to account for the time I’m forced to sit and wait on the side of the road under a transit shelter. 

They become my new waiting room. Liminal shacks placed randomly about the city that I frequent. Sometimes I scroll to fill the void. Other times I read all the vulgar graffiti scratched into the paint and yellow plexiglass.

A stick figure man with an exaggerated penis crudely drawn with black Sharpie.

“I POOPED HERE,” in jagged markings carved into the blue paint of the bench.

“SCHOOL SUX DO DRUGS,” tagged on the garbage can with spray paint.

Life is harder than before, but not as bad as it’s going to get.

In the sixth week, “The Tenants” showed up.

I get home from work later than usual after missing my bus, and pass out on the couch to scratchy surface sounds and the low thump of the record player on loop. 

It’s a nightly ritual for me. Sinking into my favorite dent in the couch and passing out to whatever random vinyl the night calls for.

I wake up around 3am to the smell of chalk dust and raw timber. I’m too cold and exhausted to figure out why my living room smells like an aisle at Home Depot, so I zombie walk to my bedroom, head low, hugging myself until I burrow into my covers.

It hits me in the face as soon as I wake up. I’m two feet out my door when I kiss sheetrock. My entire body slams into a wall. My fault for thinking I could navigate my tiny one-bedroom house by memory.

I hold my aching nose, taste blood inside my bottom lip.

My living room is gone. There’s a wall now. Painted and textured, even matches the baseboards. But no door. Instead, there’s a rod and red curtain installed.

My skin grows hot as my blood reaches a flash point, boiling beneath the surface.

*“Good fences make good neighbors, and lower interest rates!”*

I try to breathe slower, but all I can think about is how my couch, my record player, my console table I’ve packed with hundreds of vinyls collected over the years, are all locked away. 

A sound forces its way out of me. A muffled scream through clenched teeth. 

Something flashes from the corner of my eye. I twist and catch the red curtain swaying. I step lightly to the door, brace myself against the doorframe, and glance out my bedroom door. 

My heart snags when I inhale too sharply.

There’s a stranger standing in my kitchen.

Tall, muscular farmer build. He looks like the kind of guy that will apologize before borrowing your ladder forever.

He picks up a box of Rice Krispies, turns it over in his hands like he’s reading the labels. 

His movements are jarring, broken stop animations. The motion ‌between every pose is too fast or missing entirely.

Without opening the box, he plunges his hand inside, the tops folding in around his forearm as he digs violently.

He stops as suddenly as he started, standing frozen for a moment. Then he slowly withdraws his hand. 

Nothing. His hand is empty except for a few morsels clinging to his palm

He sets the box down too hard on the counter, spilling some. His neck jerks left, then right, too fast, like a bird. Then, it twists too far, over his shoulder about to sweep over me.

I duck inside my room and press myself against the wall. What am I doing? Hiding from my new roommate?

I hear footsteps thudding towards me from the kitchen.

I clench my eyes shut. The sound of his footsteps echo off the cold space growing inside my chest, louder and heavier.

Just as they reach my door, just as I think heavy hands are going to close around my neck and squeeze the life from me. They vanish.

I open my eyes and peer out the doorway. The red curtain sways, then goes still. Whatever scene’s playing on the other side is completely silent.

The AC shuts off, and the house goes dead. I exhale a breath I didn’t know I was holding.

All gravity leans toward the curtain. I can feel him standing just on the other side, even imagine his breath moving the fabric. 

My pulse drums in my neck. I try to swallow. It takes two tries.

Then, the curtain moves. Silently, the fabric pulls tight as something distends.

My heart goes into manual mode when I realise I’m staring at a face, the features becoming too defined. The curtain’s fabric no longer behaving like fabric but more like hot plastic.

It presses out from the optimization barrier’s red curtain, leaning out from the doorframe and into the hallway until they’re putting all their weight on it like a hammock.

The face moves, jaw working, lips flapping beneath. The voice comes after, but with pieces missing. I can feel them vibrating in the back of my teeth. Delayed and wrong, spoken backwards, then played back in reverse.

“We were wondering when you’d come out.”

The AC kicks back on, and I jump, my heart lurching into my throat.

“You have a sticky drawer in the kitchen,” his cadence bends at wrong angles, “I will fix it for you at no additional charge.”

The words keep echoing in the hall long after the mouth stops moving. Then, slowly, it retracts behind the optimization barrier. The curtain goes slack and still.

“Good as new,” it says.

I pull it out, then close it again. It slides along the tracks too cleanly, sealing shut at the end as if it's magnetic.

That he fixed it doesn’t bother me, but how’d he make it better than before?

I don’t see Handyman for the rest of the evening. My house is a tomb until I finally lay down in bed. That’s when I hear the heavy thump-click of the washing machine’s lock behind my headboard, water running through the pipes in the wall, the appliance's low hum as it churns and spins. 

The noise slowly drifts into the background. I almost fall asleep when again, a sudden thump-click passes through the wall, followed by an excruciating thirty seconds of digital marimbas as the washer sings its stupid chime.

All night, the laundry persists. The dryer humming and thumping, the washer churning and singing.

The next morning I call into work exhausted, leaving a groggy message on the attendance line’s voicemail.

I shuffle past the new notice on the optimization barrier and into my kitchen, navigating through eyes gluey with sleep. I freeze when I notice a slender figure sitting at my table. The window lights her back, her face sunken in shadow.

The coffee maker in the corner makes choking and gurgling sounds.
