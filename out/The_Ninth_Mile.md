I've hiked solo in the Olympics for almost ten years. I say that not to impress anyone but because I need you to know I'm not a novice who got turned around and panicked her way into seeing things. I know how to read a topo map. I know how to use a compass when my GPS dies, which it did, that day, at almost exactly the moment things started going wrong, and I don't think that timing was an accident anymore.

I was three days into a solo loop through the rainforest, the kind of deep, wet, green-black forest that exists in maybe a handful of places left in this country, moss thick enough to swallow sound, canopy dense enough that full daylight comes through as a kind of perpetual dusk even at noon. I'd done this loop before. I knew the trail markers, knew the mileage, knew that at mile nine there's a wooden signpost where the trail forks, one path continuing the loop back toward the trailhead, the other dead-ending at an old ranger cabin abandoned sometime in the sixties.

I reached that signpost around three in the afternoon, well ahead of schedule, and took the loop trail same as always. Forty minutes later, I reached the exact same signpost again.

I want to be clear about what that means. I hadn't looped back. I hadn't gotten turned around, the trail through there doesn't loop, doesn't fork back on itself, runs a straight diagonal line down toward the river for the better part of two miles before it curves. I'd walked forty minutes of forest, distinct forest, different terrain, a stream crossing I remembered clearly, and arrived back at a signpost I should have been nowhere near.

My GPS had died an hour before. I checked my compass. It was reading true, near as I could tell, no wild spin, no obvious malfunction. I stood at that signpost and looked at the two routes it offered, loop trail, ranger cabin, and felt something crawl up the back of my neck that had nothing to do with the temperature, which had dropped, hard, in the last few minutes, enough that I could see my breath for the first time all day despite it being early October and nowhere near freezing.

I took the loop trail again. I paid closer attention this time, counted my steps out of habit more than any real plan, watched the moss, watched the light. Forty-one minutes later, by my watch, I came out at the same signpost.

That's when the smell hit me. Coppery, wet, out of place in a forest that smells like rain and rot and cedar, a smell that made the back of my throat tighten like I'd bitten my cheek. And underneath it, a sound I hadn't noticed building until it was already loud: footsteps, heavy and slow, coming from the ranger cabin trail. Not running. Not stalking, exactly. Walking with a rhythm too even to be anything but deliberate, a thump that landed with a weight no animal out here carries, not elk, not bear, nothing I've encountered in ten years on these trails.

I did not go check what was making it. I want that on the record, because it's one of the only decisions I made that day that I still feel good about.

Instead I looked, really looked, at the signpost itself, and understood something had gone wrong with it in a way that had nothing to do with me being lost. The carved arrow pointing toward the loop trail, the same arrow I'd followed twice already, didn't match the trail guide I had memorized from ten years of hiking this route. It was pointing the wrong direction. Not by a little. By enough that if you followed it blind, trusting a marker you'd trusted your whole hiking life, you'd walk in a slow, wide circle back to exactly where you started, convinced the whole time you were making progress.

The footsteps from the cabin trail stopped. All at once, the way a radio cuts to static. And in that stillness, a thought arrived in my head that didn't belong to me, fully formed, the way the last word of a sentence sometimes arrives before you've finished the rest.

*You found it. Now you belong to the trail.*

I didn't take either fork. I want you to understand how hard that was, every instinct built by a decade of trusting trail markers screamed at me to just pick a direction and move, and instead I stood there, heart going so hard I could feel it in my teeth, and did the one thing that felt right even though it made no logical sense: I left the trail entirely. Bushwhacked straight through the undergrowth in the direction my compass told me the trailhead should be, ignoring every marker, every worn path, every bit of ten years' instinct telling me the maintained trail is always safer than the deep woods.

It took me almost three hours to fight my way through moss and downed timber and blackberry that tore my rain shell to ribbons, and I want to tell you every one of those hours felt better than the forty minutes I'd spent trusting that signpost.

I came out on a logging road I recognized, half a mile from the trailhead, as the light was starting to fail. I did not look back into the trees behind me. I have never gone back to finish that loop, and I don't intend to.

I reported the signpost to the ranger station on my way out, mentioned it might need resetting, gave them the mileage as best I remembered it. The ranger who took my report got a look on her face I recognized from somewhere, the same careful, closed expression Deloris had, years later, when I told a friend of a friend this story and she mentioned working graveyard at some motel out on the peninsula and knowing exactly that look. The ranger didn't say much. Just told me, quiet, like she didn't want it going any further than the counter between us, that mile nine gets reported more than any other stretch on that trail system, always the same complaint, always somewhere around forty minutes in.

She didn't tell me what happens to the hikers who don't turn back at the second signpost.

I didn't ask.
