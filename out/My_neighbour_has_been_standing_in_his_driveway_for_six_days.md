My neighbour Graham has been standing in his driveway for six days. I don't mean he spends a lot of time outside, or that he keeps returning to the same spot whenever I happen to look. I mean he has been standing on the same square of concrete, in the same clothes, holding the same white coffee mug at waist height since just after seven on Monday morning.

Graham lives across the street with nobody but an elderly ginger cat that hates everyone. He's sixty-two, retired from something electrical, and the sort of neighbour who knows which council bin goes out before you've checked the calendar. We aren't friends, but we've borrowed tools from each other, and once spent twenty minutes arguing about whether possums can open wheelie-bin lids. He usually wears shorts even when it's cold, so when I first saw him standing there in jeans and a blue checked shirt, I mostly noticed because he'd dressed like somebody was coming over.

It was 7:08 on Monday morning. I remember because I checked my phone while making coffee before work, and Graham stood halfway between his garage and the footpath with his shoulders relaxed, his right hand around the mug and his left arm hanging beside him. My wife Erin came downstairs twenty minutes later, glanced through the blinds when I mentioned him, and said he was probably waiting for somebody while she searched for Sophie's lunchbox lid. There was nothing strange about an older man standing in his own driveway at 7:30, so I went upstairs to work.

By ten he was in exactly the same place. I watched long enough to see his shirt move in the breeze, which reassured me because for a few seconds I had honestly wondered whether he'd put some kind of mannequin in the driveway. At lunchtime I walked to the letterbox even though our post doesn't come until the afternoon and waved across the road. I felt stupid before I had even opened my mouth.

“Morning, Graham.”

He gave no sign he'd heard me. I waited long enough to wonder whether I should cross the street, then remembered I had a meeting starting in ten minutes. Once it began, I managed to forget about him again.

At 5:40 Erin came home with Sophie, and Graham was still there. I had checked often enough during the afternoon that I knew how strange I was going to sound before I started explaining. Erin followed my stare through the front window while Sophie dropped her schoolbag in the hallway.

“That's weird,” I said.

“What's weird?”

“Graham. He hasn't moved.”

“He's probably been in and out all day.”

“I've been here all day.”

“You've been working all day. You went to the shops. You had calls.”

Her explanation was reasonable enough that I felt embarrassed by the relief it gave me. Sophie had followed us to the window and was looking across the street with her head tilted. She tugged once at Erin's sleeve.

“Why is he wearing his visiting shirt?”

“His what?”

“The one he wore when Grandma came over that time.”

None of us knew what to do with that, and Sophie lost interest before we did. Erin started dinner, I helped with homework, and for the next few hours the ordinary machinery of the house did what it always does when something outside it has no obvious place to fit. At eleven I looked through the front window before going upstairs and found Graham still under the driveway light, his shirt washed pale by the bulb above the garage and the mug held at exactly the same height. I waited five minutes for something ordinary to happen, and eventually he blinked once.

I woke Erin and made her come downstairs. She was annoyed until she realised I was serious, then mostly tired, and we stood together behind the curtain while Graham remained motionless across the road. The longer we watched, the more I wanted him to scratch his face or shift his feet just enough to make the whole day feel stupid. He didn't.

“Maybe he can't sleep.”

“For sixteen hours?”

“I don't know, David. Maybe he went inside and came back out.”

“He's holding the same mug.”

Erin stared through the gap for another few seconds, then rubbed her face and started back toward the stairs. She had accepted the shirt, the mug and the hour without looking frightened by any of them. At the time I thought she was exhausted and I was the one turning a neighbour's odd evening into an emergency.

“He likes coffee.”

Tuesday morning, Graham was still there. Rain had fallen overnight, and the driveway around him was wet while his shirt had darkened across the shoulders. Either he had spent the whole night outside, or he had gone in, dried himself, changed into identical clothes and returned to precisely the same patch of concrete with the same mug before I woke up. I tried again with Erin before she left for work.

“I'm not saying it's sinister. I'm saying somebody should check on him.”

“Maybe he's having a rough time. His wife died, remember?”

“Four years ago.”

“So?”

“People don't grieve by standing in a driveway all night.”

“People do strange things when they're unhappy.”

Around nine I called Graham's landline. We had the number because he'd fed our fish once while we were away, and through his open kitchen window I could hear the phone ringing inside his house while I watched him from mine. Graham didn't turn his head or move his hand, and the answering machine picked up after six rings. I left a message asking him to call me, then stood there listening to my own voice carry faintly through his kitchen window.

At noon I finally crossed the street. Up close there was nothing vague about the stillness: grey stubble along his jaw, dried rain at the collar of his shirt, a fly walking over the knuckles of the hand holding the mug. He was breathing, but so shallowly that I had to watch the fabric over his stomach for several seconds to convince myself. His eyes looked focused rather than glassy, although they did not follow me when I stepped into his line of sight.

“Graham?”

 He blinked once. His face otherwise stayed completely still, and when I moved closer I could smell damp cloth and old coffee. I had the ridiculous thought that the blink had been an answer, then felt ashamed of myself for wanting meaning from something as automatic as that. I tried again anyway.

“Do you need an ambulance?”

Nothing changed. I watched his throat for a swallow and saw none, then moved close enough that I could see the fine red veins in the whites of his eyes. He still did not look at me.

“If you can hear me, blink twice.”

He blinked once. I waited so long for a second blink that my eyes started watering from not blinking myself, but it never came. I told him I was going to call someone, stepped back onto the pavement and rang the police non-emergency number because I still felt foolish saying ambulance out loud. The woman who answered said somebody would perform a welfare check.

A patrol car arrived forty minutes later. Two officers walked up Graham's driveway while I watched from across the street, and the older one stood directly in front of him for about a minute. Graham did not move his mouth, lift the mug or change his expression, but the officer nodded several times and eventually stepped back. He looked over at me and gave a small thumbs-up.

“Is he okay?”

“Seems fine.”

“He didn't say anything.”

The officer frowned at me. The younger one, already halfway back to the car, stopped and looked over with a cautious expression. I could see Graham between them, still facing the road with the mug at his waist. Neither officer behaved as though anything unusual was happening.

“We spoke to him.”

“I was watching. He didn't move his mouth.”

“He says he's just enjoying the fresh air.”

“For thirty hours?”

The officers exchanged a glance. The older one said Graham was an adult on his own property and had declined assistance, and when I asked how he had declined anything without speaking he repeated that there was no issue requiring police involvement. For a few seconds after they left, I wondered whether I really had missed Graham talking. The thought came with such immediate relief that I almost accepted it, and that frightened me enough to drag our old security camera from a cupboard and aim it through the front window.

That evening Sophie sat at the dining table cutting cardboard rings for a model of Saturn. I'd promised to help her paint it after dinner, and I managed one half before I found myself at the window again. Erin noticed before Sophie did and told me I had checked six times since dinner. I went back to the table, turned my chair away from the window and stayed there until Sophie went upstairs to brush her teeth.

“The police spoke to him. At some point that has to be enough.”

“They didn't speak to him.”

“You said that already.”

At 11:30 Graham was still standing beneath the light. The camera recorded continuously all night, and on Wednesday morning I watched nine hours at thirty-two times normal speed while cars streaked through the frame and shadows slid over the houses. A possum crossed the driveway sometime after four, a cat disappeared under a hedge and the sky lightened behind the roofs. Graham remained fixed in the centre of the recording.

I showed Erin before she left for work. She watched the timestamp, asked once whether the camera might have skipped, then watched long enough to see the night collapse into morning around a man who did not shift his weight. For a moment I expected the proof to change something. Instead she picked up her handbag and checked whether Sophie had her water bottle.

"So he stood there all night.”

“You hear what you're saying, right?”

“What do you mean?”

For perhaps two seconds, her answer seemed completely adequate. Graham had stood outside all night; strange, certainly, but people did strange things, and I actually felt embarrassed that I had expected Erin to react differently. Then the video caught my eye again, and the feeling broke. I copied the recording to my computer because the urge to delete it had arrived so quietly that I did not trust myself with the only proof I had.

By Wednesday afternoon I had stopped working properly. A client had to repeat a question because I was looking through the gap between my monitor and the window, and when Sophie came home she put Saturn on the table and asked whether we could paint stars around the base after dinner. I told her of course and meant it. Two hours later Erin found the paints untouched beside the model while my laptop sat open on Graham's footage.

“She waited for you.”

“I lost track of time.”

“You're losing track of everything.”

“Something is wrong across the street.”

“Maybe. But something is wrong in here too.”

Sophie had apparently asked whether Graham was going to die because Dad kept watching him. She had also asked Erin why nobody had taken him to hospital, and hearing that from an eight-year-old made my non-emergency call feel suddenly indefensible. I promised I would stop watching for the night and made it until three in the morning before I went downstairs. I stood behind the curtain counting Graham's breaths until I reached one hundred and forty, then stopped because I had no idea what I intended to do with the number.

Thursday morning his beard was visibly longer. The skin beneath his eyes looked darker too, and seeing his body continue ordinary processes made the rest of him harder to explain away. At nine I called his daughter Rebecca, whom I'd met twice at neighbourhood barbecues. Helen next door had given me her number.

Rebecca sounded surprised to hear from me but not worried. She said she'd spoken to her father that morning and he was fine, and I was looking at Graham while she said it. Both of his hands were visible. Neither moved.

“On the phone?”

“Yes.”

“What time?”

“About nine.”

“At nine I was looking at him. His landline didn't ring.”

“Then he called from his mobile. Actually, he called me.”

“What did you talk about?”

“Dad stuff.”

“What does that mean?”

When she answered again, her voice had hardened. Across the street, Graham stood with the mug at his waist and his face pointed toward the road. I could see the wet shoulders of his shirt and both motionless hands.

“He says you keep watching his house and coming onto his property. He asked if I thought he should call the police.”

“Rebecca, your father hasn't spoken to anyone since Monday.”

“I'm not doing this with you.”

“He didn't call you.”

“He called me this morning.”

“Check your call history.”

For several seconds I heard nothing but faint movement at Rebecca's end and thought she'd hung up. When she came back, the uncertainty I expected was not there; she sounded irritated, almost offended. She seemed to be moving around as if looking for something else to do while I annoyed her.

“My phone deletes things sometimes.”

“Does it?”

“Leave Dad alone, David.”

She hung up, and ten minutes later Erin called from work because Rebecca had contacted her to say I was harassing Graham. I kept opening my own call history after that, feeling a small jolt of relief every time Rebecca's number remained where it should be. Once I caught myself thinking that perhaps Graham had used another phone, and the thought felt completely ordinary until I looked outside and saw both his hands in the positions they had occupied since Monday. By lunchtime a conversation I was certain had never happened had travelled through two households and become evidence about my behaviour.

That afternoon I crossed the road again. Graham's front door was unlocked, which wasn't unusual during the day, but I stood on the porch for several minutes before I went inside because concern had stopped being a complete excuse for what I was doing. I called his name through the open door, waited, then stepped into a kitchen that smelled stale and looked as though somebody had expected to be gone for five minutes. His wallet and keys were beside the toaster, his mobile phone lay dead on the table, and half a sandwich sat uncovered beside the sink.

Graham's ginger cat came out of the hallway yelling loudly enough to make me jump. Its water dish was almost dry, the food bowl held only crumbs, and the milk in the refrigerator smelled wrong despite being within date. I fed the cat, refilled its water and plugged Graham's phone into a charger, telling myself the animal was the reason I was there. When the phone started, I opened the call history.

The last outgoing call had been to Rebecca at 8:42 on Sunday evening. It lasted four minutes and eleven seconds, and there had been no calls since. I took a photograph of the screen and looked through the kitchen window at Graham standing in the driveway. Then I called 000.

This time I said sixty-two-year-old man, conscious but almost unresponsive, apparently outside for days, with no sign he had eaten or drunk anything. I told the dispatcher about the dead phone, the spoiled food and the cat without water, and I did not mention anything supernatural. She kept me on the line until the ambulance arrived.

Two paramedics came up the driveway with a monitor and a bag. A woman clipped a pulse oximeter onto the hand hanging at Graham's side, and the display settled on a heart rate of 32 while his oxygen saturation held at 97. She watched the numbers for several seconds, moved the sensor to another finger and got the same result. The male paramedic wrapped a blood-pressure cuff around Graham's arm, and the screen settled on 68 over 44 without flashing an error.

The woman took Graham's wrist manually and counted under her breath. She looked from his wrist to the monitor, where 32 still glowed beside the pulse symbol, then said the pulse felt fine. The male paramedic asked Graham to squeeze two of his fingers, but Graham's hand did not move. He wrote something on his form anyway.

“His blood pressure is sixty-eight over forty-four.”

“We've got it.”

“You're looking at it.”

The woman removed the pulse oximeter and said Graham was alert, oriented and declining transport. There were instruments between us now, numbers bright enough that I could read them from a step away, and neither paramedic behaved as though those numbers were concerning. She packed the sensor into the bag while the man crouched in front of Graham.

“You happy for us to leave you here, mate?”

Graham did not move. The paramedic waited a few seconds, nodded to himself and stood, and the woman began winding the blood-pressure cuff back into its case. Before they left, she asked whether there was somebody who could stay with me for the afternoon. I watched the ambulance pull away while Graham remained exactly where he had been.

My phone rang before I went back inside. Erin had seen me cross the street on the doorbell camera and then seen the ambulance arrive, and she wanted to know what I was doing. I told her about the cat, the call log and the numbers on the monitor. The line went so quiet that I checked the screen to make sure the call had not dropped.

“Come home, David.”

“I found the call log.”

“Just come home.”

“He called Rebecca Sunday night. Nothing after that.”

“Then send her the photo and come home.”

I did. By the time Rebecca replied, Erin had come home from work and was standing beside me in the kitchen. Rebecca said she remembered the Sunday call and that her father had mentioned I was acting strange then too. She did not answer when I asked why he would have complained about me before I had noticed him. Erin read the exchange over my shoulder and went very still.

She said she was taking Sophie to her sister's for the night. She said she wasn't frightened of me, but every conversation in the house had begun turning back toward Graham. Sophie carried Saturn against her chest because the glue on one ring was still drying. At the door she asked whether I was coming to her presentation on Friday, and I had forgotten it until she spoke.

I promised I would be there. Sophie looked past me toward the front window before she left, then asked a question so quietly that Erin didn't hear. Her eyes stayed on the narrow strip of glass beside my shoulder.

“Do you think Mr Graham is scared?”

I said I didn't know. Sophie nodded as though that was a real answer, hugged me and followed Erin outside. After their car disappeared, I opened the security recordings because I wanted to find a mistake in them. Instead I found things I had not been looking for.

Helen had walked past Graham on Monday afternoon and waved. Graham did nothing, but Helen stopped as if he'd answered, waited a moment, smiled and continued down the pavement. On Tuesday a leaflet distributor spoke to him, paused twice as though listening, then laughed at something Graham had apparently said. In the welfare-check footage, the older officer nodded through a conversation Graham never performed, and at one point shifted sideways as though allowing him to step past before moving back into place.

I watched that movement ten or twelve times. Nothing about the officer looked frightened or dishonest; his body simply made room for a man who had never moved. In another clip, Helen lifted her hand as if Graham had pointed something out behind her, then turned to look at an empty patch of street. By two in the morning I could no longer tell whether the footage made me feel better or worse, only that everybody in it seemed to inhabit a version of the driveway I could not see.

Friday morning I went to Sophie's school presentation. I sat in the back beside Erin while Sophie explained the difference between Saturn's rings and Jupiter's, and for twenty minutes I did not think about Graham. When she finished, she found us in the crowd and looked at me first. I was glad I had come, and I knew one morning at school did not repair the rest of the week.

On the drive home Erin asked whether I'd slept. I told her a little, and she said that was good. Neither of us mentioned whether she and Sophie were coming home that night. When I turned onto our street, Graham was visible before I reached the house, and I made myself park without staring at him.

For almost an hour I answered emails and washed dishes. The knowledge of the footage had started to lose its sharpness, and once I caught myself wondering whether six days was really as impossible as I'd been treating it. I stood at the sink trying to understand why that question had occurred to me. Then I went to close the blinds and saw Graham's wrist lower less than a centimetre.

A narrow brown line appeared over the rim of the mug and ran down the outside. There was still coffee in it, and from across the street I could see the surface tremble. For a moment I thought, absurdly, good, he's finally having a drink. Then I realised the mug had moved less than a centimetre and had never approached his mouth.

I crossed the road before I could talk myself out of it. The coffee was cold and had developed a skin across the surface, but the mug was nearly full, and Graham's fingers were pale where they wrapped around the handle. Up close I could smell rain, old coffee and the sourness of somebody who had been wearing the same clothes for days. His eyes remained fixed ahead until I stepped into his line of sight.

“Graham, if you can hear me, blink twice.”

Nothing happened. I moved closer until his face filled most of my vision and tried to keep my voice steady. The first time I had asked him this on Tuesday, he had given me one blink, and I suddenly could not stop thinking about the possibility that one was all he could manage. I asked again.

“Please. Just Two blinks.”

 He blinked once. There was no second blink, but the fingers around the mug trembled hard enough to make another thread of coffee creep down the side. I touched his forearm and found living warmth, then put two fingers against his wrist and found the same slow pulse the paramedic had counted the day before. Even expecting it, I counted twice because the gaps between beats felt too long.

 I had called 000 less than a day earlier. I could still see 32 on the monitor and the blood-pressure reading beneath it, and I could still hear the paramedic telling me his pulse felt fine. I did not reach for my phone.

 Then his eyes moved. His head stayed pointed toward the street while his gaze slid sideways and settled on me. I stepped backward hard enough to stumble off the edge of the driveway, and his eyes followed.

 I ran home and locked the door. Erin answered on the second ring, and I told her Graham had finally moved. For half a second after saying it, I heard how ordinary that sounded.

“Good.”

“No. His eyes moved.”

“So he moved.”

“After five days.”

“That should make you feel better.”

“How does that make it better?”

“Because you've spent all week saying he doesn't move.”

I pressed my forehead against the kitchen cabinet. Graham had moved; I had wanted him to move; therefore this was good, and for a second the sequence felt complete. Then I looked through the gap in the blinds. His head still faced the road, but his eyes were fixed on my house.

“You don't understand what I'm saying.”

“I understand you, David. I just don't understand why everything he does has to prove something.”

Somewhere behind her I could hear Sophie and a television. Erin asked whether I wanted them to come home, and I almost said yes. Graham's eyes were still on the house. I told her not tonight.

Saturday morning was day six. I kept the curtains closed, although that somehow made Graham more present because every room now had a wall I was refusing to look through. At nine somebody knocked on the front door. It was the older police officer from Tuesday.

Helen had seen the ambulance and later seen me touching Graham's arm, and Rebecca had apparently told the station her father had complained about me more than once. The officer said this as though we had already established how those complaints had been made. I kept one hand on the edge of the door because I did not want him stepping inside.

“He's asked you to leave him alone.”

“No, he hasn't.”

“David.”

“He has not said a word in six days.”

“That's simply not true.”

“Then call him.”

The officer stared at me. Irritation crossed his face first, followed by the patient expression people use when they think agreeing to a small demand will end an argument. He rubbed the bridge of his nose without taking his eyes off me.

“Call Graham right now.”

“What would that prove?”

“If he answers, I'll stop.”

After a moment he took out his phone. He found Graham's number, showed me the screen as if the gesture itself should settle something, and pressed call. About thirty seconds later, a telephone began ringing across the street.

Graham's kitchen window was still open, and the sound carried clearly enough that both of us turned toward it. Across the road, Graham remained in the driveway with his right hand around the mug. The officer put the mobile to his ear and smiled.

“Hi, Graham.”

The telephone kept ringing inside the empty kitchen. Graham's mouth remained closed, and his hand stayed fixed around the mug. The officer listened with the mild concentration of someone on an ordinary call.

“Yeah, sorry to bother you.”

The landline rang again across the street. The officer nodded once at whatever he believed he was hearing. Graham did not move.

“Just checking everything's okay.”

The answering machine picked up. Through the open window, Graham's recorded voice asked the caller to leave a message while the officer waited as if somebody else were speaking over it. His expression stayed easy.

“Thanks, mate. Appreciate it.”

He ended the call. For a few seconds neither of us spoke, and his smile faded as he looked down at the screen. I asked who he had been talking to. He said my neighbour.

“No, you weren't.”

For the first time since I'd met him, uncertainty reached his face. He looked at his phone, then across the street at Graham, then toward the open kitchen window. His mouth opened slightly, and I thought I had finally put too many pieces in front of him at once. Then his shoulders loosened.

“He must have answered his mobile.”

“You called the landline.”

The officer looked down at the screen. I watched his thumb move over it, and he shook his head. The number was still visible in his recent calls. I recognised it because I had dialled it myself on Tuesday.

“No, I didn't.”

“That is his house number.”

He stared at it for several seconds. I saw the same brief gap I had seen in Erin on Thursday, a moment in which the explanation had not yet arrived. His eyes lifted toward Graham again, and for one second he looked simply confused. The officer swallowed.

“His phone was ringing in the house. Maybe call forwarding.”

“From a landline he didn't answer?”

He did not reply. Instead he looked at me with sudden concern and said he thought I needed to talk to somebody. While he was speaking, the possibility occurred to me that I had remembered Graham's number wrong, and it arrived complete, reasonable and strangely soothing.

I pulled my own phone out so quickly I nearly dropped it. Graham's contact was still there from when he'd fed our fish, and the number matched the one on the officer's screen. The relief vanished. Six days of asking other people to recognise what was happening had led nowhere, and I stepped outside before the officer could stop me.

Graham's eyes moved toward me as soon as I crossed the street. I grabbed his shoulders and pulled, but his feet didn't slide, his body didn't lean and there was none of the resistance you would expect from a person bracing himself. He simply remained where he was. I put more weight into him and felt how warm he was through the damp shirt.

“Graham, what are you doing?”

His eyes stayed on mine. The officer was coming across the road behind me, telling me to take my hands off him. I kept hold of Graham anyway.

“What were you waiting for?”

The fingers around the mug twitched. His mouth changed by less than I can describe, one corner tightening and then releasing as though the muscles had received an instruction they could not complete. Behind me, Helen had come outside, and another neighbour was standing on his porch. Helen folded her arms while the officer reached for my arm.

“You saw that.”

“Saw what?”

“His mouth.”

“People smile, David.”

I looked back at Graham. Whatever had happened to his mouth was gone, and his face was still again, but his eyes remained on me. I asked him once more if he could hear me. His eyelids lowered once and rose.

“Two blinks,” I said. “Please.”

There was no second blink. The officer took hold of my arm and told me to go home, and I tried one last time to assemble the facts in a way another person might keep assembled. He did not even look at Graham while I spoke.

“He's been standing there since Monday.”

“So what?”

I stopped pulling against the officer. The effort of holding every fact in place had begun to feel physical, like keeping a door shut with my shoulder. I let him lead me back across the road. Graham's eyes followed until the front door blocked my view.

I spent several hours at the station while they contacted Erin and arranged a medical assessment. Nobody arrested me, and the officer described the incident as a welfare dispute that had got out of hand. Erin picked me up that afternoon and drove us to an after-hours clinic with Sophie in the back wearing headphones and holding Saturn on her knees. One cardboard ring had bent where she'd carried it around too much.

The doctor asked about sleep, stress, drugs and whether I'd ever experienced paranoia before. I answered honestly, showed him the call log photo and told him about the ambulance monitor, but by then even my own evidence felt like objects from a case somebody else had built. He said sleep deprivation can alter the significance we attach to ordinary sensory information. I wrote that sentence down because I did not trust myself to remember how it sounded at the time.

At Erin's sister's house, I slept for fourteen hours. I don't remember falling asleep, only waking once to use the bathroom and finding my phone on the floor beside the mattress. I did not look out any windows.

When I woke on Sunday, Erin was beside me scrolling through her phone. She asked whether I felt better, and I told her I did. The pressure that had been sitting behind my ribs all week was gone, and I could remember everything that had happened without feeling that any of it required an immediate response. We went home just after lunch.

Graham was in his driveway when we arrived. He wore the same blue shirt and jeans and held the same white mug. Erin took our bags from the boot while I stood beside the car looking at him. The weather had cleared, and the concrete around his shoes was almost dry.

“Come inside.”

“How long has he been there?”

“I don't know.”

“Since Monday.”

“Okay.”

“It's Sunday.”

“I know what day it is.”

Sophie came around the car holding Saturn. She glanced across the street and slowed when she saw Graham, her eyes settling on the mug and then on his face. I put a hand between her shoulders and turned her toward the front door before she could ask anything. After a few steps she looked up at me instead.

“Will you help me fix the ring before school tomorrow?”

“Yeah. Of course.”

Later, Sophie held the bent ring in place while I added fresh glue. Nearly an hour passed before I realised I had not checked the window. I noticed the fact, pressed the cardboard down with my thumb and kept working. Sophie did not mention Graham again.

I opened the document where I'd been recording everything after Sophie went to bed. There are timestamps, photographs, call times and six days of footage showing cars, rain, animals and people moving around Graham while he barely changes position. I can read what I wrote on Tuesday and understand why the person who wrote it was frightened. Some of it feels excessive now, but I have left it intact because I remember being certain that mattered.

Then I look through the window. Graham is still there, and while I'm watching he turns his eyes toward the house. A moment later he lifts the white mug a little, no more than a neighbour might lift a hand in greeting, and I raise mine back before I have time to think about it. It is the first normal thing he has done all week.

Maybe he was waiting for somebody. Maybe he likes the fresh air, or retirement gives you strange habits, or I was tired and turned something unusual into something impossible because I kept looking at it for too long. Those explanations used to sound ridiculous. Now they just sound considerate.
