Part 1: She Said Her Vows to an Empty Church — [https://www.reddit.com/r/nosleep/comments/1xlsdhx/she\_said\_her\_vows\_to\_an\_empty\_church/](https://www.reddit.com/r/nosleep/comments/1xlsdhx/she_said_her_vows_to_an_empty_church/)

I posted here once before, about my sister June, who said her vows to an empty church. A lot of you read it. One of you asked me what \*received\* means.

I can answer that now. Received is what happens to the bride. What happens to the witness is slower, and nobody in my family ever wrote it down.

I'm writing it down.

You remember how it ended. The cedar box came to me in March, the way it always comes — without discussion, the way you find a tooth under a pillow. I tried to burn the veil and the matches leaned away from it, toward my door, like the flame was listening to something on the other side. And one morning there were two veils in the box.

I didn't tell you what I did next, because I wanted it to be wrong.

I started keeping a notebook. Dates. Times. Where I wake up. I keep my shoes by the bed and a pencil tied to the notebook with yarn, because my grandmother kept records in brown ink and I am my grandmother's granddaughter in every way that matters now, and records are the only weapon this family has ever had.

August. The church drive, forty feet from the doors. The mark where Elias stopped. Frost on my mouth in high summer, lake water at the back of my throat.

September. The churchyard gate. All the way down the drive from where I'd started — I know, because my feet were cut on the gravel and the blood had dried in the shape of the whole road. I had walked farther than Elias ever did and I had done it without deciding to, the way you breathe.

October. Rose's grave.

I woke up kneeling in the churchyard with my hands in the dirt over my great-grandmother, and all ten of my nails were torn down to the quick. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have: Rose was found in 1911 with her nails broken, all ten, like in the last hour of her life she had been holding onto something with everything she had, or holding something off.

I had not been digging. There was nothing dug. Whatever my hands had been holding onto all night, it was not in the ground.

It was holding my hands.

That was the night I stopped sleeping with the box in the house. I put it in the trunk of my car. I put it in a storage unit two towns over, in a plastic bin, under paint cans. I slept one night in a motel with the chain on and a chair under the handle — my own trick, the trick I use for the box — and I woke up at home, in my own bed, in my nightdress, with the storage unit's receipt in my hand, folded in thirds. Folded the way Elias folds everything. Careful. Gentle. Like the world is a thing you can keep from wrinkling.

The letter came the last week of October. Ohio postmark. Handwriting like a ruler was involved.

I did not know Elias had my address. I have thought about that since, and I have decided not to think about it anymore.

\*I believe you,\* he wrote. \*I want that in writing before anything else, because nobody else is going to say it. I remember being chosen the way you remember a photograph in somebody else's house. I remember the drive and the frost and nothing after, until they said my name four times. But I know what I was going to that church to meet, because it still calls me, and I want you to know that it calls us on the same nights.\*

He listed the nights. August. September. October. The dates he wakes up dressed and standing at his front door in Ohio, facing out, waiting.

Every date matched my notebook. Every one. He is three time zones of impossible away and every date was right.

\*The doctor makes me write down what I say in my sleep,\* he wrote. \*For six years it was one sentence. Not yet. She's still walking. I told you that was about you and I was right, but it has changed, and I am writing to you because it has changed. Last Tuesday the doctor heard a new one. He made me repeat it three times in his office because he thought I would want to forget it, and I did want to, and I am writing it to you instead so that neither of us has to carry it alone.\*

\*She's nearly dressed.\*

He ended the letter the way a man ends a letter when he has been raised right and is terrified:

\*Ask your mother who said yes for you. A wedding runs on invitations, Miss. Nobody in that church was ever dragged. Everybody was received because somebody, once, said yes on their behalf. And if I ever stop waking up dressed — do not open your door that night. It will not be because I got better. It will be because it doesn't need me waiting anymore.\*

Miss. He calls me Miss, in writing, like a man at a door. He's so polite. That's the worst part. Cora said that about \*it\*, and here is the thing I cannot stop turning over: the politeness is not a costume it wears. It is a thing it \*catches\*, the way you catch a cold, from every groom and every guest and every well-raised boy it has ever stood beside — and Elias has been standing in its doorway for six years, catching it, one night at a time.

I drove to my mother's house that night. I did not call first. In my family we don't call; we arrive, the way the box arrives.

She was setting the table. Two places.

I stood in her kitchen doorway and watched her lay out two plates, two forks, the good napkins — for one old woman who eats standing up at the counter most nights — and I understood that I had seen this before, in a house I was taken to as a child, and had been too young to be afraid of.

Aunt Cora set a second place at every meal for eleven years. We thought it was the damage. We thought it was what was left of her after the lake.

"Mom," I said. "How long have you been feeding it?"

She didn't turn around. She folded a napkin — careful, gentle — and she said, "Since your sister's wedding. It eats where it's fed, baby. I fed it so it wouldn't go hungry to your house."

"And the box came to me in March."

"I know when it came." She set down the second fork with a sound like a small bell. "It got tired of my cooking. It doesn't want to be fed anymore. It wants to be \*expected\*. There's a difference, and I am sorry, and I have been sorry every night at this table for six years."

Then she told me the rule. The whole rule, the one underneath all the other rules, the one my grandmother took to her grave in pieces because she couldn't carry it whole.

The veil is never worn alone — we knew that; Beth's card says it every year. But it isn't a warning about the bride. It is the \*mechanics\* of the thing. A wedding is not finished by vows. A wedding is finished by a witness. Somebody has to watch it happen and hold it in their mind as having happened, the way a signature means nothing until it's witnessed. It counts who watches — because the watching is the signing. The witness is the one who makes it \*received\*.

"June knew," my mother said. "Don't you dare stand in my kitchen and let her be innocent. She read the whole contract, baby. She always read the whole contract. She knew somebody had to stay to the end and sign with their eyes, and she knew it couldn't be two of us — if two watch, it splits, and God knows where the other half goes. It had to be one. It had to be the one who would stay."

"She picked me."

"She picked her maid of honor." My mother finally turned around, and her face was not cruel, and that was the cruelest part of it. "That's what a maid of honor \*is\*. First witness. June just — used the real words for it."

I asked her about the second veil. I had carried that question up her driveway like a stone.

"One for the bride," she said. "One for the witness, when she's called to stand up." In 1911, Rose's sister Anne watched. Anne wore the second veil to church every Sunday for the rest of her life, so that if it ever looked for the witness again it would find her already dressed and already old, and be — satisfied. That was the word she used. \*Satisfied.\* They buried Anne in it. The box was empty for a hundred years.

"It isn't empty now," I said.

"No," my mother said. "It isn't."

She gave me one more thing before I left, the way this family gives everything — flatly, in a kitchen, without looking at me. "Don't walk quiet. If you wake up out there, walk back \*loud\*. Sing. Pray. Count the fence posts out loud like a child. It is very polite, baby. It cannot stand to interrupt. Make sure there's always something coming out of your mouth for it to be polite \*at\*."

Last night I set my phone to record me sleeping. I am my grandmother's granddaughter; if I cannot stop it, I will at least have records.

I am not going to describe all eight hours. I will describe minute twelve of hour three, because it is the only part that matters and because I have watched it forty times.

At 3:12 a.m. I get up. My eyes are open. I stand beside my bed the way Elias stood on the drive, hands at my sides, facing my bedroom door — and the door is already open, and in the doorway, at the very edge of what the camera can see in the dark, there is a tall shape. Perfectly ordinary. A guest. A man at a door, waiting to be asked in.

He is holding the second veil out over both hands.

The way you hold a coat for someone. The way a gentleman holds a door.

He holds it for the rest of the recording. Three quarters of an hour, in the dark, without moving, without coming one inch into the room — because I had not walked, because I was standing in my own doorway and had not said yes, and it is so polite, it is \*so\* polite, it will hold a veil out for a woman for three quarters of an hour in the dark and wait for her to step into it herself.

At 3:58 I turn my head. Only my head. Toward the camera. Toward whoever would ever watch it.

I know that face. It is my face, the way I must have looked standing in the vestry door of that church six years ago, the one who stayed. And in the recording I am — God forgive me for writing the true thing — I am \*smiling\*. The way June smiled under the veil. Like somebody who has been told a secret and is keeping it for someone she loves.

I don't remember it. I woke up in my bed this morning with the box by the door and the chair under the handle — I move the chair in my sleep now, did I tell you that? I move the chair, and the box travels, and my hands know things my mind has not agreed to.

So I am posting this for the only reason anybody in my family has ever done anything about this thing: the rule cuts both ways.

It counts who watches.

A witness can sign a thing into happening. I have six years of proof of that. But a witness can also watch \*over\*. June signed me with her eyes, and a hundred years of women in my family have been signed one at a time, in the dark, alone, by appointment. Nobody has ever tried being watched by \*everybody\*. Nobody has ever been witnessed in public, by strangers, all at once, on the record.

If you have read this far, you have watched me. I am asking you — plainly, out loud, the way nobody in my family has ever asked anybody for anything — to count.

The box is by my door. The chair is under the handle. The camera is on. Elias called this morning, and for the first time in six years he did not say his sentence. He said a new one, and then he apologized for it, politely, like a man at a door who has brought bad weather in with him.

He said: "She's dressed."

Please count.
