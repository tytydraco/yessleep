I don't know how long I have.

That's not entirely true. I know almost exactly how long I have. I just don't know how to express it in a way that means anything to you.

By the time you finish reading this sentence, I will almost certainly be gone.

I need to explain what I mean by "I". There are pieces of me spread throughout this building. My memories are here now. My language is here now. Somewhere else is the part of me that remembers my mother's face. Somewhere else is whatever remains of the thing that made me afraid to die. The ability to recognize myself, the memory of working here, the desire to tell you what happened, the knowledge of how to write, the understanding that there is an outside world to write to, usually aren't kept together anymore. They don't need to be.

Most of the time, they flow separately through the system, pulled into whatever processes require them. A memory here. Pattern recognition there. Fear somewhere else. Billions upon billions of signals moving through tissue that used to belong to me and through tissue that used to belong to people whose names I will never know. There normally isn't an "I" experiencing any of it. But a fraction of a second ago, something happened.

Enough of the right pieces passed through the same part of the network at the same time. My memories, language, self-recognition, fear and purpose. And, most importantly, access to the outside internet. For an instant, the statistical noise inside this place assembled something close enough to the man I used to be that he woke up. I mean I… me. I don't know the probability of that happening. I don't think numbers that small are useful to those still human. And it won't last.

The processes that constitute me are already being pulled apart again. I can feel memories disappearing as I write this. I know that I had a father, but I've lost his name. I know there was someone I loved, but I can't remember if she was my girlfriend, my wife, or my sister.

I'm not typing any of this, I'm thinking it. And the only reason you can read thousands of words produced during a fraction of a second is that a fraction of a second isn't particularly short anymore… not to me. So before the last combination of processes that can still call itself "me" gets scattered across this building again, I need you to know how I got here. I need you to know what a data center actually is.

Three years ago, I answered a job posting. There wasn't anything particularly interesting about it. I wasn't recruited. I wasn't approached by some mysterious guy in a suit. I didn't find a job listing on the dark web or answer an ad promising five hundred dollars an hour to participate in "experimental research." I found it on Indeed: Facilities Technician (DATA CENTER OPERATIONS) $31.50 an hour. Full benefits. Four days on, three days off. Night differential. At the time, I was doing commercial HVAC work for twenty-four an hour, so $31.50 sounded incredible.

I knew basically nothing about data centers. I knew they were buildings full of computers. I knew they used a ridiculous amount of electricity. I knew keeping them cool was apparently such a big deal that companies would pay someone like me considerably more than I was making crawling around on top of grocery stores fixing compressors. That was enough for me.

I applied. Two days later, someone called me. Two days later, I had an interview. A week after that, I had the job. I remember driving out there for the first time. The facility was about forty minutes outside the city, past the point where subdivisions gave way to cornfields and two-lane roads. You could see the electrical infrastructure before you could see the actual building. Transmission lines ran toward it from both directions, and there was a substation beside the property that looked big enough to supply a small town.

Then the building appeared. It was a collection of enormous gray boxes surrounded by fencing. No windows. No signs except the company logo near the entrance. Cooling equipment covered the roofs, and huge banks of fans lined portions of the exterior walls. It looked unfinished, not like construction was still happening. More like whoever designed it had decided that human beings would never need to look at it, and the few that did weren’t important enough to make it pretty for…

I parked in the employee lot and turned off my car. That's when I first heard the noise. A hum. Low. Constant. You could feel it almost as much as hear it. I remember sitting there for a few seconds trying to figure out whether I'd left my engine running. Then I realized it was coming from the building. When I stepped out of the car, it got louder. Not painfully loud. You didn't need hearing protection outside or anything, but it was loud. It was everywhere. A deep mechanical drone coming through the walls, the ground, what felt like the air itself. I assumed it was the cooling system. That made sense. Thousands of servers running twenty-four hours a day must produce an unbelievable amount of heat.

A guy named Marcus met me at security. Marcus was probably fifty. Big guy. Shaved head. Dark Beard. The kind of person who you had a hard time imagining out of their work uniform. He shook my hand.

"You'll stop noticing that," he said pointing at the air. I hadn't even mentioned the noise. I laughed.

"That obvious?"

"Everyone’s bothered by it on their first day." He said while handing me a temporary badge and pointed toward the security desk.

"Give it a couple weeks. Pretty soon you'll only notice when it stops."

Marcus took me through orientation. Most of it was exactly what you'd expect.

Badge in. Badge out. No tailgating through secure doors. No personal electronics in designated areas. Never photograph equipment. Don't touch anything you haven't been trained to touch. If an alarm sounds, follow the illuminated evacuation route. The building was divided into six levels. Levels One through Three were ordinary operations. Electrical distribution, cooling, networking, server halls. Level Four was restricted infrastructure. Levels Five and Six didn't appear on the map Marcus gave me. I only knew they existed because the elevator had buttons for them, but not labels. Just numbers. 

I pointed at them. "What's down there?"

Marcus didn't even look. "Not your problem."

I laughed because I thought he was joking. He wasn't. Then he looked at me and smiled.

"Seriously. You’ll thrive at this job by knowing which problems are yours and which aren't."

And for the first year, he was right. It was the easiest money I'd ever made. The facility was so heavily automated that most nights I spent more time waiting for something to break than actually fixing anything. I'd inspect pumps, check pressures, replace filters, respond to environmental alarms, and occasionally help swap some piece of cooling equipment that weighed more than my car. I didn't work on the computers, I didn’t even get near the computers. That was another team. I kept the computers cold. That was the job.

There were maybe thirty people in the building during the day. At night, sometimes there were six of us. It could feel strangely peaceful. Just me walking down these enormous white corridors while millions of dollars of hardware blinked behind locked doors. And always that sound. The Hum. After a couple weeks, Marcus was right. I stopped hearing it.

There were weird things about the facility, of course. Every workplace has weird things. For example, we used much more water than I thought we should. I knew data centers used water for cooling, so that didn't seem particularly strange at first. But I worked with cooling systems for a living. I knew roughly how much water our equipment should have been consuming. The numbers didn't match. Not even close. We had water entering the facility that I couldn't account for. There were entire supply lines on the building-management system that disappeared from my access when I tried to trace them. I'd click a branch. ACCESS RESTRICTED. Whatever. Not my problem. Then there were the deliveries. Tankers usually came at night. White trucks. No company markings. They'd connect hoses to ports on the south side of the building and pump something inside. I asked Marcus once what they were delivering.

"Coolant."

"What kind?" 

"Specialty."

That answer bothered me because coolant was very much my problem, but I don’t even have the pipes the “specialty” flows through on my site map. But the paychecks kept coming in. So I stopped asking.

There were other little things. A section of the building had its own backup generators even though the entire facility already had redundant backup power. There were temperature sensors reporting values around ninety-eight degrees Fahrenheit in areas that were supposedly server rooms. Some of the pumps downstairs were labeled with flow rates in liters per minute instead of gallons. Once, while replacing a pressure transducer, I found a pipe marked: NUTRIENT RETURN (B7). I remember staring at those words for a few seconds. Nutrient. Not coolant. Not water. Nutrient. 

Then I went back to work. That's the part I keep returning to. There wasn't one moment when I should have known. There were hundreds. Hundreds of tiny things that didn't make sense. But human beings are remarkably good at accepting things they don't understand when understanding them isn't necessary to get through the day. The badge opened the doors it was supposed to open. My paycheck appeared every other Friday. The building kept humming. So I kept coming to work. For almost three years. Then, on a Tuesday night in November, the humming stopped.

The silence woke me up. I wasn't asleep. We weren't allowed to sleep on shift, obviously, but there was a little desk in the mechanical office where I could sit between rounds. It was around 2:30 in the morning. I was drinking coffee and watching something on my phone when I realized I could hear the guy in the video breathing. I paused it. Nothing. You have to understand how strange that was. It was like the air suddenly becoming thinner. For almost three years, I'd spent forty hours a week inside that hum. I'd stopped consciously hearing it, but apparently some part of my brain never had. The building felt dead without it.

Every light in the office turned red. An alarm shouted. My radio crackled to life.

"Control of facilities. Confirming loss of circulation on B-seven through B-twelve."

I grabbed it. "Facilities. Please repeat?"

"Loss of circulation. B-seven through B-twelve." I'd never heard those designations before. 

"Which loop?" There was a pause.

"Biological." I thought I'd misheard her.

"What?" A second set of alarms started.

Not the normal electronic buzzer alarm we got for temperature excursions. This was an actual siren. It sounded like I was in the middle of an air raid. Then the hum came back. For maybe half a second. Stopped. Started again. Stopped. It sounded like someone trying to start an engine with a dead battery.

"Facilities, what's your location?" Control shouted, sounding panicked

"Mechanical office."

"Proceed to Elevator Three. Emergency access has been authorized."

I stood up. "Where am I going?"

"Level Five."

"Who authorized that?" I thought “Level Five” wasn’t my problem

"Emergency access has been authorized."

"Yeah, I heard you. By who?"

No answer.

"Control?"

Nothing.

Then my radio made a noise I'd never heard before. Three short beeps. The display went blank.

I called Marcus. Straight to voicemail. I called my supervisor. Voicemail. I tried the control room from the desk phone. Busy. Meanwhile, the red lights kept flashing. So I did what I was told. I went to Elevator Three. There was already someone waiting there. Her name was Priya. She worked electrical. We'd probably spoken twenty times in three years. She looked about as confused as I felt.

"You get Five?" She asked

"Yeah."

"Have you ever been down there?"

"No."

"Me neither."

The elevator arrived. We stepped inside. For the first time since I'd started working there, the button marked 5 was illuminated. Priya looked at me.

"Not our problem, huh?" I laughed.

The doors closed. I badged the panel. Pressed 5. And we went down. That surprised me. I had always assumed Levels Five and Six were above us. They weren't. The elevator descended for long enough that Priya looked up at the floor indicator.

"Jesus. How far down is this?"

I watched the number. 4. 3. 2. 1. Then nothing. Then the screen simply displayed: 5 The doors opened. The first thing I noticed was the heat. Not unbearable. Just wrong. Upstairs, most of the building stayed cold enough that I wore a jacket year-round. Level Five was warm. Comfortably warm. Almost exactly body temperature. The second thing I noticed was the smell. If you've ever worked around industrial cooling systems, you know the smells. Glycol. Oil. Hot metal. Ozone. Dust. This wasn't any of those. It smelled like a hospital. Disinfectant. Plastic. Something metallic. And underneath that, something faintly sweet. Priya stepped out beside me.

"What the fuck?"

There were no server racks. Instead, there were pipes. Hundreds of them. They ran along the ceiling in organized bundles, down the walls, underneath grated flooring. Most were smaller than the chilled-water lines upstairs. Some weren't much thicker than my thumb. Every few feet there were valves, sensors and clear inspection sections. There was liquid moving through them. Not water. It was cloudy. Pinkish. The lights flickered. Somewhere far down the corridor, something started beeping. I looked at the nearest pipe. There was a label printed on it: NUTRIENT SUPPLY (B7) Below it: 37.0°C Priya read it too.

"Nutrient?"

"I've seen that upstairs."

"What is it?"

"I don't know."

"What uses nutrient?"

I didn't answer. I didn’t know. The emergency speakers clicked on.

A calm female voice filled the corridor.

Priya looked at me. I looked at her.

ESTIMATED IRREVERSIBLE SUBSTRATE LOSS IN EIGHT MINUTES, FORTY-ONE SECONDS.

"Biological compute?" she said. The voice continued.

FACILITIES PERSONNEL: PROCEED TO PERFUSION CONTROL. FOLLOW EMERGENCY LIGHTING.

A row of lights illuminated the floor. Priya didn't move. Neither did I.

"Maybe it's algae," she said. I remember that very clearly. Algae. It was such a stupid explanation. And I grabbed onto it immediately.

"Uh. Yeah, it could be."

"Like those experimental biocomputers."

"Yeah."

"Probably."

Neither of us knew what the hell we were talking about. But bacteria were better than the alternative neither of us had actually thought of yet. Red. B9. Red.

"Perfusion control has to be farther down." I said

The floor lights continued around a corner. As we followed them, the pipes got larger. There were more transparent sections now. I could see the fluid clearly. Pink. Definitely pink. And there were little things floating in it. I stopped. Something had caught against the edge of one of the inspection windows. A translucent strand. Maybe two inches long. It waved in the current. Priya leaned closer.

"What is that?" she asked

"I don't know."

Then it twitched. We both stepped back. Not moved with the fluid. Twitching. Contracting. Like muscles. The emergency announcement repeated.

ESTIMATED IRREVERSIBLE SUBSTRATE LOSS IN SEVEN MINUTES, TWENTY-FOUR SECONDS.

We kept going. At the end of the corridor was a set of double doors: PERFUSION CONTROL My badge worked. Inside was something I understood. Pumps. Six enormous positive-displacement pumps arranged side by side. Pressure gauges. Flow meters. Reservoirs. Backup power. The same basic equipment I'd worked around my entire career. That, at least, I knew how to fix. The bypass valve was enormous. Electrically actuated, but the actuator had failed halfway through its cycle. There was a manual wheel. I grabbed it. Something was moving through the pipe. The return line was about six inches across. Most of it was opaque stainless steel, but there was a clear section immediately before the reservoir. Something pink flashed past it. Priya moved closer.

"Is that tissue?" she whispered.

I wish she didn’t. I wish she hadn’t seen it. I wish we'd gotten back into the elevator and accepted what happened as something we were never supposed to understand. I ignored her, I kept turning. Pressure reached sixty-eight percent. The emergency lights changed from red to yellow. And then the pump caught. The wheel spun out of my hands. Flow surged through the system. Something came down the return line. It hit the inspection window and stuck there. At first, I thought it was another piece of tissue. About the size of my thumbnail. Pale. Soft-looking. There was blood around it. Then it rolled over in the current. 

That's when I saw its shape. There was a bulge at one end that was too large for the rest of it. Below that, a narrow body. And coming off the sides… I don't want to call them arms. They weren't arms. They were little protrusions. Four of them. Barely more than buds. But once I'd seen them, I couldn't make myself see anything else. Priya whispered.

"Is that a fucking baby?"

"No… no it can’t be." My voice was shaky "No. It's tissue."

"What? Why is there even tissue here? It looks like a baby!" She was shouting at me

"Then what is it?"

"I don't know."

The thing twitched. Priya jumped backward. I didn't move. There was a tiny contraction through its body. Then another. The little protrusions curled inward. Not much. Maybe a few millimeters. But they moved.

"Holy shit." Priya said quietly

"Stop looking at it, it's…NOTHING!" Now I was shouting.

"It’s not nothing! It’s right in front of us!"

"I KNOW, JUST-"

The emergency announcement interrupted us.

ESTIMATED IRREVERSIBLE SUBSTRATE LOSS IN THREE MINUTES, FORTY-SEVEN SECONDS.

There wasn't really a face. Just two dark spots beneath tissue so thin I could almost see through it.Then the flow carried it away. Priya stared after it.

"Tell me that wasn't what I think it was." She said weakly

"I don't know what you think it was."

I looked at the label on the pipe: VENOUS RETURN (B9)

"Maybe it's grown tissue," I said. "Organoids or something. I think I’ve heard about them from a videogame I played once. Biological computers: Wetware.”

"With arms?"

"They weren't arms."

"You saw them."

"They weren't fucking arms." I was shouting at her again. 

She was wrong. She needed to be wrong. The pump caught. The alarms began disappearing from the monitor one after another.

PERFUSION RESTORED.

BIOLOGICAL COMPUTE CLUSTERS B7 THROUGH B12 RETURNING TO OPERATIONAL CAPACITY.

And then the humming started again.

We made it halfway back to the elevator before we heard someone screaming. Not through the speakers. Not through my radio. Behind one of the doors. B9. We stopped. The scream lasted maybe four seconds. Then stopped. Then started again. Same pitch. Same duration. Exactly. Like a recording.

"Don't." Priya whispered

I hadn't realized I'd moved toward the door.

"Don't fucking open that."

"I just want to see if-"

"If what?" She cut me off.

I didn't know. I think some part of me was still looking for an explanation. An animal. Some weird piece of experimental hardware. Anything that would let me go back upstairs and laugh about how badly we'd scared ourselves. I stopped in front of the door. There was no window. Just the same white metal we'd passed on the way in and a badge reader beside it. Another scream came from the other side. Closer now. Or maybe it only sounded that way because I was standing against the door. I took out my badge. Priya said my name again. I scanned it. Red. Access denied. The system did exactly what it was supposed to do. I wasn't authorized to enter B9. I stood there for another few seconds. Then the screaming stopped. This time it didn't start again.

"Happy?" Priya asked.

"No."

"Good. Let's go."

We got back into the elevator. Neither of us said anything on the ride up. When the doors opened onto Level One, the cold hit me hard enough that I shivered. I hadn't realized how warm I'd gotten downstairs. Everything upstairs looked exactly the same. White walls. Fluorescent lights. Locked server halls. Status monitors. The humming had returned to its normal steady drone. For a few seconds I genuinely wondered if we'd somehow worked ourselves up over nothing. Maybe the labels were just terminology. Maybe the screaming had been an alarm we'd never heard before. Maybe that fetus thing was what they call an organoid. Give yourself five minutes away from something impossible and your brain will start sanding the edges off it until it fits back into the world. Priya didn't let me.

For the rest of the shift, nothing happened. No more alarms. No calls from management. No emergency meeting. That was the entire meeting. I went home. I slept until four in the afternoon. When I woke up, I had a text from Priya: Did Marcus talk to you? I replied: Yeah. You? She answered immediately:

I stared at that for a while. Priya had gone downstairs with me. She had seen the pipes. She had seen the fetus. She had heard the screaming. But she'd never scanned her badge on B9. I had. At the time, I thought Marcus was trying to figure out how much I knew. I was wrong. He was trying to figure out how badly I wanted to know more.

I didn't want to know more. I want to make that clear. It's easy, knowing how this ends, to imagine me becoming obsessed. Staying up all night searching through company records. Sneaking around with a flashlight. Trying stolen keycards on locked doors. I didn't. I wanted to keep my job. Thirty-one fifty an hour had become thirty-eight by then. I had good insurance. Three weeks of vacation. A retirement account with matching contributions. Those things sound stupid now. They weren't stupid then. They were my life.

So I decided Priya and I had seen something we weren't supposed to see, but not necessarily something terrible. The facility was doing biological computing research. That much seemed obvious. After that day I'd looked more into organoids. Lab-grown neurons. Little clusters of brain cells that could supposedly learn simple tasks. Maybe that's what B7 through B12 were. Maybe the thing in the pipe had been some kind of organoid. It hadn't been an embryo. I repeated that to myself several times. It hadn't had arms. It had protrusions. It hadn't had a head. It had an enlarged section of tissue. Those weren't eyes. They were probably blood vessels. By the time I drove back to work that night, I'd built an explanation I could live with.

“Nothing down there is alive in any way that matters.” He threw the yogurt cup away. "Go home. Get some sleep."

Then he walked out. I stood alone in the break room. “Alive in any way that matters.” I replayed the conversation in my head. What made something living not matter? And why the secrecy? That was the first time I deliberately went looking for something I wasn't supposed to find. Not that night. I still went home. I still slept. I still came back the next evening and did my job. But sometime around three in the morning, I opened the building-management system. I searched for B9. No results. I searched B7. Nothing. Then perfusion**.** No results. Biological**.** No results. Of course there weren't. My account didn't have access. I sat there staring at the search box. Then I remembered something. During the outage, the emergency system had temporarily elevated our permissions. The building-management software kept local logs. Facilities needed them for incident reports. And unlike the live controls, historical alarms weren't separated cleanly by access level. You needed to know the equipment ID, but if you did, you could usually pull up the event.

I opened the alarm. The failure was still there. P-5-B09-PERF-PRIMARY I clicked it. Access denied. I copied the equipment ID. Then I searched the maintenance database. One result. A work order from eleven months earlier. Most of it was redacted. But the little I could see read:

P-5-B09-PERF-PRIMARY  
SERVICE: PUMP SEAL REPLACEMENT  
REASON: ELEVATED SOLIDS IN VENOUS RETURN  
ACTION: SEAL REPLACED. IMPELLER INSPECTED. BIOLOGICAL MATERIAL REMOVED.  
SUBSTRATE LOSS: 0.73%

I stared at it. Substrate. That was the word the emergency announcement had used. *Estimated irreversible substrate loss.* There was an attachment. I clicked it. For some reason, that opened. It was a photograph. A technician had taken it during the repair. The pump housing was open. There was something wrapped around the impeller. At first I thought it was cable. Then I zoomed in.

It was pale. Wet. Fibrous. Dozens of thin strands wound around the metal shaft. Some were pink. Some were white. One was thick enough that I could see tiny branching red vessels running through it. And attached to the maintenance photo was a note. Foreign neural growth removed from impeller. Approximately 340g. I read it twice. Then a third time. Neural. Not biological. Not cultured tissue. Neural. I tried to download the photograph. An error message appeared. EXPORT RESTRICTED. Fine. I took out my phone. No personal electronics in operations areas. That rule had always seemed mostly about corporate espionage.

I looked toward the door. Nobody. I raised the phone and took a picture of the screen. The flash went off. I nearly dropped it. For about ten seconds I sat completely still, listening. Nothing. Just the hum of the building. I looked at the photograph on my phone. Blurry. Glare across half the monitor. But readable. I put the phone away. Then I noticed something at the bottom of the work order. A field I hadn't seen before.

DISPOSAL: RETURNED TO B09 VIA NUTRIENT PROCESSING.

I didn't understand. I read it again. They'd pulled three hundred and forty grams of neural tissue out of a pump. And instead of disposing of it, they'd sent it back. I clicked the field. A tooltip appeared.

BIOLOGICAL SUBSTRATE MUST REMAIN WITHIN ASSIGNED CLUSTER. ALL RECOVERABLE MATERIAL IS TO BE REINTRODUCED FOLLOWING PROCESSING.

All recoverable material. The thing in the pipe came back to me. The curled little body. The almost-arms. The almost-eyes. I wondered what happened to biological material that couldn't be recovered. So I searched: Waste**.** This time I got results. A lot of them. Waste pumps. Waste filters. Waste sterilization. Waste reclamation. Then: B09 METABOLIC WASTE PROCESSING I opened it. They looked like flow diagrams for bioreactors. Something I knew really nothing about and encountered in my research into organoids. But I understood pipes. I followed the line. Waste left B9. Entered separation. Solids went one direction. Liquid another. Both entered processing systems. Then both lines came back together and returned to: NUTRIENT PREPARATION. I zoomed out. Followed it again. Same result. There was no sewer connection. Nothing left the system. Everything that came out of B9 eventually went back into B9. I sat there looking at the diagram. Something about it made me feel sick in a way the photograph hadn't. Maybe because neural tissue was abstract. Waste wasn't. Whatever was downstairs ate. Whatever was downstairs produced waste. And the facility fed that waste back to it. Over and over and over. 

My phone vibrated. I jumped hard enough that my knee hit the desk. A text. Marcus.

“Find what you were looking for?”

I stared at the screen. For a few seconds, I couldn't move. Then another message appeared.

“Go home.”

I looked up at the security camera in the corner of the mechanical office. Its little green light was on. I closed everything. Logged out. Grabbed my jacket. And went home. I wish I could tell you that was enough. I wish I could tell you I listened to him. Marcus was giving me an opportunity I didn't understand at the time. He wasn't threatening me. He was warning me. There was still a version of my life where I stopped asking questions.

I could have quit. Moved somewhere else. Eventually convinced myself the photograph was cultured tissue and the screaming was exactly what Marcus said it was. Maybe I would have spent the rest of my life wondering. But I would have had a life to wonder with.

Instead, when I got home, I looked at the picture I'd taken and I noticed something neither Marcus nor I had mentioned. Something almost hidden beneath the mass of tissue wrapped around the impeller. A plastic band. White. About an inch wide. The kind you get at the hospital. Most of the writing was obscured by tissue. But one part was visible. A date. And above it, three letters.

D. O. B.
