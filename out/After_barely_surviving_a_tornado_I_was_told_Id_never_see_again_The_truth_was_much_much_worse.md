Vivi and I…we were supposed to be safe.

Our local meteorologist estimated the tornado would touch down fifty miles south, and the sky was pale and cloudless as I sipped a mug of dark roast on the patio. Hell, I tried to reschedule that morning’s funeral - out of an abundance of caution - but the client wasn’t having it. Chewed me out like some overly hormonal high schooler: Pussy this, coward that. I relented without raising my voice in return. Grief is a strange disease. Makes people do some strange things. That said, I’d never been straight up bullied into putting someone into the dirt behind my home, and I’ve been in the funerary business longer than I care to admit.

The client was scrawnier than I imagined.

Usually, people who feel entitled to that level of hostility carry around enough muscle mass to protect themselves if things get hairy, but this kid was a rake with teeth and finger tattoos. An emo-looking son-of-a-bitch who had the audacity to mumble something at me on his way out. Wish I would’ve caught it. With our business concluded, I would’ve been more than happy to retaliate. To his credit, though, it looked like I’d overreacted. The sky was still that pleasant, robin’s egg blue, and the air was calm. Everything was fine.

But it didn’t *feel* fine.

My guts felt tangled. Doom gnawed at my stomach like I’d swallowed fire ants, and it was only getting worse as the minutes ticked by. Even as the sun turned to the west and the last of the mourners’ cars dissolved into the horizon, I still felt it chewing through me, this voracious, scaremongering intuition. Vivi felt it too. She didn’t say it outright, but after thirty-two years of marriage, she didn’t need to; words are blunt tools, things you use to decide which movie to see when you first start dating. I could just sense her discomfort; it dripped from her slender frame and pooled in our living room with a stench like gasoline.

I wrapped my arm around her shoulder, pulled her close.

“Weatherman said there’s nothing to worry about, and the sky doesn’t typically lie. Everything…everything will be alright.”

She shot me a weak grin.

“I hope so, love.”

Funny how poignant the universe can be when it wants to punish you. I trusted my eyes over my gut, so, naturally, the storm took my vision, and I suppose there’s some small justice in that, but everything that happened *after* the tornado tore through our home? 

There was no justice in that.

None at all.

\- - - - - 

I woke up hazy, aching, unsure where I was, and when I opened my eyes, something was wrong. 

There was nothing. 

I *saw* nothing. 

Pure, unfettered blackness, no matter which way I turned my head. 

My breathing grew frantic. What was the last thing I remembered?

Stairs.

Rickety, wooden steps, descending into our unfinished storm cellar. Blaring sirens. Gale-force winds battering the rafters like the breath of God. Vivianne was close behind me. She shouted my name. I looked up just in time to see the wedge of concrete above my skull; the falling rubble eclipsed my vision, and before I could even make a sound - *CRACK*.

Now, I was somewhere else, and I was broken.

I waved my hand an inch from my eyes; nothing.

“I can’t see, Jesus Christ, I can’t see…”

Pain to erupted along my flank. My palm located a damp cylinder jutting from my abdomen. There was something inside of me. I started to howl. Then, from the blackness, a female voice, authoritative yet honey-sweet:

“Ray, you’re in a hospital! You’re safe, but you’re injured. You need to calm down!”

Ambient noise dripped into focus.

The steady beep of medical equipment. Distant chatter. The sputtering growl of a PA system. The familiarity didn’t soothe me; I was too far gone. I dragged my fingertips across my eye socket and felt meat. The machinery was still there. Why the fuck wasn’t it working?

An icy palm curled around my wrist.

“My name is Dr. Westfield.”

Her touch broke through my panic. I ignored the oppressive dark and anchored myself to the sensation: the delicate grip of her thin bones and the metallic kiss of her many rings.

“You were caught in the wake of the tornado and barely survived. Been here for a few days now.”

Gently, she lowered my probing fingers.

“You came to us on the brink. Blood in all the wrong places; in your abdomen, your skull, even behind your eyes, and we did what we could, but your optic nerves were…crushed. We can’t say for certain that you’ll remain blind forever, but…well, just know that I’m very, very sorry.”

“I…I…”

Silence.

After a while, the terrible question fluttered from my lips.

“W-where’s Vivianne?”

A dulcet voice responded from the blackness:

“There isn’t an easy way to say this…she’s comatose. Multiorgan failure. Even if she survives, she’ll likely never wake up again.”

Wild sobs bubbled up my throat. I clawed at the skin of my thigh, desperate to create a physical pain powerful enough to silence the heartache.

And I could’ve sworn I heard something, something that made no earthly sense.

Hushed snickering from somewhere in the room.

\- - - - - 

Alone in that hospital bed, tethered to machines that were supposedly keeping me alive, I struggled to make sense of how much time was passing. With no shifting brightness to mark day from night, my internal rhythm became derailed. I developed the strange habit of calling nurses to my bedside just to read me their watch. Wasn’t much comfort, really. Time seemed to jump and skip in a way that made me decidedly uneasy.

“It’s *4:03 PM*, Ray.”

The voice was high-pitched, almost squeaky. Think she said her name was Andrea. 

“Hey, um, I know how this sounds, and I apologize, ‘cause I’m sure you have more important things to be doing, but…are you sure?”

The room went quiet.

“It's just...the last time I asked, you told me it was eight in the morning, and that felt like half an hour ago. Trying to get my bearings, is all. I’m sure you understand.”

Nothing.

Had she left?  

I listened for any sign of Andrea. Footfalls, a muted cough, the faintest exhale - *anything* to indicate if she was still here, and if that was the case, where the hell she was. My heart raced. I perked my ears: there was one noise that could’ve been her, but it was exceptionally faint, to the point where I began to wonder if I was imagining it. Amidst the monotonous chiming of the medical equipment, I heard chewing. Slow, wet, and grinding; like a kid with a mouth full of taffy doing their damnedest to eat quietly.  

Then, without warning, at a volume that seemed only possible with her lips nearly pressed against my ear:

**“Yup! Looking at the clock right now -** ***5:03 P.M.*****”**

I rocketed away.

My forehead collided with the bed’s guardrail, which was strangely damp. A wet *squelch* cut above the ringing in my ear, and the pain in my flank truly ignited.

“Oh careful now, sweetheart: don’t want to dislodge your drain, though that would get you out of the hospital faster…just, you know, in a *different* sort of way.” She chuckled. Her voice sounded distant, like she’d sprinted across the room.

I nodded, shut my goddamned mouth, and lay still, praying the inferno in my gut would settle or have enough courtesy to just kill me dead. Living wouldn’t be worth the trouble without Vivi, and as far as I knew, she might already be gone; Westfield was playfully reluctant when it came to updates, dancing around the truth of her status without ever providing anything concrete.

Didn’t bother asking for the time after that.

Thankfully, there were other ways to orient myself.

During the day, the ward around me roared with activity, and at some point, Dr. Westfield would stop by, striding in without announcement, spouting a laundry list of numbers in my general direction: blood counts and the like.

“Wait…those are new labs? Like, tested recently?” I asked, having no memory of anyone coming in to draw my blood.

“Mhmm! Fresh as fresh can be. I pulled them off your IV a few hours ago.”

Dread trickled down my spine. Sure enough, there was something thin jutting from the crook of my left elbow. From there, she’d go on to review my situation. Why I needed to be there. Why it was important to constantly drain fluid from my abdomen. Why I received all my nutrition via the IV, instead of being allowed to eat.

“Wouldn’t be safe - the debris did a real number on your face, Ray. Be glad you don’t have the eyes to see it!”

I quickly learned three signs to suggest the sun had set. The first two were pleasant. The unit’s ambient bustle would die down, and the custodian, a kind, soft-toned man named Pete, would drop in. I dreaded the last sign.

Every night, there’d be knocking.

Sharp tapping of knuckles against glass, urgent and slightly muted, like someone was staring at me through a window; if I listened close, I’d swear I could hear them repeating my name. Pointed whispers, designed to signal me without drawing attention. I’d call out. Every time I asked who was there, the knocking would abruptly stop. When I questioned Dr. Westfield about it, her response froze my blood.

“Hah! You’re on the tenth floor, my dear. Unless you're close with a few angels, I doubt there’s anyone rapping on your window in the dead of night.”

The doctor’s trademark cheerfulness melted away. Tiny sparks cascaded through my gut. I wondered if she was fiddling with the drainage tube.

“But if it keeps happening,” she paused, smacking her lips like she was working through a tough stick of gum, “please let me know.”

It didn’t make sense. None of it made sense. I lived in the backwoods of Oklahoma; the nearest hospital was little more than an urgent care with a few extra vending machines, and it sure as hell didn’t have ten floors. So, where the fuck was I? Had I been airlifted to a more advanced institution, some place better suited to manage my injuries? No idea. No one would give me a straight answer. Dr. Westfield claimed I was *“in the city”*. Nurse Andrea stated I was *“where everyone goes”*. So, I asked the only person who seemed even the slightest bit normal.

“Pete, remind me: which floor are we on?”

The sloshing of mop bristles against tile paused.

“You’re in the intensive care unit, mate.”

“That’s not what I meant.”

“…I know.”

A sulfurous odor crept into my nostrils. Reminded me of the time my nephew left half a hamburger on the floor of my sedan after a Fourth of July party. Festered in the raw summer heat for a good forty-eight hours before I needed to drive anywhere.  The odor in my room was faint in comparison, but just as breathtakingly foul. Made me wonder what in God’s name Pete was mopping up, but I suppressed the urge to ask. One thing at a time.  

“Listen, Ray: I like you. But…you shouldn’t be asking questions like that.”

My chest tightened. I squeezed the bedding to keep from screaming; the material felt weathered and soggy.

“You gotta help me, man. Tell me where I am. Tell me where I am or so help me God -  “ I leaned forward, ready to spring from that godforsaken bed, but a searing pain in my abdomen threw me back. One wrong move and the alleged *drainage tube* began to feel like an embedded harpoon, scraping my skin from the inside, pureeing my viscera one slow twist at a time.

Pete released a long, weary sigh.

I heard footsteps toward where the door to my room seemed to be. A pause. Then, urgent movement toward the other side, the side where the knocking came from, followed by the heavy *click* of a sliding deadbolt.

“Don’t say anything to Westfield. For both of our sakes.”

“I…I won’t.”

Without another word, he bolted, his echoing steps rapidly fading - a steady decrescendo that left me alone in the blackness.

But not for long.

That night, instead of knocking, I heard a soft, ticking creak: the unmistakable squeal of a rusty hinge being forced around its axis. 

Then, I understood what Pete had done. 

He unlocked the window, and someone had entered, deft and delicate like a seasoned home invader. 

I pivoted my head, listening, trying to hear their approach over my own thumping heart.

“Hello?”

No response.

My mind began to spin. This was a mistake. I squirmed. Shifted. Waved my hands around, feeling for the intruder. It must have looked pathetic. I was a pinned moth wriggling futilely in its display box: weak, blind, and hopelessly impaled.

Then, finally, I heard them. 

Not from across the room. 

Not from my bedside or in front of me. 

From *beneath* me.

They had crawled under the bed.

“No time, Ray. Listen close.” Each syllable was coarse and crackling, like they were slowly stretching their larynx against thick knots of barbed wire. Young or old, male or female, whispering or screaming, I couldn’t tell.

“They found you. Took you. Far away, for harvest. They drain you, bit by bit. Sometimes, they don’t even leave when they eat. They dance around you, rapturous, silent, chewing.”

Fear ripped through my chest. I couldn’t breathe fast enough to sate my burning lungs.

“Where’s my wife?! Where the hell is Vivi? How do I get out of here?”

“Pull tube.”

“W-what?”

“Pull tube. Pull tube. Pull tube.”

I plunged my hand into the darkness and wrapped my fingers around the cold cylinder jutting from my flank, poised to tear the ghastly device from my flesh no matter the pain. Anything to escape the nightmare. Anything to feel the warmth of Vivi’s presence one last time, even if the chances seemed impossibly slim. She was here, trapped in the very same nightmare, comatose and slipping away with every wasted second. This person, this crackling voice beneath the bed - they were free. They knew me. They were risking their safety to help. I couldn’t just do nothing.

I grit my teeth.

Tightened my knuckles.

And…

let go.

This wasn’t right.

It didn’t *feel* right.

I didn’t understand where I was or how I ended up there, but I knew one thing, felt it down to my very marrow: I was being manipulated.

The nightmare sensed my resistance.

Everything began to change.

The sulfurous odor from before saturated the atmosphere, wafting over me in a series of thick, nauseating waves. The voice turned feral; a discordant, ear-wrenching screech bursting from beneath me like a geyser splitting the earth.

“PULLTUBEPULLTUBEPULLTUBE - “

I rested my arms on the bed and lay still.

The frenzied command was suddenly interrupted by the *clack* of high heels.

“Morning Ray! Dr. Westfield here. Hate to be the bearer of bad news, but your wife died.”

A reflexive panic swept through me, but I snuffed it out. This nightmare was vying for a reaction. It wanted me to panic.  

“Did you hear me? She *died*. *D - I - E - D.* Bled out. Suffocated on her own juices. A terrible racket, screamed the whole way down.”

Despite my relative calm, it was becoming harder and harder to breathe.

Westfield changed the story.

“Actually! She made a fabulous recovery. Discharged her earlier today. Don’t believe me? Here, I have her on the phone.”

A voice laced with static curled into my ear. The tone, the pitch, the timbre: it was all nearly identical.

“R-ray?” she sobbed.

I swallowed hard.

“Is…is that you, love - “

A shrill, animalistic wail surged through the static. Decades of unimaginable pain, distilled into a single, static-kissed scream.

“It’s not her,” I whispered, over and over again. As the wail gradually faded, Pete’s voice emerged from the nightmare.

“I’m just trying to help.”

A familiar hand came to rest on my temple, five delicate fingers covered in ice-cold rings.

“You’re teetering, my dear. Right on the edge. I’d be delighted to push you over myself, but rules are rules, and it isn’t worth vexing the downstairs-folk for your pitiful ass. Thankfully, there’s always a loophole. Not my fault if you do the deed yourself.”

The odor became unbearable. I twisted my neck, frantic for clean oxygen, but I couldn’t get far: every tiny adjustment dragged the tube further into my guts.

“Now: Breathe quick, breathe quiet, and *die*.”

My lungs were on fire. My breaths were reduced to short, desperate pants. Darkness whirled around me like a deathly carousel. The blackness was deepening. I felt the tempting pull of finality. Giving in would be a sort of bliss, wouldn’t it? This would end. It would all end.

Then, it hit me.

The nightmare divulged a little too much.

Why would it want me to be quiet?

I sucked in a chestful of fetid air,

and I screamed.

“HELP!” The space around me erupted with furious static. A guttural moan seemed to vibrate the bed. The delicate fingers dug their nails into my cheek and dragged them down, flaying strips of skin from muscle.

“HEEEEEEEEELP!”

I heard something above. Rustling motion. Thumping footsteps. A muted voice shouting, *“someone’s here!”.* The darkness overhead seemed to churn and buckle.

Then, to my absolute disbelief,

I saw a speck of light peek through.

To the sound of snapping wood and shuffling dirt, the speck grew, and grew, and eventually, multiple figures became visible, cast in the shadow of harsh daylight. My eyes gradually adjusted. Sometimes, I wish they hadn’t.

I was at the bottom of a tiny, box-shaped hole, lying on a stack of coffin bedding, with pallbearers' handles on either side and a blood-stained piece of rebar jutting from my abdomen.

A hollowed-out grave.

And I wasn’t alone.

Sitting in the corner, propped against the dirt wall like a discarded rag doll, legs straight, arms crooked, there was a corpse, freshly dead, bristling with violet pockmarks of early rot but otherwise intact. Their withered hands were adorned with countless silver rings, their cataract-stricken eyes were fixed directly on me, and their throat was…

moving.

trembling wildly.

quivering with profane ventriloquism.

I could still hear them. All of them - Dr. Westfield, Nurse Andrea, Pete - radiating from the corpse, but there was one more voice, a new one.

It was the client’s voice, repeating the same four words. Suddenly, I understood what he mumbled under his breath as he left my home, hours before the tornado:

*“She’s your problem, now.”*

*- - - - -* 

17 hours.

That’s how long I was underground.

Not weeks, not months.

17 measly hours.

Doctors claimed my survival was nothing short of miraculous. My guts were a disaster, whole lengths of bowel eviscerated, arteries severed to hell and back. I would’ve died from the internal bleeding if it weren’t for the rebar clotting off some of the bigger vessels. If it dislodged, I would’ve been dead in minutes. Rescuers arrived in the nick of time, too. The hollow grave they found me in? Airtight. *Perfectly* airtight. Against all reason, the tornado had excavated a 10-by-10-foot box of soil and deposited a continuous seal of debris on top. A lightless tomb with a limited supply of oxygen. The circumstance beggar's belief, and that’s just the beginning. The rebar was from my house, but rescuers found me at the complete *opposite* end of the cemetery, a good half-acre away. The fake hospital bed I was lying on? It was made from at least a dozen different coffins, none of which belonged to the woman entombed with me. No fucking idea who she is, by the way. I know she was the last person we buried, but that's about it. The funeral paperwork was forged. The client’s number was disconnected when I called. The list goes on, and on, and on…

None of it matters, though. 

Not in the end.

Guess it goes without saying that I wasn’t actually blinded when that rubble knocked me unconscious. Just a broken nose and a nasty concussion. That fact should’ve been cause for celebration.

But Vivi’s missing. Presumed dead. Her right hand's been discovered. That’s it, though.

Been a few months since the tornado.

I haven't found her yet, but I haven't given up, either. 

Every day, I dig. Doing it systematically, following the storm’s path of destruction, from the house outward. A Sisyphean venture, undoubtedly, but I need something to occupy my mind. When my hands are idle, I start thinking about my time in the grave. Sometimes about why that dead woman tormented me, dwelling on how she attempted to drive me so far into madness that I accidentally ended it all, but mostly, I think about those last few minutes.

About that static-tinged voice that sounded so much like Vivi’s.

And the horrible, endless scream.

I’ve convinced myself it wasn’t real. Couldn’t have been, right? It’s nonsense. Completely devoid of logic. And I believe that, for now. But I know that’ll change someday. I’ll catch a glimpse of my face in the mirror, and my eyes will trace those five diagonal scars across my cheek, the physical echoes of that corpse dragging its blackened fingernails through my skin, and I’ll be forced to confront the truth.

Even now, Vivi is in pain.

I don’t know how, where, or why. I don’t have to.

I just
