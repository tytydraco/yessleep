Margaret and I were married for thirty-one years. I know that's the kind of thing everyone says at the start of a story like this, so let me try to explain what I actually mean by it, because it matters for what happened after.

I didn't just love her. I organized my entire life around the fact of her. Every habit I have, I built next to her habits, the way a tree grows around a fence it's been pressed against for years. I made coffee the way she liked it even on mornings she wasn't drinking any, because making it any other way felt like admitting something I wasn't ready to admit. I still don't know how to explain what it's like to lose someone who was that load-bearing. It's not like losing a person. It's more like losing the wall that was holding up half your house, except nobody tells you the house is going to keep standing anyway, crooked, creaking, technically still a house, for years afterward.

She died eight months ago. Heart, sudden, no warning, gone before the ambulance even finished pulling into the driveway. I keep saying that sentence to people because I think if I say it enough times my brain will finally agree to believe it. So far it hasn't.

I still set two plates every night. I know how that sounds. My daughter has gently mentioned it twice now, the second time not so gently, and I told her both times that I was just used to it, that old habits take time, that I'd stop eventually. I don't think that's true. I think some part of my mind simply refuses to process the math of one plate instead of two, the same way it refuses to process a house with only one name in it now instead of two.

For the first few months, that was the whole extent of it. Sad, maybe embarrassing, but harmless. I'd sit at the table, eat across from an empty chair, sometimes talk to it a little, the way you'd talk to someone in another room who you know can still hear you. Then I'd clear both plates, wash both plates, and go to bed alone in a bed that still somehow feels too small for one person.

About two months ago, that changed.

I came down one morning and her plate  the one I'd left completely untouched the night before, same as every night had food missing from it. Not all of it. Just a little, the way someone eats when they're not really hungry but are eating anyway out of habit or politeness. I stood there in my kitchen for a long time just staring at it, convinced I must have eaten from it myself and simply not remembered, because grief does strange things to memory, everyone tells you that.

I started testing myself after that, the way you'd test a hinge you don't trust. I'd fold her napkin a very specific way before bed, a fold I knew I wouldn't accidentally replicate. I'd turn her fork at an angle no one would naturally leave it at. Every single morning, something had changed. The napkin refolded differently. The fork moved. Small proof, every day, that someone was joining me at that table after I went to sleep.

I told myself it was Margaret. I know exactly how that sounds, and I don't care. When you've built thirty-one years of your life around a person, some part of you would rather believe in a ghost than believe in the alternative, which is that you're simply losing your mind alone in a house too big for the one name left in it.

I decided, about three weeks ago, that I needed to see it for myself. I don't know if I wanted comfort or proof or just an end to not knowing. I made coffee that night, real coffee, strong enough to keep me up, and I sat in the living room where I had a clear view of the kitchen table, and I waited.

I don't remember falling asleep. I want to be honest about that, because it matters. I remember telling myself I wouldn't, and then I remember waking up in the armchair at just past 3 a.m., neck stiff, coffee cold and untouched beside me.

I got up to check the table anyway, more out of habit than hope at that point.

Her plate had food missing from it. Fresh. Still slightly warm, like it had only just been left.

And my own chair — the one I always sit in — was pushed back at an angle I never leave it at, the way you push a chair back when you're getting up in a hurry, like whoever had been sitting there had heard me stir in the other room and left just quickly enough not to be seen.

I stopped sleeping properly after that night. I started setting up my phone to record the kitchen while I slept, propped against a cereal box on the counter, angled at the table. Every morning I'd wake up and check the footage before I even made coffee, hands shaking a little more each time.

The footage never showed anything. Not once. The camera would record the empty kitchen, the two plates, my own chair, hour after hour of nothing, and then, always somewhere around 3 a.m., the recording would simply cut out for eleven minutes. Always exactly eleven. When it resumed, Margaret's plate had less food on it than it had before the gap.

I don't know what happens during those eleven minutes. I've watched the footage on either side of the gap so many times I could describe every shadow in that kitchen from memory. Nothing in the room before the cut looks any different than after it, except for what's missing from her plate, and except for the fact that, twice now, my own chair has moved slightly closer to hers by the time the recording starts again.

Two nights ago, I finally understood something I don't think I can undo now that I've understood it.

I'd left a small kitchen calendar out, the kind you flip a page on every day, and something about it caught my eye that night as I was cleaning up after dinner. It was still turned to a date from eleven weeks ago. I hadn't noticed. I hadn't turned that page in eleven weeks, not once, and yet I could have sworn, if you'd asked me any morning in between, that I knew exactly what day it was.

I checked my phone. The actual date matched the calendar in the kitchen exactly — not the real date, the wrong one, the one frozen on that page. My phone had somehow been showing me the wrong date for eleven weeks and I never once noticed, the way you don't notice your own handwriting has changed until someone else points it out.

I sat down at that table for a long time after that, just staring at both plates, mine and hers, and I finally let myself think the thought I'd been keeping at the very back of my mind since the eleven-minute gaps started.

I don't think Margaret has been joining me at that table.

I think some part of me has been getting up during those eleven minutes every single night, sitting down in her old chair instead of mine, eating from her plate instead of leaving it untouched, folding her napkin the way she always used to fold it, without any memory of doing it, because some part of me still cannot accept that I'm the only one left at that table, and so it's been quietly, patiently, splitting itself in two to make the math feel right again.

I don't know which one of us is real anymore some mornings. I genuinely don't. I know that sounds insane written out like this. I don't have a better way to say it.

I'm not writing this because I think anyone can fix it. I don't think there's a fix. I think this is just what's left of a man who built his whole life around someone else's, discovering thirty-one years later that a life like that doesn't know how to become one person again, not even when it's forced to, not even when the person it needs is already gone.

I'll set the table again tonight. Two plates, like always. I already know what I'll find in the morning.

I just don't know anymore which chair I'll wake up in.
