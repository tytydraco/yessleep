There are rules for everything on the ranch.



How to handle a bull that gets loose. What to do during a lightning storm. Where to take an injured horse. And what to do if one of the cows starts talking.



I always thought that last one was a joke.



I don't anymore.



I’ve been riding for the Three Oaks Ranch for nearly five years now. They say it earned that name because only three oaks were found growing on the property when the original owner bought the land. None of them survived. Lightning got one. Drought took another. The third supposedly stood for more than a hundred years before finally dying.



The ranch didn't.



Here on the plains of Texas, a man could feel closer to civilization on the far side of the moon. Nothing but dirt, grass, and sagebrush for as far as the eye can see. All it takes is a few 100,000 acres to forget about most modern problems; the only things to worry about here are riding, roping, and the cows.



Like all new cowpokes, I was given a slip of paper when I started; the foreman Dan told me to memorize it quickly and tear it up. I’ll never forget those rules. The paper read



“Rules for dealing with strange cows:

If a cow crosses the outer river, shoot it right away.

If a cow starts speaking, don’t answer it.

If a cow starts crying like a human, take your hat off.

If a cow laughs, leave the ranch immediately. Don't look back until you reach the gate.

If a cow has no shadow, call Dan.

If a cow is standing on two legs, don't shoot it. Pray.”



I never paid much attention to the cow rules; they seemed to me to be just a joke on the new guys. In my years here, I only saw a few cows get shot when they crossed the outer river; it always seemed like a waste of a perfectly good cow to me. Nobody ever seemed particularly upset about losing one. They'd just leave the carcass where it lay, mark the number in the ledger, and move on.



I never heard a cow speaking.



Until last week.



We were pushing a herd to the far pasture, something I'd only done a handful of times in my five years there. Dan, three others, and I planned to be out for a few weeks. Lately, I had been herding cows and their calves closer to the bunkhouse, so heading out to the farthest reaches of the ranch was a welcome change.



The first two days were uneventful. We rode from sunup to sundown, stopping only to eat, sleep, and check the herd. Dan was always very strict about getting the right count every time we stopped. It never made much sense to me; there’s no place to lose a cow on these barren plains. But I’m not one to question what the boss says. We pushed the herd hard on the third day; it was late afternoon by the time we reached the far pasture. We began circling the herd up for the night, when I noticed one of the cows watching me from the other side of the herd.



I slowed my horse for a moment and stared at it. It was an ordinary looking Hereford, except for the way it was looking at me. Not watching me, not exactly. It was studying me. I moved left. Its head followed. I moved right. Its head followed again.



I laughed nervously to myself and kept riding. But I couldn’t help but look back over my shoulder; it was gone.



“Settle in,” Dan announced from his saddle



“We’re camping here for a few days while the herd grazes, then we’re pushing them south.”



Silent nods and low grunts of agreement rose from the riders, and it didn’t take long for the tents to be sent up and the chuckwagon unloaded. That night we relaxed around the fire. Jokes and old cowpoke stories echoed in the vast darkness of the plains. The night grew long, the stars grew brighter, and the coyotes cried to each other in the distance. The older riders turned in for the night, leaving just Dan and me around the dying fire.



After a few attempts at small talk, I worked up the courage to ask



“Why do we need to count the cows?”



Dan glanced at me before staring intently into the fire.



“I mean, it’s not like we could lose one out here.” I tried to joke



“Ain’t about losing one,” he said quietly



He paused, then said



“It’s about making sure we don’t take home more than we brought out.”



I didn’t answer; his response didn’t make much sense, but he didn’t seem like he wanted to say anymore.



We sat in silence for a while, and I began to think about heading off to my tent when I saw the silhouetted shape of a cow, blocking out the stars where the sky met the earth. I pointed



“Looks like one wandered off,” I said to Dan.



Dan didn't answer. He stared at it for several seconds. Then he stood and grabbed his rifle.



“Come on.”



I followed.  



We walked over to the shape, stopping about ten feet away. The cow's black, beady eyes watched us the whole time. It didn’t move as we approached. Dan raised his rifle to his shoulder and shot the cow in the head. With a thud, it fell limply to the ground. Dan turned and walked back to the fire. I ran to catch up.



“Why did you do that?” I asked



He shrugged



“Wasn’t one of ours.”



As we walked away, I glanced back at the carcass. Something bothered me about it.



"Dan?"



He stopped.



"That cow is facing the wrong way."



He looked back at it.



"What do you mean?"



"When we walked up, it was facing us."



"Yeah."



"But when you shot it, it fell backward."



Dan stared at me.



"And?"



I pointed. The dead cow was now facing the direction we'd come from. Dan looked at it for a long moment.



"Don't worry about it."



He turned back toward camp.



"And don't look at it again."

 

The fire was low, barely more than embers



“Get some sleep,” Dan said. “I’ll watch it till it goes out.”



The next day the camp was awake before the sun. After a hearty breakfast, we saddled our horses and prepared for another long day in the saddle. Dan pulled his horse up alongside mine



“Count the herd.” He said



I nodded as he rode off.



It took about ten minutes to get the right count: 63. The same amount we had yesterday. We rode till noon, then rested for lunch. As we headed back out, Dan looked at me and said



“Count them again, just to be sure.”



I nodded and got to it. I rode around the pasture and counted the cows: 64. I frowned; that can’t be right, I thought, and started a second count. It was the same 64. I looked around, confused. Seeing Dan off in the distance, I kicked my horse to a gallop and headed his way.



“Dan!” I yelled as I neared him



He turned to look at me, but in that moment, a horrific wail resounded across the plain. It's hard to describe what it sounded like. At times it sounded like a woman weeping, then it would shift to the sound of a crowd of kids crying, then grow deeper like a heartbroken man. It was all of these at once.



Every horse stopped dead. The riders stopped with them. Even the herd went completely still. Only one cow was moving; it moved slowly to and fro across the pasture. I could tell that it was the source of the crying; its mouth moved rhythmically as if it were singing, but only the terrible constant crying came out.



I froze; terror filled my heart as I saw the animal move towards me. It didn’t run; it lazily trotted towards me. My heart beat faster, and my hands began to shake; I couldn’t take my eyes off it. The crying grew louder as it got closer; its black eyes were locked on mine. Soon it stood right next to me; its coarse fur slid across my pant leg like a cat wanting attention. Its crying began to change from sad wailing to rage-filled screaming.



In my peripheral vision, I noticed Dan off to my right. He stared at me; he was white as a sheet, and he’s eyes held a primal fear. Through gritted teeth, he said



“Your hat.”



The screaming grew louder.



“Take your damn hat off.”



It took effort to move my hand to my head; it was like trying to move my hand through molasses. The cow stared at me the whole time. After some time, I finally pulled my hat from my head. The crying stopped for a moment as the cow just stared at me, then it started again as the beast lumbered by, heading off into the distance, crying as it went.



It wasn’t till it vanished beyond the horizon that I felt able to speak. I turned to Dan and, in a panic, asked



“What the hell was that?”



He stared in the direction the cow had gone, still white as could be.



“That is why we have the rules.”



We were done for the day; the cows huddled together in fear by camp, and we huddled in our own circle around the campfire. We all looked at Dan, waiting for his guidance.



“It’s been a long time since we’ve had to deal with this.” He began



“But you all know the rules; I know you do.”



He glanced at me before continuing.



“They’ve worked in the past, and they’ll work now, so just stick to them. We’re only here for a few more nights, so watch each other's back in the meantime.”



Silent nods told him we understood.



“From now on, we’re going to take turns watching through the night.” He looked around the circle



“Any volunteers for tonight?”



I don’t know why, but I raised my hand.



The afternoon moved slowly; we all tried to busy ourselves around camp as best we could. Dan counted the herd three or four times. No one was in the mood for conversation. Night fell, and unlike the night before, there was no laughing and stories around the fire. I sat there while everyone else headed to bed. Dan walked by on his way to his tent; all he said was



“Remember the rules.”



I was alone; the cool desert night was quiet, except for crickets and the occasional coyote far in the distance. A rifle lay across my lap; the shaky fire was my only companion. Its flickering light warmly illuminated a ten-foot radius, but plunged everything beyond that into deeper darkness. My eyes scanned the horizon, but despite my alertness, my mind began to wander.



I thought of the strange happenings of the day, trying desperately to find a reasonable explanation. I found none. My mind jumped from theory to theory as I stared deeply into the hypnotic movements of the fire. I don’t know how long I was daydreaming, but when I came back to reality, A cow was standing in front of me.  



The fire separated us, and its large round eyes reflected its orange light. We stared at each other for a moment; my hands tightened around the rifle. The cow’s mouth started to move, its jaw rigidly moved up and down. Then a strange clicking sound came from its lips. Its eyes never blinked. The clicking shifted to broken syllables.



“Wh, Wh, Wh,”



It sounded like a monotone parrot coming through a broken record player.



“Wha- What.”



I could feel my jaw tightening



“What”



“Your”



“Name”



“Stop-”



I covered my mouth with my hand. I didn’t mean to speak; it was subconscious. The cow stopped; we stared at each other for a long while. I felt a bead of sweat roll down my forehead. Then it smiled. Not like a cow, like a man.



“What. Your. Name.”



It grew faster and longer.



“What. Your. Name. What. Your. Name. What. Your. Name.”



I couldn’t take it any longer. I quickly rose to my feet, shouldered the rifle, and shot it between the eyes. The bullet flung the cow’s carcass back several feet; its limp and mangled body flopped on the ground just beyond the firelight. Still standing, I kept the rifle trained on the pile of flesh for several minutes; it didn’t move. Finally, I lowered the rifle to my side, and it slipped from my shaking hand and fell to the dirt. I ran both hands through my hair as I tried to calm down. I took deep, slow breaths and reached down for my canteen.



A loud snap reset my attention on the dead cow. With a volley of twitches and snaps, its spine realigned. Its four legs stretched and moved with quick muscle spasms.



Its front legs folded inward. The joints bent the wrong way. Its spine cracked. The thing's chest rose until it was almost upright.



“Name.”



crack.



“Name.”



Then it stood. Its hulking form balanced delicately on its thin back legs. Its body was wrong. The cowhide hung loose around its frame, almost flapping as it moved. At the same time, the hide was stretched tight horizontally. As if the body beneath was too thin and too tall for the skin. Its joints clipped with every movement it made.



Its eyes were no longer the black eyes of a cow; instead, they were a thin, yellow, reflective color. It took a creaking step towards me. Loud, wheezing breaths escaped its mouth. I closed my eyes. I’m not very religious, so I prayed the only prayer I knew.



“Our Father.”



I muttered aloud.



It took a step towards me



“Our Father.” A lifeless voice mimicked me.



“Who art in Heaven, Hallowed be thy name.”



It took another step towards me.



“Thy kingdom come, thy will be done.”



Its hot breath blew against my face.



I opened my mouth to continue.



“Our Father.” It repeated



Fear overtook my voice. No words came out. Hoof-like fingers grabbed me by the shoulders. I whimpered in fear as a jaw of cow's teeth closed around my right shoulder. I screamed as the rounded teeth punctured my skin. It tightened its grip on my flesh and began to drag me out of the camp. But it didn’t get very far before Dan's knife pierced its eye. It released my shoulder and howled in anger. Dan left his knife in its eye socket and grabbed my arm, pulling me to my feet.



“Run.”



Was all he said. The others were up, and we ran after them towards the horses. I glanced back at the ‘cow’; it writhed in pain and screeched. Almost as if answering a call on the distant horizon, a dozen dark, strange shapes and yellow eyes appeared. They reached the camp by the time we mounted our horses, and fled.



We rode hard, harder than I’ve even ridden before. I’ve never ridden much at night; it was like driving on a dark road with no headlights. The ‘cows’ followed. They cried and screamed and called to use the whole way.



“Hello.”



“Your. Name.”



“Please. Help.”



“Stop. Stay.”



Their inhuman chatter was constant. The horse of one of the older cowboys stepped in a gopher hole and fell, throwing him violently to the ground. I hope the fall killed him. When they reached him and his horse, we heard things like



“Thank. You.”



“Please. Stay.”



“Love. You.”



It distracted them long enough for us to get to the bunkhouse. But they weren’t far behind. The rest of the night they banged on the doors, as we barracked them with our bodies.



“Let. Me. In.”



“Let. Me. In.”



“Let. Me. In.”



A dozen broken voices all in unison.



The voices began to fade as morning drew closer, and soon everything was quiet again. We waited till the sun was up before looking out the windows. They were gone. There was nowhere for them to hide; they all left. They cleaned and bandaged my wound; one of the guys said he thinks the bite cracked my shoulder blade. I told Dan I wanted out. He offered to drive me to town; I took him up on the offer.



We drove in silence for a bit before I looked at him and asked



“You’re leaving too, right?”



He watched the road for a bit and replied



“I don’t see how I can.”



“Are you serious? Why would you stay?”



He shrugged



“Three Oaks has been good to me, don’t really have much else, don’t really know much else.”



A short silence followed; Dan broke it.



“You weren’t supposed to answer it.”



He wasn’t mad; it was a statement void of emotion. I didn’t answer.



We rode in silence the rest of the way. He dropped me off at the airport; I thanked him, and with that he was gone. I’m writing this as I sit and wait for my flight out East. I think I’m done cowboying, hoping for a fresh start on the East Coast. At least, that's what I thought.



Until a man with no shadow walked past me.
