The first time my left hand knocked on the bedroom wall, I was awake.

The second time, something knocked back.

It started about six weeks ago, while I was drinking my morning coffee. A little movement beneath the skin near my thumb. I watched the muscle twitch a few times before putting the cup down. It stopped immediately.

I didn't think much of it until the following week, when I felt something move toward my wrist.

Instead of a twitch, though, it was something sliding. I could feel its shape if I pressed hard enough. Long and narrow, with what seemed to be tiny ridges along its body. I thought of the earthworms my wife, Margaret, sometimes found in her flowerbeds, those pale, awful things that contracted when she lifted them out of the dirt.

My doctor couldn't find anything wrong. He suggested cutting back on coffee.

By the end of the month, it had developed a habit. Whenever I sat quietly, my thumb and forefinger would begin rubbing together. The movement seemed deliberate, as though I were rolling something between them.

I could stop it by opening my hand. But the moment I relaxed, it started again.

Margaret began noticing.

"You're doing it again," she'd say, and I'd look down to find my fingers moving.

Then came the knocking.

Four little taps against the kitchen table. Always four, evenly spaced, with a pause between each set.

I thought I was doing it unconsciously until one evening, when I deliberately held my left wrist against the table with my right hand. My fingers continued tapping. I could feel something tightening beneath my skin, pulling the tendons down one at a time.

That night, Margaret woke me because she thought someone was at the door.

I was lying on my left side, facing the bedroom wall. My hand was resting against the plaster.

Four knocks.

Then, from somewhere inside the wall, four more.

Margaret sat upright.

"Did you hear that?"

I nodded.

My left hand began knocking again.

She grabbed my wrist, and the movement stopped so suddenly that something seemed to recoil all the way up my forearm. We didn't sleep much after that.

The next morning, I examined the wall. There were no pipes running through it, no loose panels, nothing that could have made the noise.

Margaret suggested mice, and I desperately wanted her to be right.

For the next few nights, I slept with my hand tucked beneath my thigh. Sometimes I'd wake with my arm aching from the pressure, but at least the knocking stopped.

Then something began moving in my shoulder. I felt it while buttoning my shirt one morning, a horrible dragging sensation beneath my collarbone.

My left arm went stiff. I could still move it, but only with considerable effort. As though something inside was resisting me.

Margaret found me sitting on the bed, unable to fasten the last button.

She helped me dress.

I remember the warmth of her fingers against mine. I also remember the worm moving toward them.

I pulled away so violently that she stumbled backward. She looked frightened. I tried explaining, but the words sounded ridiculous even to me.

"There's something in my arm."

She wanted to take me to the hospital.

I wanted to know what was answering the knocking.

That evening, I propped my phone against the bedroom lamp and recorded us sleeping. I watched the footage the following morning.

At 2:36, my left arm emerged from beneath the blankets. It moved slowly across the mattress, fingers dragging over the sheets. Then my hand reached the wall.

Four knocks.

A few seconds passed.

Four knocks answered.

I watched my sleeping body remain perfectly still; something beneath the skin of my forearm began to move. It looked like a thick piece of string being pulled through a sleeve. The shape traveled toward my wrist, paused, and continued into my palm.

Then my fingers opened. Something small and pale pressed against the underside of my index fingernail. I watched it move there for several seconds.

The skin beneath the nail stretched. And then my hand turned toward Margaret. I stopped the video. She was standing behind me, watching over my shoulder. Neither of us spoke.

We watched the rest together.

My hand traveled across the pillow until my index finger hovered over Margaret's sleeping mouth. I could see the pale thing shifting beneath the nail. It pressed forward. The nail lifted slightly.

On the recording, Margaret stirred and turned her head.

My hand followed.

Then she opened her eyes.

I watched her sit up, grab my wrist, and shove my arm away. The thing beneath my skin retreated so quickly that a visible ripple passed up my elbow.

Margaret got out of bed. The recording ended a few minutes later.

She left that afternoon. I helped carry her suitcase to the car with my right hand. She kissed me goodbye, but I wouldn't let her touch my left arm. I could feel the worm waiting beneath the skin.

We agreed she'd stay with her sister until we figured out what was happening.

That was three days ago. Since then, the movement has become almost constant. Sometimes I feel it wrapped around my elbow. Sometimes it seems to be resting behind my shoulder blade. Yesterday, I felt something contract beneath my jaw while I was eating.

Last night, I lay on the living room sofa with my left hand pressed firmly beneath my body. Around 3 in the morning, something began knocking from inside the wall.

Four slow taps.

I kept perfectly still.

It knocked again.

I could feel the worm struggling beneath me, contracting against my ribs, trying to drag my arm free. I held it down until morning.

I don't know what these things are. I don't know how many there might be, or how long they've been living inside the walls. But I know they can communicate. And I know mine was trying to reach Margaret.

At 2:11 this morning, she called me. I could hear her breathing, but she wouldn't speak.  
  
Finally, I asked what was wrong.

"Do you remember that thing your hand used to do?" she whispered.

I sat up.

"What thing?"

"The tapping."

Before I could answer, she moved the phone closer to something.
