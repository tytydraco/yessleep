[[Part One]](https://www.reddit.com/r/nosleep/s/P7CrBLN63m)

Eli is ***safe***.

I’m telling you that first because I know a lot of you have been refreshing this page, looking for any sort of update. I’m sorry I haven’t been able to answer everyone. As you can tell, a lot of strangeness has been going on. I will get to your messages. Thank you for every one of them, even the ones calling me an idiot, or subtextually calling me a sexist. For the record, I’m a brick mason. I work seventy-five hours a week building industrial plants, and I have since the quarantine lifted. My wife was more than happy to look after our son and keep the house clean, and I still haven’t fully adjusted to her being gone. I know it’s been two years, but it feels like I said goodbye to her yesterday. So, no, I’m not saying cleaning is a woman’s duty. I’m saying it was a job I never had to learn. I would also like to retire my Sherlock Holmes detective glasses.

 They didn’t work.

I’m writing this from Stephanie’s kitchen table. It’s a little after four in the morning. Eli is asleep on her couch with the lamp left on, and from here I can see his feet sticking out from under his Power Rangers blanket. I won’t be sleeping. I needed something to do with my hands, and after what I saw tonight, I need to tell somebody, even if all of you are strangers. Strangers have shown me a lot of kindness these past few days. A few of you said you’ve been praying for us. Thank you. Somebody ought to be.

I’m going to tell you things in order. Mostly. There’s one thing I’ve been keeping back. It’s been rattling around in my head since my wife passed, and I haven’t been able to set it down. I’ll get to it, and when I do, you’ll understand why I’m asking for your forgiveness in advance. I hope you can give it.

The officers at the 7-Eleven recommended a towing company for my car. I called, and they hauled it to the county lot. I thanked the officers for their understanding, and they thanked me for the entertainment. After they left, I took Eli by the hand, walked back into the 7-Eleven, and bought a roll of duct tape. I knew how that must have looked. I’m self-aware, you know. So when the clerk asked how my day was going, I smiled, lied through my teeth the way any good American does when asked that question, and said, “Living the dream. You?”

She rang up the tape without looking at it. “Oh, you know,” she said. “Another day, another dime a dozen.”

The Uber showed up forty minutes after I booked it. I’d never used one in my life; the last time I paid a stranger to drive me anywhere, it was a yellow cab. The driver stepped out and asked if the address I’d entered was right. When I told him it was, he made the sound a man makes when his wallet has just gained a measurable quantity. He was a heavyset man in his early sixties named Dennis. A wooden rosary swung from his mirror, the Our Father beads rubbed pale, so I took him for a good Catholic, and I liked him from the start. I liked him even more when he offered me a swig of chicken noodle soup from his thermos.

“So that’s about a day’s drive, pal.”

I told him I knew, and that I’d tip him like it was Christmas when all was said and done. He rolled his eyes until I showed him the amount on my phone.

“Sir,” he said, “my wife is going to kill me and thank me at the same time. Hop in.” He popped the trunk for the luggage we didn’t have. That’s how I knew he was a gentleman.

“Say,” he said, looking at our empty hands. “What’s brought you all out here?” He walked around to the back of his Nissan and shut the trunk.

“It’s a long story,” I said.

He raised an eyebrow and spread his arms as if to say, *Do we not have the time?* I really liked Dennis. So I told him. I told him almost everything. When he didn’t run off, I asked if he’d ever driven a cab. He had.

“Twenty-two years,” he said. “I’ve heard worse.”

I didn’t believe him, but I appreciated it.

Once we were rolling, I asked Dennis to turn off the A/C and crack the windows. He pointed out that it was forty-one degrees outside. I said I was aware. He studied me in the mirror for a long moment, a father with no wallet, no luggage, and a roll of duct tape in his lap. Then he shrugged and turned the dial to OFF. I taped over every vent I could reach. I wasn’t taking any chances. He watched me do it, shaking his head. “Knock yourself out, kid.”

For the first hour we sat in a blanket of quiet. Eli held on to his own blanket and looked out the window, worrying a loose thread between his fingers. Once we passed the second county line, I did something I should have done back in June. I asked Eli a question, shut my mouth, and waited.

“Buddy, I need you to tell me about Papa. All of it. I’m not mad, and I’m not going to be mad. I just want to understand what’s been happening. Can you tell me about him?”

Eli twisted the thread around his finger and kept his eyes on the blanket. “Even if I broke the rule?”

I tried to think of any rule I might have given him and came up empty. “Which rule, bud?”

“The no-telling rule.”

I turned my face away, covered my mouth with my hand, and counted to five. “Who made that rule, Eli?”

“New Papa did.” He said it as if it were obvious, and maybe it should have been. “He said if I told you, you’d get scared and make them go away. He said they’d have to go away like Mommy went away, and like Lily went away. And then I thought maybe you’d go away too, and I’d be all alone. I don’t want to be alone, Daddy.”

My hand closed around the door handle and stayed there.

*Lily.* Eli had no way of knowing that name. I’d said it out loud exactly once in my life, and the only other person who heard me was dead. Whoever—whatever—was in my vents had been talking to her.

His pediatrician had told me that kids who lose a parent make up imaginary friends. She never said anything about the friends handing out rules.

I asked him everything after that, slowly, walking my words across the thin ice of that conversation, and I let every silence run as long as it needed to so neither of us went under. If you take anything from this post, parents, take this: give your children time to explain.

New Papa started talking to him in the spring, a few weeks after we moved in. “He sounded scared at first,” Eli said. “Then he started to sound like Papa.”

I asked what he meant by scared.

“He was lost. He didn’t know where he was, or who I was, until I told him. He had trouble remembering, so I reminded him.” Eli went back to the thread. “I told him I was Eli, his grandson, and you were my dad, and we moved away after he left us to go be with Jesus. I told him I was happy to hear him again. He said he was happy too.”

He paused.

“After that, New Mommy came back. She sounded like Old Mommy, and she remembered me like Old Mommy did, but she was new. I don’t know…” He trailed off.

Dennis turned the radio off.

“Papa said they were hungry under the house,” Eli went on. “So I told them I’d bring food for their dinner. Papa doesn’t eat it, though. He says he eats under us. He hears us chew, and he likes it. But Mommy and Lily come to dinner too. They eat at the other table.” He pulled at the thread. “He said I could come if I wanted. They have a chair waiting for me.”

He ripped the thread clean off the blanket and looked up at me for the first time. “I told him I had to ask you first. He didn’t like that.”

Eli was watching me with pride, waiting to find out if he’d done right.

I had to look out the window for a while.

“What did Papa say after you told him that?”

“He said you’d say no.” Eli shrugged. “He was sad. He said that’s why we had to be patient.”

I asked whether Papa ever talked to him outside the vents, like in the car. I asked if he knew how that had happened.

“He knows where to find me,” Eli said. “He knows how to move in-between spaces.”

I asked what that meant. He shrugged, as if to say he wasn’t sure either. Then he looked up at me again.

“You said Mommy was with Jesus.”

I didn’t answer him. I looked at the strip of duct tape across Dennis’s dashboard. One corner had already started to peel. Up front, Dennis reached up and closed his hand around the beads on his mirror, and his lips started moving. I watched the fence posts go by until he was done.

An hour later, without my asking, Eli said, “Lily wants to know if you’d like her.”

“Tell her…” My voice came out wrong. I tried again. “Tell her I would’ve liked her very much.”

I heard it as it left my mouth. Would’ve. So did Dennis. His eyes flicked up to the mirror and away. Eli didn’t notice. He paused and grew excited, the way any child does when they receive a letter addressed to them, and pressed his face into the blanket.

“Okay, Daddy.”

He was asleep against the window ten minutes later.

We pulled up to Stephanie’s building after nine that night.

 She lives in a second-floor apartment above a hardware store in town, and she was waiting at the curb in her coat. She hugged Eli so hard his feet left the ground. Dennis wouldn’t leave until he’d seen Eli safely inside. I tipped him what I’d promised on the app. He looked at his phone, then at me, and asked if I’d fat-fingered it. I told him to buy his wife something nice.

Eli was asleep on his feet by the time we got upstairs. Stephanie laid him on the couch, I tucked the blanket around him, and for a second his eyes opened.

“Take it,” he mumbled, and pushed the blanket into my hands. “So you don’t get scared.”

I told him I’d bring it back before bedtime. He said, “After bedtime,” and went back under. I had never once seen him hand that blanket to anybody. He used to sleep under it through the whole homily at St. Anne’s.

I know. I should have kept my son with me. But I wasn’t about to take a six-year-old to see whatever had Torres at a loss for words, and there wasn’t anybody else.

Stephanie turned on a lamp and made tea nobody drank. She’s twenty-one and studying to be a nurse. She’s watched Eli since April, and I had never once seen her cry until she set a mug in front of me and her hands shook so hard it slopped over the rim.

“I need to tell you something,” she said, “and I need you to not fire me.”

“Steph—”

She wiped her face with her wrist. It didn’t help. “I just thought he was… I don’t know what I thought.”

I put my hand on her shoulder. I couldn’t be pissed at her. I was the one who’d spent a month baiting mousetraps. “Take your time, Steph.”

She nodded, eyes on her tea. “The first time Eli asked for more snacks, I asked him if he was still hungry. He said he wasn’t, so I asked why he needed more, and he told me he wanted to share them with Mommy. It took all my might not to tear up. How do you tell a six-year-old he can’t bring snacks to his dead mother?”

She looked up at me then. “I’m sorry.”

“It’s okay,” I said, and waved for her to go on.

“Well, my aunt passed away several years ago, and to this day my uncle makes a small plate for her and leaves her chair empty. I think it’s sweet. You know? Like paying homage to her or something.”

I nodded.

“So when Eli started asking for snacks for his mommy, I thought of that. He’d take the plate to his room after he finished eating at the table. One day I followed him in and watched him push cheese and crackers through the slats of the vent. I scolded him for it, and he broke down crying. I told him he couldn’t shove food into the air vents or it would attract mice, bugs, whatever, you know? He stopped asking for a second plate after that, but I caught him more than once sneaking snacks from the pantry. I got on to him about that too. He insisted on feeding Mommy. Then, as the weeks went by, he started feeding Papa. Then he started feeding Lily.”

I picked up my mug with both hands and held on to it.

“I should have said something, because I know food ain’t cheap. Some days I’d catch him carrying a whole meal to his room. He talks to it, too,” she said. “He’d sit on the floor by that vent and tell stories. About your dad. About your wife, how she hummed him lullabies, how your dad’s truck smelled like cigarettes. Over and over, like he had talked with Papa in his later years of dementia. He told me about that.”

She took a breath. “And it talks back. He goes still, like he’s listening. Then he tells me things he can’t know. Three weeks ago, he told me my grandmother’s name. She died when I was a child. I never say it. I never talk about her. Do I miss her? Yes! Last Sunday, I drove up the road and put marigolds on her grave. On Monday, Eli looked up from that vent and said, ‘She says thank you for the marigolds.’ I hadn’t told a soul I’d gone.”

I set the mug down.

“I don’t think he knows he’s doing it,” she said. “It’s like he’s a net. Like thoughts or things or—whatever—are flying through the air, and he just happens to catch them.” She looked down at her tea. “I should have told you. I know. But I figured you’d already laid enough brick with your own two hands. I figured you’d want support instead of bearing all the weight yourself.”

I thought about all those afternoons. Eli on the floor by that vent, telling stories into the slats. I’d been at work for every one of them.

I asked if she’d keep him tonight. She said she wasn’t going to sleep anyway. I gave her what was left of the duct tape and showed her the register behind the couch. She didn’t ask why.

Officer Torres was waiting on the sidewalk when I came back down, leaning against his cruiser with his arms crossed against the cold. I’d called him from the county line, and he’d said he would come to me.

He was younger than his voice, mid-thirties, with a flashlight in his hand that he never once turned on. He shook my hand and held on a second too long.

“How’s your boy?”

“Asleep.”

“Good. That’s good.” He looked up at the lit square of Stephanie’s window. “I’m not going to try and describe it to you, Mr. \[Redacted\]. I’d get it wrong. But you ought to know a little before you walk in.”

He told me they hadn’t found a man. Every room, attic to cellar, twice. There was nothing alive in my house but a spider in the upstairs bathroom.

“What we found is the other thing.”

“What other thing?”

“A house,” Torres said.

He let that sit.

“I’ve been doing this twelve years. I’ve pulled a guy out of a drainage tunnel under the interstate and a woman out of her ex-husband’s attic after two years. I’ve never seen anything like this. Under the front half of your house, there’s another house. It’s got a front door, Mr. \[Redacted\]. It’s got a doormat.” He rubbed his jaw. “And somebody’s been living in it. More than one somebody, from the look of it. The deputy who found it went back in once, and he won’t go again.”

“Where are they?”

“Gone,” he said. Then, quieter: “I think.”

The ride out took forty minutes. Torres drove with the heater off and his window cracked an inch. I hadn’t asked him to. He’d noticed me watching the vents. The last ten miles were gravel, with corn stubble on both sides and nothing above us but black sky, and I held Eli’s blanket in my lap with both hands.

Somewhere in the dark, he said, “My abuela used to say nothing’s ever really lost. Somebody else is just keeping it for a while.” He glanced over. “She’d say it every time I couldn’t find my keys. Drove me nuts.” He laughed once, without much in it. “I’ve been hearing her all night.”

I thought about my father’s reading glasses, and the spare key, and my wife’s hair clip, all sitting in evidence bags somewhere. The clip was the only one I ever went looking for.

And I thought about a shoebox in my attic, taped shut. I hadn’t let myself think about it since April.

Two cruisers and a county van sat in the gravel, and floodlights on tripods threw the shadows of the trees out long across the yard. A deputy stood on the porch with a thermos and his back to the door. All day I’d been picturing the house the way I left it, dark and breathing.

Now every light shone with the illusion of someone being home, the way you leave them on when you’re on vacation to scare away any intruders.

The basement door in the hallway was open. It had been open when I ran out, and it was open now, with a work light clamped to the rail and a rope of orange cable running down the steps. Cold air came up the stairs, damp, smelling like wet stone. I’d been paying a mortgage on that cellar for eight months, and I had never once flipped the light switch.

It was just a cellar. Dirt floor, a boiler the size of a chest freezer, and a wall of shelves holding jars of something that had been tomatoes during the Clinton administration. The work light buzzed on its clamp.

Torres put his shoulder to the end of the shelves, and the whole unit swung out on oiled hinges, jars and all, without a single clink. Every jar had a ring of rag around the bottom. So they wouldn’t rattle.

Behind it, somebody had taken a section of the fieldstone foundation apart and stacked the stones beside the hole, biggest on the bottom, like they meant to put them back. I lay brick for a living, and I couldn’t have done it cleaner. They’d set an oak beam across the top so the wall above wouldn’t sag, and raked the old mortar out of every joint by hand. The scratch marks were too fine for any tool I own. A spoon handle, maybe. Or fingernails.

We went through on our hands and knees. The clay under my palms had been swept clean of pebbles. It was fifteen feet, maybe, and it got warmer the farther we crawled. It smelled like dirt and canned beans and, under that, something I knew and couldn’t place. Then the tunnel ended, and I stood up in front of a door.

A blue door. A real one, painted the soft blue of a robin’s egg, with a brass knocker shaped like a lion’s head. On the dirt in front of it lay a doormat that said WELCOME. It was the mat from our porch, the one that went missing in August. I’d blamed the wind.

Torres knocked. Three polite knocks, with his other hand on his holster.

Nobody answered. It wasn’t locked.

It opened onto a hallway. Faded yellow wallpaper, roses gone the color of tea. A runner rug, its fringe combed straight. Not a cobweb in the corners. Beside the door hung a row of pegs, and on the pegs, coats: a man’s canvas barn coat, a woman’s wool one, and two small ones patched at the elbows. All of them stiff and old, older than anything I’ve ever owned.

Under the pegs stood a table, and on the table were the lost things.

A brass bowl of keys, hundreds of them, each one tagged with a year in pencil. A basket of gloves, every one a single. Enough reading glasses to stock a Walgreens, folded in rows. Jars of buttons and earrings and baby teeth, each jar labeled on a strip of masking tape with a name and a year in careful block capitals. DUNLEAVY 1961. PRUITT 1989. OKAFOR 2007. And on the last one, the tape still bright and clean, our name.

Inside ours were one of Eli’s mittens and a pearl earring of my wife’s. Coiled at the bottom was a black rosary with a chipped crucifix. My mother gave it to me for my confirmation. I hadn’t noticed it was gone.

“Nothing in here’s stolen,” Torres said, his light moving over the jars. “That’s the part I can’t get past. I thought it was, till I read the dates. Every one of these, somebody dropped. Somebody set it down and never came back for it.”

The kitchen was at the end of the hall, and it was warm. It smelled like bread and, under the bread, lavender. A feed-store calendar hung by the stove, turned to March, 1919. Canned goods lined one wall with the labels turned out, every can of my beans lined up with the rest. A bread box. A kettle, ticking as it cooled.

In the middle of the room stood a long plank table, scrubbed white and set for supper, every fork squared to the edge of its napkin. Directly over it, a register was cut into the ceiling, and through the slats I could see light, and the legs of our kitchen chairs.

It could have seated twelve. The near end was set with chipped tin plates. A farmer’s cap sat on the chair at the head, a faded apron hung over the chair beside it, and two small chairs had two pairs of tiny boots under them, the leather gone to cardboard.

At the far end sat my family.

Somebody had built them out of clothes. My father’s green cardigan, buttoned to the throat, the sleeves laid along the armrests as if there were hands in them. The plaid work shirt I wear on Fridays, sleeves rolled the way I roll them. A little blue hoodie I’d searched for all summer. And my wife’s gray sweater, with the loose thread at the cuff. After the funeral, I’d put that sweater in a bag and taped it shut, because I couldn’t throw it out and I couldn’t smell it without having to sit down. The tape lay on the table in a neat little curl. In front of the sweater sat her mother’s china, the good set I’d left in the attic in March because I couldn’t stand to look at it. Every plate had been washed.

The sweater smelled like lavender, like the lotion she rubbed into her hands every night before bed. It was fainter than I remembered. I’d kept a sweater that smelled like my wife for two years, and somebody had used up half of it since spring.

We stood there with our lights on that table. Then, somewhere above us, the furnace clicked on, a draft came down through the register, and the sound started.

Silverware on tin. A chair creaking. Somebody shushing somebody else. A child laughing into a hand. Then a man cleared his throat with two short, dry coughs, and I knew that cough. I’d heard it in June, the night Eli stood by my bed counting, and I’d told myself it was my nerves. My father had that cough the last few years of his life. The cigarettes gave it to him.

And a woman humming, soft and a little flat. For one heartbeat, it was my wife again, the way it had been in the hallway that night.

“Mr. \[Redacted\],” Torres said, real quiet. “Tell me you’re hearing that.”

“I’m hearing that.”

“Okay,” he said. “Okay. Good. I didn’t want to be the only one.”

Neither of us moved. At the near end of the table, the apron lifted a half-inch at the neck, the way fabric does when someone sitting in it breathes in. At the head, the cap tilted. Under one of the small chairs, a boot toe scuffed the dirt.

At our end, nothing moved. Nothing breathed in my wife’s sweater. I stared at it until Torres touched my elbow.

There was a folded card at every place at our end, printed in the same careful block capitals as the tape on the jars. PAPA, in front of the cardigan. MOMMY, in front of the sweater. ELI, in front of the hoodie. And in front of the plaid shirt, with its sleeves rolled the way I roll mine: DADDY.

The cards at the near end were blank. Somebody had rubbed at them until the paper went thin.

He had trouble remembering, so I reminded him.

The tune the woman was humming was my wife’s, the one she hummed while she folded laundry. Eli hums it when he colors.

A crayon drawing stuck out of the apron’s pocket, folded small, the creases worn white from being opened and closed. I didn’t have to unfold it. I could read the crayon on the outside. FOR MOMMY.

Soft and stretched out, like talking through a pillow, Eli had told me that summer, and I’d taken it for a stranger. It was my wife’s breath, carried by the lungs of an air compressor.

Torres swung his light along the wall behind the table, and I saw the rest of the drawings.

Forty of them, maybe more, taped up in rows like a refrigerator door. Crayon on notebook paper. A house. A big yellow sun. Stick figures holding hands, and across the top of every page, in my son’s wobbly capitals: FOR PAPA. FOR MOMMY. FOR **LILY**.

There was a drawing like that on our fridge at home. Our whole family in a row: Dad, Mommy, Papa, Eli, and one small figure at the end with a pink dot on its head. I’d told myself it was a bunny. Eli draws a lot of bunnies.

I had been hoping it was a bunny. How could he know?

“He fed them down the vent in his room,” Torres said.  “They come out at the bottom, in your cellar wall. Somebody’s been bringing them in here and putting them up.” He was quiet a second. “Your boy’s been writing to these people for months.”

He’d carried crackers down. Pictures. A key. A tooth. Every story he knew about the people he missed. And whatever lived down here, he’d carried back up, one whisper at a time, because a lost man wearing a dead man’s name tag told him that was how you keep the people you love.

They couldn’t come up on their own. They needed somebody tiny and mighty, with a voice that carried through the wind. They found one the spring we moved in.

Torres’s light left the drawings and stopped.

At the seam of the table, between their end and ours, somebody had set a high chair.

It was handmade from crate slats, sanded smooth, the joints pegged, and it faced our end of the table. On the tray sat a tiny knitted cap, no bigger than my fist. Under the cap, folded in quarters, was a sheet of glossy paper, cracked along the folds.

I knew before I touched it. Torres said, “Sir,” and then he saw my face and didn’t say anything else.

It was an ultrasound. Gray and grainy, a little bean-shaped thing in a field of static. On the back, in blue ink, with the little curl she put on the foot of every L she ever wrote, was one word.

*Lily*.

The furnace hummed. A thin draft came down through the register.

“*Daddy*?”

It came from close. From the chair, or from the air over the chair, small and breathy, the voice of somebody who hadn’t used it much.

“*Eli said you would’ve liked me.*”

Torres crossed himself. My own right hand made it as far as my forehead before I caught it, and I wiped my eyes with it instead.

The ultrasound was still in my other hand. At the far end of the table, nothing in my wife’s sweater moved. At the near end, every chair seemed to lean a little toward me.

“Mr. \[Redacted\],” Torres said. “Who is that?”

“I don’t know,” I said.

Torres looked at me a long time. He didn’t ask again. He worked the printout out of my fingers and slid it into an evidence bag, and I let him. I told you strangers have been kind to me this week. He let me burn in silence.

That’s the lie I told. I’d known Torres for one evening, and he’d crawled into a hole in the ground with me, and I looked him in the face and lied.

It’s been two years and eight months since I last said any of this out loud. I’m saying it to you because I can’t say it to anybody I’d have to look at.

My wife was eleven weeks pregnant when they found the cancer. We hadn’t told anyone. We were waiting for twelve weeks, the way you do, so we’d have something safe to say. My father had been dead since October. Eli was three. Two days before the diagnosis, the blood test came back: a girl. My wife went to a craft store that afternoon and bought a pink ribbon, because she said you can’t have a daughter without one. She tied it around the ultrasound and kept it in her purse, tucked in with her rosary.

I told you in my first post that ribbon wasn’t ours. I looked right at it with a flashlight and didn’t know it.

The oncologist gave us eleven days to decide. I took every one of them off work, the first shifts I’d missed in three years, and I know exactly what that says about me. I’m not going to describe those days. There was a box of tissues on every table in that building and a poster about washing your hands in every room, and I read that poster so many times I could still recite it for you. We never told Father Mike. We already knew what he’d say.

We ended the pregnancy. That’s the soft way to say it. The word is abortion, and I’ve never typed it before tonight.

I’m not going to tell you it was right or wrong. I’ve gone back and forth for two years, and I’ve never once gotten both halves of me to agree. We did what the doctors said would give my wife the most time. She got eight months. I spent most of them on my knees.

In the parking lot afterward, she wrote a name on the back of the printout with a pen from the front desk. She said it out loud, once. I said it back. That’s the only time either of us ever did. We didn’t tell Eli. We didn’t tell anybody. There was no Mass for her. There’s nowhere to bring flowers.

After my wife’s funeral, I put the printout, still tied in its ribbon, in a shoebox with her glasses case. I taped the box shut and carried it up to the attic of this house in March, and I told myself it couldn’t hurt anybody up there.

Eli found it. I know because I found the lid on the attic floor in April and told myself I’d knocked it over. He took the only picture in the box and carried it down to the people who’d been asking him for pictures. He didn’t know what it was. He probably thought it was a drawing of a bean.

Eli said the man used to talk to the little girl who lived here before. Lily never lived here. She never lived anywhere. She was Eli’s sister. And I’ve been trying not to think about how cheap this house was.

Torres’s abuela had it right. Lily was mine, and I set her down in a shoebox and never came back for her, so somebody else picked her up.

Since spring, my son has been feeding his little sister, and drawing for her, and carrying her messages, and nobody ever told him who she was. Eli’s always been good at obeying rules.

So here’s the second thing I need you to take from this post. Listen to your children. But when it’s the thing you can’t say out loud, say it to them anyway, before the opportunity strikes in an open room. Because you can never know for certain if it’s empty.

Torres drove me back to Stephanie’s. Neither of us said a word. I got there at a quarter to two and tucked the blanket around Eli, and he didn’t wake up.

It’s 4:51 a.m. now. I’m looking at my son’s feet. They just twitched.

The tape Stephanie put over the register behind the couch has come up at one corner. I can hear the furnace running.

He’s talking in his sleep. Low and quiet and patient, the way he does. For months I told myself that meant he was dreaming.

I’m not going to tell myself it’s the pipes. I’m going to sit here and listen.

“She says it’s okay, Dad,” my son says into the blanket. “She says you can tell me now.”
