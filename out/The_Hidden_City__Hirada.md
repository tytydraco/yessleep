I still don't know how I ended up in Hirada. One moment I was somewhere normal, and the next I was standing on a street that smelled like rust and wet paper, buildings on either side looking abandoned for a hundred years and lived in yesterday at the same time. I didn't know the name of the place yet. I just knew the air felt wrong, like something was watching me breathe.

I found out why about ten minutes later, when something ran past on all fours, way too fast to be human. I dropped behind a rusted car and didn't move for what felt like an hour.

That's when I met the bird.

He wore a mask shaped like a bird's face, blue, feathers stitched along the sides. I thought it was just a mask until I saw his hands — thin, feathered, tipped with something closer to talons than fingernails. He clamped one over my mouth before I could make a sound and whispered, "Don't. She hunts by sound."

He told me to call him Wren.

Over the next few days he explained it in pieces, because that's all I could take at once. Hirada is a city sealed off from the rest of the world on purpose. The government knows it exists. They just make sure nobody else finds out. Inside, there's a community — people and things that stopped being fully people — who work together for one reason: catch ordinary humans who fall in, like I had, and break them down into something else.

Wren wasn't one of them, even though he looked like one of their creations. He said he used to be fully human, before this place changed him. Whatever happened, it left the good part of him standing. He spent his time finding people who'd fallen into Hirada and keeping them alive.

He was the reason I survived my first week. And about two weeks in, he told me the real reason he hadn't left. Somewhere deep in the city, the community was holding his daughter. Her name was Sable. Dog had taken her almost a year earlier, during an escape that got most of the children out but not her. He'd been trying to get back to her ever since.

I said I'd help. He looked almost surprised that I meant it.

The first person meant to help us was a woman named Coe, who knew the old subway tunnels better than anyone left alive. She agreed to guide us toward the block where Dog kept her prisoners.

She led us three streets the wrong way, then walked off without a word, back toward the community's side of the city. Wren yanked me into an alley seconds before Dog rounded the corner behind us, already moving like she knew exactly where to look. We got away. Barely.

Wren didn't say much after. Just that trust gets people killed in Hirada faster than the monsters do.

The second time we got close, Stefhen was waiting instead, sitting on a crate like he'd been expecting us for hours, smiling that same grandfatherly smile. He never attacks directly. He just talks. He told Wren that Sable was "settling in nicely," that Wren could end all of this by simply walking in on his own.

Wren's hands shook through the whole conversation, but he didn't take the offer. Stefhen didn't chase us when we ran. He just laughed behind us, the sound following longer than his footsteps ever could have.

I asked Wren why Stefhen let us go so easily.

"He didn't," Wren said. "He wanted us to know he could've stopped us and chose not to. That's worse."

We finally reached the block, and Dog was already there, like she'd known exactly where we'd end up. Wren told me to run. I didn't, and it nearly got us both killed. She was faster than anything I've seen move. Wren stepped in front of me and took a hit meant for my chest — a claw across his shoulder, deep enough that blood soaked through the edge of his mask. He didn't go down. He held her off long enough to get us through the door behind her.

We found Sable inside. Scared, but alive.

I thought that was the end of it. It wasn't close.

Getting Sable out meant crossing through the one part of the city Wren had warned me about from day one — where Rotaerc stayed. He said the name like it was carved into a warning sign. But it was the only way through.

I expected a monster. What I found was an old man sitting alone in a room with no locked door, nothing stopping him from leaving if he wanted to. He looked at the three of us for a long moment, then looked only at Wren.

"You brought her back here," he said. "Into the one place they'll always look for her first."

Wren told him to shut up. Rotaerc kept going anyway. He said everything the community did — Stefhen's games, Dog's hunts, every child taken — ran on power that had once been his, yes. But he hadn't given it up willingly. Someone had been draining it from him for years, slowly, just enough each time that he grew weaker while the community grew stronger. He said whoever was doing it wanted him gone more than anyone else in Hirada did, because once he was dead, whatever was left of that power would have nothing left to hold it back.

He looked straight at Wren on the word "whoever."

I laughed. I actually laughed, because it sounded like such an obvious lie. Wren had saved my life. Taken a hit meant for me. Spent a year trying to get his own daughter home.

Then Rotaerc said one more thing, and the laugh died in my throat.

"Ask him why Dog let you both walk out of that building today. Ask him why Stefhen only ever slows you down instead of stopping you. Ask him why the only doors in this whole city that open easily are the ones leading to me."

I turned to Wren. For the first time since I'd met him, he had nothing to say.

It was Sable who spoke instead, quiet and flat, looking at her own father like she was afraid of him for the first time in her life. She said she hadn't been "kept" this past year the way he thought — she'd been shown things, taught things. She said Dog and Stefhen had answered to someone else for a long time now, and every rescue attempt Wren had ever tried had somehow ended a little closer to Rotaerc's room than the last one.

Every betrayal. Every convenient escape. All of it herding him here, the exact same way Stefhen herded children during his games — except this game had taken over a year, and Wren never once realized he was the one being led.

Wren took the mask off. I'd never seen his real face before that. It wasn't angry. It looked calm. Almost relieved.

"He's had that power for decades and done nothing with it but hide," he said. "I can actually use it."

I don't remember every detail after that. It happened too fast, and some of it I don't want to remember clearly. Rotaerc told me to run and not look back — the same words Wren had told me once, in the same city, for reasons I now understood were completely different. I remember light that wasn't really light. I remember Sable screaming her father's name, and it didn't sound like she was trying to stop him. It sounded like she was cheering him on.

I ran.

I don't know what happened to Rotaerc, or Sable, or Dog, or Stefhen. I don't know if Hirada has a new leader now, standing very still at the end of some street in a blue mask, watching everything unfold exactly the way he always meant it to.

I woke up in my own bed, heart slamming, checking my face in the mirror for feathers that weren't there.

I've told myself it was a dream. A long, detailed, unbelievably real dream. I mostly believe that.

But three nights ago I found a single blue feather on my windowsill, on the inside of the glass, and I still haven't been able to throw it away.
