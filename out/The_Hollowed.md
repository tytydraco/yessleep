Part 1: The First Empty Space

My name is Elias Crowe. I'm thirty-eight years old, and six months ago I killed my best friend without laying a hand on him.

At least, that's what the police report says. Vehicular manslaughter. Wet roads, too much speed, a curve I'd driven a thousand times. Marcus was in the passenger seat laughing at some stupid joke about our college days when the truck hydroplaned. The last thing he ever said to me was, "Slow down, man. You drive like your mom still owes you money." Then metal screamed, glass exploded, and everything went black.

I woke up in the hospital three days later with a shattered pelvis, a punctured lung, and the news that Marcus had died instantly. His wife, Lena, didn't even scream at the funeral. She just stood there holding their four-year-old daughter's hand and looked straight through me like I was already a ghost. I tried to tell her I was sorry. The words came out sounding like every other empty apology the world has ever heard. She nodded once, polite as a stranger, and walked away.

I sold everything after the settlement. The house, the car, the consulting business I'd built with Marcus straight out of grad school. I bought a one-way ticket to a small coastal town in Oregon where nobody knew me and rented a cabin on the edge of the forest. I told myself I was going to disappear quietly. Write the great American apology novel. Drink myself into liver failure. Whatever came first.

The first month was exactly what I deserved. Nights blurred together with cheap whiskey and the sound of rain on the tin roof. I didn't dream. Or if I did, I never remembered them. Then, on the night of the new moon, I woke up choking.

Not from vomit or acid reflux. It felt like something was missing from inside my chest. Not pain, exactly. Absence. A hollowed-out place where something vital used to sit. I stumbled to the bathroom mirror and pulled up my shirt. My skin looked normal under the harsh bulb, but when I pressed two fingers between my ribs, I swear the flesh gave way too easily, like pressing into warm dough. For a second I felt cold air moving inside me, brushing against organs that suddenly felt too large for the space they occupied.

I told myself it was a panic attack. Residual trauma. I took three sleeping pills and went back to bed.

The next morning the hollow feeling was still there, but smaller. Manageable. I made coffee and noticed my hands were shaking so badly I spilled half the pot. When I reached for the paper towels, my reflection in the window caught my eye. For just a moment, Marcus was standing behind me, smiling that crooked smile he always had when he was about to call me on my bullshit. I spun around. Nothing. Just the empty kitchen and the dark trees pressing against the glass.

That was Day One.

By Day Seven the hollow space had grown teeth. I'd be chopping vegetables for soup and suddenly feel a sharp, phantom cut across my knuckles. No blood, no mark, but the pain was real enough that I dropped the knife. Later that night I dreamed I was Marcus in the passenger seat, watching my own face twisted in concentration as the truck fishtailed. I woke up screaming his name.

I started keeping a journal because the internet told me writing down intrusive thoughts helps. The entries got weird fast.

October 12 – Felt Marcus's wedding ring on my finger all day even though I never wore one. My left ring finger is swollen now. No reason.

October 15 – Woke up tasting Lena's lipstick. The exact cheap drugstore brand she wore in college. I don't even like the taste of makeup.

October 18 – Found a child's drawing on the kitchen table. Stick figure family. One of them has X's for eyes. The handwriting is Sophie's. Marcus's daughter. I've never met her.

I burned the drawing. The ashes spelled out "sorry" in the sink before they washed away.

The town has one bar, one grocery store, and one ancient doctor who looks like he died in 1987 and just never got around to lying down. I went to see him on Day 22 when the hollow feeling moved into my throat and made swallowing solid food almost impossible. He poked at me, took blood, shrugged, and prescribed antacids. "Stress," he said. "Guilt'll eat a man alive if you let it."

He had no idea how right he was.

That night I got drunk again and called Lena. She answered on the third ring. Her voice was flat, exhausted. I told her about the dreams, the phantom pains, the way I sometimes caught myself walking exactly like Marcus used to, left foot dragging slightly from an old basketball injury. She was quiet for a long time.

"You don't get to do this, Elias," she finally said. "You don't get to make his death about your haunting. He's gone. You're still here. Live with it."

She hung up. I sat on the cabin floor crying like a child until the sun came up. When I stood, my knees cracked loudly and for a moment I felt Marcus's old knee brace tight around my leg even though I wasn't wearing one.

The next morning I found the first photograph.

It was tucked under my pillow. Polaroid style, slightly warped. Marcus and me at twenty-two, arms around each other's shoulders, grinning like idiots at some party. Except in this version, my face was wrong. The smile was too wide. My eyes were Marcus's eyes. His face had my old scar above the eyebrow. I tore it in half. The two pieces crawled back together on the nightstand overnight, the seam healing like skin.

That was when I started to understand this wasn't guilt. Guilt doesn't grow teeth and learn how to wear your dead friend's face.

I tried to leave town on Day 31. Packed the car at 4 a.m. and drove toward the highway. Ten miles out, the engine died. Not a normal stall. Every warning light flashed at once, then the radio came on by itself playing the last song Marcus and I ever argued about. I sat there in the dark while the forest pressed in close and felt the hollow space inside me expand suddenly, violently, like something was being born.

When I finally got the car started again and turned around, the odometer had rolled backward exactly 217 miles, the distance Marcus and I drove the weekend we first met Lena at that music festival in 2009.

I haven't tried to leave since.

Now it's been six months. I weigh 127 pounds. I used to be 198. My hair is falling out in clumps that look exactly like Marcus's coarse curls. Sometimes when I speak, his voice comes out instead of mine, deeper, warmer, the laugh still trapped in the back of my throat like it doesn't know it's supposed to be dead.

Last week I found Sophie's backpack on my porch. Pink. Covered in stickers. Inside were her kindergarten worksheets, all dated last month. Every single drawing showed a tall man with hollow eyes standing behind her father. The man is always smiling with too many teeth.

I sat on the steps holding that backpack until the rain soaked through my clothes and thought about what Lena said. You're still here. Live with it.

But I'm not sure how much of "me" is still here to live with anything.

The hollow space is spreading. Yesterday I felt a small, perfect hand reach up from inside my chest and press against my ribs from within, like a child trying to get comfortable. It felt like Sophie's hand. It felt like forgiveness and accusation at the same time.

I keep thinking about the moment of impact. The way Marcus looked at me right before the truck hit us, not angry, just surprised. Like he'd finally seen something in my face he'd never noticed before.

I think whatever took him that night didn't finish the job. It left something behind. Or maybe it took part of me instead and has been slowly replacing what it took with pieces of him. A perfect trade. One life for another, balanced to the ounce.

Tonight the cabin is quiet except for the sound of small bare feet running back and forth across the ceiling. Sophie never learned to walk without stomping. I recognize the rhythm.

I'm going to post this because someone needs to know what's coming. Because if I disappear completely, I want there to be a record that Elias Crowe was once real. That he owed a debt he never knew he was accumulating every time he laughed too loud, loved too selfishly, survived when better men didn't.

If you're reading this and you've ever walked away from an accident when someone you loved didn't, check the spaces between your ribs tonight. Press hard.

See if anything presses back.

I'm going downstairs now. The basement door just opened by itself. There's a red sneaker sitting on the top step that wasn't there yesterday. Marcus's. The one he lost in the wreck.

The hollow space is almost full.

I think it's almost ready to walk out wearing my face.
