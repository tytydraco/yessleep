# My grandfather had one rule about the cattle. Never count them after dark.



My grandfather raised cattle for sixty-three years on a little over four hundred acres in southern Missouri. He wasn't particularly religious, didn't believe in ghosts, and was generally the last person you'd expect to be frightened by anything.

He was, however, absolutely terrified of his cows.

I didn't really understand that until after he died.

When I was growing up, I spent most of my summers on his farm. Both of my parents worked, and Grandpa was happy to have me around, even if I wasn't particularly useful. I was much more interested in catching tadpoles and bothering the barn cats than helping with anything that actually needed doing.

There were always cattle in the south pasture. Sometimes twenty, sometimes thirty, depending on the time of year. Mostly Herefords, with a few Angus mixed in. Grandpa didn't name them, and he never let me name them either.

"Don't get attached to something you can't recognize," he'd tell me.

I always assumed he meant because the cattle would eventually be sold for slaughter. It seemed like a pretty reasonable explanation at the time, especially considering how upset I got whenever one of the barn cats disappeared.

There were a few other rules about the farm that I never really questioned as a child.

The barn doors had to be closed before sunset, no exceptions. I wasn't allowed to whistle toward the south pasture, and if I happened to notice a cow standing near the fence facing the house, I was supposed to close the curtains and leave it alone.

The strangest rule, though, was that I was never supposed to count the cattle after dark.

I asked him about that one a few times, but he always gave me some vague explanation about it being bad luck. Which was strange, because Grandpa wasn't the superstitious type. He thought practically every superstition was ridiculous.

Anyway, I broke that rule when I was nine.

It was late August, and I remember the humidity being absolutely miserable. Grandpa had fallen asleep in his recliner after dinner, and I was sitting on the kitchen counter eating peanut butter straight from the jar.

The kitchen window overlooked the south pasture, and most of the cows had gathered near the fence. I couldn't tell you why I decided to count them. I was nine, and there wasn't exactly much else to do at the time.

I got to twenty-nine.

That confused me a little because Grandpa had mentioned earlier that morning that there were twenty-eight cattle in that particular pasture. I thought maybe one of the cows had given birth, so I leaned closer to the window to see if I could find a calf.

I counted again and got thirty.

Then thirty-one.

I was starting to get frustrated because the animals kept shifting around, and it was difficult to tell which ones I'd already counted. There was also one cow standing toward the back of the herd that looked a little strange.

Even from that distance, I could tell it was taller than the others. Its back seemed to rise a good foot above the surrounding cattle, and its neck looked unusually long. It was standing with its head lowered, so I couldn't see much else.

I remember thinking it looked sick.

I started counting again, taking my time and trying not to lose track.

Thirty-two.

That was when the cows turned toward the house.

I'm not sure how to describe it without sounding ridiculous, but it was almost like they'd all heard something at exactly the same time. Every single one stopped what it was doing and turned to face the kitchen window.

It startled me badly enough that I dropped the peanut butter jar.

Grandpa woke up when he heard the glass shatter and came into the kitchen to see what I'd broken. He was halfway through asking me what happened when he noticed where I was standing.

I've never seen anyone's expression change so quickly.

He grabbed me by the shoulders and asked what I was doing.

I told him I was counting the cows.

"How many?"

I didn't answer right away because he'd frightened me more than the cattle had.

He asked again, louder this time.

"How many did you count?"

"Thirty-two."

He looked toward the window, then back at me.

"Did you count them twice?"

I nodded.

Grandpa walked over to the window and pulled the curtains shut so hard that the rod came loose from the wall. He told me to go upstairs and get into bed, and for once, I didn't argue.

That night, he slept in a chair outside my bedroom with his shotgun across his knees.

I remember getting up to use the bathroom around two in the morning and finding him sitting there, wide awake. When I asked what he was doing, he said he was keeping an eye on things.

The next morning, there were twenty-eight cattle in the south pasture.

Grandpa never brought it up again, and after a while, neither did I.

Grandpa died in February.

He was eighty-six, and according to the coroner, his heart simply stopped sometime during the night. He'd lived a long life, and while his death was upsetting, it wasn't exactly unexpected.

What did surprise everyone was his will.

He left me the farm.

All four hundred acres, the farmhouse, the equipment, and the livestock. My uncle was furious about it. He'd spent the better part of twenty years trying to convince Grandpa to sell the land to a developer, and I think he'd always assumed he'd eventually inherit the property himself.

There were some unusual conditions attached to my inheritance, though.

I had to maintain the south pasture, and I wasn't allowed to sell any of the cattle. I could keep the farm running however I wanted, but those particular animals had to remain on the property and be cared for until they died naturally.

I thought it was sentimental. Grandpa had spent practically his entire life raising cattle, and I figured he'd gotten attached to his last herd.

Looking back, I really wish that had been the explanation.

I moved into the farmhouse in April.

I work remotely, and I'd recently gotten out of a pretty ugly relationship, so the idea of living somewhere quiet without having to worry about rent was appealing. Besides, I'd always loved that house.

The first few weeks were uneventful.

I hired a local ranch hand named Daniel to help with the livestock. He'd worked for Grandpa for almost fifteen years and seemed genuinely pleased that I intended to keep the place running.

Daniel was a nice enough guy, although he wasn't particularly talkative. He knew the property better than just about anyone, so I mostly left the day-to-day decisions to him.

On his first morning, while we were going over the feeding schedule, he asked whether Grandpa had explained the rules about the south pasture.

I laughed and told him I remembered the counting thing.

He didn't laugh.

"There's a few others, too."

I told him I remembered those as well, and he seemed satisfied with that.

"Good. Just don't go changing anything."

I thought it was an odd thing to say, but Daniel had worked for Grandpa for years. I assumed he was just used to doing things a certain way.

I didn't really give it much thought.

The first incident happened in May.

I was working at the kitchen table when I noticed a cow standing outside the fence near the driveway.

That wasn't especially unusual. If you've ever been around cattle, you'll know they're surprisingly good at finding weaknesses in fencing. I'd already spent several weekends helping Daniel repair sections that looked like they hadn't been touched since the Nixon administration.

I figured one had gotten out again, so I put on my boots and went outside.

It was a Hereford, reddish brown with a white face. I recognized her because she had an unusual white marking on her left shoulder that looked almost exactly like a handprint.

I'd seen her in the pasture earlier that morning.

I approached slowly, talking to her the way you do when you're trying to convince a large animal that you're not about to make its day any worse.

"Come on, sweetheart. Let's get you back where you belong."

She didn't move.

That wasn't particularly concerning at first, although I did think it was strange that she wasn't even turning her head to look at me.

I walked around behind her, intending to guide her toward the gate, and noticed something odd about her udder.

She had seven teats.

Now, I'm not a veterinarian, and I know extra teats aren't unheard of in cattle. But these were arranged strangely. There were four in the usual positions, two smaller ones farther back, and a seventh near her belly.

I was trying to get a better look when the seventh one moved.

At first, I thought it was just swinging slightly as she shifted her weight.

But then it curled inward.

It was a slow, deliberate movement, almost like someone closing their finger into a fist.

I stood there for a few seconds, trying to figure out what the hell I'd just seen.

Then the cow turned her head toward me, and I heard Grandpa's voice.

"Don't get attached to something you can't recognize."

It was so clear that I actually turned around, expecting to find someone standing behind me.

There was nobody there.

I can't say for certain that the sound came from the cow. It seemed closer than that, almost like someone had spoken directly over my shoulder. But there wasn't anyone else around, and the voice was unmistakably his.

I ran back to the house and locked the door.

When Daniel arrived about an hour later, the cow was gone.

I told him what had happened, including the part about hearing Grandpa. By that point, I was beginning to wonder whether I should've kept that detail to myself.

He listened without interrupting, then asked whether the cow had a white marking on her shoulder.

I said yes.

He went quiet for a moment.

"Well, that's not possible."

I asked what he meant.

"That cow's been dead since 2019."

Apparently, she'd developed a severe infection after giving birth, and the vet hadn't been able to save her. Daniel had helped Grandpa bury her behind the old equipment shed.

He remembered her specifically because of the marking. Grandpa had called her Handprint, which was unusual because he never named his cattle.

I asked whether there could've been another cow with the same marking.

Daniel shrugged and said it was possible, but he didn't sound particularly convinced.

Then he mentioned something Grandpa used to say.

"He always reckoned they borrowed the ones we remembered."

I asked him what that was supposed to mean.

He looked out toward the pasture.

"You'd have to ask him."

"Daniel, he's dead."

"I know."

That was about as much as I managed to get out of him.

Three days later, I found Grandpa's journals.

They were in a locked cabinet in his bedroom, underneath a stack of old property tax records. There were eleven of them, dating all the way back to 1962.

Most were exactly what you'd expect from a man who'd spent his entire life farming. Feed costs, breeding records, weather observations, equipment repairs. He'd written down practically everything.

But every few pages, there would be an entry written in red ink.

The first few didn't seem particularly alarming. They were usually about cattle appearing where they weren't supposed to be or numbers that didn't match his records.

Then I found one from April 17, 1964.

Twenty-three at sundown. Twenty-four at sunrise. The extra one was gone before feeding.

Another, dated June 2, 1967, said:

Found a calf nursing from an animal that had no udder yesterday.

There was an entry from September 1971 about a man named Harold who'd apparently seen his deceased mother standing in the pasture.

And one from Halloween, 1978, that I must have read a dozen times.

One of them stood on its hind legs again.

On the following page, he'd added:

It wasn't trying to walk. It was trying to see over the fence.

I remember sitting on the bedroom floor with that journal in my lap, wondering whether Grandpa had been experiencing some kind of psychiatric problem.

The later entries were even stranger.

He'd drawn pictures of cattle with distorted limbs, unusually long necks, and things that looked like human hands hanging beneath their stomachs.

There were notes about animals appearing in places they couldn't possibly have reached and cows producing milk years after they'd stopped lactating.

One sketch showed a cow standing in profile. Its body looked fairly ordinary, but there was something strange about its underside. Grandpa had drawn what looked like five fingers extending from its belly.

Underneath the drawing, he'd written:

The cows are not who we think they are.

I recognized the phrase immediately.

There used to be an old illustration hanging in the hallway when I was little. It showed six cows, alternating directions, with those exact words arranged between them.

I'd always assumed it was some kind of farming joke.

I found the final journal at the bottom of the stack. The last entry was dated about three weeks before Grandpa died.

There were several pages filled with the same sentence, written over and over.

They aren't pretending to be cows.

I flipped through them, hoping there was something else that might explain what he meant.

At the bottom of the last page, he'd written another sentence and underlined it three times.

The cows are pretending that we are people.

I had absolutely no idea what to make of that.

Part of me thought it was evidence that Grandpa had been losing his mind toward the end of his life. He'd been getting forgetful, and while he'd never received a dementia diagnosis, I knew that didn't necessarily mean much.

But another part of me kept thinking about the cow near the driveway.

And that voice.

I decided to call Daniel.

He answered on the second ring.

I told him I'd found Grandpa's journals and asked whether he knew anything about them.

There was a long pause before he answered.

"You probably ought to leave those alone."

I asked why.

"Just think it'd be better."

I was starting to get frustrated. Between the cow and the journals, I felt like everyone knew something I didn't, and nobody wanted to explain it.

I told him I wasn't going to put them away until someone told me what was happening.

He sighed.

Then he asked a question I wasn't expecting.

"Have you noticed anything different about yourself since you've been back?"

I thought he was joking.

"Like what?"

"Your memory, mostly. Or your dreams. Anything like that?"

I'd actually been having some strange dreams.

In most of them, I was standing in the south pasture at night. I could feel wet grass beneath my feet, and there was always something enormous breathing somewhere behind me.

They weren't particularly frightening dreams, which was probably the strangest part. If anything, I usually felt comfortable in them.

I told Daniel about them.

He didn't seem surprised.

"Your granddaddy used to say the longer you stayed here, the harder it got to remember."

"Remember what?"

He was quiet for a moment.

Then he asked whether I remembered my grandmother.

I told him of course I did.

"What's she look like?"

It seemed like such a ridiculous question that I almost laughed.

But when I tried to picture her face, I couldn't.

I remembered practically everything else about her. Her birthday, her perfume, the way she'd brush my hair while I sat on her lap.

I even remembered the sound of her laughing.

But I couldn't remember what she looked like.

I told myself that wasn't particularly unusual. She'd been dead for years, and childhood memories are notoriously unreliable.

Still, it bothered me enough that I went into the hallway to look at the family photographs.

There was a wedding picture of my grandparents hanging near the stairs.

Grandpa was standing beside a woman in a white dress, but her head was turned away from the camera.

I checked another photograph.

Same thing.

In the third, her back was turned completely.

I started going through the pictures more carefully.

Almost every photograph of my grandmother had been taken from behind or at an angle that obscured her face.

Finally, I found one of her standing in the south pasture with the cattle.

She was facing the camera.

I wish I could explain exactly what was wrong with her face.

It was recognizably human, but the proportions seemed slightly off. Her face was unusually long, and her dark eyes were set farther apart than they should've been.

I stared at it for a while, trying to decide whether the photograph had been damaged or distorted somehow.

Then I dropped the frame.

Daniel was still on the phone.

He asked what happened, but I couldn't get an answer out.

Instead, he asked me how many cows were in the south pasture.

"Twenty-eight," I told him.

"You're sure?"

"Yes."

"Have you counted them?"

I looked toward the kitchen window.

The cattle were standing near the fence, just as they had been that night when I was nine.

I don't know why I started counting. Maybe I wanted to prove to myself that everything was normal.

I got to twenty-eight.

Then I noticed another one standing farther back.

Twenty-nine.

I remember hearing Daniel raise his voice over the phone, but I wasn't really paying attention anymore.

Because all the cows had turned toward the house.

The twenty-ninth cow was standing upright.

I don't mean it had reared up onto its hind legs. I've seen cattle do that before, usually when they're trying to mount another animal.

This was different.

Its back was almost perfectly straight, and its front legs were hanging loosely at its sides. Its head was tilted back at an angle that looked horribly uncomfortable.

There was also something moving beneath the skin of its belly.

At first, it looked like a muscle twitch.

Then a small shape pressed outward.

I could make out five separate points.

Fingers.

It looked like someone was pressing their hand against the inside of the animal, trying to push through its skin.

Another hand appeared beside it.

Then a third.

I could hear Daniel shouting at me to get away from the window, but I couldn't seem to make myself move.

The cow opened its mouth.

And I heard my grandmother's voice.

"Sweetheart, it's time to come home."

I don't remember anything after that.

Daniel found me unconscious on the kitchen floor.

According to him, the windows were covered in condensation, even though it was nearly eighty degrees outside.

There were also handprints on the glass.

Dozens of them, overlapping each other.

All on the inside.

He wanted me to leave the farm immediately, and for once, I wasn't interested in arguing.

I packed a suitcase, grabbed my laptop, and drove to a motel a couple of towns over.

That first night, I actually slept pretty well.

I didn't have any of the dreams I'd been having at the farmhouse, and when I woke up the next morning, I almost felt normal.

Then I noticed the mud.

There were four muddy impressions on the carpet between the bathroom and the foot of my bed.

They were spaced strangely, and they didn't look like footprints.

They looked like hoofprints.

I checked my shoes, the bathroom floor, and even the bottoms of my feet. There wasn't any mud on any of them.

I called Daniel.

He didn't answer.

I tried again about twenty minutes later and got nothing.

Then I received a text from his number.

Don't come back. They know you remember.

I called him immediately, but it went straight to voicemail.

A second message arrived a few minutes later.

I'm sorry about your grandfather.

Then another.

He wasn't supposed to die before you were ready.

I haven't heard from Daniel since.

I've been at the motel for six days now, and things have gotten considerably worse.

Yesterday, I found an old photograph in my suitcase.

I don't remember packing it, although I suppose it's possible I grabbed it with some of the other things when I left.

It showed me as a little girl standing beside Grandpa in the south pasture.

I was holding a bottle, feeding a calf.

At first, I didn't notice anything particularly unusual about it. Grandpa was smiling at the camera, and the calf was looking directly toward me.

Then I looked at myself.

My face was pressed against the calf's side, and my mouth was open.

It looked like I was trying to nurse from it.

I was so disturbed by the picture that I threw it into the trash outside my room.

This morning, it was sitting on the nightstand.

I spent almost an hour trying to figure out how it could've gotten there.

Eventually, I called my mother.

I asked her whether she remembered all those summers I spent at Grandpa's farm.

She sounded confused.

"Honey, you didn't spend summers there."

I laughed because I thought she was joking.

"Yes, I did. I practically lived there when I was little."

She insisted that wasn't true.

According to her, Grandpa wouldn't let me visit the farm until I was nine years old.

I told her that was ridiculous. I had memories of being there when I was much younger, and there were photographs to prove it.

She got quiet.

Then she said, "Honey, that's when we got you back."

I asked what she meant.

She started crying.

Apparently, when I was three years old, I disappeared from Grandpa's farm.

My parents had gone out of town for a few days, and he'd been watching me.

He told the police I'd wandered off while he was working outside. They searched the property, brought in dogs, and eventually organized a search that covered several miles of surrounding land.

They never found anything.

Eleven days later, Grandpa called my parents and told them he'd found me.

I was standing in the south pasture, completely naked and covered in mud, surrounded by cattle.

My mother said I'd been in remarkably good physical condition, considering I'd supposedly spent eleven days alone outdoors. I wasn't dehydrated, and aside from some scratches, there wasn't anything obviously wrong with me.

But my behavior had changed.

I wouldn't speak to my parents. I didn't seem to recognize them, and I became extremely distressed whenever anyone tried to touch me.

For almost a year, I refused to eat anything except grass, raw vegetables, and milk.

My mother said I also made a strange, low noise in the back of my throat whenever I got upset.

The doctors attributed it to trauma.

Grandpa had a different explanation.

He told my parents that the pasture had given me back.

My mother said he never elaborated on what that meant, and eventually, they stopped asking.

I wanted to know why nobody had ever told me any of this.

She said they hadn't wanted to upset me, especially after I'd started behaving normally again.

I asked whether Grandpa had ever mentioned anything unusual about the cattle.

She didn't answer.

I asked again.

Finally, she told me that when he'd first brought me home, he'd said something that had frightened her badly enough that she still remembered it more than twenty years later.

He'd said he wasn't sure he'd brought the right little girl back.

I started writing this because I genuinely don't know what else to do.

I realize how insane all of this sounds. If someone else posted this, I'd probably assume they were making it up, too.

But I need to get it written down somewhere, because I've been having trouble remembering things, and I'm starting to wonder how much of my own life actually happened the way I think it did.

There have also been some physical changes that I haven't been able to explain.

My fingernails have gotten noticeably thicker over the past few days. I first noticed it when I tried trimming them and realized the clippers wouldn't cut through one of them.

I've been waking up with dirt between my teeth, even though I haven't been outside.

Yesterday, I noticed a patch of coarse hair growing along my spine.

I thought about going to the hospital, but I wouldn't even know where to begin explaining what's happening.

And there's something wrong with my reflection.

At first, I thought the mirror in the motel bathroom was warped. It's an old building, and the mirror has some discoloration around the edges, so I didn't think much of it.

But this afternoon, I was brushing my teeth when I noticed my reflection wasn't moving at exactly the same time I was.

I'd stopped brushing and was looking at something on the counter, but my reflection was still moving its jaw.

It looked like I was chewing something.

I stood there watching it for what felt like a full minute.

Eventually, it stopped.

Then it looked directly at me.

I know that sounds stupid. Obviously, a reflection looks at you when you look at it.

But this was different.

Its eyes moved before mine did.

I covered the mirror with a towel and haven't looked at it since.

I tried calling my mother again, but her number has apparently been disconnected.

Daniel's number doesn't work anymore either.

I even tried looking up the farm on the county property records website, thinking maybe I could find another contact number for someone who worked there.

The address doesn't come up at all.

I've tried several different searches, including the parcel number from the paperwork Grandpa left me.

Nothing.

It's like the property never existed.

And before anyone suggests it, yes, I've considered that I'm having some kind of psychological breakdown.

At this point, I'd almost prefer that explanation.

Because I've started remembering things that I really don't think I'm supposed to remember.

I've been thinking a lot about that night when I was nine.

The night I counted thirty-two cows in the pasture.

Grandpa asked me how many I'd counted, and then he asked whether I'd counted them twice.

At the time, I thought he was just trying to figure out whether I'd made a mistake.

Now I'm wondering whether he was trying to find out if I'd counted myself.

I know how ridiculous that sounds.

But the memories I've been having are getting harder to ignore.

I remember being in the south pasture at night, surrounded by cattle.

I can remember the smell of wet grass and the warmth of the animals standing around me.

I remember being able to hear them breathing, all at slightly different intervals.

It's a strange thing to remember because I can't recall ever feeling frightened.

If anything, I felt safe.

I also remember Grandpa standing on the other side of the fence.

He was holding a little girl's dress.

He was crying.

There was a little girl standing beside him, holding his hand. She was crying, too.

She looked exactly like me.

I remember Grandpa begging me to come out of the pasture.

He kept telling the little girl beside him not to look at me, but she wouldn't stop staring.

I wanted to go to him. I remember that clearly.

I tried calling his name, but I couldn't make the words come out properly.

All I could manage was this low, frightened noise.

It sounded like a cow.

And I remember the little girl beside him smiling.

Not at Grandpa.

At me.

I've been sitting here trying to make sense of that memory for the better part of three hours.

I don't know whether it's something that actually happened or whether my brain has somehow pieced it together from everything I've been reading.

I don't even know which little girl I was.

Someone knocked on my motel door about twenty minutes ago.

Three slow knocks.

I wasn't expecting anyone, so I looked through the peephole before answering.

There's a cow standing in the parking lot.

A Hereford, with a reddish-brown body and a white face.

It has a white marking on its left shoulder that looks like a handprint.

It's standing directly outside my room, facing the door.

I haven't opened it.

I don't intend to.

The knocking continued for several minutes, even though I couldn't see anyone standing close enough to the door to make the sound.

Then I noticed something moving beneath my shirt.

At first, I thought I was imagining it. My stomach had been hurting all afternoon, and I'd barely eaten anything since yesterday.

But when I pulled up my shirt, I could see a small lump moving just beneath my ribs.

It pressed outward.

There were five little points at the end of it.

It looked like a hand.

I touched it, and something pushed back.

I'm sitting on the bed now, and I can still feel it moving.

The cow outside has started making noises.

I thought it was mooing at first, but then I realized I could understand what it was saying.

It sounds like Grandpa.

He's telling me not to be afraid.

He keeps saying that the little girl who came home from the pasture wasn't really his granddaughter.

That he knew something was wrong from the beginning.

He says he tried to find the real little girl for years, but eventually, he stopped looking.

He says he couldn't bring himself to abandon the one who'd come home.

And that he loved me anyway.

I don't know whether I believe him.

I want to.

God, I want to.

But I'm remembering something else now.

It's difficult to explain because it doesn't feel like a normal memory.

I don't know how old I was, or even whether I was a person when it happened.

I remember standing in the pasture with the others.

I remember watching the farmhouse windows light up in the evenings.

Sometimes, if the curtains were open, we could see the people inside. They'd sit around the kitchen table, talking and laughing while they ate their dinners.

I remember being fascinated by the way they moved.

How easily they picked things up.

The way they touched each other.

I remember wanting to go inside.

I wanted to know what it felt like to sit in one of those chairs, to be wrapped in a blanket, or to have someone brush the hair out of my face.

Mostly, I wanted someone to look at me the way Grandpa looked at that little girl.

I remember how long we watched them.

And I remember how long we practiced.

The knocking has stopped now.

I can hear something moving away from the door.

At first, it sounded like a person walking.

Two footsteps, followed by another two.

Then the sound changed.

Now there are four.

I finally worked up the nerve to look through the curtains.

There are twenty-eight cows standing in the motel parking lot.

I counted them twice to make sure.

Twenty-eight.

But I can hear twenty-nine breathing.

And the last one is coming from inside my chest.
