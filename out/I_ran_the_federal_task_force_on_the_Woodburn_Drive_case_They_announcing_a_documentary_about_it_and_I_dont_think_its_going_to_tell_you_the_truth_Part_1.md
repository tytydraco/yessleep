I'm not supposed to be writing this. I'm probably not legally allowed to write it, which is why this account is new and why I've changed most of the names, including the name of the town. If you followed the case closely you'll know which one it was anyway. Go ahead and know. I'm past caring about that part.

You might remember the story. A young woman born in captivity, found alive in a cell in a suburban basement. A neighbor girl across the street who finally saw something and picked up the phone. A pastor, and some woods. If you're the kind of person who listens to true crime podcasts on your commute, you probably think you know how it ends.

Last week my wife sent me a link to an article in one of the trades. A streamer has picked up a documentary series about the case. There's no release date yet, no episode count, nothing but a logline, a production company I'd never heard of, and the words "coming soon." It could be six months away or two years. There was a forty-second teaser attached to the announcement: slow pushes on a house I've stood inside of, set to a cover of a nineties pop song slowed down until it sounds like a funeral. There's a shot of me in it, actually, from behind, walking across a lawn with my partner. I didn't consent to that and neither did he, and I'd bet my pension the release forms are a work of art. The teaser ends on the neighbor girl, lit beautifully, staring past the camera and saying one line about how we see what we want to see.

She's right, and that's the problem. She's the only person in that teaser who's telling the truth, and she's telling it in a way designed so nobody will hear it.

So I'm going to tell it the way it actually happened, in the order I learned it, because that's the only way you'll understand how somebody who does this for a living missed what I missed. I believed almost everything I was told. I want you to keep that in mind the whole way through: I believed her, almost to the end.

It started with a 911 call on a summer evening, a little after seven.

The caller was a twenty-year-old woman I'll call Jenny. She told the dispatcher she'd just seen a young woman standing in the front window of the house across the street, and that the young woman had the words HELP ME written across the front of her white t-shirt. Jenny said she thought it was written in blood. The dispatcher asked her how sure she was, and Jenny said, and I've listened to this tape probably forty times, "She waved at me. She was waving at me like she knew me."

Jenny had lived on that street her whole life. In twenty years she had never once seen a young woman come or go from that house, and neither had anyone else on that street. The house belonged to a man I'll call Ryan, and it looked exactly like every other house on that street: vinyl siding, a lawn mowed a little less often than the neighbors would've liked, a white cargo van that came and went at the same times every day. Ryan was fifty years old and worked nights as a custodian at a memory care facility. He was the quiet kind. Everybody on the block knew him the way you know a mailbox. Jenny's family saw him more than anyone, just because their front windows faced each other across the street.

Local PD rolled first and took the door within the hour. They found the young woman in the basement, curled on the concrete floor beside a twin bed in a locked room with a steel door, eyes open and not really seeing anything. They also found the hallway outside that room. I'll get to the hallway.

I wasn't there that night. I was in a CrossFit gym two hundred miles away at six the next morning, hanging off a pull-up bar, when my phone cut off my music. By noon I'd been assigned to run the task force, because the Bureau took it over the second somebody counted what was in the walls.

Here's what was in the walls.

The hallway outside the basement cell was lined on both sides with metal utility lockers, the ordinary kind you'd find in a garage or a high school gym, built flush into the wall like someone had drawn up plans for exactly this. Twenty-two of them were full. Each one held a body sealed in a heavy plastic bag with a commercial vacuum sealer, the kind restaurants use to store meat, pulled down so tight against the skin that you could read the shape of the people inside like a relief map.

When the agent briefing me said the number out loud, I made him say it again. It didn't sound like a real number a human being says about other human beings. Eighteen women and four men. The women went back years, some of them so far back there wasn't much left for the ME to process. The men were all recent. The newest of them had been in that locker a matter of weeks.

A local cop in that first briefing made a crack about the guy switching teams, and the room laughed until it noticed I wasn't laughing. I gave a little speech instead. I said men like this chase a fix, and every time they get it they want it sooner and they want it more, and eventually the type stops mattering because the type was never really the point. I said it with total confidence in a room full of men who'd been waiting all morning to see whether I knew what I was doing.

I was right about the pattern. I want to be honest that I was right about the pattern, because I was wrong about nearly everything else.

Nobody could find Ryan.

His van was in the driveway, engine cold. There was no other vehicle registered to him anywhere in the country. No phone that we could find a record of, no email, no social media, no credit card that had been used in years, no family. His coworkers described him as quiet, harmless, forgettable, and then visibly struggled to describe him any further than that. One of them, a nurse who'd worked alongside him for six years, couldn't tell us his eye color.

The only photo we had to start was his driver's license, which was a month from expiring. I pinned a blown-up copy on the wall of the conference room we'd commandeered at the local precinct and stood in front of it for a long time. He had a symmetrical face and a neutral expression and was not handsome, not ugly, not anything. It was like looking at a man who'd practiced being unmemorable his whole life in case a day like this ever came. Your eyes slid right off of him.

No neighbor saw him leave that night. No neighbor saw him come home, either, in the days before the call. When police arrived, he was simply not there, like he'd known it was coming and had already stepped out of the frame.

There's one more thing about that night that I need to put down now, because it bothered me then and it bothers me a lot more now. Anna told me, in our second interview, that she knew she could wave at Jenny that evening because she'd heard the garage door go up and watched Ryan's van back out of the driveway. That was how she knew she was alone. She was very specific about it: the groan of the garage door, the engine turning over, the sound fading down the street.

The van was in the driveway when SWAT hit the door. The engine block was cold. It hadn't moved all day.

I wrote it off. I told myself that someone who'd spent twenty years in a box had folded a memory from some other afternoon into this one, which is a completely normal thing traumatized people do. I wrote it in my notebook with a little question mark next to it and I didn't look at it again for a long time.

The young woman's name was Anna. There were no records of her anywhere. No birth certificate, no hospital record, no missing persons report that matched her, because she had never been missing. As far as the world was concerned she hadn't existed before the evening she managed to ask for help.

I met her for the first time that first night, in her room at the hospital, during magic hour, with the sun coming through the blinds and turning everything orange. She was sitting up in bed watching the local news on mute. She was small, malnourished in a way you could see in her wrists, with fine blonde hair and a face that looked about fifteen until she looked directly at you. She had a stuffed bunny in her lap. It was missing an eye and a leg, and somebody had restuffed it over the years with old socks and strips of bedsheet.

I introduced myself and she said, "Hello, Special Agent Sherry. My name is Anna." She called me that every single time we spoke afterward, the whole title, like it was my first name. I'll keep calling myself Sherry here. It's what everyone called me on that case anyway.

The first thing she told me about, before she told me almost anything else, was the mask.

Ryan always wore a mask. A white theater mask, the tragedy half of the comedy and tragedy pair, carved into that fixed, weeping expression with a mouth frozen open mid-scream that never moved when he talked. She said she had never once seen his face. Not as a little girl, not as an adult, not on the last day she saw him. She talked about the mask like it was the first fact you needed to understand before the rest of it would make sense. For the first several interviews, the entire physical description she could give us of the man who'd kept her for her whole life was the mask, a pair of off-brand white tennis sneakers, and blue jeans that were a little too blue. That was all that had ever been visible to her.

So think about where that left us. We had a man whose own coworkers couldn't describe him and a driver's license photo designed by God to be forgotten, and the only living person who'd spent real time with him had never seen his face.

When I told my wife that part, she said, "Well, that's convenient." I told her she'd watched too much television.

Over the next week I interviewed Anna probably a dozen times. Some of it was me and a social worker. Some of it was through a two-way feed while a hypnotherapist the hospital brought in walked her back through her childhood, which I had real doubts about as an interview method and still signed off on, because we had nothing else.

Here is the life she described.

The TV in her cell was always on. Old sitcoms, always reruns, always with a laugh track, the kind of show where any bad thing that happens gets solved in twenty-two minutes with a hug. She described it less like entertainment and more like weather. It was just always there, a noise that was supposed to make the room feel like a home instead of what it actually was. She learned to talk from it, more or less. She'd do the voices. She had a catchphrase from one of those shows that she said to herself, and later to me, more times than I can count: "A promise is a promise is a promise." She'd say it in a deep, motherly voice and then answer herself in a little girl's voice. "I'm sorry, Mommy."

There was music too. One band more than any other. The first time I asked her about it she hummed me the melody perfectly, start to finish, and had no idea what the song was called or that it had ever existed outside of that basement. I didn't recognize it right away. It was Sugar Ray. One of those breezy, sun-warmed late-nineties radio hits about someone who makes you feel like nothing bad has ever happened to you. Ryan listened to that band exclusively, on headphones, every time he came down the stairs. Eventually he gave her a Discman and one CD, and when she was a good girl he'd leave two AA batteries in the crack of her door. She had that Discman and that CD in a hospital gift shop bag the day she left the hospital. It was the only thing she owned besides the bunny.

And then there were the mothers.

From the earliest age she could remember being told anything, Ryan told Anna that the women who came through that house were auditioning. That was the word she used with me, unprompted, more than once, like it was a job interview. Like a competition she was somehow the judge of, even though she had no say over anything that happened to any of them. He would bring a new one down and chain her to the wall across from Anna's bed and tell Anna that they were trying this one out. That if it worked, if this one was good, Anna would finally get a mother of her own. Someone permanent, someone who'd stay.

And Anna, at four years old, then six, then eight, then a good deal older than that, would sit on her bed with the bunny in her lap and watch a stranger chained to the opposite wall and hope that this one might be the one who stayed.

She told me he would have them teach her things. He wanted them to love her, and he wanted them to never be sad around her, and they were always sad around her, because how could they not be. And when they broke that rule, when they cried or begged or broke a promise, he'd punish them. When she was little he took them into the other room to do it. Later on he stopped bothering and did it in hers.

None of them worked out.

When Ryan was done with the mothers, Anna helped him seal the bodies into the bags for the lockers.

She told me that in our fourth or fifth interview, in the same voice she'd used to tell me about the laugh track. She said that once she was old enough it became her job to seal them up and clean up the mess he made, and that she didn't really mind it, because she wanted a clean room like the girls on television had.

The bags weren't always sealed over the dead. Twice, she told me, twice that she remembered clearly enough to describe, the machine was already running before it was over. She said you could watch the sides of the bag pull in tight around a woman's ribs with each pump of the little motor, watch the plastic go from loose to skin-tight to something with no give left in it at all, and that the sound changed near the end into the frantic, useless little heave of a chest trying to expand against plastic that had already won. She said that as a child the thing she noticed most was the fog on the inside of the bag, because the fog meant it wasn't over yet. She said the fog always slowed down before it stopped, like a window defrosting in reverse.

I have been doing this a long time. I have sat across from men who did things I will not describe even on an anonymous account. I had to excuse myself from that room and stand in a stairwell for a few minutes with my forehead against the cinderblock.

What stayed with me wasn't the detail. It was that she never once described any of it as something that had been done to her. She described it like a chore, the same as the floors.

Because she did the floors, too. That's something else I want you to hold onto. At some point, when she'd been "a good girl for a long, long, long time," her world got bigger. First the cell, and then the house. The kitchen, the bathroom, the den. By the end she'd spent years upstairs on her hands and knees, scrubbing the hardwood with a toothbrush and a jar of vinegar while Ryan watched from the hallway in his mask and told her that the real secret to old wooden floors was a little olive oil. She had the run of that house for years. She told me all of this freely. She was proud of how clean the floors were.

Near the end of that week I asked her, as gently as I could manage, whether she'd ever hurt any of them herself. Whether her hands had ever done more than clean up after his.

She wasn't offended so much as appalled, like I'd asked whether she'd ever eaten one of them. She pulled the bunny up to her chin and wouldn't look at me for the rest of the interview, and a nurse came in and asked me to give her some space. I gave it to her, and I felt like a monster for asking.

I never could shake the feeling, though, that I didn't have an accurate picture of what life inside that house had really been like. I told myself that was just the size of it. That no one ever could.

There was one more piece of her story that I didn't understand the importance of for a long time, and it's the piece that ends up mattering more than anything else.

Up near the ceiling of Anna's cell there was a ground-level egress window, painted over except for a thin slit at one edge. If she pushed her bed under it and stacked a wooden chair on top of the mattress and stood on her toes on the chair, balanced like a cat on a fence, she could see out through that slit at grass level. She could see a slice of the street. And directly across that street, she could see Jenny's house.

She'd done that, she told me, for most of her life. When Ryan was gone she'd climb up and watch for Jenny. She watched Jenny learn to ride a bike. She watched her get picked up for prom. She watched her leave for college with two friends and a Jeep full of duffel bags, and she counted the days until Jenny came home. She'd make the bunny talk in Jenny's voice. She'd have whole conversations with her. She told me Jenny was her only other friend, and that she used to imagine what it would be like to have a life like Jenny's. She wanted to go outside and to do so much. That was a phrase she used constantly, "to do so much," like a thing she'd been saving up.

The first time she said the name "Jenny" to me, on that first night in the hospital, I stopped her.

"How did you know her name?" I said.

She told me the social worker had said it to her that morning. I checked, and the social worker had. I checked a lot of things Anna told me over the course of that case, and nearly all of them turned out to be true. I want you to understand how much of what she said was true.

And there was the bunny.

The one she held in her lap through every audition, every mother who didn't work out, every year of her life. During one of our last interviews before she left the hospital, I finally asked her where it came from. She said Ryan had brought it home to her one day as a reward for being a good girl.

That tracked, honestly, because that's something men like Ryan do. It's practically in the manual.

What I didn't know yet, and what Jenny told me later, was that the bunny was Jenny's. It had come in her Easter basket when she was little and she'd loved it, and one day it was just gone, and she'd cried about it for a week. Ryan must have picked it up off the sidewalk or out of the gutter and carried it across the street and down the stairs to the little girl in his basement.

Jenny recognized it immediately, that first night, when the paramedics carried Anna out of the house on a stretcher with the bunny clutched in her right arm. Jenny had been told by her parents not to move from their front lawn, and she didn't, at first. One of the first officers on scene told us later that the two of them looked at each other across that street for maybe four seconds, two young women who had never spoken a single word to each other in their entire lives. He said it didn't look like two strangers at all.

Then Jenny ignored her parents and crossed the street and got to the ambulance before the paramedics could close the doors, and Anna grabbed her hand and wouldn't let go. One of my agents was there and told Jenny they needed to get her friend to the hospital. Everyone who was there says Anna's face lit up at that word like nothing that had happened to her had ever happened at all.

I want to tell you about Jenny's family now, because whenever that documentary comes out it's going to tell you about them too, and it's going to be wrong in a different direction.

Jenny's father was a pastor. I'll call him Blake. Her mother, Karen, was the kind of woman who makes three cups of tea for two FBI agents and her husband even though she knows her husband won't drink his. The first time I sat in their living room, Blake spent most of the conversation telling my partner how nice and normal Ryan had always seemed, and how Karen used to invite him to their Christmas party every year, and how he'd always had some excuse. He said that some years, looking across the street at Ryan's dark house with the TV flickering, he'd wanted to trade places with him. It looked more fun over there. He laughed at his own joke, and nobody else did.

Blake did not like me. That's fine, plenty of people don't. He made it clear almost immediately that he didn't like that I was a woman, and that he liked even less that I was the kind of woman I am, and within a few days I'd heard secondhand what he called me at his dinner table. I'm not going to repeat it. It was creative, for a man of God.

Karen was the one who handed me the photograph.

It was a framed picture off their bookshelf, taken in their front yard the previous fall: Jenny and her two best friends posing in front of a Jeep, the day they left for college. Karen held it like it was the last good thing that had happened in that house. And there, in the background, out of focus across the street in Ryan's driveway, was Ryan, getting out of a car. And getting out of the passenger side of that same car was a woman with long blonde hair in a white dress.

It was the only photograph of Ryan anyone had ever taken that we could find. It was also the only photograph of anyone, ever, standing in that driveway with him.

That night I showed it to Anna. I asked her if she knew the woman. She stared at it for a long time. She clenched her jaw and started breathing loudly through her nose, and finally she nodded. She said she never knew their names. She said yes, Ryan had killed that woman. She said yes, that woman had been in the house.

The next morning the ME sent over photos of the remains from one of the older lockers. White fabric, what was left of it, and long blonde hair. I pinned that photo up next to the Jeep photo on the evidence wall and felt, for the first time in that case, like something had actually clicked into place.

I want you to remember that photo. I want you to remember the white dress.

A few more things, quickly, because I'm running long and my wife has come downstairs twice to ask me to go to bed.

At the end of the first week, the DNA came back on Anna. No familial matches anywhere in the system. She was over eighteen, almost certainly, which made her a legal adult with no ID, no money, no family, and nowhere to go. The hospital wanted the bed. Social services told me she was, quote, "too old for the usual options." She repeated that phrase to me later like she was quoting something in a language she didn't speak.

The same week, I dropped my laundry off at a dry cleaner downtown. It was a little family place with an old metal cash register and a dollar store calculator duct-taped to the counter, run by two sisters. I'd packed in a hurry, and when the woman at the counter started going through my mesh bag she pulled out a child-size sock with blood on it, sealed in an evidence bag. She didn't even blink. She said they cleaned blood all the time. I apologized and took it back and she told me I needed more sleep, which was fair.

I'm telling you about the dry cleaner because I didn't think it mattered at all, and I need you to know that I didn't.

During one of the hypnotherapy sessions, Anna told the therapist about her real mother, the first one. She said she remembered big, soft curls that she touched once, maybe twice, and that her mother used to hum a song she couldn't remember but could still feel, and she put her hand flat on her chest when she said where. Then she said the mommies always broke their promises, and Ryan didn't like that, and he would "punish" them.

That was the word I'd used with her the night before, the first night, when she asked me coldly why I wanted to find Ryan and I told her he needed to be found so he could be punished. It wasn't just the word. It was my delivery. My exact pause in front of it. I watched her say it on a little black and white security monitor in a room full of hospital IT equipment and the hair on my arms stood up, and then I told myself that traumatized people mirror the people they're trying to trust, which they do.

Here is where I made the decision that I'll be living with for the rest of my life.

Jenny started visiting Anna in the hospital. Nobody asked her to. She talked her way past a security guard the first time and then just kept coming back. When I caught her in the hallway one afternoon, she told me something I've never forgotten. She said that when she was a little girl she thought Ryan's house was cursed, fairy tale cursed, and that she used to imagine a princess trapped down there behind the basement window, guarded by some evil thing. She said it made her sick now, because she wondered if she'd imagined it because she'd actually seen her, and her brain had just turned it into something it could live with.

Then one night Jenny called my cell a little before midnight. She was in her car behind the hospital with the headlights off. She told me Anna had shown up at her bedroom window an hour earlier, grinning, scaring the hell out of her, just to say hi. Jenny had asked her how she'd gotten out of the hospital. Anna had said, "Through the window," like it was obvious, and Jenny had driven her back and sat in the parking lot and watched her climb back in "like a cat," which were Jenny's words, and then called me.

She asked me if Anna could come live with her family.

I want to be clear about what I did next, because the documentary is going to make it sound like the Khourys took Anna in out of Christian charity, and I'm sure Blake will be shown saying the words "What would Jesus do" on camera, because he said them on camera, to a local news anchor, on his own couch. Charity had nothing to do with it. That was my call. I stood in front of a room of agents and told them that Anna would move in with the Khourys, quietly, off the books, across the street from the house where she'd been kept. I told them that if Ryan was out there, if he was watching, he would come back for her. I told them I wasn't betting her safety on a hunch, I was betting it on everything we know about how predators work.

One of my agents asked if we were really going to dangle a survivor in front of her abuser. I said I was open to better ideas, and nobody had one.

So we put her in that house as bait. I want that on the record somewhere, even if the only record is an anonymous post on a horror forum.

Three nights after Anna moved in, the Khourys' house phone rang during dinner.

Jenny answered it first. She went white and handed it to her father, and Blake told me later that all he could hear on the other end was heavy breathing, and not the scared kind. It was the other kind, like a man in the middle of something. Blake shouted into the phone, asking who it was and what he wanted, and the breathing turned into muttering, and then into laughter, and then a man's voice said:

"My daughter. Yours too, Pastor."

And hung up.

We put a trap on the line and a surveillance van on the street and doubled the cars in the driveway. Anna had a panic attack on the Khourys' living room carpet, flat on her stomach, feeling the floor with her hands and saying "He's here, he's here, shh," over and over, the same thing she told me she used to say to the mothers when she heard Ryan's van on the gravel. Blake asked me, in front of his family, what my supervisors thought of my crazy little trap, and what they teach at Quantico these days about how all men act the same. I told him we'd learned it doing our job.

Two nights later, the phone rang again, right after the local news ran a puff piece on the Khourys taking Anna in. It was the same breathing, and this time we traced it.

It came from a payphone outside a gas station about four miles from the house. I didn't know there were still payphones. I was jumping rope in my hotel room when my partner called, and I drove there in my sweat-soaked workout clothes, and local PD already had it taped off. My partner walked me up to the phone and pointed at the back of the receiver.

The gas station camera had caught a figure in a hoodie hang up the phone and press something onto it before walking out of frame. It was a sticker. A little cartoon banana, the kind they hand out to kids at the grocery store.

The first night I met her, in that orange hospital room, Anna told me that Ryan had a nickname for her. He called her Banana. I wrote it in my notebook. As far as I knew, at that moment, the only people on earth who'd ever heard that nickname were Anna, the man who'd raised her in a box, and me.

I stood there at that payphone for a long time. My partner didn't say anything. The wind picked up, and somewhere off to the west there was a wall of black clouds coming in fast, and I remember thinking, very clearly, that we were finally going to get him.

I'll post the rest when I can. My wife thinks I shouldn't post any of it. She thinks I'm doing this because I can't sleep, and she's right about that too, but that's not the whole reason.

The whole reason is that a documentary is coming, I don't know when, and the person telling the story in it is the person who knows the most about what really happened in those houses. And I've seen the way she looks into that camera in those forty seconds, and I've seen what she did with her hair.

She dyed it blonde, the same shade as the woman in the white dress.
