I restore old buildings. Salt damage, cracked plaster, rotting drainage: unglamorous work that doesn't make newspapers. So when my firm got a small contract to survey the brick foundations along the Yamuna, behind the Taj Mahal, I took it without a second thought.



The tourists gaze on white marble, but they overlook whatever holds it up.



Beneath the river terraces, a second city sprawls: brick corridors, limestone arches damp with sweat. Mughal storehouses, long abandoned. An easy job, taking moisture readings and compiling a structural report - six or seven weeks, a halogen lamp, and a damp meter. I should have asked why no one else wanted it.



There is only one way in, a narrow set of stone stairs, sixty-three steps down. I counted them every day. By the second night I had noticed things I told myself weren't strange. There were no bats, no insects, no pigeon droppings, no rats, none of the life that clings to every shadow in India. And the air didn't smell of the river, not even though I stood a few meters from it. It was cold, the inside of a coin.



On my third night, my lamp caught a glint from behind a collapsed pillar.



A low alcove, four feet wide, was sealed with lead. Not a plate or a patch of it, but sheet after sheet, blackened and layered like scar tissue, attached to the stone with iron bolts the thickness of my thumb. The bolts driven in from the outside.



I remember thinking that whoever did it wasn't keeping something in, they were making sure nothing was ever taken out.



And I remember thinking, God help me, this is going to make my career.



It took forty minutes with the crowbar. The last sheet peeled away with a sound like a long exhale, and cold air spilled over my boots. It smelled of old copper, of wet clay, and beneath that, something faintly sweet, like the room of someone who has been sick a long time.



Inside was a stone sarcophagus, life-sized and plain. No inscription, no calligraphy, no floral border. Mughal tombs are never plain.



Then I raise the lamp on the back wall and freeze.



It is brick. Not Mughal lakhori brick, the thin flat lime-set bricks I have worked with for fifteen years, but modern red brick, thick and machine-pressed. Every one of them with a small stamp in the corner: a lotus in a circle.



I know that stamp. I grew up with it. My grandfather had an old kiln in Shahganj, and built our family home out of his bricks. I used to trace that lotus with my finger on the wall of my bedroom window.



That wall is here, in a chamber sealed with lead for centuries.



I don't know how long I stand there. Long enough for the lamp to begin to tick as it heats.



Then from inside the stone box: Tap. Tap.



A pause.



Tap.



Something shifts in the darkness of the alcove, and a voice speaks softly in Hindi. It uses my childhood name, the one only my family has ever called me. Warm and tired and unmistakably my mother's.



"Munna... beta, tune hawa kyun aane di? Ab usse seedhiyan dikh jayengi."



Munna... son, why did you let the draft in? Now it can see the stairs.



I don't remember dropping the lamp, or the climb. I only remember reaching the top with my hands bleeding, and somewhere behind me, far below I heard metal scrape on stone. It sounded like someone dragging a sheet of lead back over the floor.



I haven't gone back. My firm has been asking for days. I told them it's a structural hazard and the site was unsafe, which isn't a lie.



I called my mother the next morning. She is fine. She is alive, living in Shahganj, in the same house as before. She was confused that I sounded so frightened, so I laughed it off. Then she fell quiet and said:



"Munna, I wanted to ask you something. Why do you keep calling me from inside the wall of your old room? Every night, at three. Since the monsoon."



The monsoon was months before I took this contract.



That was five nights ago. Every night since, at exactly 3:00 AM, my apartment door rattles in its frame. Not like someone is trying to get in, but slower. As if someone leans their entire weight against it as they hammer something into the frame. Bolt after bolt, and I hear the metal ring. I've checked the peephole every night, but there's never anyone there. Yesterday morning I found a thin grey line of dust along the threshold, and when I rubbed it between my fingers, it was heavy and soft and left a dark smear on my skin.



Lead.



It was on my side of the door.



I've been thinking about what she said. Now it can see the stairs. I used to believe the voice was blaming me, but now I think it was warning me. I don't think the lead was keeping something in. I think it was keeping something out, and I opened the only door that was keeping it back.



Last night, after the rattling stopped, I took a screwdriver to the wall beside my bed and pried off a patch of plaster about the size of my hand. My apartment is built of 2011, of concrete, plaster, cheap fly-ash brick. I've been checking the building plans. I look at the wall, expect to see thick brick and I see red brick, thick and machine-pressed. A small stamp in the corner.



A lotus inside a circle.



I haven't slept. I'm writing this from a café across town at 12:13 PM, in full daylight, surrounded by people, because I told myself this was the safe part of the day.



An hour ago I went back to the apartment just to get my charger. I stood next to this hole in the wall for maybe ten seconds.



And from somewhere behind it, very softly, at noon:



Tap. Tap.



Pause.



It never used to knock before three.



I don't think it needs the dark anymore.



I'll update if I can.
