Content warning: body horror and eye injuries.

"Go on, try to blink."

The new barista at our hospital café said it as soon as I'd finished the espresso she'd salted in front of me, and I tried, and I couldn't.

I'm a nurse on a locked psychiatric ward in southern Italy, three floors above that café. It was twenty to eight on a Saturday in late October last year, just before my night shift. She'd sprinkled the salt from a twist of paper in her apron pocket. When I asked why, she said it was an old trick to take the bitterness out. I told her the coffee wasn't that bad.

"I didn't mean the coffee," she said.

My eyes stayed open while the air dried them, and I kept swallowing salt that wasn't in my mouth anymore. Nine seconds. The café clock has a red second hand, and with my lids stuck open there was nothing to do but watch it move.

"There," she said, when they finally closed. "Now you'll see it through."

Her name tag said IDA, a name from my grandmother's generation, and she couldn't have been thirty. She'd started on Monday. My espresso had been on the counter before I reached the register, and I hadn't been in all week.

Giulia, our nursing assistant on nights, had already let her read her palm. Ida traced one of the lines with a fingernail and said, "You'll sleep tonight. Really sleep, for once." Giulia laughed. She claims she hasn't had a decent night's sleep in four years.

Nunzia was the third of us. She's our head nurse and usually works days. The nurse scheduled to work with me had called in sick, and Nunzia couldn't find anyone to cover. She'd given up her Saturday off rather than bring in someone from another ward. As we were leaving, her elbow caught her empty orange juice glass and knocked it off the counter. She bent for the pieces, and before anyone could move, Ida was around the counter with her hand closed around Nunzia's right wrist. I heard a small dry snap.

"Don't," Ida said. "You'll need that hand tonight."

Nunzia told me afterward it was a static shock, the doorknob kind, except it went all the way to her elbow. We went upstairs.

A night shift with Nunzia turned out to be exhausting. She had me recount the money in the patients' property envelopes because one had a crease she didn't remember, and sign the fire-safety training sheet again because my September signature looked rushed. She runs that training herself, in the same words every time: in smoke, one hand on the wall, and you don't take it off until you're outside. I signed it with my eyes stinging. Twice that evening I'd realized I hadn't blinked in a while.

Around midnight the woman in room 2 couldn't settle, and on our ward one person awake at midnight turns into three by one o'clock. Nunzia sat on the end of her bed and breathed with her, counting out loud, in for four, out for six, until she fell asleep. Back at the nurses' station she told me my handwriting on the observation chart was a disgrace.

At two Giulia went to the staff kitchen for her hour-long break, with her alarm set for three. My lower back had started its usual complaint about the station chairs, which were chosen by someone who has never sat in one past midnight. Nunzia stayed at the desk with the paper forms while I started my two o'clock rounds. In room 1, both beds had been stripped down to their iron frames. The patients were gone.

The walls had gone a flat green I'd only ever seen in old photographs of this building, and the light was dim and gray and came from nowhere.

Nunzia said my name from the station, in the voice she uses on relatives.

Four people stood in the corridor between us, very thin, their clothes from different decades. One man wore a gray work coat. A woman had a long dark skirt and her hair tucked into a net. All of them stood still, facing the same way, like people waiting for an elevator. My shoulder found the doorframe and stayed there. For a few seconds the only plan in my body was to back into that bare room and shut the door.

She said my name again, and I walked down the middle of the corridor between them. As I passed the man in the work coat, he turned his head and followed me with it, slowly, the way a houseplant follows a window. His eyes were open.

Nunzia was at the desk with both hands raised near her face, not touching it. Her eyelids were sewn shut. Black thread, small stitches, close and even, neat as a hem. No blood. I looked at it like a nurse, because that was the only way I could look at it at all.

"I can't open them," she said. "Matteo, there's something in my lashes."

"Don't touch it," I said. "It's thread."

She put both palms flat on the desk, which is what she does when she's decided something. "Describe," she said.

I described the station, the dead monitor, the paper forms, the green walls. I didn't describe the corridor. When I told her the beds in room 1 were bare, she was quiet, and then she said, "Then the patients aren't here. We are." The other half came to me a second later: if the patients weren't here, nobody was with them.

The station phone had no tone. My cell had no signal, but its clock still worked: 2:06. Nunzia came with me, one hand on the wall the whole way, just as she teaches it. Giulia wasn't in the staff kitchen. Where our ward door should have been, there was green plaster. My key turned in the staff fire door to the service stairs, and behind it was a brick wall. We spent nearly half an hour on those two doors before I believed them.

On our way back past the service corridor, we heard knocking from inside the storage room. Soft and heavy, with a few seconds of silence between knocks.

We keep the patients' belongings in a locked section at the back of the storage room. There are shelves, a drawer for the property envelopes, and a tall metal cabinet at the far wall. In the dim light, the shelves stood empty and the cabinet was gone. In its place was a doorway, bricked up to waist height, and the opening above the bricks was black. An old man stood with his face pressed against it.

It was the man in the gray work coat. Up close he was so thin I could see the hinges of his jaw. He kept leaning over the bricks into the dark, which stopped him like glass with a soft knock of his forehead. Each time he straightened and tried again. His eyes had gone white, like a fish's on ice, and he had no eyelids left. There were no scars. They looked worn away.

"Is it three yet?" he said, toward me, though I hadn't made a sound.

My phone said 2:41. "Twenty to three," I told him.

"Then wait." He leaned into the dark again, and the dark knocked him back.

Behind me, voices passed it along, "twenty to three," one to the next, back up the corridor, quieter each time.

The others had moved while I'd been watching him. Now they stood between us and the station. I kept Nunzia close to the wall and led her through. They made room for us. Then they followed. I locked us inside the station, and they gathered on the other side of the glass.

There were five of them now. I'd passed four in the corridor. I counted again, and still got five. Their faces were close to the glass, every one turned toward me and none toward Nunzia. Every so often one would say "Is it three yet," and the knocking from the storage room kept time underneath. Nunzia sat with her palms on the desk and asked me the time every few minutes. I told her, and I didn't tell her about the glass. The woman with the hairnet pressed her fingertips to the glass in front of Nunzia's face. She picked at it, patiently, the way you'd work at a stitch you wanted out. I moved my chair between them, and her face came around to me and stayed there. My thumb kept waking my phone. 2:52. 2:55. At three Giulia's alarm would go off. She'd wake and find us missing. I believed she'd come looking for us the way you believe in the end of a shift. 2:58. 2:59.

Then it said 2:00.

I'd known it all night, in the part of my head that does the paperwork. I'd put the paper forms out myself. Here in Italy, on the last Sunday of October, the clocks go back from three to two. We chart that repeated hour by hand because our software can't hold the same hour twice. My phone had just handed me another hour of this. Giulia's alarm was still an hour away.

Outside the glass, all five turned at once, away from me, toward the storage room. The knocking had stopped. Something at that door had changed.

"We're going," I said. "Now." Nunzia was already up, one hand finding the wall, the other knotted in the back of my scrub top, and we went after them.

At the back of the storage room, they crowded the half-bricked doorway. The black above the bricks had gone soft. One after another, they leaned into it. It swallowed each face as far as the eyes, then pushed them back out. When the woman in the hairnet covered her eyes, two of the others caught her wrist and pulled her hand away. She tried again, and they dragged her back from the opening. They were keeping each other there.

All of them had their eyes open. Nunzia's were sewn shut, so I reached past them and guided her right hand into the black. It went in to the wrist, as if into cold water. She leaned forward until her whole face was inside, and nothing pushed her back. When she drew her head back, I tried the same. The black let me in up to the eyes and pushed me out, the same as them.

The old man found me before I could help her over. His hand closed on my wrist, very cold and very light, like a bundle of pencils. "You can see," he said.

He brought his white eyes close to my face.

"Did she salt yours too?"

I told my lids to close. They stayed where they were. At the café I'd had the red second hand to count by. Here, nothing could tell me how long it would last. I covered my eyes with my free hand. Other hands closed around it, three or four of them, cold and patient, and pulled it down against my side.

"The stinging stops," the old man said, as if he were doing me a kindness. "After the first few years."

"Matteo." Nunzia had turned her face toward me. "Are your eyes open?"

"I can't close them."

She took her hand back out of the dark and put it over my eyes.

Everything went black and warm and smelled of the hand cream she keeps in the station drawer. Her other hand was on the wall. "One hand on the wall," she said, in her training voice, "and you don't take it off until you're outside."

The hands on my other arm let go and came for hers. Fingers hooked under the edge of her palm and tried to pry it off my face. A thumb slipped beneath the heel of her hand and drew down my lower lid, gently, as if checking me for anemia. Voices kept saying "look" and "look at me" against my ear. The old man's hand stayed closed around my wrist. Every hand that grabbed Nunzia's wrist let go with that same small dry snap. None came back. My knee struck brick. Her other hand slid down onto the top row of bricks.

"Up," she said. "Over."

I got my free hand onto the bricks. Nunzia's shoulder stayed against mine as we climbed. The old man held on all the way up and over, his grip sliding from my wrist to my hand to my fingers. The black was setting around my trailing leg like wet cement. I pulled, and my foot came out and found a floor, and his fingers weren't there anymore.

When Nunzia lifted her hand from my eyes, we were in the narrow space between the property cabinet and the wall. We pushed the cabinet out a foot and squeezed past, white to the elbows with mortar dust. The shelves were full again, the envelopes in their drawer. Nunzia blinked beside me. My phone said 2:04.

Behind the cabinet, the half-bricked doorway was still there. A trowel was embedded in the top course of bricks, its blade caught in mortar that looked older than I am. Whoever had been sealing it up had stopped halfway.

All twelve patients were asleep in their beds. I checked each one with a flashlight, twice. Giulia was asleep in the staff kitchen with her alarm still nearly an hour away, and it took me three tries to wake her.

I locked the storage room and called the doctor on call, then the night supervisor. Two nurses came up to cover before Nunzia and I went down to the emergency department, still white with mortar dust. Under the brighter lights I saw rows of tiny red dots along her eyelids, evenly spaced, like a hem with the thread pulled out. They took blood samples and kept us under observation. At five, Nunzia asked what I'd kept from her. I told her, the glass included.

Before my next night shift on Sunday, I went back to the café. Ida hadn't shown up. She hadn't called, either, and her week's pay was still waiting. The owner said she'd told him on Monday, before she'd made a single coffee, that she'd only need the job until Saturday.

He asked if I wanted my usual and reached under the counter, and I stood very still until he came up with a clean cup.

I drank it plain, and I blinked the [whole time.](https://www.reddit.com/u/FireDoor_RN/s/cnJqihGKvA)
