My neighbor Todd mows his lawn on Saturdays, wears a wide smile, and takes his family to Florida once a year. He drives the speed limit. He returns your snowblower before you even ask for it back. That's the whole picture.  
   
But that wasn't always the case. Before Megan and Nora came along, he used to make that exact same trip to Florida every single year—just with a different girl. A blonde, long hair, always laughing. Lisa. Then one year she vanished, and he came back alone. A year later, he married Megan.  
   
This time around, he asked me for a favor: water the plants, feed the cat, and grab the mail so the place wouldn't look empty to potential burglars. Standard routine for our quiet neighborhood.  
   
When he was handing over the keys, I helped him load his suitcases into the car. There were three of them. Heavy as hell, like they were packed with bricks. I cracked a joke about it.  
   
"Megan's been packing all summer," Todd replied with a smile.  
   
The rear windows of his Ford Explorer were tinted pitch-black. You couldn't see a thing inside.  
   
"You know, it's going to be a quiet ride," he said, almost to himself. "Things are pretty tense right now."  
   
I just nodded, not knowing what to say. The whole block knew Todd and Megan were hitting a rough patch. Word was she'd already been talking to a divorce lawyer.  
   
The car pulled away. I stood there for a bit, watching it disappear down the street.  
   
Todd left last Monday. The first three days were completely normal. I'd come over after work, scoop some food for the cat, water his damn ficus trees, and leave. Being inside that empty house made me think of my own. I lived alone—my fiancée had walked out on me six months ago, and coming back to a silent house every night was a total nightmare.  
   
I keep thinking about what a normal guy he was. When my fiancée left, he was the only person on the block who brought me dinner. Meatloaf. In a Tupperware container. He didn't say anything. Just handed me the container and went back across the street.  
   
That's the Todd I knew.  
   
I remember a couple of months back, when I was sitting on my front porch, Todd walked over to keep me company. He brought a couple of beers, listened to my drunk rambling, and gave me an encouraging pat on the back. I remember his eyes going completely blank then, staring out into nothing, as he muttered under his breath, almost in a trance:  
   
"You have to bury the past so deep that it can never remind you of itself."  
   
At the time, it felt like profound, hard-earned wisdom. I thought he was genuinely trying to help me turn the page.  
   
Everything changed on Thursday when I went to grab the mail. The box was stuffed with flyers, water bills, and one thick, brown envelope with no return address. Written across it in a messy, rushed handwriting were the words: "To Todd, from M."  
   
I don't usually read other people's mail. But the envelope was barely sealed, and as I pulled it out of the box, a photo slid right out.  
   
I picked it up. The picture showed the corner of my own house at night. A Polaroid—one of those instant ones, with the white border at the bottom. The shot had been taken from Todd's second-floor bedroom window. But that wasn't the terrifying part. Right there in the frame, staring directly out of my own window, was the silhouette of a person. And a bit further back, near the very edge of the photo, loomed a second silhouette—taller, broader in the shoulders, standing off to the side like a watcher. In the bottom border, someone had written in black Sharpie: "Wednesday. 4:11 AM. He's still awake."  
   
I read it twice. It said the same thing.  
   
Todd had left for Florida on Monday. Who took this photo from his window on Wednesday? And who was the second person?  
   
I rushed into Todd's house, holding the photo in a trembling hand. First thing I did was check the cat—he was fast asleep on the couch. The house was dead quiet. I started making my way up to the second floor, toward his bedroom, when I suddenly noticed a folded, smudged piece of paper sitting on the third step of the stairs. It looked like someone had dropped it in a hurry.  
   
I unfolded it. It was a page ripped from a standard notepad, with Todd's handwriting meticulously logging dates and times spanning the last three months. And my name was everywhere.  
   
"June 12. 7:10 AM – left for work. 6:24 PM – returned. 11:45 PM – lights out."  
   
"August 4. Came out into the yard at night. Stared intently at the fence. Checked the ground."  
   
The very last line was double-underlined:  
   
"Thursday. He will find the letter. He will panic. He will run to hide at his own house. He thinks his house is safe. He will lock himself inside. At 11:00 PM, we finish the job."  
   
We.  
   
I stared at the word. Then I looked back at the photograph. The second silhouette. The taller one. I suddenly remembered Todd casually mentioning his brother once—Michael, who "lives somewhere up north and doesn't care for people." I hadn't thought anything of it back then. Now, it was the only thing I could think about.  
   
I turned toward the dresser. And among the family photos, I noticed one that didn't belong. Lisa. The blonde from before Megan. Same smile. Same hair.  
   
What was it doing there?  
   
Then I heard the front door open.  
   
Quiet. Slow. Someone stepped inside.  
   
I stopped breathing. Not on purpose. I dropped into a crouch, pressed my back against the wall by the staircase, and listened.  
   
Footsteps. Slow. One. Another. Someone was moving through the living room. They stopped. Then started again.  
   
I looked down through the railing. Nothing. Just a shadow sliding across the floor and disappearing toward the kitchen.  
   
I didn't wait. I stood up, took three steps down, trying not to make the stairs creak, and bolted for the front door. It was already ajar. I slipped out onto the porch, jumped the last two steps, and ran across the street to my own house.  
   
Only when I was standing on my own porch did it hit me: I'd left Todd's front door open. I hadn't locked it. And whoever walked in was still inside.  
   
Or already out. And following me.  
   
The clock read 10:50 PM.  
   
I fumbled for my phone and dialed emergency services.  
   
"Emergency services, I need the police! Someone is setting me up, they're going to attack me!" I screamed into the receiver.  
   
"Calm down, sir, what is your address?" the dispatcher replied.  
   
I gasped out my address, stumbling up the steps of my porch.  
   
"Officers are en route," the dispatcher said. "Lock all your doors and stay inside. I repeat, stay inside the house, it is the safest place for you right now."  
   
"Stay inside the house..."  
   
I froze on the threshold, my key hovering millimeters away from the keyhole. My hand went limp. In my head, slowly, like a voice that wasn't mine, that line from Todd's notepad replayed: "He will panic and run to hide at his own house... He will lock himself inside."  
   
I stood on my own porch, two inches from my own door. My legs stopped moving. I didn't tell them to. They just stopped. I hadn't been targeted. I had been mapped out. Every single step I took had been calculated months in advance, down to the exact minute. They knew I would call the police. They were counting on it. They knew the dispatcher would tell me to lock myself in. And they knew I would obey.  
   
I slowly lowered my hand, letting the keys drop against my palm. I took a step back off the porch. Then another. My shoes sank into the damp, dew-covered grass. I looked up at the dark window of my second-floor bedroom. The curtains were drawn tightly shut.  
   
Right then, behind the heavy fabric, in the absolute pitch-black of my own home, someone slowly turned a flashlight on and off. Exactly three times. A pause. Then three times again.  
   
Someone was already in there. Waiting. And if I had listened to the dispatcher and locked myself in—I would have trapped myself in a cage with him. With them.  
   
From around the corner of the street, the distant, saving wail of a police siren pierced the night. I turned and ran toward the flashing lights. I didn't look back.  
   
UPDATE:  
   
I'm writing this from the back seat of a police cruiser. My house is completely taped off. They caught Michael—Todd's older brother—right inside my living room. He was hiding behind the couch with zip-ties and heavy-duty duct tape inside his duffel bag.  
   
The detective had just finished questioning me and let me sit in the car while they search the property. I'm staring through the window at my front yard, and only now, with the adrenaline finally fading, the pieces of the puzzle are clicking together.  
   
I finally get why they did it. And I get why Todd had been so twitchy around me these past few months.  
   
It's all because of my front yard.  
   
Two months ago, I hired a crew to rip out the old trees along the fence line separating our properties and put in a new drainage system. I remembered how Todd kept hovering around the workers back then. He tried to act friendly, bringing them cold lemonade, but his smile was completely forced and his hands were shaking. He kept casually asking exactly how deep the excavator was going to dig near the property line.  
   
There is something buried under my lawn, right by Todd's fence. And Todd knew that the new drainage system would sooner or later wash the soil away. The truth he'd been hiding for years was bound to surface this autumn.  
   
I wasn't their enemy. I never crossed them. I became a death threat to my "perfect neighbor" simply because I decided to fix up my own damn lawn. He needed to get rid of me—through his brother's hands—before the ground gave up his secret.  
   
The detective listened to me in silence. Then he called the other cop over and asked if I had any shovels.  
   
A minute later, they were walking toward the far corner of my fence.  
   
They start digging where the dirt has slumped a bit from the recent rain. One. Two. Three.  
   
Then the sound changes. Metal thuds hard against something solid. Not a rock. Rocks sound different.  
   
Then the first cop plunges his arm into the mud all the way to his elbow, feeling around. He grips something. Pulls.  
   
A plastic card emerges from the mud. Wet, caked in dirt, but intact. A driver's license.  
   
He wipes the grime away with his thumb. Turns it toward the flashlight.  
   
A brunette. Long hair. Young.  
   
I'd been expecting a blonde.  
   
The shovel went quiet. Or maybe I just stopped hearing it.  
   
One of the cops steps away from the pit and brings his radio to his face. His voice is flat, like a man who has done this way too many times.  
   
"Dispatch, this is seven-twenty-three. We need additional units at this location. Notify the coroner and crime scene tech."  
   
I look away from the window. And right then, the thought I've been pushing down all evening finally surfaces.  
   
You live next to someone for years. You say hello, you chat, you trust them with your house keys.  
   
And you never really know who they are. Not until you start digging.
