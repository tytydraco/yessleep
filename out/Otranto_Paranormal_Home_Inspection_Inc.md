Something weird happens when you’ve been unemployed for too long. Time starts acting weird, and sometimes forgets you altogether. It was about six months in when I really stopped to notice.

At first, I still had a routine. I’d wake up early, make coffee, check for any new job postings, rewrite my resume for the hundredth time, and tell myself it’s only temporary. 

I still had a social life early on in my unemployment. Went to bars. I had friends. But I got tired of the questions. What are you doing for work these days?   
When asked, I’d say I was between jobs. I thought it sounded more dignified than simply saying I’m unemployed. When people stopped asking, that somehow made it worse. I couldn’t handle that, so I stopped going out as much until I was hardly leaving my apartment. 

Three months in, I stopped setting my alarm. At six months, I couldn’t tell you the day of the week. I sent more than two hundred applications before I stopped keeping track. 

Most went unanswered, and the ones that did answer arrived within minutes: “Thank you for your application. At this time we have decided to proceed forward with another qualified candidate. We wish you luck in your job search.” 

An algorithm had decided I was unsuitable before a human being had the chance to do it themselves. Somewhere an AI had filtered my resume for keywords, and no matter what combination of magical HR friendly jargon I used, I could never crack the code. 

Eventually, I stopped applying. 

I would never admit that out loud, and I can hardly stomach typing it now. There’s a special kind of shame and helplessness in giving up.   
When the fight in you is gone, it doesn’t feel like you’re making your own choices. It feels like free will is an illusion, and even the simplest task feels impossible. 

Next thing you know, you end up where I was. Sitting on a couch you bought with a buy-now-pay-later plan, flipping through streaming services you're about to lose access to, in an apartment you’re about to be evicted from, with seventeen dollars in your chequing account. 

That’s where I was when Midnight Haunting came on. 

I hadn’t watched the show in years, but when I was a kid, staying awake for it felt like I was unlocking a world of hidden knowledge. 

I would lie under the blankets with the television turned low, watching three adults wander through a supposedly haunted house with flashlights and equipment I assumed NASA had built. 

I believed every second of it. 

But I wasn’t twelve anymore. I was thirty-five, broke, cynical, and trying not to laugh while the co-hosts repeatedly asked thin air if anyone wanted to communicate. 

An EMF meter began beeping loudly, sounding like a Geiger counter on helium. One of the men jumped, and I laughed. 

“Oh, grow up,” I yelled at the television. 

The camera panned to a woman, apparently a psychic. She ignored me and held a digital recorder toward an empty hallway. 

She repeated the question, “Is there anyone here with us right now? If so, please knock. Give us a sign.” 

“If you're a psychic, shouldn’t you already know?” I mindlessly said.

She paused, stopped the recording, rewound the tape and played it back. Static filled the room. Then, beneath the audio snow, something sounded almost like a voice. 

Then two men who co-hosted the show leaned toward the recorder, and so did I. They played it again. 

Compression artifacts. Background noise. I knew what I was hearing, but it was still as entertaining as it had ever been. I wanted to hear a voice, and so did they. 

They stopped the recording, and all began to argue about what they had heard. 

“Get the hell out.” 

“No, it said let them all go.” 

“I heard Gerald Stefalt.” 

They let out a collective shocked gasp. The host faced the camera and addressed the audience, “If you remember from our preliminary research, Gerald Stefalt was the HOA groundskeeper who went missing in 2006. Have we found his last known location? Is there more to this investigation than we initially thought? What dark and nefarious forces led to the disappearance of a well-loved man? It's almost as if the gates of hell themselves opened and swallowed him whole.” 

With that, the show ended. I sat back looking at the rolling credits. I wanted to watch more. I didn’t believe in ghosts, but I didn’t NOT believe in them either. 

There’s no god damn way I’d ever live in a haunted house, I thought to myself. 

That’s when the idea occurred to me. It was stupid. So stupid that I laughed, but I couldn’t help but think about how pissed someone would be if they unknowingly purchased a haunted house. 

You spend all that money to find out there’s some annoying ghost of a grandmother smothered by a niece or grandson for inheritance. And there she is, trying to bake a cake at 3 am for eternity. 

As I said, I didn’t believe in ghosts, but other people did. Midnight Haunting was incredibly popular even during it’s 30th season on air. Not all their fans were as skeptical as me. Or broke. If they had the money to buy a home; couldn’t they hire a home inspector for Ghosts?  
I couldn’t find a job to save my life, and I was a half step away from homelessness. What if I could make a job out of this? 

I picked up my phone and searched for paranormal home inspections. The top result was for a short-lived television show from 2012, followed by a Reddit post about the same show. 

No one was offering a similar service anywhere in my province, let alone my town.

For the first time in six months, I felt a spark. It felt like a shot of espresso straight to the heart, like parts of me were coming alive again.   
I spent the next hour trying to convince myself it was a terrible idea, but the more I searched the internet, the more it made sense. 

Inspectors offered services for roofs, foundations, plumbing, electrical, mould, radon—just about anything that could use an inspector. 

But ghosts? 

Apparently you were on your own. 

There wasn’t a hole in the market. The market was a hole. People were sitting on their couches just like me, watching Midnight Haunting. People who believed in ghosts, or at the very least wouldn’t want to take the chance.

While researching, I found out that paranormal home inspections did exist in Japan. So I hadn’t completely lost my mind. It just wasn’t happening in 

I’d be lying if I didn’t stop for a moment and wonder if I was designing an elaborate scam. I sat for a while and wondered what would give in first: my morals or my bank account. 

I could investigate the property, find nothing, and tell them the truth. Or, if the property had some dark history attached to it, I could package it and hand it over to the client.

It wasn’t a scam if I was honest and provided some measurable value to the customer. At least that’s what I told myself. 

Opening a word document, I started to make a flyer. I decided to call my company Otranto Paranormal Home Inspections Inc.  
I’d read The Castle of Otranto in first-year Gothic Literature, and the nod felt clever. 

At the very least, the name sounded respectable and vaguely European which was as close to professional as I was likely to get.

**PROFESSIONAL PARANORMAL PROPERTY ASSESSMENT**  
**BUYING A NEW HOME?**   
**WONDER IF IT’S HAUNTED?**  
**CALL FOR A QUOTE.** 

When it was done, the flyer looked like something someone might staple to an electrical pole for a garage sale. Still, I exported it as an image and posted it to a few local Facebook groups, and my town’s subreddit.

The next morning I walked down to the local office centre and printed 50 posters. I was out of gas and couldn’t afford to fill my tank, so I took the bus around, stapling posters where I thought nobody would immediately tear them down. 

And then I waited. 

For six days, nothing happened. But I felt motivated and capable enough that I went back to applying for jobs. I had almost forgotten about my ridiculous attempt at starting a business when, on the seventh day at 3:50 pm, my phone rang. 

It was an unknown caller, and I almost let it go to voicemail, thinking it might be a collections agency. When I answered, an older woman’s voice came through the speaker.

“Is this Otranto?” 

I sat up. “Yes. Oliver speaking—“ I cleared my throat. “How may I assist you?”

“The name’s Maude,” she said. “I need a home inspection.”

I panicked. “Absolutely. What kind of inspection?” 

I could feel my insides physically shrivel at the sound of my own voice. I sounded like an idiot. I was unprepared. Admittedly, I hadn’t thought about what I would do if someone actually called. 

There was a pause on the other end of the line. 

“I want to know if it’s haunted, honey. Isn’t that what you do for a living?” 

“Of course. Can you give me more details about the property?” 

That felt like something a home inspector would say, but admittedly I was out of my depth. Thankfully, she was a no-nonsense older woman with no time to spare. 

She told me her son was buying her a mobile home in a trailer park called Deer Creek, and she wanted it inspected before she moved in. Supposedly, the house she currently lived in was also haunted, hence her paranoia. 

“I have a window available this Friday,” I said after checking my imaginary calendar. 

“Friday? No. I need you to do it tonight.” 

“Tonight,” I repeated flatly. 

I looked at the clock; it was almost 4 p.m. Even if I knew what I was doing, was prepared and had everything ready to go, that was the furthest thing from a reasonable request. 

“Yes, dear.” She sounded annoyed. “I need to know before I make a decision. My son’s hounding me; the real estate agent is a pain in my rear end. Tonight. It has to be tonight.” 

Her certainty made me uncomfortable, as if I’d already agreed to something. I hesitated. “Tonight's going to be tough. I can do tomorrow.” 

“No.” 

She didn’t raise her voice. Maude had decided tonight was the night, and she wouldn’t compromise. Lucky for her, beggars can’t be choosers, so I was in no position to argue. 

“I’ll have to charge extra for the rush.” 

“How much?” 

The flyer said to call for a quote. Not because I had a pricing system in mind, but because I had no clue what else to write. I had no competitors, so how was I supposed to know what a reasonable rate was? 

I thought back to my only exposure to paranormal investigations. Midnight Haunting had a surprisingly rigid structure for a show about ghosts. 

There was an initial survey where they collected information about the property in advance. What was the land used for? Who had lived in the house? Who had died in the house? The usual. 

Then there was the investigation. A series of repetitive questions, some feigned fear, green night vision footage. 

Then a review with the client, presenting the evidence or lack there of. A little history, some theatrics and that was it.

“Four hundred for the inspection,” I said, hoping I was in the right ballpark. “And another hundred and fifty for the rush.” 

“All in, five-fifty?” 

“Yes.”

“That’s all?” 

I pinched the bridge of my nose. Damn, I was too low. I thought about my overdue rent and my seventeen-dollar chequing account. 

I let out a defeated sigh, ‘Otranto is a value-driven business.” 

“You really should consider raising your prices, dear.”

I stared at the wall. How much more could I charge? There were few jobs I’d ever had where I could name a price, and none where I could make five hundred and fifty dollars for an evening’s work, outside a lucky one-off bartending shift. 

“Eight o’clock work for you?” I said, still wondering how much I could have charged had I known better.

She gave me the address, and then repeated it twice more to make sure I’d written it correctly. She insisted on paying cash, which worked for me, and then we said our goodbyes. 

I hung up and let out a deep sigh. I felt a tension I didn’t know was there leave my body. 

I could afford groceries. I could pay my cell phone bill. If I got a few more jobs like this, I might even stop worrying about rent. 

There was only one problem now. 

I had absolutely no idea what I was doing. 

The next three hours were spent in a mad dash of figuring out how to do a job I’d already agreed to perform. 

At least where I live, there was no such thing as a paranormal home inspection. There were paranormal investigators and home inspectors. The two professions were in separate worlds, and I was trying to figure out how to borrow the best of both. 

I watched a series of YouTube videos explaining how to conduct EVP sessions, and found several websites belonging to a man named Damon Morningstar who described himself as a demonologist. As far as I could tell, his only qualifications were owning an all-black wardrobe. 

I took notes anyway. 

Between my parents' home and mine, I was able to get infrared lights off my parents' backyard security cameras, two camcorders, and the same old-school voice memo recorder with the little tiny cassette tapes. 

With the thirty minutes remaining before I had to leave, I did my best to gather history on the property. If nothing else, I could tell Maude whether anyone had died on the premises, or if anything untoward had happened there. 

That was something people cared about when buying a home, even if the dead weren’t actually coming back to bother them. 

Deer Creek was a trailer park. The mobile home in question was manufactured in 1992 and moved into the park a few years later when the park acquired new land and expanded into a second fifty-acre lot. 

Initially, it looked like I wasn’t going to find anything interesting; that is, until I found an old public forum residents of the park used. It was mostly park gossip, but it led me in the right direction. 

A post made by Mayor\_of\_Deer\_Creek in 2009 titled “The Curse of Lot 17” did almost all of my work for me. In it, Mayor\_of\_Deer\_Creek detailed 3 unusual deaths at the property. 

The first was John Mercer, who died in 1992, only 9 months after the mobile home was moved into the park. He was the original resident. The cause of death was suicide. 

On its own, this wasn’t suspicious, but when residents, including the Mayor of Deer Creek, helped the family clean out the property, they found a large pile of threatening letters addressed to John. 

As explained in the post, they never found out who had been harassing him or why. By all accounts, he had been a well-liked man who kept to himself, mostly tending to his garden. 

The second death was Bertha Callahan, who died years later after falling in her home. She broke a hip and was unable to reach the telephone to get help. She eventually died of dehydration. 

The third was Annie Bell. Unlike the others who’d both been elderly, she’d passed at eight years old. Her death had been the result of prolonged abuse and neglect. 

I stopped reading. 

There are things you can learn about strangers that stop feeling like information and begin to feel more like voyeurism. I closed the page and sat back. 

Three deaths attached to a property in thirty years wasn’t a lot, but it was more than I expected to find. It wasn’t so much the deaths but the way in which they happened. For such a small corner of the world, there was an awful lot of sadness attached to the property. 

Before I knew it, I was jumping into my car. I used some change from my toonie and loonie reserves I kept for emergencies to grab some gas. 

It took me about thirty minutes to get across town and reach Deer Creek Park. I anxiously looked over at the backpack of supplies as I drove. 

It was pathetic. It was also, technically, a business. I was an entrepreneur now, and for the first time since I lost my job, I was nervous about being late. 

When I arrived, I found that Deer Creek wasn’t what I expected. In my head, I saw rows of aging mobile homes packed together, with rusted, broken-down vehicles between them. 

Instead, I found a quiet community. One that was meticulously maintained. The entrance had a carved sign sporting the silhouette of a stag's head, and while the posts holding it had begun to rot away, the sign itself was beautifully handcrafted. 

As I entered Deer Creek, I saw the trailers I expected, but they were all freshly painted. The front lawns were clean and cut, lined with flower beds. I smiled when I saw garden gnomes, dotted here and there.

I passed an older man as I drove further into the park. He was whistling as he watered a hydrangea bush beside a miniature koi pond, and another was sitting underneath an umbrella doing a crossword puzzle in the paper. 

It looked less like a trailer park and more like a retirement community where everyone happened to live in mobile homes. 

I drove slowly down the main arterial road that curved gently through the fifty-acre adjoining property, and searched for Lot 17. The road signs were small and easy to miss, so I had to circle twice before I found the cul-de-sac where the trailer sat waiting for me. 

The address Maude had given me was toward the back of the property, where the lots became slightly more spread out. The plots backed onto woodland here, and it was slightly more shaded and noticeably quieter than the front Deer Creek Park. 

I pulled into the driveway and turned off my car. I looked at my watch: 7:56 pm. Four minutes early. I quickly fixed my hair in the rearview mirror. 

Sporting khakis with a tucked-in polo shirt, I looked more like a waiter at a golf course than a paranormal investigator, but it was all I had. At least I shaved. 

I got out and stood beside the car. The trailer looked ordinary. It was almost disappointing. It was an older model, but it had been well cared for. 

Like all the other plots, it had flowers lining the driveway and two solar lights beside the edge of a ramp leading up to the narrow porch. A little plaque beside the door read WELCOME in faded black letters, with butterflies that were slowly disappearing in flecks of dried paint. 

I knocked on the door, but there was no answer. I knocked again. Nothing. I wondered if Maude had changed her mind.

I turned around, looking for someone who fit Maude's description, but I was alone in the cul-de-sac. I noticed the blinds in the trailer directly across from Lot 17 move. Someone was watching me. 

I stared back, and the blinds immediately fell back into place. I smiled to myself. I was apparently about to become the neighbourhood gossip. 

Laughing, I walked back to my car to grab my backpack full of equipment. When I turned around, an older woman was standing on the porch. I nearly jumped. 

She was smaller than I’d imagined. A cigarette hung limply between her creased lips, and a look on her face suggested I had kept her waiting instead of the other way around. 

She coughed. “Oliver?”

“Maude? 

She took a long drag from her cigarette. “Expecting someone else, honey?”

“I didn’t hear you come up,” I replied, looking around, trying to figure out how she had suddenly appeared out of thin air. 

“Would have if you were paying attention.” She said, without hostility. Something about her manner made me feel like I was failing a pop quiz. “Come on, then.” 

I slung my backpack over my shoulder and followed her inside. 

The first thing I noticed was the smell. It wasn’t particularly bad, but it was unmistakable: old cigarette smoke, and the faint sourness of a place where old people had lived.

The wallpaper had yellowed around the edges. It was a bit dated but nice. The furniture looked like it had been purchased off of a shopping channel in a matching set back in the mid-90s. 

The living room opened directly into the kitchen, seperated by a waist-high pony wall. A narrow hallway continued toward the back of the trailer, with two doors branching off it, and a third at the far end. 

Maude pointed toward the hallway. “Bedrooms back there. Bathroom’s right before the master at the end there.” 

I stared toward the end of the trailer and noticed the light fall into darkness in an easy gradient. 

I felt Maude staring at me. I realized she was waiting for me to do something. “I’m going to start a walkthrough.” I blurted out nervously. 

“That’s what you call it?” 

“That’s what I call it.” 

I could tell she was wondering whether I was an idiot. 

“You take a look around,” she said, reaching into her pocket and grabbing a pack of DuMaurier. “I’ll be outside. I’ve got a smoke calling my name.” 

Noticing she still had a lit one hanging out of her mouth, I just nodded and set my backpack down. She walked toward the front door, and then stopped. 

“And don’t open anything.” 

“What do you mean, don’t open anything?” I asked. 

“Drawers. Closets. Cupboards. You don’t need to.” 

“I usually check every inch of a home during an inspection.” 

She coughed. “In your experience, you find any ghosts snuggling up to the silverware?” 

“No.” 

“Then don’t.” 

Not that I intended to argue, but she stepped outside before we could carry on the conversation any further. I watched through the kitchen window as she finished her cigarette in one last herculean drag, tossed the butt into the bushes, and lit another. 

Five hundred and fifty dollars to look for something that didn’t exist. If Maude wanted me to stay out of the closet, I had no problem with that. I’d be lying if I said I wasn’t curious, though. 

The instruction was odd. She wanted me to look for ghosts, but her line in the sand was opening closets and cupboards. I shook my head and picked up my flashlight, and started down the hallway. 

Maude had mentioned the unit's electricity had been shut off because it was unoccupied. I was losing light fast. The natural daylight had already fallen behind the line of trees at the back of the lot. 

Entering the first bedroom it contained a bed, a dresser.

I stopped in the doorway and felt goosebumps rise on my arms. My first thought was air conditioning, but the unit had no power. My second thought was the window, but it wasn’t closed.

Despite everything I believed, my third thought was ghosts. I stepped farther inside. The room was silent. 

If I didn’t know any better, I’d say the world ended at the doorway to that room. It felt like the quiet of a first snowfall.

Then I felt something move across the back of my neck. I quickly turned, feeling my heart leap into my throat. There was nothing there but an empty doorway. 

I nervously laughed under my breath, and rubbed the back of my neck. I could still feel the cold trail of what felt like a finger being dragged just across the top of my collar. 

I shouldn’t have been surprised to find I was alone, but I was, and that reaction embarrassed me more than I would have liked to admit. 

I’d been in the trailer for less than ten minutes, and already some part of my brain had decided every draft or creaking beam in the framing deserved its own episode of Midnight Haunting. 

I turned back to the room, and stared at the window. It was the obvious culprit. I crossed the room and found it open two inches at the bottom, just enough to let in a thin current of evening air. 

“There you are,” I groaned as I tried to push it closed, but it was stubborn. The old frame felt rusted in place. I shifted my stance, found some leverage, and pushed as hard as I could. 

This time the door closed easily as if it glided on butter. It slammed into place, and I shuddered, expecting the window to shatter. 

Must have loosened the rust, I thought to myself, refusing to let myself get carried away with my thoughts. I made a mental note of the window and tried to push it to the back of my mind. 

The rest of the room itself offered little else. A bed frame without a mattress, an empty dresser, and a cardboard box against the far wall. Someone must have forgotten it in the move. 

The movie Seven came to mind, and I half-whispered my impression of Brad Pitt: “What’s in the box?” 

Naturally, I was curious what little treasures might be hidden behind that unassuming cardboard. But Maude had specifically asked me not to open anything, and it wasn’t worth missing out on five hundred and fifty dollars. 

By the time I returned to the living room, Maude had finished her cigarette and was standing in the doorway blocking my exit. Not that I wanted to run, but she was giving me no option. 

“Well? What do you think?” 

“I’ve been inside for ten minutes.” 

“Doesn’t take ten minutes to know when something feels hinky, honey.” 

I couldn’t tell if she was joking. “The back bedroom is noticeably colder, but there was a window left open. I’d want to give the room some time to normalize before I call it anything unusual.” 

Maude stared at me. 

“Is there an issue?” I said quickly. “I didn’t open anything - no drawers or closets.” 

She coughed. “The window.” 

“What about it?” 

“You said it was open.” 

She stepped past me and glanced down the hallway herself. For the first time since I’d met her, something in her expression changed. 

It wasn’t fear exactly. She looked irritated, as though the mobile home had disobeyed her. 

“Probably the realtor,” she said. 

“That’s what I figured.” 

That’s when I began to feel that nagging feeling that she wasn’t being completely honest with me. Maude nodded, staring at that back bedroom. 

I waited for her to say something else. Instead, she pulled out that pack of Du Maurier and grabbed a third cigarette. “So, what happens now?” 

I watched her light up, not bothering to walk outside this time. “I’d like to ask you some questions before I set up.” 

“Oh, Christ.” 

I reached down to my backpack on the floor and pulled out a pad of paper for notes. “Anything the realtor mentioned. Why you wanted the inspection in the first place. Anything odd that might have made you suspicious initially.” 

“Told you why.” 

“Your current living house.” 

“Damn, things been haunted since my husband was alive to scare ‘em away.”

“Right,” I cleared my throat, trying to hide my skepticism. 

“Things move. Footsteps upstairs. Sometimes doors open.” 

“While viewing the property, did anything happen here, though?” 

Maude rolled the lit cigarette between her thumb and forefinger. “Not that I know of. Haven’t bought it yet” 

Reasonable enough, I thought. I followed up by asking if the realtor had disclosed any deaths on the property. 

“No.”

“Anything about the previous owners?” 

“No.”

“Any stories about the place?”

Maude threw her hands on her hips and stared at me. “Oliver, honey. If I knew anything about the damn place, I wouldn’t be paying you.”   
She looked toward the front windows that looked out into the street. I could see the unit across the cul-de-sac with its blinds pulled back again. Maude noticed it too. 

“People talk,” she said. “Especially around here. Nothing better to do than gossip.” 

“About this place?” I asked, knowing for certain now that she knew more than she was letting on. 

“About everything,” she said, putting the cigarette between her lips. It hung lazily as she pulled a white envelope out of her back pocket. She counted the bills out and handed them to me. 

Two hundred and seventy-five dollars. 

“Half now,” she said. “Half when you’re finished.” 

We hadn’t discussed that, but I was thankful she took the initiative. I was wondering how I’d broach the subject. 

“How long does this sort of thing take?” 

I pulled my eyes away from the money in my hands. “Uh, three hours. Maybe four. Then I’ll need some time to review everything.” 

Maude started walking to the door, “Just call me when you’re finished dear.” 

“Will do.”

She stopped with one hand on the doorknob, for a second I thought she was going to say more. Instead, she looked around the living room. There was something in her eyes, and then it was gone. 

“Don’t be putting holes in the wall or anything like that,” She closed the closed the door behind her. 

For the first few minutes after she left, I sat down on the couch in the living room, and scrolled through my phone. It wasn’t quite fully dark outside yet, and it felt weird to investigate while it was still partially light out. 

I got sucked into playing Candy Crush. When I finally looked away from my phone I was at 40%, and it was nearly pitch black. The only light came from a lone street lamp outside. 

During the day the trailer simply seemed old. But now that night had fallen, the change was immediate. Though not paranormal, it was unsettling.

I pulled everything out of my backpack and grabbed the camcorder I had, pulled out a roll of duct tape, and attached the infrared light to the top of it. 

Using the small screen on the camera I navigated the trailer going room by room, dictating anything worth remembering. The open window went into the record, along with a loose heating vent in the hallway, and a water stain beneath the kitchen sink. 

None of it had anything to do with ghosts, but that was beginning to feel like the point. Maude hired me to investigate the paranormal. But what she wanted was peace of mind. 

After I casually avoided the master bedroom I came back to the living room and pulled a coffee table from beside the couch and placed it in the middle of the far wall facing the kitchen. 

I got out my second camcorder, attached an infrared light to the top of it as well, and placed it facing the kitchen. That would be my static camera incase anything happened while I wasn’t watching. 

I looked down at the little fold-out screen. The darkened room looked terrifyingly similar to what I’d seen on Midnight Haunting. 

I adjusted the camera slightly until the hallway sat just inside the right edge of the frame. The infrared light reached further than I expected and I could just barely make out the master bedroom door at the far end of the trailer. 

“The time is currently 9:45pm,” I said, reading the time from my phone. 

I picked up the second camera and the voice recorder and sat in a recliner that sat opposite the couch in the living room. It was more difficult than I thought to speak to absolutely no one while sitting in the dark. 

I pressed record on the voice recorder and asked, “If there’s anyone with me now, my name is Oliver. I’m not here to bother you.” 

I waited in silence, unsure how long I should remain silent. The only sound in the trailer came from the little wheels inside the recorder turned underneath the plastic window. 

“Is there anyone here with me right now, if so, please give me a sign.” 

Nothing answered. 

“John Mercer? Are you here with me tonight? I read about what happened to you. I know about the letters.” 

Again I waited, and again I was met with nothing but silence. 

I tried again, “John?” 

Something creaked in the hallway. I spun the camcorder toward the hallway. I had to admit this was more than uncomfortable. I was beginning to feel afraid. 

I couldn’t bring myself to speak. I could hardly look down at the fold-out screen. I sat perfectly still, breathing as quiet as I could. 

“John was that—“ I was interrupted by another creak. This time farther back. It almost sounded like someone was in the master bedroom.   
“Old trailer,” I scoffed, nearly whispering, but saying ithelped just enough for me to gain my healthy skepticism back. 

I stood slowly, and crept toward the hallway. Staring at the screen, I squinted trying to make out the slightest movement in the dark. 

Then I heard another creak. This time under my own foot. I stopped, and shifted my weight. It creaked again. Stepping to my left and right, it was the same. 

“There,” I said. “Mystery solved.” 

My guess, at the time, was that at night cold air entered under the trailer causing the support beams, and flooring to contract. Maude would need to make sure she insulated the skirt around the bottom of the trailer. 

Feeling confident, I decided it was time to tackle the master bedroom. The loose floor beneath me groaned every couple steps, but it was easy to ignore now that I knew it was caused by something rational. 

Staring into the viewfinder on the fold-out screen, I made my way to the room. I passed the first bedroom, and then the bathroom, and right as I was about to enter the master bedroom, the door begin to close slowly with an ungreased, anxious whine. 

My legs froze. 

Slowly the door swung till it was nearly shut. It kissed the doorframe, and then with a firm deliberate push the latch click and caught.   
“What the fuuuuuck,” I whispered. 

I didn’t dare move. Some animal part of my brain believed if I stayed still enough, whatever predator lurked wouldn’t find me. That’s when something knocked three times from the other side of the bedroom door. 

Not loud. 

Not violent. 

*Knock*

*Knock*

*Knock*

That’s when that animal part of my brain told me to run. I didn’t want to turn my back to the door, but I did. I felt the skin on my back tighten as I feared hands from nowhere grabbing at me. 

I skidded to a halt in the living room, pressing myself against the furthest wall. I was surprised I hadn’t dropped the camcorder or voice recorder, until I looked down, and in the dim light of the street lamp I saw my knuckles were white. 

For a while I stood exactly where I was staring down that hallway, with my back pressed against the wall. My heart beat thumped loudly in my ears, and I felt light headed. My breathing was too fast and too shallow. 

I tried to calm myself. I tried to remain calm, but nothing else happened in all that time I waited and that was somehow worse. There was a unfamiliar dread seeping into my bones, and I knew I wouldn’t shake it until I was out of there. 

If the door had slammed shut, or some poltergeist had screamed, I think I wouldn’t have been out the front door before I had time to think. But now the trailer was silent again, and the longer it stayed that way the more I doubted what I had seen. 

Maybe the supports on one side of the trailer had contracted enough to shift the door, or maybe the floor had always been uneven. I never checked. Maybe the knocks had been from a neighbouring lot. 

I knew I was grasping at straws, desperate for a rational explanation. At that moment, I could have told myself anything to help beat back the anticipation of something horrible coming. 

I wanted to leave. Every instinct in my body told me to grab my things, get in my beat up mazda and put as much distance between mean and that back room as an eighth of a tank of gas would get me. 

I looked toward the front door. Reaching down with a hand I could feel the keys in my pocket. I could be outside in five seconds.
