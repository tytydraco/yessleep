I work in a small solar system group at NASA and today is a perfectly normal day like every other. My colleagues are standing around discussing the possibility of getting more telescope time. Our boss is generously making a pot of coffee for his underlings. The instant messenger channel is still captivated by yesterday’s discovery of a large and sturdy cardboard box and the subsequent haggling over such a rare and priceless artifact. And I’m sitting at my desk, checking my emails, making small talk with my neighbors and trying to conceal the fact that this is all utterly insane. People should be panicking. The press should be hammering at our door, demanding answers. Hell, the military should have taken over days ago, and I for one would have welcomed them with open arms. But instead, here I am, checking last month’s expenses and wondering if $20 is too much to claim for lunch, all while trying very hard not to scream.

 

It all started about a month ago. The first data from the Vera Rubin observatory was trickling in and we were running some of it through a new machine learning algorithm to see if it picked up anything. To our surprise it did. Nothing spectacular, just an object, probably a small asteroid, orbiting among Jupiter’s Trojans. But it was a lot brighter than its neighbors, as if it was made of something much more reflective.

 

I would love to say at that point we looked each other knowingly in the eyes, said “Aliens”, and got on a direct line to the White House. But we are all too old and jaded for that. No, instead we merely agreed that it looked unusual enough for further attention, scheduled a more detailed look and went to lunch.

 

Skip forward a couple of weeks and we’re standing around a monitor looking at data from a large telescope. And while no one was saying anything out loud, by now we were all most definitely thinking “Aliens”. Or at least I was. That and what I was going to spend my Nobel Prize money on. And for good reason. Not to get all technical but the damn thing was shinier than a disco ball. And what’s more, we could tell from the periodic way it glinted that it was regular in shape. Far too regular to be a normal asteroid. Now, I’m not saying that we could tell if it was some sort of giant flying saucer, but even from what we had it looked odd. Very odd.  
  
But we were professionals working at one of the most prestigious scientific institutions in the world, so we’d need a hell of a lot more than “looks really weird” before calling the press or the government. This was my discovery, so I sent Bill off to scrounge some time on an infrared telescope, which should give us a better idea of exactly how big this thing was, then dispersed the rest of the team to see if they could get anyone else to confirm what we were seeing. Even the boss deigned to stop by and congratulate us on the unusual find, probably with visions of a budget increase dancing before his eyes. But to his credit he did offer to speak to the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) people about taking a peek at whatever this thing was, but I wasn’t going to hold my breath.

 

Meeting adjourned, I turned around to find we had attracted a small audience from across the office, most of them just admin staff trying to rubberneck a potential discovery in an otherwise dull day. I can’t say I blame them. Organizing meetings and paper deliveries at NASA is no more interesting than doing it anywhere else. But then I spotted someone I knew. Joanne was a radio astronomer who we’d worked with in the past and who did good work. We got along well, despite her being twenty years my junior, so I beckoned her over, partly to see if she’d ever seen anything like this before. She hadn’t but she did have an idea.

 

“Want me to point a dish at it and see if it’s transmitting?” she said, with half a smile. We both knew the chances of it doing anything like that were practically zero.

 

“Sure, can’t hurt. At least then if anyone asks if it’s beaming out ‘We come in peace’ we’ll be able to say no.”

 

I honestly didn’t think any more about it that afternoon, too caught up sifting through the mountain of paperwork our discovery was generating. I had even been asked to prep a preliminary press statement and went home that night trying to work out the best way to balance laudable scientific modesty with much more satisfying self-aggrandizement. But at that point this was an interesting discovery, nothing more. At best it might transform my life and career, at worst it might get me a glowing end-of-year review and maybe a raise. But I still wasn’t convinced it was going to change the world.

 

So it was with some surprise that I arrived the next day at 6am sharp to find Joanne standing by my desk, clutching a wad of data printouts. She was scared. Really scared. Fear’s not an emotion you see around here that often. Sure, if there’s a crewed mission that’s having issues, people might get a little unnerved or worried, but no one looks like they’re about to drive home and build a bomb shelter in the backyard.

 

“You’re kidding me, right?” I said, knowing that she’d only be here for one reason. “The thing’s transmitting?”

 

Without saying a word, she laid out the data plots on the table. It’s been a while since I worked on radio telescopes, but even I could see what was so startling. Instead of the broadband, fuzzy distribution of most natural signals, this was narrowband and the signal was being modulated. Rapidly.

 

“It must be man-made interference of some sort.” I offered, trying to sound confident. “A comms satellite probably passed in front of the dish or a pirate radio station decided that this was a great morning to go on the air.”

 

She just shook her head.  
  
“It’s been transmitting steadily for hours and the signal is only from that tiny patch of sky.”  
  
“Has another dish had a look at this yet?” I persevered. “You could just be the victim of a very localized form of interference. In fact, until we get corroboration from another source, that’s still the most likely answer.”  
  
“I know. I’ve got a video meeting with two other observatories in an hour. If you’re right, then we’ll find out soon enough.”  
  
She still looked shaken, so I offered to attend the meeting. Partly as moral support, partly because this object, whatever it was, had been found by my team, and I wanted to be in on every discovery.

 

So, at the appointed time I was sitting next to Joanne in our small conference room, awaiting our esteemed guests and trying to maintain the composed grace of a future Nobel laureate. Then the screens flickered to life and the two professors appeared. And for the second time that morning I saw facial expressions completely at odds with my expectations. Wonder, excitement, joy, any of those would be appropriate for what I had just seen on those plots. But instead they looked annoyed. Not angry, just annoyed, maybe even bored. It was the sort of look you give a waiter when they’ve presented you with the wrong meal for the second time in one night.

 

“Good morning, and thank you for agreeing to have a look at our mysterious source on such short notice,” began Joanne. “It is simply…”

 

But before she could continue, one of the attending astronomers, a white-haired man in his 60s, cut her off.

 

“It really is not very mysterious at all. In fact, the only mystery is why you issued an urgent request for verification. It is neither artificial, as you suggested, nor is it located inside the Solar System.”  
  
The other figure looked a little more sympathetic, but nodded along just the same.

 

“We had to pause some time-sensitive work to observe this signal, and I’m disappointed that you failed to conduct even the most basic analysis to rule out common radio sources.”

 

“It’s a narrowband, clearly modulated signal!” exclaimed Joanne, having recovered from her initial surprise. “I accept that terrestrial interference might produce a signal like this, but not an astronomical source.”  
  
“It’s probably just a highly unusual pulsar,” added the second man in a conciliatory tone. “A find that will probably make an excellent research paper, but perhaps not one that required an urgent corroboration request.”  
  
“Yes, it’s most probably a pulsar,” agreed the first man. “But I’ve already wasted too much time on this wild goose chase. I have real research that can’t be delayed any further. So unless you have another message from ET to share with us, I will bid you good day.”

 

I was dumbfounded. None of this made any sense. The data looked nothing like a pulsar, even I knew that. And besides, a highly unusual pulsar wasn’t just something you dismissed as commonplace and moved on with your life. It was the sort of discovery that careers were based on.

 

“Can you at least send us the data you observed so we can compare it with our own results?” asked Joanne.

 

The white-haired man nodded curtly, then his screen went black.

 

“Of course, we’ll send you what we have. I hope it turns out to be something interesting in the end,” the second man added, in the tone of a kindly nursing home worker who has just been informed by a resident that they were, in fact, Elvis, before he too vanished from the screen.

 

We just stared at each other, completely lost for words. Under normal circumstances I should have been furious, but the exchange had been so weird that I just couldn’t muster any kind of coherent response. Their explanation for the signal was so wrong as to be absurd. And the speed at which they ended the meeting was unheard of for a serious scientific discussion between leading experts. I mean, they were on a call to NASA for God’s sake. Most people would give the institution some respect even if they thought the two of us were wackos.

 

“What the hell just happened?” I asked.

 

“I have no idea. That was… I can’t even begin to list all the things wrong with that meeting.”

 

Joanne’s eyes narrowed and she looked down at the paper plot, still on the table in front of her, its lines still as unambiguously artificial as before.

 

“Do you think…” She hesitated. “Do you think someone told them to brush us off? I mean, if this is what we think it is, then I can only guess who might be involved now.”

 

I thought about it long and hard.

 

“No. If someone had told them to shut up, they could have stalled for a time and come up with something more convincing. But all they’ve done by feeding us this load of bull is make the whole thing even more mysterious than it was before. And I don’t know about you, but they seemed completely genuine to me. Out of their minds, sure, but genuinely so. They really believe that this signal is natural, which I admit, doesn’t make a lick of sense.”

 

Joanne nodded.

 

“I thought so too. They seemed genuinely annoyed that I was wasting their time. But, given what’s just happened, what do we do now?”

 

“Let’s get back to my office. There should be data coming in from the infrared by now.” I smiled. “Even if the radio astronomers have collectively gone gaga, we should have plenty of other evidence that something’s up.”

 

But once back at the office, I was shocked for a third time in as many hours. Which for someone of my advanced middle age is not a good thing. My team was all back at their usual workstations, continuing with the same routine analysis that we had been doing before the discovery. Two questions sprang to mind simultaneously. One, what the hell were people doing abandoning the find of a lifetime, and two, who had pulled my team off the assignments I had given them. I think the second part annoyed me more, but at least the answer to that quandary arrived promptly in the form of my boss. He sauntered over, coffee cup in one hand, a file bursting with reams of paper in the other.

 

“Bad news, I’m afraid,” he announced, and I was surprised to see his usual unflappable bonhomie bore a tinge of sadness that my little triumph had fallen through. “Seems like it’s just a regular old asteroid. Infrared data’s in and it’s conclusive.”

 

He waved the file at me, before gesturing to a dozen or so flashing message symbols on my monitor.

 

“I couldn’t get JWST to have a look, but that’s probably for the best given the circumstances.”

 

“Have you gone over it yourself?” I asked, again caught completely off guard by the morning’s events.

 

“No, I’ll leave that to you, I trust your judgement. Why don’t you spend the next day or so typing up the findings and then we can get back to that machine learning stuff from Rubin.”

 

He deposited the file on my desk with a melancholy thud, then ambled off to tend to the rest of his fiefdom.

 

I opened the file and began to read. It was mostly printouts, along with some brief summaries. And none of it made any sense at all. Every report listed finding a normal asteroid and the conclusions were all identical: “Not worthy of further consideration”. Everyone had taken a look and decided that it wasn’t worth their time.

 

But the printouts and the dozens of electronic graphs on my monitor told a totally different story. The size had been fixed, more or less, which, together with the amount of sunlight it was reflecting, told me that it was bright enough to be made of something very reflective. Very much not usual. Then I looked at the thermal infrared data and I nearly knocked over my coffee. It’s fairly easy to work out the maximum amount of heat that an asteroid should be radiating. But this thing was pumping out more energy than should have been physically possible. A lot more. We’re talking about full-on ‘multiple nuclear reactors in space’ levels of heat.

 

I felt that it was now the correct time to think aliens. And not just aliens. Aliens with an enormous power source smack-dab in our Solar System. That wasn’t just big news for NASA, that was big news for the entire country. Hell, it was the biggest possible news for every soul on Earth. How was this not worth further study? It might even have been the correct time to start screaming.

 

Then I saw one of the messages had an attachment labeled JWST, which was odd given what my boss had told me. I opened it and found a complete report. From a telescope that shouldn’t have been looking at our object. The waiting list for JWST was measured in months, if not years, yet someone had managed to divert it on impossibly short notice to look at our object, only to conclude that it was “Not worthy of further consideration”.

 

“What the hell is going on?” I asked myself. Again.

  
“I’ve got data from one of the other dishes,” said Joanne from the terminal next to me. She pulled up the trace and once more I was looking at a modulated narrowband signal. Pulsar, my ass.

 

“Well, if you like that, you’re absolutely going to love this,” I replied, opening the JWST data. Turns out I spout clichéd soundbites when faced with existential dread in the form of an alien mothership in our figurative backyard.

 

Her face drained of any remaining color. For my part, my heart decided to try out some new and unusual rhythms.

 

“We’ve got to do something,” she said. “I don’t know what’s happening or why the entire field of astronomy seems to be in collective denial, but we need to tell someone.”

 

I nodded and looked over to where my boss was standing, patiently listening to the bunch of grad students we had sequestered in the far corner of the office. I knew what to do. Much as it pained me to admit it, my boss was smart and one of the few people in the building who would understand every aspect of what we had found. But more importantly he was well connected. I knew people would listen to him, even if they wouldn’t listen to us.

 

Swallowing my pride, I went over and asked him for help, something I hadn’t once done in the eight years I’d worked for him. Within a few minutes he was standing beside Joanne and me, scanning through the evidence with mounting disbelief. Then, when he reached the JWST data, a shade of professional annoyance came over his face.

 

“They explicitly told me that they hadn’t looked at it, those lying…”

 

He snapped the file shut.

 

“This is unprecedented. All of it,” he exclaimed. “Fabricating reports, deliberately mislabeling data, lying to a department head. We need to speak to someone senior, really senior. Right now.”

 

“Who did you have in mind?”

 

“I know the deputy director. Not well, but enough that he’ll take me seriously,” he replied, turning to Joanne. “I need you to come with me. He’s going to want details on that signal and my radio astronomy knowledge is a little dated, to say the least.”

 

He turned to me.

 

“I need you to stay here and look after the office. Anything unusual happens, make a note of it but try not to cause any panic. We still don’t know exactly what’s going on.”

 

I nodded, relieved that I wouldn’t be the one explaining all this to the higher-ups.

 

“I’ll patch the trace of that signal to your workstation,” said Joanne. “It’s been steady so far this morning but I’d like someone to be here if it changes.”

 

Fifteen minutes and several very heated phone calls later, they were gone and I was on my own, staring at the signal pulsing happily away in a corner of my screen. My ego tried to tell me that this was all just some misunderstanding and that I’d probably come out of it smelling of roses, but I couldn’t shake the sense of unreality that had surrounded everything this morning.

 

My thoughts were rudely interrupted by an email alert and I saw that the second dish had finally sent its results over. And sure enough, another clearly artificial, narrowband, modulated signal. But then something else caught my eye. Whoever had made this plot had annotated the trace.

 

The first comment was unambiguous:

 

“Wow, clearly artificial, very strong as well. Need to pinpoint where this is coming from”

 

Then just below:

 

“Don’t think it’s terrestrial, this is the weirdest thing I’ve ever seen.”

 

But two pages later:

 

“I still think it’s unusual, but maybe I was hasty calling it artificial. The signal appears to be shifting into something much less well defined.”

 

I looked at the section he had annotated. The signal was completely unchanged and every bit as artificial as before. Then after two more pages:

 

“Was a mistake to think that the signal was anything but natural. Not sure what it is exactly, maybe an unusual pulsar. Not worthy of further consideration, whatever it is.”

 

The signal hadn’t changed one bit in the entire trace. Something was very, very wrong. I hoped to God that I’d been mistaken earlier and that this was all just the result of some spook in a black suit pressing a revolver to the temple of the astronomer annotating this, but I doubted it. I stood up and scanned the office, looking for any signs of unusual behavior or people pledging allegiance to their new alien overlords, but all was as it should be. Rogers had finally worn something that didn’t appear to come from the 1970s, but it was probably a stretch to attribute that to insidious mind control.

 

I looked back down at my screen, half scared it was going to start pulsating hypnotically in the best Twilight Zone fashion. It didn’t, but I did see that the signal amplitude was up from earlier, and a quick check showed that it had been strengthening for the last ten minutes. Rapidly. Whatever this thing was, it would soon be transmitting with a hell of a lot of power.

 

I spent the next hour switching between monitoring my colleagues for subversive behavior and watching with mounting alarm as the signal strength grew. Part of me felt like standing up on my desk and screaming “Guys, do you see this?”, but I decided to wait until the boss returned before fully committing to a nervous breakdown.

 

I therefore greeted his return through the far door with some relief, Joanne a few steps behind. The boss was collared by Rogers en route but Joanne made it through unscathed and was soon at my desk.

 

“So how did it go?” I asked in a hushed whisper.

 

She just ignored me, and began to gather up her notes.

 

“Joanne?” I said a bit louder, hoping she just hadn’t heard. “How did it go?”

 

“It’s Dr Moreau to you. God, I can’t believe you talked me into this.”

 

“Talked you into it? Did he look at the trace?”

 

I felt like I had entered some alternate reality where nothing made sense. She turned to look me square in the eye, burning with what looked like embarrassment.

 

“I just barged into the director’s office, on your instructions, and showed him a trace of a pulsar. God, I’ve never been so humiliated. Even he could see what it was right away, I can’t believe you convinced me to second-guess myself.”

 

“Joanne, that’s not a pulsar and you know it!”

 

I was almost pleading with her by now and we were starting to attract attention from the rest of the office.

 

“It’s not worthy of further consideration, whatever it is. And I told you, it’s Dr Moreau. We are not friends.”

 

She brushed past me and stormed off, sending a crowd of open-mouthed gawkers scampering back to their cubicles. I was still standing stock-still and staring after her when I felt a hand on my shoulder. It was my boss.

 

“We need to talk. My office, now.”

 

I followed dumbly, crushed by the enormity of what was happening, as he regarded me with his best “I’m not angry, I’m disappointed” look.

 

“You’ve upset a lot of people today, and more importantly you’ve let yourself down,” he began, as if I’d just smashed a keyboard over someone’s head in a fit of rage. “Your bull-headed insistence on claiming some sort of extraterrestrial contact has dragged in the deputy director, me, and most damaging of all, Dr Moreau. You saw how distraught she was. Now, I’m not saying you bullied her into going to the deputy director, but you should have considered your relative ages and experience when making your case so forcefully.”

 

I just listened, too dumbfounded to even argue.

 

“Now, I’ve apologized to the deputy director and taken full responsibility, so hopefully Dr Moreau’s career won’t suffer too much, but I hope you appreciate the reputational capital I’ve burned trying to smooth over your actions, and I expect you to let this matter drop completely. Do I make myself clear?”

 

I nodded, not knowing what else to do.

 

“Good. I’ll try to forget that this episode ever occurred when I write your end-of-year report. You can go back to your team now. I want you working on that stuff from Rubin. Oh, and your expense report is late, please complete it promptly.”

 

I skulked back to my desk, trying to avoid the sideways glances of my colleagues and ignore the whispered gossiping. The most important event in human history was in full swing and all I could think about was my career and reputation. The signal trace was still pinging away on my screen, a mocking reminder of what had sparked all of this. I went to close the app but paused mid-click. The signal had changed again. It took a while to work out exactly how, maybe I just didn’t want to accept what I was seeing, but there could only be one conclusion. The object was heading towards us, fast. Faster than any human object could possibly go. Hand shaking, I grabbed a pen and paper and started to do some rough calculations. There were many things I had to estimate or outright guess, but in my heart I knew it was coming here and that made things easier.

 

Two days. That’s how long we had before it arrived at Earth. Two days to try to raise the alarm or run to the hills. I knew the first option was hopeless, I’d probably just spend what little time was left in a jail cell. And the second was just ridiculous. I was hardly what you would call an experienced woodsman. Honestly, I struggle to survive without air conditioning and a fully stocked fridge at the best of times. Then I thought about leaving early and heading off to a bar somewhere to drown my sorrows and meet the coming horror in a blissful, drunken stupor. But that didn’t feel right either; it would be like abandoning my post as the enemy advanced. So I resolved to stick it out, watch the object approach and take any opportunity that arose to help, somehow. I’d probably just end up as hypnotized and oblivious as my colleagues, but maybe that was for the best.

 

So here I am, dutifully filling out my expense report and wondering if I’ll even go home tonight. The temptation to go completely nuts and yell “the aliens are coming!” is strong. Hell, I could even have a little fun with all of this. But at the end of the day, that’s not me. I’m just a mild-mannered middle manager. So, I’ll stay here at my desk and await the inevitable. If you’re reading this then I hope you have some ingenious way of making this right, but if not, I just hope you find some meaningful way of spending the next two days. More meaningful than sitting in an office and pretending that everything is all perfectly normal.
