CW: Suicide and Claustrophobia. 

“Let’s say you want to go to France, right? You leave Gatwick Airport at 8pm and arrive in France at 11pm but the flight was only two hours!” Mr Milcheck said, his left eye squinting as if to gauge whether or not I understood.

 He was explaining an urban legend he had heard during his time as a commercial pilot. The old man had a tendency to ramble on during his sponge baths, I suppose it was a way to fill awkward silences. It was an awkward circumstance, having someone who is essentially a stranger, hose you down naked, whilst your wife lies in the adjacent hospital bed. But for Mr and Mrs Milcheck, it was just another Tuesday.

They were checked into our geriatric ward six months prior. Both with the typical ailments that come with age. Bronchitis for Mrs Milcheck and a broken hip for her husband, which slowly drained more and more of his ability to move.

Their mobility was limited for the most part, being fixed to a wheel chair and a walker respectively.

“You're confusing the poor girl, Bertie,” Mrs Milcheck chimed in.

“It’s ok, I think I understand, so because the flight was two hours and the difference in time zones is one hour you’ve essentially lost an hour.” I said drying off the last of the soapy water on Mr Milcheck’s freckled back.

“Exactly! And that nut, what was his name…” Mr Milcheck responded, his memory escaping him once again.

“Alexander, that nut, said that missing time has to go somewhere. That it all adds up and you get it before you die, just a few more hours before you go. O after” He continued, his arms raised as I helped him put his shirt back on. 

“That's a nasty thought, Bertie. Don’t say things like that, we’re not going to be zombies” Mrs Milcheck responded with a scornful look in her eyes as she looked up from her magazine.

“No no, it’s not like that.” He pondered on the thought for a moment. “We just… get a little longer to say goodbye.” He said in an attempt to reassure his wife. 

I pulled Mr Milcheck's trousers up his legs and passed him his belt, he liked putting it on himself. I think it was a way of having some kind of control over his own life as he grew to rely on others more and more.

“Hm, how long do you think we’d have then?” Mrs Milcheck replied. Her demeanor slightly more light.

“God knows at this point, I was flying for 15 years before you became a stewardess. After that it was only about six months before Stewart was born and we had to stay on the ground. It’s all hogwash anyway” Mr Milcheck said. 

The truth is the couple wouldn’t have to guess for much long. I’ve of course seen many deaths as a nurse, yet the night Mrs Milcheck passed and the days leading after it will stick with me for the rest of my career.

The couple had settled in for the night and as easy as it was to drift off to sleep, the light inside Mrs Milcheck faded away.

Her heart rate monitor began screaming out a continuous **BEEEEEEP.** Harmonizing with Mr Milcheck whaling out for his wife.

“NO, DON’T TAKE HER, DON’T TAKE MY PATTY.” Mr Milcheck called out, tears streaming down his face, his declining health limited his mobility effectively trapping him on his hospital bed, forcing him to watch his wife be ripped from this world.

I rushed into the hospital room alongside Juliet, one of the geriatricians and a close friend of mine. We had slipped away to the rooftop for a brief much needed smoke break, only to be thrust back into our responsibilities. My heart was pounding as we approached Mrs Milchecks bed. I knew the inevitable was coming yet something deep down inside me wanted her to hold on a little longer. 

I attempted to resuscitate Mrs Milcheck, but my efforts were futile. She had no pulse, no air in her lungs and no life in her body. But as Juliet called the time of death Mrs Milcheck's lips started to move.

“W-what’s happening?” The elderly woman said, her eyes slowly opening. 

A rush of relief fell through the room, a miracle by the very definition of the word. 

“P-Patricia? you’re ok? We thought we lost you” Mr Milcheck replied, his throat hoarse. He reached out to grab Mrs Milcheck's hand. 

“Don’t be daft, I'm fine, I just fell asleep that's all” she said, squeezing his hand.

Mrs Milcheck was right, she was fine. Not just fine. Better. Her once shaky breathing was now fluid despite the bronchitis which ravaged her lungs. 

It ,of course, was strange yet the following day was even stranger. I had come in to check the couple’s respective levels, but to my surprise Mrs Milcheck was standing by the room's window fixing the bouquet of flowers that sat on the room’s window. She had been bed ridden for the past 4 months yet there she was, picking the dead petals off one of the arrangement’s tulips.

“Mrs Milcheck? How are you…” In my utter befuddlement I strained to find the word. “How are you feeling?”

“How’s she feeling? Look at her! Right as rain. As bouncy as the day we met and still just as beautiful!” Mr Milcheck beamed through an enormous grin.

“Oh shush, Bertie." Mrs Milcheck blushed. 

This woman had been on her deathbed mere hours before but now stood before me, with the eager posture of an energetic 20 year-old. A wave of comfort blew over me, maybe Mrs Milcheck was recovering. 

Then she collapsed to the floor.

I cannot overstate how sudden it happened. One moment she was placing the last tulip into the vase. The next she was a limp pile of limbs, flat on the ground.

I’ll spare you the details of Mr Milcheck’s bereavement, that 12 hours were a whirlwind of emotional torment I don’t wish upon anyone who reads this. We checked that Mrs Milcheck had in fact passed and took her to the morgue.

Myself and Juliet had a ritual. We’d say a few words about what kind of place the world would be without the recently deceased and promised them, even if their body had already been put away, that we’ll work and live for them. I know many in our field see death as the end, where our responsibilities stop and we move onto the next. But our hospital saw fatalities amongst just as many long stay patients as it did short stay ones. No one was safe, really. 

It started as a way of giving John and Jane Doe’s a funeral service they may otherwise not get, but after a while it felt odd not to give all of those under our care the same treatment. Juliet finished the eulogy as always. “I’ll keep working, keep cooking and travelling with the same energy and love for life you did patricia.”  
   
Understandably, Mr Milcheck requested to move rooms. He asked that we donate any leftover clothes Patricia kept at the hospital to a local charity shop. We obliged. The only room we had left was at the very back of the hospital, by a staircase which led to the roof but only had a small window to light the place. We asked if he’d wait another day to see if a new bed would come up, he declined. I didn’t blame him, I just wish I made him wait. Maybe keeping him from the staircase would’ve helped keep the idea from his mind. Maybe another bed would’ve opened on a lower floor.

I wasn’t in on the day it happened, I only got a call from Juliet as I was doing a weekly shop. The following day I went to the morgue alone, the workers there knew of our ritual and where to keep his body out for me.

One of them looked up from their phone as I entered and spoke.

“Oh, yeah. You’re here for the old guy right.” He said, apprehension in his voice.

“Yeah, I promise I won’t be long.” I responded.

“That’s cool. Listen, I know you’ve worked here for a while and I’m sure you’ve seen just as much terrible shit as anyone else…” He said before wincing. “I’d, if I were you, Just keep the bag shut. It’- I know you knew him well, is all.”  
  
“I’ll be alright.” I said, walking to the centre of the morgue.

I opened it, feet first. Those were intact, save for a single missing toe, his knees weren’t. His thigh bone had pierced the skin and stabbed through and popped off his kneecap.  His stomach was crumpled leaving his ribs hanging over his waste. Despite falling feet first he caught an injury to the head, a canyon in his temple that pushed his eyes seemingly further apart and out of his head. I took a deep breath and said a few words. 

“You were kind, Albert, kinder than a lot of men your age. You didn’t care that the world passed you by and that life grew out of what you understood. All you wanted was to be a good father to your son and a loving husband to Patricia. You were both, and I hope you knew that. I hope you’re with them both now. I’m sure by you’ll be remembered as good pilot by co-workers but to me you’ll always be a good-” 

I swear to god, his eyes moved. 

Not sporadically or in a random spasm. They moved to look at me.  I heard a gargle from his torn throat. It was so loud, so suddenly, I lost my footing and stumbled back into a nearby table. It moved away and clattered into another, the nearby worker shot up and headed over. 

“I told you- It’s fine, let me put him away.” He stuttered as he zipped the bag shut. 

“I swear, I swear his eyes moved.” I said, regaining my breath. 

The worker sighed. “Yeah. It’s… yanno gas and like sometimes the body will spasm. I know they teach you about it but it’s different seeing it up close. I get it.”

“Yeah…” I muttered. I couldn’t get the image of his perceiving blue eyes looking at me. They weren’t dead, dead eyes are wide and still. His were small, they reacted with the light as they moved. 

The Milchecks's funeral was held the following month. I think it took the embalmer longer to put Albert together. Stewart’s widow had arranged it, she’d invited us as I think they’d speak about me and Juliet every now and then. We sat at the back and when it was our time to say our goodbyes we went up together. 

They put Patricia in a floral dress, she was wearing her wedding ring, one hand placed over the other. Albert was stiff, like he was frozen in place. It looked like they couldn’t get his eyes shut. He was staring at the ceiling. Whilst Juliet was saying her goodbyes to Patricia, I tried something stupid. Quietly to Albert I said “If you can hear me. If you didn’t die. Give me any sign.” I waited until after Juliet asked me to come sit down with her. 

No signs of life.

As I walked away I looked back to him one last time. I thought, maybe I didn’t, I thought I saw his fingers slowly rise from his chest then just as stiffly lower.

I’d like to think they didn’t. I’d like to think he was dead and that he didn’t face the same resuscitation as his wife did. But I know that not to be true, as when his casket was slowly lowered into the dirt and all eyes were fixated on the priest’s prayer. I heard a faint sound of knocking against the wooden wall of his tomb.  What could I have done? Stop the ceremony based on a dying man's story, I'd seen his body. Everyone had. 

I visit them every few months. 

I leave flowers and prayers that he’s in the sky. Flying with Stewart and Patricia. Not deep below the earth stiffly knocking on a door that’ll never open.
