I won’t pretend I’m a good person. If you’ve ever seen a photo of a pop star crying outside a rehab center, there’s a decent chance it was mine. My line was always the same; they’re the ones who wanted the attention, they don’t get to pick what kind.

Tips from unknown numbers are normal in my line of work, so I didn’t think twice when one sent me an address in the hills above Malibu and a name, Lena Vale. If you’re over thirty you remember her, she had two Oscar nominations by twenty-five, and then she just stopped existing. Nobody had gotten a photo of her in six years, and people had tried. I’d tried.

A second text came maybe a minute later.

*6 AM. She walks the north fence alone.*

I got there around 4:30 and sat in the car eating a gas station muffin until it was light enough to hike up. At 6:02, she came out of the trees in a grey cardigan, no shoes. I’d fired off forty something frames before I noticed she wasn’t walking anymore. She was looking straight down my lens three hundred yards away, on a ridge I’d picked specifically because there was no way to see it from the house. She smiled, put her hands up like she was holding a camera, and pressed down with one finger.

Something hit the back of my neck, and I was out cold.

When I woke up, I was in a concrete room so bright the hum of the lights reverberated in my bones. I looked around; steel door. drain in the floor, a camera in every corner. Then a speaker crackled and a warm voice said, “Oh, he’s awake. Everyone, he’s awake!”

The people clapped for way too long.

I wasn’t alone in there. Teddy was there, who I’ve known for ten years and who still owes me four hundred dollars. And two shooters I only knew by name: Brandon, a skinny kid who did reality TV people, and Sylvie, who worked the Paris shows. Both of them had a disheveled look, like they’d been here a while.

Once a day the door opened for what the speaker called “arrivals.” There was a red carpet down a hallway with velvet rope on both sides, and behind the rope were tripods, dozens of them, all with flashes, and when you walked past they all went off at once. The speakers in the walls played voices, and it took me a second to place them. They were ours.

*“Over here, sweetheart!”*

*“Why won’t you smile?”*

*“How’s the divorce going?”*

Years of us yelling at people, looped. At the end of the hallway there was a mark taped on the floor; you stood on it and posed until a little green light came on, and if you blinked the clock started over. This was the requirement if we wanted to eat. I watched Brandon stand there for forty minutes once, the longer he lasted the louder the crowd got.

The food was honestly good, but the toilet had two cameras on it and the shower a glass box facing the tripods, and the lights never went off. Every ninety seconds a flash went off, not bright enough to hurt, just bright enough that you never actually got to sleep. I lost track of the days pretty fast.

Every night a wall slid open and there was a screen, and it played a highlight reel cut exactly like entertainment news. TEDDY’S MELTDOWN: WHAT REALLY HAPPENED. Then it would switch to a live feed of the room right above us, where about thirty people in robes sat eating popcorn and watching us watch ourselves. Nobody talked up there, and Lena Vale was always in the front row.

Here’s the part I keep getting stuck on. Some of that footage wasn’t from the cell, it was us at home, in our kitchens, in bed, going back years. I know what a long lens shot looks like and these weren’t that, these were from inside. Whoever these people were, they’d been on us long before anybody texted me an address.

We each got an episode. During Sylvie’s she finally snapped out of her vegetative state, went at the nearest camera, and screamed at it in French until she didn’t have a voice left.

The audience laughed.

Teddy stopped talking after that. Day nine he wasn’t at arrivals. I stood at the door and asked the camera where he was, over and over, until the speaker said, quiet, “Teddy is taking some time for himself. Let’s respect his privacy.”

They laughed at that too.

The celebrities came down in white robes. You’d know them, they host the types of shows your mom watches. One afternoon a singer, the stadium kind, pulled up a stool outside the glass while I was eating and watched me for a while before she said anything.

“You never stopped,” she said. “Not at my dad’s funeral. Not when I lost the baby. You were just there. Like a storm. I want you to know what that’s like.”

That night, the screen showed my daughter.

She’s nine. I have never put her face online, not once, not even on my private account. It was her bedroom window at night, from across the street, long lens. I know because it’s how I would have shot it.

Then the door opened and Lena came in by herself carrying this old camera, an antique, with a flash bolted on top. She sat down on the floor across from me like we were old friends catching up.

I asked her why she was showing me that.

She smiled. “Because next season’s about her.”

She brought the camera up to take my picture and I lost it. I grabbed it with both hands and drove it into her face. The lens cracked on her cheekbone, and my thumb was on the shutter, and the flash went off about an inch from her eyes. She screamed and went over backward with her hands on her face and I ripped the keycard off the lanyard around her neck.

I ran down the arrivals hall with every flash going at once. Halfway I turned around for Brandon and Sylvie.

They hadn’t moved, they were still on their marks, posing…waiting for the light.

I left them behind, taking the stairs three at a time and came out into the screening room, and thirty celebrities in white robes turned around in their velvet seats and looked at me. Nobody got up, nobody tried to grab me, but a couple of them were smiling. I ran down the aisle past the screen, through some double doors, and then I was outside in the cold and dark.

I went west until my feet were bleeding. A landscaping truck picked me up on the road and I told the guy everything. He kept looking at me, then at his phone, then at me.

That was five weeks ago. Detectives went to the address and found a wellness retreat, a very polite lawyer, and no basement. They asked if I’d been using. Teddy’s phone goes straight to voicemail, and his sister thinks he moved to Portland, which, Teddy hated Portland. Nobody’s ever heard of a Brandon or a Sylvie.

I sold my lenses and I keep the blinds shut. My daughter and her mom are staying somewhere I’m not going to write down.

About ten minutes ago my phone buzzed. It was a picture of me from behind, sitting at this kitchen table, typing this. You can see the screen. You can read the first line of this post.

It was taken from inside the apartment.

There was one line under it.
