I am writing and posting this because I don’t know how to deal with it without some support from outside, and I would like to know if anybody else has ever experienced something similar. This is all quite unsettling to me – remembering that day always gives me a feeling of vertigo and loss of reality – so I hope I won’t forget to correct the spelling and grammatical mistakes after writing the first draft. Please forgive me if my writing is too intense and emotional at times. I will probably have to edit and polish it later, but right now I feel the need to share what happened to me and my family when I viewed that strange house that looked almost like our own.

But my story will make no sense if I don’t give you some background first. Call me Masego; I’m not giving any real names in this account, but the places I am going to mention are very real. I live in the capital of Botswana, Gaborone, with my husband Thapelo and his elderly and frail mother, Violet. We don’t have any children yet, but we are planning on changing this. Thapi’s sister has two of her own, so everyone calls Violet ‘Granny’. Currently, with both of us working (he as a software engineer, I as a real estate agent) and taking care of my mother-in-law, we are not sure whether we can manage having a baby, and my parents live and work in Maun, a ten-hour drive from our place, so babysitting would be very difficult for them.

It all began on the 12th of November 2025, when my boss, a lovely and hard-working lady called Elizabeth, called me into her office. Elizabeth, or ‘Lizzie’, is the managing director of Molefe Estate Agents, and she’s almost always there before I start at 9 and still there when I finish at 5. Normally, mornings are quite relaxed, with staff meetings scheduled at half ten and business picking up a little later, and most meetings with clients are in the afternoon.

But this morning, she called me into her office almost immediately after I had arrived.

“*Dumela*, Masi. Sit down. I have good news and bad news for you.” Lizzie’s not one for idle chitchat, she comes to the point. By the way, I am reconstructing all dialogue in my story from memory, so it may not always be 100% accurate, but I think I can give you all the important points. I couldn’t give a word-for-word account anyway because most conversations were held in SeTswana, one of the languages of my country. I’ve left a few SeTswana expressions, I hope that’s OK. None of them are important for understanding my story.

“Am I in trouble?” I asked. She smiled at me and said: “Of course not. Sorry for calling you before your first cup of coffee. Do you want the good news or the bad news first?”

I said I’d rather start with the good news.

“*Go siame*! A house has landed on my desk that is exactly up your street, I think – forgive the pun. It was built in 19— and, from what I can see here, hasn’t been changed much. I’d like you to go there and view it first-hand. It was designed by Kagiso Seitz.” Then she paused for effect and waited for my reaction.

Kagiso Seitz! He was one of the most distinguished Botswanan architects of the 20^(th) century. His father was from Germany and he grew up bilingual, so he was later able to study in Munich, where he joined a group of international architects that positioned itself clearly against Bauhaus and Le Corbusier-style architecture. His style was a mixture of traditional European architecture and more typically African, above all South African, elements. I know all this because I’ve always been interested in architecture in general and Seitz in particular.

And he actually designed the house my family live in. That was way back in 19--, before he became famous and my grandparents wouldn’t have been able to afford him – his “early years”, so to speak. And here was Lizzie telling me that she had a client who’d like to sell another house designed by Seitz, built only two years after ours.

So, what was the bad news?

“It’s in Ghetto.”, Lizzie said in a slightly apologetic tone. “That’s why I got you here in my office so early.”

Don’t misunderstand that. ‘Ghetto’ is an affectionate name for the city of Francistown in the East, not very far from the border with Zimbabwe. It’s a lovely place, but it’s a five- to six-hour drive along the A1, and that’s why Lizzie told me it was bad news, because if I wanted to arrive there today at a reasonable time, I would have to leave very soon. And I’d definitely need a place to stay.

But before I left her office with the documents she had prepared for me, there was one thing I wanted to know: why would a client from Francistown use an estate agent from Gaborone when there must be at least twenty local agents who knew the area far better than I did?

“That was me.”, replied Lizzie. “I knew about your love of architecture, and when I heard the name ‘Seitz’ and saw the plans, I made the seller an offer they couldn’t refuse. And I told them I’d put my top woman on the job.” (I hope the readers will forgive me this little bit of immodesty!)

I thanked her and began preparing for my trip. First I called Thapelo and told him I was sorry our planned evening dinner would have to be postponed, then I booked a hotel for the night and went home to pack my suitcase, say goodbye to Violet and kiss Thapi with the promise to be back as early as possible the next day. After filling up my Corolla and having a snack, I hit the road. Luckily, November is a good month for travelling, although it can get very hot.

But I was looking forward to the trip, not only because of the house and its design but also because I hadn’t been to Francistown for a long time and I’d always wanted to visit the Supa Ngwao Museum with its fascinating artifacts and its deep insight into the art and culture of the Kalanga people from North-East Botswana.

So, an enjoyable drive through Nature, a visit to the museum, then a viewing of that interesting house that looked a lot like our own, at least from the listing Lizzie had given and e-mailed me.

At least, that was my plan. It didn’t quite go that way, but more about that later.

 

If you don’t know Botswana, imagine a country the size of France with a population only a little larger than Paris’. This means there is a lot of unspoiled Nature, and driving along the A1 gives you a good opportunity to see a part of that. It’s mostly flat countryside with lots of thorn trees, acacias, fields and shrubs, and you often see farms whose livestock are roaming through the savanna. However, the drive can become a little monotonous after a while, so I took a break in Mahalapye and another, shorter one in Palapye where I bought a copy of *The Voice*, one of our newspapers. You can actually read in it while driving if you put it onto the passenger seat – big headlines, only a little text. It’s mainly gossip, though. In Francistown, I went straight to the museum, because they close at five, and from there to my hotel to check in. Then it was time to do what I had come for.

The house was located on the outskirts of Francistown and surrounded by a big garden that had become quite wild. I could see the remains of once-splendid Bougainvillea and Hibiscus, surrounded by Lantana that had taken very well to being neglected, and the size of the garden meant that the nearest neighbors were quite some distance away. From the outside, the house looked in good condition, although it had been on the market for quite some time, but this didn’t look like a neighborhood with squatter or gang problems, and there were no cameras or other security devices. A tall elderly man wearing a crimson T-shirt, blue jeans, and old Velcro slippers was standing on the other side of the road in the shade of an umbrella thorn tree behind a wallpapering table, selling home-made lemonade and a few types of sweets. Not very many people were passing by, it was very hot.

I greeted him and asked for a lemonade, and we began to chat a little. He was a Kalanga, one of the several peoples living in Botswana, and his SeTswana and his English weren’t very good, but we more or less understood each other (my Kalanga is basically nonexistent, so shame on me, really).

That is, we understood each other until I told him that I was about to enter the empty house across the street. He looked at me and said:

“Don’t do that, *Mma*. I think it’s not a good place. I think there’s something wrong with it.”

I asked him why he’d say that – maybe it wasn’t in good condition and not safe to enter?

“Not safe to enter, *ke nnete*, *Mma*. But not because of the floors or the walls. Because of the grey flashes.”

He then told me about how several people during the last months had seen how some ‘grey shape’ seemed to move around in the house, sometimes obscuring the windows so it became visible to passers-by and neighbors. He was very nice and obviously worried about me, but I admit that I didn’t take him seriously. Maybe it was a trick of the light or some kids were playing pranks on the neighborhood.

“Be careful, *Mma*.”, he told me when I said goodbye. “This is a bad place. A bad place.”

I am a rational, science-oriented person, but, still, I found it difficult to shake off a certain feeling of … dread? queasiness? So, I took a deep breath and quickened my step to show determination and courage, mainly to myself. Something inside me was telling me I should hurry the whole thing up, but I was too interested in the building to do that. A Seitz house very similar to my own, could you believe that? *Ke ne ke tlhoka go itse mo go oketsegileng* ***–*** I needed to know more.

I climbed the steps to the front door and turned around to wave to the lemonade seller, but he wasn’t there anymore – but the table, the lemonade, and the sweets had been left behind. Maybe he was taking a break, I thought, and opened the door.

Now I would like to give the readers a detailed description of the house, but I can’t, for obvious reasons: I don’t want anyone to be able to identify the place and, by extension, my own. So please forgive me if the details of the place’s interior will be a little sketchy. All I can tell you here is that it was a bungalow, like many houses in Botswana, and that the owners had done considerably less to it than Thapi’s parents had to our place, so it was much closer to the way it would have been in 19--.

The first twenty minutes or so went exactly as planned. I compared the listing to the actual house, took some notes, and called up a few websites dealing with Seitz’ architectural principles. Then I took a break, drank some lemonade and enjoyed a Nosh before resuming my activities.

Then I saw it. I was busy taking pictures of the hallway – very similar to our own – when the light behind me seemed to change somehow, and I turned around, expecting to find a flickering light bulb. What I actually saw was … well, even after weeks, I still don’t really know how to describe it. Its shape was roughly human and it had what looked like arms and legs, but as I was watching it, those arms and legs grew and shrank or moved upwards or downwards on its ‘body’, which seemed to be flat and featureless, and it was all grey. It had some kind of ‘head’, but there were no eyes, no neck, and no face, and the ‘head’ looked like a cross-section of a hill, so there were no shoulders, either – everything seemed to be one, and its contours were shifting all the time. It was doing something with the kitchen unit, but I had no idea what.

I froze on the spot. I had never seen anything like this, and before I was able to react, the thing stopped what it was doing and ‘turned around’ or maybe simply shifted its body in such a way that it could perceive me. It was now four or five meters away, and I was trembling like some prey meeting a predator. I wanted to run, but I couldn’t; my legs felt completely numb, as if they had been anaesthetized. I now know what it means to be ‘scared witless’, I couldn’t think straight, I really couldn’t think at all. I was just watching, but completely unable to process what I saw. My cellphone was still in my right hand, but I wasn’t even able to move my fingers to press any buttons.

Then the creature quickly came closer. The way it was moving looked somewhat like running, somewhat like jumping, a little bit like floating … I honestly have no idea how it managed to cover the distance, but it did. I tried to brace for the impact, but there was none. Instead, the creature appeared to go right *through me*, coming out on the other side of my body and keeping a similar distance as before.

The second it was passing through my body gave me the strangest feeling I had ever had in my life. It didn’t hurt, and it didn’t feel like electricity or vibration, it was more as if my body was made of water and somebody was pulling a close-knit net through it. Although it wasn’t painful at all, my heart was racing and I began to sweat, maybe because of shock.

But the thing seemed to need some time to recover from the contact, too. It didn’t move anymore and started to flicker in different colors, with the original grey completely gone. This gave me time to calm down a little, and I dialed 999 and lifted the phone to my left ear. At the same time, I began moving in the direction of the front door, but the creature was standing in my way. Its colors were changing rapidly, and it looked threatening and scary, but also quite beautiful.

“Botswana Police Service, emergency. How can I help you?”

A female, reassuring voice, obviously a person with a lot of routine. I felt a little better; at that moment, it was the most melodious voice I’d ever heard.

“There’s … someone in the house. The address is ---------- in Francistown. I’m scared.”

“Is anyone in immediate danger, Mma?”, she asked.

“I think I am.”, I replied. “The … person looks threatening and has already attacked me.”

“Are you injured, Mma?”

I shook my head, which was of course pointless. “No, but I can’t run away, and I’m all alone. Please send - ”

Suddenly, the creature seemed to wake up and moved towards the kitchen unit, where it stopped and hesitated for a second. I was so startled that I couldn’t finish my sentence. Then it produced two tentacle-like appendages and began to push them into one of the drawers, take them out again, push them in again, several times – it didn’t need to open them because its body apparently had no substance, and there was no sound at all.

Maybe that was my chance. I continued to listen to the officer but didn’t say anything and slowly walked backwards to the door, trying to avoid any kind of noise that might alert the thing. It had begun repeating the process with the cupboards: in and out, in and out, all in ghostly silence without any parts of the unit moving. It reminded me of pantomime. Step by step, I moved closer and closer to the exit while it was busy.

I already had my hand on the door handle when my phone rang. Loudly. I glanced at the screen: Thapi. The creature stopped pretending to open and close the drawers and instead formed a long arm-like thing and made sweeping gestures on the worktop, as if it wanted to clean it.

Now or never. I opened the door and ran down the stairs. Then, while still running, I touched the screen and shouted: “Thapi? I’m in trouble. Can you – “

No reaction; he didn’t say anything. But I heard something else: the sound of things crashing onto the floor, wood splintering, metal clanging … in short, chaos seemed to have broken out at home.

“Thapi? Darling? Are you alright? What’s going on?”

I had reached the street, and the creature had not followed me out of the house. Ten seconds ago, I wanted my husband to help me – now I wanted to help him, but I had no idea how.

Then he picked up.

“Masi, things are being thrown around in our kitchen, cutlery, plates, cups, everything … The coffee maker, the toaster, the breadbasket, they’re all on the floor, broken. It’s like a tornado in our kitchen!”

The cutlery had been in the drawers. The plates and cups in the cupboards. And the appliances had simply been standing on the worktop.

That couldn’t be. Impossible. Somehow, the ‘pantomime’ of this creature was causing real events in *our own house*, more than 400 kilometers away. And it looked as if these events were all destructive in nature.

“Can’t talk anymore, darling. I’m going to push the table in front of the drawers and take off everything on the worktop before it falls down. I’ll call you when – “

Another loud noise, this time like a creaking, as if some heavy object were pulled out of its support. “The fridge, oh Christ, I have to – “ He must have dropped the phone, because his voice was coming from farther away, and he was shouting “Oh no, no, no, come on, come on, you bastard.” The creaking stopped, but I could hear something else.

I took a deep breath. Thapi and Violet were obviously in danger, and it was somehow connected with this strange creature in the house.

I knew I had to go back. I’m not a very brave person, but this was my family. I had to protect them any way I could.

I ran back to the door and stormed into the kitchen. I had no idea how to stop the thing, but there had to be something I could do. The creature didn’t pay attention to me, but instead it seemed to push something with a number of its ‘arms’. There was nothing there that could have been moved, but the place where it was apparently attempting to topple some heavy object was exactly where our beautiful oak kitchen table stood. Eighty kilos. Frantically, I screamed into my phone:

“Thapi, Thapi, I think it’s trying to topple the table. Where are you? *Be careful!*”

The only thing I heard was a loud *Thud!*, followed by the sound of falling chairs and breaking glass and chinaware. Three endless seconds later, Thapi’s voice came through the phone, obviously from some distance:

“The table almost crashed onto me, it moved by itself, like the fridge. *What is this?*”

I looked up. The creature wasn’t moving, so I shouted:

“I’m in the house in F-Town. There’s something here, some ghost, some spirit, *ga ke itse*. It’s moving stuff around and destroying things that are not here but in our kitchen.”

Suddenly, the creature began to contract and expand, once, twice …

“I know how that sounds, but you have to trust me. I can see it, but I don’t think I’m in danger. At the moment, it’s going to – “

Jump. It made a prodigious leap of at least five meters and ended up at the window, next to where, in our house, the radiator was installed.

“Honey, it’s going to rip out the radiator, I think. Can you stop it?”

“I’ll try.”, Thapi shouted, and in my mind’s eye, I could see him pressing the radiator down and keeping it from moving as much as he could, and I was horrified but also fascinated by the way the creature was ‘miming’ pushing and shaking the radiator as forcefully as possible.

Then, all of a sudden, it jumped back into the middle of the kitchen and extended one appendage to the floor, where it seemed to pick up something. I couldn’t see what it was, of course, but –

Knives. We keep our knife block on the worktop, and it had swept everything onto the floor. Thapi was probably still trying to hold on to the radiator and hadn’t noticed that the creature was somewhere else. How could he?

“Thapi, leave the radiator. I think it’s picked up a knife. Oh God, please don’t let it hurt you!”

This time it didn’t jump back, it covered the distance on the ground more slowly. I heard Thapi’s voice from even farther away than before: “Masi, our chef’s knife is floating in the air, and it’s coming at me. What the – “

Enough. So far, I’d only reacted, now I wanted to act. I was afraid, yes, but I also was angry, angry at this evil monster that was trying to destroy our lives, God knows why. I put down the phone, ran over to the unit, ripped out one of the already wonky drawers and threw it at the thing. It went straight through its body and crashed on the floor, and the creature showed no reaction. However, I had the impression that some of its color patterns had changed a little.

At least it had stopped. “Honey, it’s not moving right now. *Get away from it!*”

While talking to Thapi, I ripped out another drawer and tossed it at the thing. Again, the patterns changed slightly, but I guess it wasn’t surprised anymore, because it started to act again right after I’d hit it. This time, though, it simply moved towards the wall between the kitchen and the hallway and passed through like a phantom.

“Thapi!”, I shouted. “It’s no longer in the kitchen, it’s in the hallway. Where are you?”

“Still in the kitchen.”, he cried out. “The knife just floated against the wall and fell to the floor. Where in the hallway is it?”

Oh God. Violet. Her bedroom was down the hallway, with a window to the garden. If that thing got to her …

“I think it’s going after your mother, Thapi. Go to her! *Run!*”

I myself started to do the same, leaving my cellphone behind. There was the creature, shambling down the hallway, only a few meters now from Granny Violet’s bedroom door. For a moment, I visualized the same scene in our house, with Thapi running exactly the same way, and, in a certain sense, both would have ‘passed through’ each other. My mind was racing. How could Thapi protect his mother from this? How could he protect himself? We couldn’t keep this up forever, but if we stopped, the monster would kill my husband and my mother-in-law for sure and then do who knows what.

Wait a minute. Passing through. After I had entered the house, the creature passed through me. It didn’t hurt, but what if it stole something from me? Memories? Thoughts? Emotions?

The monster was now directly in front of the door and obviously about to enter the bedroom.

There was only one chance. Maybe I simply had to take back what was mine. But that would mean –

I was afraid, but I was also sad. I thought I was about to die and never see my family again, but I still fervently hoped my idea would work. I was crying when I ran down the hallway and directly into the creature, just like it had moved into me before. I think I screamed something like “No, you won’t!”, but I’m not sure. The contact felt like hitting a waterfall, but, again, it didn’t hurt at all.

What I am sure about is that I rammed the door open and stumbled into the room behind it. The thing was still standing there, all color gone from its body, back to the original grey. I was too exhausted to do anything except look, and the creature appeared weakened as well. One moment later, its shape swelled and dissipated into the air, leaving nothing behind.

I lay down on the floor, took deep breaths, and went on crying. If this was what victory in battle felt like, I wouldn’t want to be a heroine.

 

Except for one thing, what happened next is unimportant for my story. The police came, I told them about an intruder, they checked and declared the house a crime scene and told me they might need another statement from me during the investigation, but I was free to go. Lizzie accepted more or less the same story, although I had the impression she suspected something else, but she never mentioned it again.

I didn’t see the man with the lemonade stall when I left, and I didn’t tell the police about him.

Thapi and I are the only ones who know all the details of what happened, until now. I felt that writing about it would help me come to terms with it, and I think it’s working. I must say that Thapi has been absolutely marvelous through all this, never doubting me for a second. He’s the greatest.

*Thapi, ke a go rata*.

I said there was one thing missing from my account, and that’s the photo. Right before I actually saw the creature, I had been taking pictures with my phone, and later I found that I had taken a picture of the thing, too, without realizing it at the moment.

In the photo, the creature’s ‘body’ is not grey. Instead, it looks like some sort of chaotic dance of energy, with flashes, small objects without any fixed shape but connected in complex patterns I don’t understand, multicolored dots apparently flying around – and behind all that, the emptiness of space.

I have no idea if this makes any sense at all.

But I’ll do some research, and if anyone has any ideas for my investigation, I’d be very grateful.

After that’s done, I’ll go back, better prepared this time.

*Ke tlhoka go itse mo go oketsegileng.*

I need to know more.
