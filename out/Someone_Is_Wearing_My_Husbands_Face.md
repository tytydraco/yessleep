It began a week ago. After arriving home from a shift at the hospital—working overtime, as usual—I noticed my husband sitting on the sofa facing the TV. His palms were flat on his thighs and he sat upright, like a boy trying to impress his girlfriend’s family. When I asked him if he’d had a good day at work, he muttered that it was okay, but kept his eyes focused on the screen. When I asked him, “Are you planning to turn the TV on?”, he paused, then answered:

“No, the show I was watching just finished, I’m getting up.”

Looking back, I could believe that he was watching something, I could believe that he was preparing to stand up, hence his stance, but—I hate to say this about someone I love—something in his tone made me doubt him.

I had the Saturday off that week, so we planned on going into the city and visiting a market. He loved walking around each stall smelling the food and learning where it’s from, building a culinary atlas that he would try to reproduce in our kitchen. It’s one of the things I loved about him. I’m more into sweet treats, though it would make me smile seeing him follow his nose toward different dishes. We arrived at Spitalfields Market and I waited for him to lead us through the scents. He didn’t move.

I nudged him in the ribs. “Come on chef, take us round.”

“Hmm?”

“I said we should have a look around.”

“Yeah, I think so too. Where shall we start?” He said, gazing into the distance.

“You’re the food connoisseur.”

“There’s a doughnut stall over there. You like desserts, Mia. Shall we start with that?”

This might come across that I’m being paranoid, but you’ve got to understand, he always takes the lead, and I like that. If I wanted to grab a bite, I’d tell him; I don’t need him to guess where I want to go first. Nevertheless, we started there. I smiled at the server and told them that I’d be back later—which you say to every person no matter whether you intend to. As we strolled through parts of Nigeria, India and Brazil, he commented on what he could smell, but it felt insincere. Like he was doing it to show me that he could rather than because he wanted to.

“Are you feeling okay? You seem a bit down.” I said.

“Me? I’m fine. You?”

“Yeah I’m good, but you just seem a bit distant. We can go grab a drink instead if you’re not feeling the market. There’s a pub out on the corner.”

“I’m fine, let’s carry on.” His mouth was stretched into a smile, but his eyes remained blank. I worried he wasn’t answering truthfully. I wanted to hug him, to tell him that he could speak to me, but his smug look made my stomach coil; he was usually somewhat stubborn—a rock that I could always lean on—and, especially with the pressure of my job, it helped knowing that I could rely on him to give his honest opinion, even when it went against what I might think. But he had a sense of apathy while at the market that made me feel as if I was walking with a stranger.

 

I worked overtime Sunday and Monday and wasn’t in the right headspace to speak to him about his distant attitude. Besides, he had dinner prepared both nights for when I arrived home, which felt a lot more like something his old self would do. One of them was inspired by a Nigerian dish we ate at the market.

 

On Tuesday, I opened the door to no sign of him. His car was in the driveway but all the lights downstairs were off. Someone was walking around above me. I crept through the house. Each step felt like I was getting closer to a home invader who’d have my husband knocked out and who, when seeing me, would charge over and hurt me too, leaving me unable to do anything but feel the rough carpet on my face and stare at my husband’s static body. With a sigh of relief, I spotted my husband looking in our closet. He was rubbing the clothing between his thumb and forefinger while nodding his head.

“What are you doing? Why were all the lights off downstairs? You gave me a fright.”

“I didn’t need those lights on as I’m up here, but you can turn them on if you like. I wanted to see what looked nice on you. I’ve booked us a table at Luca’s for Friday.”

Luca’s is an Italian, if you couldn’t guess by the name, that we go to every anniversary. But our anniversary wasn’t for another couple of months.

“That’s a special place. What have we done to deserve going there?”

“I just thought it would be nice to take you. Here, you’d look good in this red dress.”

His behaviour made my skin crawl; it’s like he read my mind and knew that I expected him to be more decisive at the market, but this felt even worse. My husband, my real husband, would never book something without checking I’m free and certainly wouldn’t tell me what to wear.

“What’s gotten into you? I’ve never taken you for being a fashion icon. Besides, I might blend in with the walls and disappear.”

“You’ll look good. Wear it.” He answered, with a smile that said this is not up for discussion. I walked downstairs shaking my head, trying to forget that grin. After grabbing some leftovers, I sat on the back porch, locking the door behind me. I’m not sure if it was the emotion on his face or the glare in his eyes or even just the subtle way he shrivelled his nose, but something about that man upstairs was different from the man who was my husband only a week prior.

 

I went to lunch with my friend Lisa the following day—we both do shift work and had the Wednesday off—to inform her about my husband’s behaviour. The café was narrower than I remember; we found a seat in the back corner, away from anyone else, where we could talk. She spoke first, telling me something about how her boss was accused of embezzlement. Lisa paused for a drink, swallowing half of her oat latte, which gave me time to mention my husband. I told her everything, trying to emphasise how the behaviour was unlike him; how he could be stubborn, as I have told her on other occasions, but how I found security in knowing that he cared enough to be stubborn. Now, I felt like I was married to a shadow with a face.

“Sounds like he just wants to make you happy and treat you well. Are you sure your job isn’t just stressing you out? I can relate.”

“I didn’t marry him for the sole reason of me being happy. I married him because I loved who he was, even the parts that could be annoying, and it feels like he’s disappeared overnight.”

“I’m sure he’s okay. Maybe he’s just had a hectic week at work.”

“We always speak to each other though. This feels different. He doesn’t even smile like he used to.”

“You’re overthinking it Mia.”

“I’m not overthinking it,” I snapped at her, “If you knew him, you’d think the same. He’s a different man”. She pulled her head back, surprised and disgusted by my tone.

“How could he possibly be a different man? Listen to yourself. You need to take a break.”

I wasn’t getting through to her, I could tell. She’s always been there for me, Lisa, but every time we’ve been on a double date with her, she’s always with a different man. She might not be the best person for relationship advice.

 

On Friday, yesterday, I arrived home to a smell that burnt my nostrils and made the room spin around me. I could almost see it sticking to the walls and the sofa: cigarette smoke. I thought that I must be wrong, because neither of us have or ever will smoke, but it’s not an easily mistaken odour. My husband would never bring cigarettes into the house; he knows how I associate that stench with the yellow walls of my childhood. I walked into the kitchen and asked him, “Can you smell that?”

“Smell what, Mia? I’m cooking steak in garlic—”

“Cigarettes. The house reeks of cigarettes.”

“Oh, that. It’s nothing. Mike came over after work. You know how his old habits are, he lit one while I was in the kitchen. It must have been a few minutes before I noticed and told him he’s got to be outside when doing that.”

“But it’s so pungent.”

“He only left a little while before you came in. Here, taste this.”

He shoved a wooden spoon in my face with a creamy sauce speckled with herbs. I tasted it.

“A bit more garlic would be nice.” I said. He smiled at me and turned back to the pan.

“You’ve probably had a very busy day at work. Go get changed, I’ll prepare the table.”

I walked out, not responding, and tried to remain calm as I left that man cooking our dinner. When he lifted the spoon up, I could smell some garlic, and I think a bit of rosemary, but overpowering both was the stink of ash on his fingers.

 

Today, I came home knowing I had to speak to him about his behaviour. I wasn’t sure how he would react—I know how my husband would react, but not this person wearing a mask. When I opened the door, he was again staring at a blank TV screen.

“Goddamit! What are you doing!” I screamed, trying to mask the shaking in my voice.

“My show has just—”

“Don’t lie to me. I’m sick of you lying to me. Tell me why you were just sitting there.”

“…Your red dress has been dry cleaned. It’s hanging off the pantry door.” His face was shrouded by the overhead lamp, but I could discern the sharp edges of his smile. I stormed upstairs, partly as I didn’t want to argue with someone who was avoiding my questions, partly because that “someone” wasn’t my husband.  

I’m sitting upstairs now, typing this out, not sure if he’s going to creep in at any moment telling me to try on the red dress, the red dress he wants me to wear so badly, the red dress that will make me blend in with the walls; he—as far as I can tell—is still sitting downstairs in silence; he knows I have to come down eventually so why make me rush; he probably has that smug grin that has been plastered across his face for the last week, God I wish I could go downstairs and walk over to the man who could tell me what country a dish came from just through its smell and who would bring home fresh flowers without me even needing to ask, where has he gone all I have now is that shell of someone I used to love and I feel that no one else can see it. Our date is tonight. I don’t know what to do.
