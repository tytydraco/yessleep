“Nothing exists within a vacuum. Everything that exists does so under the weight of influence. The influence of people, media, and programmed instinct stops any one work from being wholly original. Even the most innovative concepts are nothing more than a collection of inundated inspirations and borrowed ideas. There is not a single piece of art that exists without all encompassing influence.

However, you may disagree after having played the 2005 classic ‘To Wander and to Wonder’.

To fully appreciate everything this game does we have to take a trip back 21 years. Here’s what’s written on the back of 2005’s most popular game:

“US Agent Leon must investigate the kidnapping of the president’s daughter. His search brings him to a rural village, where he encounters hoards of unimaginable horrors and must fight to uncover who or what is behind it all.”

The synopsis is short, intriguing, and gives you very few hints to the overarching story. Now let’s take a peek at the back of TWatW:

“Explore…”

That is it. A single word followed by three dots nearly as long as the sentence itself. A command for you to wander and to wonder at the frozen world of Nos. And those two actions make up nearly all the game has to offer.

The thing that makes TWatW unique, and its biggest point of contention is that there is very little about it that can be considered game-like. There’s no objectives, no enemies, very little story, and nothing to do besides wandering and wondering. It is, essentially, a walking simulator.

Now hold on, I know that phrase has a negative connotation with the gaming community as the vast majority of lazy, indie horror games involve walking around until a spooky jpeg jumps at the screen. However, TWatW is different in that it doesn’t place you in a series of repeated assets and shortcuts. It drops you in the abandoned kingdom of Nos.

Rolling planes, vibrant forests, ancient cities overtaken by Mother Nature. Every screenshot from this game is a work of art. Reducing the experience down to something as trivial as a walking simulator would be like insulting the miles of beauty in Yellowstone. You see, to me and other fans of TWatW, playing this game is like staring at a painting or reading a story. In all aspects, we as the player/viewer/reader are passive. We can’t interact with this lower layer of reality. It exists without our input and that is what I want to focus on in this presentation.

There is an idea that has begun floating around a lot recently of a digital wanderlust. It may not be called that directly, but I’m certain some of you have seen its effects online. The idea that there was just something different about the world of GTA San Andreas. That modern games don’t capture the same kind of detail as Mario 64. That we lost something as the pixel count increased. There’s a desire to explore virtual worlds free of narrative implications, to make a wrong turn from your designated route and end up somewhere it feels like you shouldn’t be. And I believe that desire is what appeals to fans of TWatW.

The game made many creative innovations to ensure that the world doesn’t exist because of you. Even implementing a controversial mechanic that runs the world in real time on your console even when not being played. Any game you boot up right now would be revolving around you. Video games are made for the player’s perspective. Oftentimes, if you glitch out of bounds you can see the seemingly immersive worlds are essentially sets built to trick you into seeing something more. But TWatW it is something more.

Everything you see is fully explorable, you’ll find no invisible walls or empty interiors. No set dressing. No smoke and mirrors. What you see is what exists. It’s a fully detailed alien world confined to fiction. The ones who Wander treat it with the reverence that deserves.

Given its unorthodox approach to game design, it should be no surprise that the game was a massive failure on launch. This, combined with lawsuits from a writer identified as Harlan E\*\*\*\*\*\* and various rumors about the lead designer, Mr. Shiyuu’s, extra marital affairs, killed the studio a month after the game's release. The only physical copies of the game exist in Japan. There were over 70,000 copies distributed in the country and now, less than a 100 are known to exist. The way most fans have to engage with the game is through emulation and emulations of emulations. Most fans of TWatW have never played the actual game, just copies of copies of copies. For a while, that was enough. To 90% of the world, playing the emulator was no different from what they imagined was the real thing. That is, until 2010.

At the start of that year, one of the few people in possession of a physical copy of TWatW uploaded a video to YouTube, showing off a cool detail about the game. Five years after the game’s release, this player discovered that the game contained an adjustable sea level tied to the amount of rain fall in your real life location. The player, who was living in New Mexico, found a drowned city off the eastern coast of the world map, normally flooded under gallons of digital water but now free to explore. This detail changed online discussion of the game from a weird little experiment, to an underrated masterpiece from a man ahead of his time.

And I think the fans of his game would agree, because there’s something I didn’t mention about that drowned city. It does not exist on any emulator of the game. You heard that right, the way 90% of the world can access this game is missing a massive chunk of content. And, of course, that led to the next question:

What else could be missing?

The ingredients of this situation have mixed into a perfect recipe to create what amounts to digital folk lore. Similar to how many kids swore bigfoot was in San Andreas, people whisper rumors of ancient skeletons lining the foundation of the game’s central castle. A dancing automaton buried within the crystal caverns below the map. A great dam holding back a gleaming silver ocean. Many of these have been confirmed or denied by the few who still have physical copies of the game. However, the one thing that has never been confirmed is the biggest mystery in To Wander and To Wonder.

Stretching across the entire map is a monolithic bridge that leads to a split in the mountain keeping us contained within the kingdom of Nos. By bringing several seemingly pointless objects to the map’s central castle, you can access this bridge and walk all the way to the map’s edge to find a path further on into a place no one has ever explored. And what do you find? Your character, when prompted to step forward, utters the only line of dialogue said within the game.

“No.”

All of that work for your character to say. “No.”

What was the point?

Why put it in?

The answer, for some, is clear:

It’s an anti-piracy feature.

Anti-Piracy features are implemented to stop consumers from simply stealing the data from the game via emulators, some of these have been fairly unique, such as EarthBound allowing you to play the entire game up until the final boss which then erases the file. It could be that TWatW implemented this feature to stop thieves from discovering the world’s greatest secret. And the worst part? All known owners of physical copies refuse to state what, if anything, lies past the end of the bridge.

This has cemented To Wander and To Wonder as a staple of gaming history. And it shows how captivating it can be to search for the last secret of a digital world.”
