Back in November of nineteen eighty-six, right before I locked the door on my first night alone in the Appalachian Mountains, an old store clerk down at the base of the ridge gave me one piece of advice. If you hear someone calling your name from the tree line after dark, he said, stay away from the windows and keep your mouth shut.

I was twenty-four, fresh out of a community college electronics program, working a two-week contract for a private geological outfit out of the valley. Naturally, I laughed it off as mountain superstition meant to spook flatlanders. Only now, forty years later, I know why that rule exists. And it has nothing to do with ghosts.

On paper, the gig sounded easy. For fourteen days, I had to stay at Station Nine, a decommissioned one-room Forestry Service cabin twelve miles up an unpaved logging spur deep in the mountains. My employer had bolted four low-frequency acoustic microphones along the ridge to track subterranean tremors from old, flooded zinc mines. Every afternoon, I was supposed to hike the ridge, swap out the magnetic reel-to-reel tapes on the recorders, and log the barometric pressure.

They offered double overtime because the previous tech, a quiet guy in his fifties named Arthur, had walked off the job three weeks earlier without a word. County deputies found his eighty-two Dodge pickup parked dead center in the cabin clearing with the driver's door hanging open, keys still in the ignition, and the battery drained flat. Nobody ever found Arthur. Stranger still, his heavy winter boots were sitting neatly beside the cabin woodstove.

When I stopped for gas at that crossroads store on the way up, the clerk eyed the magnetic survey decals on my Ford Bronco and shook his head. He muttered that the ridges around here had been full of strange folks ever since the timber mills went under in the seventies, men who wandered into the deep woods and simply forgot how to be human.

I pulled into the clearing around four in the afternoon as a thick, freezing fog rolled off the bald peaks, swallowing the hemlocks until I could barely see thirty feet past my bumper. The cabin looked bleak: faded pine paneling, a squat iron woodstove, a bare table holding a heavy black rotary phone, and a narrow cot shoved against the back wall. No TV, no radio reception down in the gorge, and the nearest neighbor sat eleven miles down the mountain.

Unpacking my duffel bag, I spotted Arthur's ceramic coffee mug still resting on the windowsill with a ring of dried brown mold inside. Right above the front door's brass deadbolt, someone had taken a flathead screwdriver and gouged three jagged words deep into the soft pine: CHECK THE WIRE.

Figuring two weeks of isolation had finally cracked Arthur's head, I lit a kerosene lamp, brewed coffee on my camp burner, and pulled the spare reel-to-reel deck out of the equipment crate to test it. A half-used magnetic reel was already threaded onto the spindles. Out of curiosity, I flipped the toggle to PLAY and slipped on the padded headphones.

For forty seconds, all I heard was empty tape hiss and rain tapping against a tin roof. Then the mic picked up Arthur snoring lightly on the cot. Around the two-minute mark, a second sound bled through the static, so close to the microphone capsule that the low end rattled my eardrums. Someone else was breathing right inside the room, a slow, wet rasp, followed by a voice whispering barely above a sigh:

*"He sleeps on his left side."*

I yanked the headphones off so fast they clattered hard against the glass chimney of the kerosene lamp. My chest was heaving as I scanned the dim room. The whole place was barely a twenty-by-twenty pine box, nowhere to duck, not even a broom closet, just the main room and a cramped washroom tucked into the back corner with a dry sink. Hands still shaking, I rattled the brass deadbolt on the front door and double-checked the rusty metal latches on both windows. Everything was locked tight from the inside.

Telling myself the tape must have been a prank recorded by the survey crew back at the valley warehouse to haze the new guy, I locked the reel inside the metal footlocker and tried to sleep.

Somehow I dozed off around three, though the wind kept groaning through the old pine rafters until gray light finally bled through the fog. Bitterly cold, I threw on my coat, grabbed my canvas pack, and hiked a mile up the steep deer trail to Acoustic Station Three at the bottom of a limestone ravine choked with rhododendrons.

The moment I reached the fiberglass weather box chained to an oak tree, my boots stopped dead in the mud. The heavy padlock lay unclasped on the iron hasp, unlocked with a key rather than pried open or clawed by a bear. Inside the housing, the seven-inch tape reel had been stripped off the spindles and unspooled in glistening brown ribbons across the wet ferns. Sitting squarely on top of the metal battery casing was a pair of thick gray wool socks, folded into a neat square.

I didn't even have to touch them to know they belonged to the empty winter boots waiting beside the stove back at the cabin.

Prickly cold sweat broke out under my flannel shirt. Whoever held Arthur's spare equipment key was still up here on this ridge. Skipping Station Four entirely, I turned around and half-ran back down the rocky trail, glancing over my shoulder every few seconds at the wall of gray fog between the trunks. Every snapping twig sounded like a boot heel.

By the time I stumbled into the cabin clearing at four-thirty, dusk was already swallowing the ridge. I slammed the oak door shut, threw the deadbolt, and wedged a wooden chair under the doorknob. All I wanted was to call my supervisor down in the valley, tell him I was packing up, and get off this mountain before full dark.

Crossing straight to the table, I snatched up the heavy black rotary receiver. My boss had warned me the old copper party line went dead whenever wind knocked branches across the poles, so I expected either a dial tone or dead air. Pressing the cold Bakelite handset to my ear, I got total silence, not even the faint sixty-cycle hum of live current from the valley switchboard. The line had zero power.

Three seconds later, a sharp metallic click echoed through the earpiece. Someone had just lifted a second receiver off its hook on the other end of that dead copper wire.

My knuckles went white around the plastic. "Hello?" I croaked, my voice barely working.

Nobody spoke. Instead, through the tiny speaker pressed to my ear, old floorboards creaked slowly under someone's weight. At that exact second, a pinch of dry sawdust drifted down from the ceiling seam directly onto my shoulder.

I forced myself not to look up. If I tilted my head back and stared at the planks, whoever was up there would know I'd caught on. Keeping my eyes glued to the tabletop, I eased the receiver back onto the cradle with a trembling hand.

CHECK THE WIRE. Arthur's warning flashed back into my head.

Pretending to dig through my canvas pack on the floor, I dropped to one knee and traced the gray rubber cord snaking out of the back of the phone. Tacked neatly along the pine baseboard, it ran straight toward the back corner. A real line heading out to the logging road poles would have passed through a porcelain insulator drilled through the exterior wall.

This one went straight up. Hidden in the dark shadow behind the iron woodstove pipe, the gray wire bent ninety degrees upward, climbing the corner seam and vanishing through a quarter-inch hole drilled cleanly into the ceiling boards.

A cold knot tightened in my gut. There was no line to the valley. Someone had wired two rotary phones together in a closed intercom loop powered by a six-volt lantern battery. One phone sat on my table. The other was lying in the pitch-black attic crawlspace six feet above my cot.

I hadn't been alone for a single minute since carrying my gear through the door yesterday afternoon. While I was hiking up to Station Three that morning, someone had dropped down through the square attic hatch in the washroom hallway, walked right past my table, and climbed back up before I returned. Right now, he was stretched flat on his stomach across the ceiling joists over my head, listening through the cracks in the dried pine.

My truck keys were hanging on a brass cup hook screwed into the wall by the washroom hallway, directly underneath that attic hatch.

Before I could take a step toward them, the kerosene lamp flame dipped from a sudden draft under the front door. Out in the darkness beyond the north window, the side facing a sheer rock drop-off, a voice called through the fog.

*"Hey! Out here by the Bronco!"*

Every hair on my arms stood straight up. That raspy drawl belonged to me, right down to the exact words I'd muttered to myself twenty-four hours earlier while hauling my gear out of the truck.

For two seconds, nobody breathed. Listening closer to the hollow echo behind the wall, I caught on to the trick: the voice came straight down the brick chimney pipe from the attic, thrown like a ventriloquist's act to bait me into unbolting the front door and running outside without my keys or my boots, just like Arthur had done three weeks before.

As the echo died, the rotary phone on the table let out a single sharp metallic ding, like a fingernail tapping the hook switch upstairs.

Then came the dry, gritty slide of wood scraping against wood in the shadow of the washroom hallway twelve feet away. Turning my head just an inch, I watched the square wooden hatch cover lift upward in the dim amber glow of the lamp. Two inches, then three. Four pale, dirt-blackened fingers curled silently around the edge of the framing timber. A heavy stench drifted down into the warm room, wet wool mixed with rancid fat and unwashed skin.

He was done waiting for me to step outside. He was coming down.

Forget the keys under that hatch. I always kept a magnetized spare key box slapped inside the rear steel bumper of my Bronco. Grabbing the heavy glass kerosene lamp by its base, I hurled it down the narrow hallway. Glass shattered against the wall right beneath the hatch, throwing up a blinding sheet of orange flame as I lunged for the front door.

Above the roar of burning kerosene, a shrill, furious shriek tore out of the ceiling, raw and animal, followed by heavy boots hitting the hallway floorboards right behind me. I kicked the wooden chair out from under the knob, twisted the brass deadbolt, and threw myself off the porch into the freezing fog.

I didn't look back. Rounding the rear bumper of the Bronco, I ripped the magnetic box off the frame rail with bleeding fingernails, jumped into the cab, and slammed the door lock down just as a heavy body slammed against the rear passenger glass. My fingers shook so badly I nearly dropped the key, but the old V8 caught on the first crank. I jammed the shifter into reverse, backed blindly right through the wooden split-rail fence, and spun the wheel toward the logging road.

As my high beams swept across the front of the burning cabin, the fog parted for two seconds. Standing on the porch, backlit by the orange glow pouring out of the hallway, was a gaunt, hollow-eyed man with matted gray hair hanging to his shoulders. He wore Arthur's oversized green Forestry Service parka. In his right hand, trailing ten feet of ripped gray cord, he held the second black rotary receiver pressed calmly to his ear, watching me go.

Four hours later, I stumbled into the sheriff's office down in town. By the time volunteer firefighters and state troopers made it up the spur at dawn, the cabin had burned down to its stone foundation. They found nobody in the ashes.

When deputies searched an abandoned zinc mine shaft four hundred yards up the ridge behind the clearing, though, they uncovered a dry camp stocked with Arthur's driver's license, the wallets of three hikers who had vanished in those mountains since nineteen eighty-two, and eleven stolen seven-inch audio reels.

Playing through the tapes, state investigators pieced together that the man had been living in the attic of Station Nine every autumn for four years. Whenever a lone surveyor or hiker sheltered inside, he would record them talking in their sleep or speaking on the radio, spending days in the dark practicing their exact cadence and pitch.

The last reel in the crate was the one that made me pack up and leave those mountains for good. Recorded on a battery-powered portable unit from inside the main room on my very first night, it captured three straight hours while I lay fast asleep on the cot. You could hear the attic hatch creaking open, soft footsteps crossing the pine floor, and the man standing two inches from my pillow, whispering in my own stolen voice over and over until dawn:

*"Almost got it right. Almost got it right."*
