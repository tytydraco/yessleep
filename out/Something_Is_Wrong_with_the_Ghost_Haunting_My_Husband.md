I was the one who woke up in the middle of the night, because my mother's intuition said something was wrong. I was the one who slipped into our daughter's room to check on her. I was the one who found her facedown, not breathing. I was the one who called 911 and frantically gave her CPR until the ambulance arrived. My husband didn't wake up until the sirens blared outside our house. I was alone in the waiting room when the doctor came out, her face slack with a practiced compassion. My husband had gone to the bathroom. For the second time that night, I was sobbing alone.



He's been catching up to me, though. When I went back to work, because someone had to, he said he wasn't ready. At our grief support group, he weeps uncontrollably, until it feels like there's no room for my tears. He's too fragile to wrap his arms around me and tell me he's sorry; he needs me to do that for him. And now, he's being haunted by her ghost.



It started about a month ago. He called me at work, which he knows my boss doesn't like, blubbering about hearing her cry. The baby monitor, which we hadn't been able to bring ourselves to get rid of, somehow turned itself on. He heard her voice, but the screen showed her crib was still empty. Of course it had stopped by the time he called me. 



The next time he heard her cry, it lasted only a moment. It didn't come from the baby monitor, but from the nursery itself, right after he walked past it. He threw open the door, and he swore he could smell her scent stronger than ever, that soft fragrance of baby wipes clinging to every surface. He lost it. When I got home, I told him I couldn't smell anything different. Even though I could. 



The third time it happened, I told him I couldn't do this anymore. I told him he needed to "dump out"--talk to a friend, a family member, or a professional, someone in a ring outside ours. His brother called me a few days later, full of concern about the hallucinations, and I told him abruptly the reason I had my husband call him was because I couldn't keep being the strong one for both of us. Then I hung up on him. 



I was so relieved when he told me he was planning to check himself into inpatient care the next morning. It was like watching the storm clouds begin to part, and a hazy gray sliver of sun begin to poke through. That night after I'd turned out the light, after I thought he had fallen asleep, I felt his fingers graze my back. "Sweetie?" he whispered.



"Hmm?"



"You're not asleep?"



"No." Though I wanted to be.



"Are you upset with me?"



"No." I said it reflexively, the way you say "fine" when people ask how you're doing, even when you know they know you just lost a child. For a few minutes, we were both quiet. Then I rolled over to face him. "Are you upset with yourself?"



He seemed startled by the question. 



"I just mean...if you think I'm upset with you, could that be your own voice you're hearing?"



He looked stricken. "Why should I be upset with myself?"



"You shouldn't be." I paused, searching for the right words. "But maybe all of this—the hallucinations, the projecting on me—maybe you feel guilty because you're the one who put her to bed that night."



He turned away from me, and I could feel him shaking. Finally he said, "I put her down on her back. I know you don't believe me. But I did." 



Neither of us spoke again that night. His brother picked him up early in the morning, while I pretended to be asleep. Then, just to be safe, I went into the nursery and removed the tiny Bluetooth speaker and pack of baby wipes from under the dresser.
