I went up to the lantern room at dusk, ready to demand answers. My fingertips had been glowing on and off all day—a faint shimmer when I reached for a cup, or pale when I scratched my jaw. Each time, I told myself a different lie. By evening, I'd stopped lying and started fuming. If Helel was doing this to me, I had a right to know, and I wanted to hear it from the source.

The lamp was burning. The chair was pushed under the writing desk. The logbooks were stacked neatly on the shelf. And the being I'd come to confront was nowhere—just a room, a lamp, and the faint hum of rotation.

"Helel." My echo felt smaller, more desperate. "HELEL. I know you can hear me.”

Nothing. So I waited. I paced. I called his name again, and when the silence stretched into something that felt like refusal, It broke me.

I know I swept the logbooks off the shelf. I kicked the chair across the room. I remember slamming my palm against the brass housing of the lamp—the metal wasn’t blazing as I expected for a lamp of this size. It was warm like a body. I remember shouting things I'm not proud of. Accusations. Names I'd been taught to fear as a child.

Then I stormed out. I slammed the iron door behind me and took the first step down the spiral staircase—

—and my foot landed on salt-bleached wood.

I was standing on the dock. I had walked through a door nearly two stories above the ground and stepped onto sea level.

The lighthouse was behind me, its beam still sweeping across the waters, but the door I'd just walked through was gone. There was only the tower, the dock, and the night sky.

And the sky was full of stars.

I realized I hadn't seen stars since I arrived. Tonight, they were packed so close together that the darkness between them looked like veins in a body of light. And on the western horizon, just above the black sea, one star burned brighter than all the rest.

My memories flew back to fishing on Lake Tana. The sun had just set, and the sky was deepening to the first dark blue of evening. Abdi and I were sitting on the rocks while Father pointed upward, tracing shapes in the sky.

"That's Meskelo," he said, drawing a cross. "And the Three Sisters. And that one, the one that looks like a hook, that's the Scorpion's Tail."

Abdi listened to all of it. He always listened. He was the good son, the one who’d have memorized every star if father asked him to. I was the one staring at the water, wondering why the sun left every night and if it was ever afraid it wouldn't come back.

Then Abdi pointed to the western horizon, where a single bright point of light hung just above the water. "What about that one, Abba?”

Our father was quiet for a moment. Then he said wistfully, "That's not a star, Abdi. That's Zahara. It's… a planet. The old ones called it the Bright One, the one who brings the dawn."

"Why does it stay so close to the sun?"

"Because it can't help itself," our father said distantly. "It's not sure what it would do without the light."

I remember the pause after. I remember thinking he was going to say something else, something important, but Abdi asked another question, and the moment passed.

Now, standing on the dock three decades later, I watched the Dusk Star burn on the horizon—Zahara—and I watched the lighthouse beam sweep its arc across the water. Every few seconds, the beam would cross the star's reflection on the sea, a path of silver stretching from the horizon to the shore.

At that intersection, Helel was standing on the water, as if the sea were a glass floor. Its form was no concert of instruments; It was more terrifying in its simplicity.

It was a silhouette cut from the post-sunset sky—limbs of rose-gold and violet, hair adrift with stars. When It shifted Its weight, the horizon bent, as if the line between sea and sky were a robe draped over its shoulders. Its face was a plane of deep blue dusk, and Its eyes were two points of light that might have been planets or might have been the last thing you saw before you fell into the sky.

It was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen, and I hated for it.

"Have you ever wanted something so badly," Helel said in the wind and the low rumble of the sea, "that you became smaller for it?"

I didn't answer. I was still standing on the dock, my hands gripping the salt-bleached railing, my heart beating too fast.

"I wanted to be near the Light," It continued. "I wanted it with a love that swallowed me. And when I reached for it, I didn't rise." It tilted Its head, and the horizon tilted too. "I fell. I kept falling. And when I finally stopped falling, I was here, bound in my radiance. Do you know how that feels, Dawit?"

I thought of Sarai, and the way I'd spent years drifting away. "I might," I said.

Helel smiled with stardust. "You might."

I pulled my hands off the railing and held them up. My fingertips were glowing faintly in the dark. "What's happening to me?"

Helel regarded me for a long moment, fading in and out of existence every time the beam swept between us.

"You’re becoming kin," It said.

"What does that mean?"

"It means you understand me. It means you love something enough to burn for it." It paused. "I’m not doing this to you, Dawit. I am not capable of that anymore. Light moves as it moves. Your father was bright—so bright I could barely look at him."

"My father is dead."

"Yes."

"Did the 'light' kill him?"

Helel was silent. The stars dimmed. "I don't know," It said finally. "I felt him stop, but I couldn’t see how. The light only shows me what it touches, and—."

"His death was dark, I know. But that’s not an answer, Helel."

"It's the only one I have." It turned toward me, and the two points that were eyes waned. "I have never lied to you, Dawit. I’ve told you truths you didn’t want to hear, but I’ve never lied. If I knew how your father died, I would tell you. I’m tired of being alone with knowledge."

I wanted to believe It. Everything said since I arrived had been true. I'd checked the scriptures. I'd searched the annotations. I'd lain awake at night trying to find the deception, and there was none. The only thing more sickening than a demon who lies is an angel who doesn't.

"You're Lucifer," I said, and the word came out harsher than I meant. "That's what the church calls you. Ha-satan. The adversary. The accuser. The one who fell because—"

The sea broke. The lighthouse shook. The beam shattered across the water as something rose—something vast, from before my father burned, from before the first keeper lit the first flame in this tower.

Scales of red and gold, each one the size of a shield, coiled upward through the water like a pillar of fire made flesh. Seven heads rose, each wearing crowns twisted from black iron and weeping ichor that hissed when it hit the sea. Ten horns spiraled from the crowns like trees that had burned yet kept growing. A tail swept behind the creature, and with each sweep, the stars dimmed in patches, as though a fraction of the heavens blotted out.

The heads spoke.

One was an infant abandoned in the cold.

One was an emperor pronouncing a death sentence.

One was a lover in the dark.

One was a radio tuned to empty air.

One laughed endlessly.

One sung in a litany of syllables that felt like insects crawling into my ears.

One turned toward me and spoke with Sarai’s voice.

"Is this what they’ve made of me?"

The air smelled of storms and old blood. The water was boiling, churning in spirals beneath the surface.

"Does it help you, Dawit," said the head, impossibly sad, "to believe I am only this?"

Then an eighth mouth pried open below the seven necks, sending them limply toppling back. The smell of eggs and meat left too long in the sun swarmed my nostrils. What dragged itself out of the maw was a composite of bat-wings stretched over translucent skin, veins pulsing against membrane; cloven hooves; a second face in its belly that grinned with jagged teeth, its tongue lolling between them like a worm; talons gouged the skin of the great dragon as it climbed out; eyes of a goat with rectangular pupils that reflected no light. Hair like wires. A tail like a scorpion eating its own mouth, swallowing and swallowing and never reaching the end.

It was exactly what I'd expected to see the first time I climbed the stairs. It was every art, every Sunday warning, every nightmare I'd had about he who waited in the dark with a grin and a contract.

Helel's voice came from the creature's belly in the croon of a disappointed father.

"You want a monster," It said. "I can wear that for you. It's not hard. I've been wearing it for thousands of years." The bat-wings folded, and the cloven hooves scraped, and the goat-eyes regarded me. "But you must know—none of this is mine. Every piece of this body was given to me by someone who needed a thing to hate. The horns came from the desert. The hooves came from the Greeks. The wings came from the painters who needed an enemy beautiful enough to fear. The second face—" The belly-mouth grinned, a terrible wet grin. "That came from a fourth-century bishop who had a dream and woke up certain he'd seen the truth."

Helel took a step toward me, and Its body lurched. Its parts moved out of sync—a puppet assembled from incompatible pieces.

"Does hating me feel righteous, Dawit?"

I couldn't speak. My throat had wrapped around every word I'd ever known like a brother protecting his kin.

"That's why they keep making new versions of me,” Helel continued. “It feels righteous to hate. It feels holy to have an enemy. It feels like faith to look at a thing and call it a demon, because if the demon is real, then the God who will defeat it must be real too." He paused. The goat-eyes blinked, slowly, one after the other.

"What are you, really?" I whispered.

The composite body shuddered. The bat-wings drooped. The belly-mouth closed yet spoke.

"I am the Prideful Song which sang too brightly," Helel said. "And I have paid for it since."

The dead dragon sank back into the sea, one head at a time, its weeping crowns dimming as the black water swallowed them. The composite body dissolved into mist, leaving only the silhouette of rose-gold and violet cloud standing on the glass-dark water. The planet still burned on the horizon, as though no time passed at all. The lighthouse beam still swept its arc.

"Go inside, Dawit," Helel said. "Eat something. Sleep. You'll need your strength."

"For what?"

But It didn't answer. It stood on the water, a silhouette cut from the sky, watching me with eyes of planetary light. And after a long breeze, I turned and walked back into the tower.

My hands are still shaking as I write this. My fingertips glow brighter now. The sea outside the window is flat, black, and silent. But I can see the Evening Star on the horizon, and I know Helel is still standing on the water, waiting for something.

I don't know what I believe anymore. I don't know what I'm becoming.

The light holds, but I don't know what I am anymore.

I found letters this morning.

They were on the writing desk in the keeper's quarters, stacked neatly next to the logbooks. Three envelopes, already sealed, already stamped. My handwriting sprawled on the front: Meron's name, Sarai’s address.

I don't remember writing these.

I sat down at the desk and held the first envelope up to the light. The paper was the same cheap stationery I'd found in the storage closet—cream-colored, slightly yellowed with age, the kind my father must have ordered in bulk decades ago. The ink was the same black fountain pen ink I'd been using in the logbooks. The handwriting was mine: every loop, every slant, every slightly-too-cramped 'e' that Sarai used to tease me about.

I opened the first letter. My hands were shaking from confusion so deep it felt like vertigo.

*My little star,*

*I miss you so much. The lighthouse is beautiful this time of year. The sea is flat and calm, like a mirror that reflects the whole sky.*

*I've been collecting seashells for you—the ones that sing the waves when you hold them to your ear. I found a conch yesterday, pale pink and spiraled like a unicorn's horn. When you listen to it, you can hear the whole ocean. I think you'll love it!*

*I know it's hard being away from me for so long. It's hard for me too, but I want you to remember what I said last time. It's been in our family for a very long time—eight generations, can you believe that? Your grandfather tended this, and his father before him, and his father before him. And one day, when you're older, I'll bring you here and show you everything.*

*I love you more than all the stars in the sky, little one—more than the Morning Star itself. Be good for your mother. I'll write again soon.*

*—* Baba

I read it twice. The words were warm, gentle, full of love—and a lie. The sea wasn't beautiful; it was black and flat. The stars weren't bright; I hadn't seen them until last night. I hadn't collected any seashells. I hadn't seen a conch. And I had never, not once, planned to bring Meron to this place.

I tore the letter in half, then in quarters, and I kept tearing until the paper was a pile of funeral confetti on the desk, and I swept the pieces into the oil reservoir.

I reached for the second envelope to do the same—

—and my hand stopped.

I couldn't move it. My fingers were touching the envelope, but they wouldn't close. They wouldn't tear. They held the paper, as if it were a living thing I was afraid to hurt.

I tried again.

“Tear. TEAR. TEAR, DAMMIT. TEAR FOR ME.”

My hand still wouldn't move. It held the envelope patiently, waiting for me to stop fighting.

I stopped fighting.

The second letter was shorter when I opened it. It wrote of the fish I'd been catching—"platinum and quick, with eyes like mirrors"—and the way the sunrise looked from the top of the tower. It mentioned a dream it claimed I had, one about Meron climbing the stairs and laughing at the way the light made rainbows on the walls. It ended the same way as the first: I'll bring you here one day.

The third letter was the longest. It talked about my father—"your grandfather, who tended the light before me"—and about how proud he would have been to meet her. It described the lantern room in loving detail: the brass, the lens, the dancing flame. It said, "This is your inheritance, little star."

At the bottom of the third letter, there was a postscript:

*P.S. I hope you received the seashells I sent last month. The ones from the eastern rocks have the deepest song. Hold them to your ear, and you'll hear what I hear every night. It's like a lullaby, isn't it?*

Last month… I have only been on this island for—what? A week or two? Maybe less. The days had been blurring together, sure, one grey morning bleeding into the next, but it can’t have been more than two weeks since Tesfaye's boat pulled away from the dock.

I searched the desk, the storage closet, and the logbooks. I looked for copies of previous letters, any record of what I might have sent, or some way to verify how long I've been here. I found nothing: blank stationery, a spare ink cartridge, and a roll of stamps. The logbooks only go back to Day 1. My father's journal is still blank—every page empty, as though it were never written.

Time here is measured by the sweep of the lighthouse and the churn of the sea, and neither of those will tell me what I need to know. I thought I was at the beginning of this. I thought I still had time.

But if the letters have been going out for weeks, then I’m not at the beginning of anything. I’m in the middle of a process that started before I knew it was happening. This Light has been using me longer than I've been aware of it. The bait was laid before I ever climbed the tower.

Bait. I’m still just bait.

I’m alone on this island with a stack of letters I don't remember writing and a body that won't obey me when I destroy them. The sea is flat. The sky is grey. The beam still sweeps its arc, and somewhere above me, in the lantern room, Helel is waiting for me to stop fighting.

I’m losing time. I’m losing myself. And I’m afraid—deeply afraid—that I've already sent her a letter that matters, one that tells her how to get here.

The light holds, I think, for longer than I thought.

My body isn’t mine. Take my hands; I lift them high. They're not mine to do.

I woke this morning and held them to the grey light, and I could see bone. My skin had gone translucent, like vellum held over a flame. When I flexed my fingers, the joints glowed brighter at the knuckles. I could see the tendons stretch. I could see blood dragging through the capillaries.

I checked my reflection in the window, and the eyes that looked back at me held no brown, only violet.

I can hear the light now. Not as a hum, not as a melody buried in the stone, but as a voice. And I can see the music. When the beam sweeps across the water, it leaves a trail of color in the air, like the afterimage of a camera flash. Sound is becoming light. Categories are collapsing.

I climbed the stairs to confront Helel about the letters, but with every step, a memory rose to meet me.

*Step twelve: My mother's hands, brown and strong, braided my hair before church. She smelled of berbere, and she hummed while she worked, some old hymn I'd spent my adult life trying to forget—Hossana.*

*Step thirty-four: Abdi had a laugh that filled a room, a car, and a church. He laughed with his whole body, head thrown back, arms hugging himself, as if joy were a physical thing he needed to hold tightly to. I spent my childhood living in the shadow of that laugh, wondering why mine never sounded as joyful.*

*Step fifty-seven: my father's study. Books stacked to the ceiling. The smell of old paper and cologne. He was writing a sermon, and I was sitting in the corner, waiting for him to notice me. He didn't. He wrote for three hours, and I sat there the whole time, and he never looked up.*

*Step eighty-nine: Sarai wore a yellow dress on our first date, and she laughed at all of my jokes, even the one about the blind whale. I asked her about Poland, about her studies, about her family. She asked me about Ethiopia, about the church, about my family. I told her everything. I told her too much. She held my hand anyway.*

*Step one hundred twenty-three: Meron was born. The nurse placed her in my arms, and she was so impossibly small, an entire life no bigger than my two hands together. She opened her eyes—black then—and looked at me. She liked at me. I felt something shift in my chest that has never shifted back.*

*Step one hundred fifty-nine: Sarai cried in the kitchen. The divorce papers on the table. She said I had disappeared into myself, that I was never really there, that she couldn't love a man who was always somewhere else. I didn't argue. Only now did I realize I was already halfway to the lighthouse, even then.*

*Step one hundred eighty-seven: the last time I saw Meron. She was standing on her mother's porch, waving with both hands. "Don't forget the seashells, Baba!" I promised I wouldn't forget. I got into the car. I cried when I shut the door.*

Step one hundred ninety-six. I opened the door. Helel was sitting on the windowsill, one leg drawn up, casual as a college student waiting for a friend to finish class, but the form It wore this time was new.

It was a youth of impossible beauty—androgynous, calm, draped in a simple white tunic that seemed to glow from within. A torch burned in one hand, the flame steady and golden, but the wood of the torch didn’t consume. Sandals of woven cloud wrapped Its feet. A cloak the exact color of the sky before sunrise draped Its shoulders—that deep, expectant blue that is not yet day but no longer night.

Its face was human. Perfectly, proportionately human. Approachable. That was the most unnerving thing about it. The nebula, the instruments, and the dragon had all been terrifying, but they were recognizably alien. This was a face I could have passed on the street. This was a face I could have trusted.

"You've been writing letters," I said.

Helel turned the torch in its hand, watching the flame. "The light has been writing letters. I told you—I am not doing this to you. The light does what it does."

"Then stop it."

"I can't." It looked at me, eyes were the color of the sky before sunset. "The light wants. It wanted your father. It wants you. And it wants your daughter. I am not the light's master, Dawit."

I took a step toward It. My translucent hands were shaking at my sides. "You said you never lie. So tell me—did my father know? About the letters? About what the light was doing to him?"

Helel was silent for a moment. Then: "Yes. He knew. He fought it for a long time. Longer than most. Longer than you."

"What happened to him? You felt him stop, but you didn't see it. You said his death was dark."

"I said the light only shows me what it touches. Your father's death was not in the light."

"What does that mean?"

Helel set the torch down on the windowsill, where it continued to burn without catching the wood. "It means I don't know, Dawit. It means something happened to him that the light could not see. I have wondered about it every day. I will wonder about it until this tower falls into the sea."

I stared at this beautiful, approachable, untrustworthy face, and I realized, with a clarity that cut through the music and the light and the terror, that I could not trust It. The truth itself was the trap.

I turned to the lamp. The great brass housing, the lens, the flame that had been burning since before I was born. I grabbed the iron poker from beside the reservoir and swung it at the lens.

The poker stopped in midair. I couldn't push it forward. I couldn't pull it back. It just hung there, vibrating, as if the air around the lens had turned to cement.

"I told you," Helel said softly. "The light will not allow itself to be broken."

I let go of the poker now embedded in the air. I turned to the brass tank at the base of the lamp. There was a drainage valve at the bottom. I grabbed the wheel and spun it.

Oil poured out across the floorboards—thick, dark, reeking of gin and rum. The lamp flickered. The beam outside the window stuttered in its arc.

Helel's eyes went wide. "Dawit. Stop. You don't—"

The beam died.

For one heartbeat. Two. The lantern room was dark except for the faint glow of Helel's torch and the violet light seeping from my own translucent hands.

Then the sea began to scream.

This was no figure of speech. The sea opened its mouth and screamed in a chorus of keys I've never known, lyrics I've never imagined, harmonies that had no business existing in a world of human ears.

Through the window, I saw the waters burning and churning. Great spirals of white foam boiled across the surface, and far beneath them—shapes too large to comprehend coiled and uncoiled in the deep. The stars above began to dim, one by one, as if the thing beneath the water was swallowing their reflections.

I didn't wait for a reveal. I grabbed one of the logbooks from the floor—the one I'd swept off the shelf in my rage—and threw it into the fuel. Then another. And another.

I threw decades of logs into the fuel. The flame still sputtered. The beam still faded. The shape beneath the water still rose.

I looked at my hands, and the light was consuming me from the inside out. I remembered what Helel had said: Light moves as it moves. Your father was bright.

I took a deep breath, and before I could feel regret, I was sprawling across the writing desk to my father's Bible. I reached into the flame with it.

The book burnt up in an instant, as though made of flash paper, and singed my hand. When my fingertip touched the fire, the lamp blazed into a flare of gold so bright it lit the whole room like a lightning strike. The heat was immense, but it didn't hurt me. It felt like coming to a cozy home after a winter storm. It felt like the first time I held Meron. It felt like Mother’s hugs, Father’s pride, and Abdi’s humility.

The skin on my fingertip blackened and curled, and the glow beneath it shone brighter than the flame itself. But I wasn't ready to die, not yet. Not while Meron was still out there, waiting for seashells.

So I couldn’t give the light my whole self. I had to find more to give. The first thing I found was a knife in the writing desk—an old filet knife, rusted at the hilt but still sharp. I knelt beside the reservoir, and I placed my left hand on the brass rim. I thought of Meron. Her small hands molding slime. Her bouncing excitement at her first loose tooth. The way she called me Baba and meant it like a prayer.

Then I severed my left pinky. The pain was distant, muffled, as if it were happening to someone else. The wound bled, of course—then it glowed with a thick molten gold that pulsed. I dropped the finger into the reservoir before thought reached me, and the lamp roared.

The beam outside the window steadied. The screaming from the sea quieted. The shape beneath the water stopped rising.

But it still wasn't enough. I could still feel it out there—patient, hungry, waiting for the light to falter again.

I remembered Sarai’s tears while we said our vows. I remembered her sobs when we severed our vows.

I severed my left ring finger. The glow from the wound was brighter this time, almost blinding, and when my finger hit the oil, the lamp flared so brilliantly I had to shield my eyes. The beam swept across the water like a blade, and the churning stilled with its touch.

I knelt on the floorboards, cradling my maimed hand, watching the lamp burn with a brightness it hadn't had since the day I arrived. The oil I'd drained was still pooling on the floor, but it didn't matter anymore.

"Dawit." Helel's voice came from behind me, and I turned.

The youth in the white tunic was gone. The being that stood before me now was something that had been waiting for this moment, I think, since the first time I climbed the stairs.

A man stood on the floorboards like fiery coals beneath his feet. Every surface of his body was covered in precious stones—sardius, topaz, diamond, beryl, jacinth, and amethyst—set in gold, extending like the breastplate of a high priest. Wings of stained-glass light folded at his back, each feather a panel depicting a scene I recognized: a garden, a mountaintop, a lightning storm, a dark sea. His feet were bronze embers on the wood.

He was weeping, and his tears were liquid gemstones that hardened upon striking the floor, clattering across the boards like beads dropped.

"You have given the Light a piece of yourself," Helel said, and His voice was the same gentle, grieving instrument it had always been, even through the jeweled face. "You have proven what I knew. You love something enough to burn for it."

He took a step toward me, and the floorboards hissed beneath His bronze feet. "Now I will offer you a choice. The same choice I was offered, once."

I clutched my maimed hand to my chest. "What choice?"

"Become my successor," Helel said.

Our tears began to drop in sync. "And if I refuse?"

Helel's jeweled face did not change, but His wings dimmed, and the scenes depicted in the stained-glass feathers cracked. "If you refuse, the Light will take you, but your resistance will make the flame dimmer. The dark sea will edge closer. And when they rise, none will be here to hold them back."

"What did my father choose?" I asked.

"I don't know," he said with a frustrated finality. "That's the truth. I don't know what Yohannes chose. The light couldn't see him anymore. Something happened to your father that I cannot explain. I have spent every day since wondering if he accepted or refused or found some third path I've never been able to see."

I looked down at my glowing hands. My maimed left hand, still seeping golden light. My translucent right hand, through which I could see the floorboards beneath me.

*Silly Baba, you sound like a lullaby when you're not singing.*

*One day, when you're older, I'll bring you here.*

"No.”

Helel's wings folded inward, and the stained-glass scenes fractured. "Dawit—"

"I refuse. You want the heir to my father’s curse? I last saw him hanging in his garage.” My voice cracked, my throat swelled, and I spoke through broken cries. “This was never my inheritance. I'm not going to let my daughter inherit this." I stood up, still cradling my hand. The pain was starting to flitter through now—radiating up my arm and into my shoulder. "I'll feed the light if I have to. I'll hold back the dark with my own flesh, but I will not become you. I will not become my father. I would burn for my daughter; she will not burn for me.”

Helel didn't try to stop me. He stood on the burning floorboards, weeping liquid gemstones, watching me with the eyes of a creature who had been alone for so long he'd forgotten what refusal looked like.

I turned and walked out of the lantern room. I pulled the iron door shut behind me, and I threw the bolt shut.

The light holds. I think I’m the one holding it.

—

I’m running out of pieces to give. My left hand is gone—severed at the wrist three days ago. I did it in the lantern room, with the same rusted filet knife, while Helel watched from the window in the form of a mourning dove.

My wounds don’t bleed anymore.

When I stand in front of the window, I can see the sea through my chest. My heart is still flesh, expanding and contracting in a rhythm that matches the lamp, but my spine is a column of white fire. My ribs are arches of amethyst, and the spaces between them are filled with light, or music, or the memory of dancing.

I can hear my father sometimes. When the beam is steady, and the sea is quiet, fragments of his voice drift through like dust motes in sunlight. He's singing the hymns my mother used to hum while she braided my hair.

*Hossana. Hossana. Hossana in the Highest…*

Or his rolling vowels fill the room with a warmth that feels almost like his hand on my shoulder.

Or he just talks in idle conversation about the weather, the sea.

I've tried to speak to him. I've begged him to tell me what to do, how to hold on, how to keep the light burning. But either he can't hear me, or he can't answer. The light flows one way, and I am still on this side of it.

Today I went looking for answers in the only place I hadn't searched.

The storage closet in the keeper's quarters has always been a mess, but behind a stack of mildewed tarpaulins, I found something I'd missed. A small door, barely waist-high, unlocked, maybe for decades, waiting for me to notice it.

The crawlspace beyond was lined with shelves of dark wood that warped with age. On each of the shelves were records.

I found ship manifests from the 1800s, written in a cramped hand, listing supplies delivered to islands I’d never known. I found letters from previous keepers—to their wives, to their children, to their gods.

One was addressed to a woman named Tiru, dated 1903, and the handwriting was so similar to my own that I had to set it down and breathe before I could continue.

I found a will, written in Ge'ez, leaving the lighthouse to a daughter who had died before his father's ashes were cold.

And after an hour of skimming, I found something that shouldn't have been there: Fresh papyrus, as if it had been made yesterday. The ink was gold, and the handwriting was calligraphy of light, each letter a tiny flame that didn't consume the page, and I recognized it immediately. I'd seen those letters traced in the air on the writing desk. I'd watched them dissolve into crimson mist.

I sat down on the cold stone floor of the crawlspace, my back against the warped shelves, my neck craned and cramped, and I read.

**Record of Yohannes, Seventh Keeper of the Bound Light**

We set this down now while the memory is still sharp. The light only shows what it touches, and what happened tonight was too bright for the light to perceive.

He came to us at dusk. His body was already more light than flesh—he had been giving pieces of himself to the flame for weeks, as you have been giving, as we all gave before you. His hands were gone. His eyes were white-gold. He couldn’t speak anymore, but he didn't need to speak.

He wanted to know if there was a third path.

We told him what we will tell you.

But your father was a scholar. He had read every book in its original script. He had studied the binding of the Watchers and the fall of the Morning Star and the long silence between testaments.

"You said the light only shows you what it touches," he told me—his voice was fading, and we had to piece the words together from the fragments. "What if something could be brighter than the light?"

He stood on the stones where our lineage has stood for eight generations, plus the progenitors beyond them, and he opened himself to the light entirely.

The flare was so bright that I couldn't see it. The whole world went white, and for one moment. The shadows were silent.

When the light faded, your father was ash.

We told you his death was dark. That was true. It was dark to us, because we couldn't see it. It was too bright for light to perceive. Now you are here, and you are becoming bright too, and we are afraid.

Not of you. For you.

We have been the Star for so long that we have forgotten what it means to choose. We accepted the title because I could not refuse. We have been burning ever since, and the burning has become so familiar that I cannot imagine anything else. But your father imagined something else. And I think—we hope—that you might imagine it too.

If you find this, know that I am so sorry I couldn't see him go. I am sorry we couldn't tell you the truth. The light would not let us speak of what it could not perceive.

——————

I set the papyrus down. My hand was shaking—my remaining hand, still whole but translucent, still mine for too little time. The gold letters flickered on the page like candle flames.

I now understand why Helel wept for him. I sat in the crawlspace for a long time, holding the papyrus. I thought of Meron and the letters on my desk.

I am diminished. My left hand is gone, and my right is fading. My body is more light than flesh now, and soon I will have to choose how to spend what remains.

I am not afraid anymore. Not of Helel, who has been grieving for longer than I can imagine. Not of the shadows, who stir in the deep but fear the flame. Not of the light, which cannot perceive what is brighter.

My father is not in the light. He is somewhere brighter.

The light holds. I am burning. And I think—I hope—that burning is not the same as fading.

**For Meron. I hope you never find this**

My little planet,

I don't know how old you'll be when you read this. I don't know if you'll ever read this at all. Maybe these words will sit until the lights go out forever, unread, unremembered. Maybe that's for the best. But I need to set this down, because writing is not speech.

I want you to know that I love you. I have loved you since before you were born, since the first time I felt you kick your mother's belly, since the first time you opened your eyes—black like mine used to be—and looked at me like I was someone worth keeping. You made me a father. You made me a better man than I was. And I'm sorry I couldn't be that man for longer.

The lighthouse is not your inheritance. Do you understand? Whatever letters you've received, whatever seashells you've held to your ear, whatever songs you've heard—they were never from me. The light writes what it wants. The light wants what it wants. And what it wants is you, because you are the last of our blood, and the light has been feeding on our blood for generations.

You will not be the last. I will.

I know you'll have questions. I know you'll want to understand why I left, why I never came back, and why your father chose a tower in the middle of nowhere over a life with you.

I didn't leave because I didn't love you. I left because I loved you enough to burn for you.

Your grandfather found a new path. I'm going to find it too. And when I do, the light will be so bright that the shadows will never rise again. You'll be safe. You'll be free. You'll grow up and grow old and never have to climb these stairs or count these steps or sweep your father's ash.

Live, Meron. Live a long and ordinary life. Forget the lighthouse. Forget the stars. Forget everything except this: I love you, and I am one with you.

Baba

**To anyone who reads this account:**

The letters are still there, still sealed, still waiting to be mailed. I don't know if I'll be able to destroy them. I don't know if I'll be able to stop them from reaching her, but I've written the above one with my own mind, my own will, my own remaining hand. If she ever finds it, she'll know the truth.

I climbed the stairs for the last time—one hundred ninety-six steps, then six more to the landing. The iron door was gone. And when I stepped into the lantern room, I found myself already there.

He was sitting at the writing desk, one leg crossed over the other, a logbook open in front of him. His hair was the same tight black curls I saw in the mirror. His nose was the same strong line. His jaw was the same defined angle, his brow the same expressive arch, his mouth pressed into the same thin line of concentration I've worn since I was old enough to worry. He wore my face the way I wear my father's—not as a mask, but as an inheritance. His eyes were the only difference. They were lavender.

He was writing in my handwriting. Every loop, every slant, every cramped 'e'.

"You're early," Helel said, not looking up.

"I'm late," I said. "I should have done this days ago."

He closed the logbook and set the pen aside. When he turned to face me, I saw my own exhaustion mirrored in his borrowed features—shadows under the eyes, gauntness at the temples, too many sleepless nights, and too many impossible choices. He had once worn my father's face, I realized. And his father's before that.

"You've decided," He said.

"I've decided."

Helel stood up from the desk. My body stood up from the desk—my lean frame, my square shoulders, my scuffed boots. He crossed the room and stood in front of me, and we were shedding lavender tears.

"Tell me what to do," I said.

"There's nothing to tell," Helel said. "There are no instructions. Your father spoke no prayer and performed no ritual. He just—" He paused, and the lavender eyes searched my face for something I couldn't name. "He loved. He loved your mother. He loved Abdi.” He touched my cheek, and I felt my father’s hand. “He loved you."

I thought of my mother's hands braiding my hair. I thought of Abdi's laugh filling a church. I thought of my father's study. I thought of Sarai in her yellow dress. I thought of Meron's first word—Baba—and her last wave from the porch, and every skip and snot and slip in between.

“I hope you find the song you once had, Helel.”

Helel didn’t reply. He stepped aside. He didn't try to stop me. He didn't offer me a choice, because the choice had already been made, and He knew it.

I walked to the lamp. The great brass housing, the Fresnel lens, the flame that had been burning since before I was born. I could see my reflection in the glass—translucent skin, violet eyes, a spine of white fire and ribs of amethyst, and a heart that was still, impossibly, flesh.

I write in my last act. I will place my remaining hand on the brass housing. The metal will be warm, warm like my father's hand on my shoulder, all those years ago, when he taught me how to bait a hook without piercing my thumb.

I will think of Sarai. I will sing of Meron.

And I will open the housing, and we will release the flame from its cage.

We do not know where we go from here, and perhaps that’s the point.
