Everyone has probably learned some form of the research process in school, right? It goes something like you have an idea or theory, you create a hypothesis, you test that hypothesis, you analyze the hypothesis, and report, over and over again. My job is a research coordinator, and I help facilitate that process every day for the government, day in, and day out.

 Every properly done research project uses an institutional review board, or IRB. They approve research studies and oversee the process so that participants aren’t unduly harmed or abused. When something happens, you have deadlines and mandates so things can be addressed in the proper manner, or, at least, they are supposed to.

The research study seemed straightforward as far as projects can go. Our facility had received a grant to see if a new drug formulary could work effectively on people. We had already completed the initial testing on animals as is required and now were moving on to efficacy and safety in people. The goal of the study was to determine if we could manipulate the thyroid into having someone grow a small amount, nothing major. The argument for the grant was that it is much easier and cost effective to mass produce one uniform type hundreds of thousands of times versus varying shapes and body types. Soldier physique could improve by relieving back tension resulting in better performance in the field. Plus, what person doesn’t want to be just a bit taller? Thus, the **S**trengthening **T**horacic **Re**gulation **T**hrough **C**apsule **H**andout (S.T.R.E.T.C.H.) study was born. That’s the part that makes me sick, there always must be a catchy name, and this one sold itself.

The approvals went fairly fast, simple blinded study, one person gets thyroid pill, another, the placebo. We could break the blind if anything went wrong by conferring with the central distribution pharmacist. Otherwise, standard questionnaires about health, mood, and self-perception changes noticed by the participants. A few eyebrows were raised before the initial approvals came in at first, but the preliminary animal data suggested no more than a 5-centimeter difference before stopping. What is that, like 2 inches? We wanted to take it slow and safe. The review board agreed, slow, and safe, but an acceptable risk. Approvals and acknowledgments peppered the various consents and protocols. With that, we were on our way.

The ads were eye-catching, get a little taller, one pill a day, good compensation for any new shirts you may have to buy. If you were healthy without any major health conditions, you were probably eligible. The younger population near the facility loved it the most. They flooded in, and more data is always useful. Initially we were approved for 300 subjects by the IRB. We briefly mentioned over the first lunches having to revise our protocols to allow even more in. We never got the chance after what came next.

The project began Monday morning. After patients consented to taking the pills, they were randomized and set to have a pill mailed to them by our on-site pharmacist. They were told to take one before bed each night and come in briefly each day to measure any differences starting the following Monday for two weeks. Every three to four days, they would answer the questionnaires themselves when they came in. There was such a flurry of activity those first few days we needed the backup coordinator to consent as well. By the end of the week, we had 48 subjects consented and mailed one of the drugs.

The first few days as subjects came in, everything seemed to flow well. Questions answered, very small centimeter differences, if at all, noticeable to the research assistants measuring the subjects. Expected. The mid-week questionnaires showed a trend of content across the subjects. No major life changes and a little taller, easy-peasy.

The problems began on Thursday. Subject 37 came in for their daily check-in. The research assistants were backed up with other subjects at the same time, so I oversaw 37’s study visit. I had seen them for the consent a week ago, but this time, they were taller. Noticeably so. 8 centimeters from baseline. I ran it through Google, 3.14 inches. More then expected, and noticeable to my eye, but within a standard deviation I supposed. I asked if they were alright and they said they were adjusting to the new world-view, but otherwise seemed okay. We both laughed it off while I made a mental note to take it to the head doctor for review. I asked the subject to please come back the following day to see me personally and wished them well.

After Subject 37 left, I was able to pull the head doctor aside for a few minutes. I explained the difference but he didn’t seem overly concerned. Was the subject alright? Then they may just have a genetic blip and it would balance out. The doctor thanked me for my concern and told me to follow up again the following day after their next visit and hurried to see their next patient in their clinic. Doctors are always busy, the reason my job existed in the first place.

Have I mentioned humans are strange? Each person has a unique physiology, psychology, and so on. This thought came to mind after seeing Subject 37 on Friday. While I expected to see Subject 37, I had asked the research assistants to sit in with me to make sure everything was recorded properly. Unfortunately, things popped up with a few other subjects and so it fell to me. Annoying, but everyone needs care. When Subject 37 walked through that door, I immediately knew something was wrong. 16 centimeters difference from baseline. Just over 6 inches. Starting the study, they were 2 inches shorter than me. Now, they towered over me. Intimidating to say the least. They were concerned but I explained that the doctor thought they might have something genetically different than other subjects. Still, the subject was worried. I pulled the doctor away from his Friday meetings, and while slightly perturbed, he came to see the subject himself. He did what he was supposed to do, he listened, he validated, he told the subject to withhold the pill that evening, and to contact the 24-hour emergency number on their consent if anything changed. The doctor said he would review them personally when they came in on Monday. Dejectedly, they, for lack of a better term, lumbered away.

 After the subject left, the doctor pulled me aside. He said we needed to report this as it was an unanticipated problem related to the medication. He pointed out that the initial animal tests showed a small linear growth before stabilizing but this seemed way beyond that. While he consulted with the research assistants to see if it was a pattern or a one-off, I was to submit the forms needed per our required timelines (within 24 hours). By the time everything was said and done, it was the end of the business day. I submitted the forms required and worried, but still, I had a family to return to. I went home, my work was done. **I did what I was supposed to do**. All I thought was that Monday would be a day of forms and phone calls. Nothing could’ve prepared me for what awaited me instead.

 I came into the office, early, quiet, but not unusual. My answering machine had 6 new messages, 4 of them were forwards from the facility’s main line. All to a varying degree of, “This research patient keeps calling us and we told him to contact the doctor, finally we just told him to come into the ER for an evaluation.” The second to last message was an urgent one from the IRB saying to stop all study protocols immediately and to notify patients to stop the drug. The final message was the head doctor telling me to come to the facility ER as soon as I heard it. I put my bag and lunch down and ran to the facility ER as fast as I could.

**Dear God**. Tall-stick like figures dotted the entrance to the facility. Every nurse or average-sized person on staff looked disgusted and ill. The head doctor was there drastically trying to corral and direct. It looked like he hadn’t slept since we last spoke. In the chaos, I looked around and saw Subject 37. At least, I think it was. 128 centimeters. 50 inches. 11.28 meters or just over 4 feet. They had reached 9 feet and 8 inches in height. Did you know the tallest man on record was only 8 feet 11 inches? Only. Hah.

Subject 37 stooped to see me. Their skin thin, stretched far beyond what was expected. Cracked and broken open with deep fissures as their body tried adjusting to their new dimensions. It wasn’t just them though, 18 of our subjects were all in varying degrees of the same shape. The older subjects toppled over, their bones unable to support themselves in their age. The research assistants were crying thinking they were the ones who had ruined these people’s lives.

Subject 46 was there, their chart said they had some previous anger issues but were deemed eligible anyway. They decided to use their new height and anger to literally toss people around the room, yelling to fix it over and over again.

We spent the day trying to get everyone settled down and into rooms for observation. All pills were stopped, but it could still take time to get out of their systems. We tried to flush their systems in numerous ways, but people are strange and the body finds ways to adapt. I finally had a moment to ask Subject 37 about their pain. They pointed a gangly finger in my direction and only grunted, their vocal chords having ripped a day ago.

I spent the night that night. My workday ended at 5:00 but both the head doctor and I knew that this wasn’t going to magically get better. At best, these subjects would be scarred for life, physically and mentally. At worst, they might not make it at all. I tried to monitor everyone throughout the night, but by the end of it all, fatigue took me and I fell asleep on a waiting chair.

The following morning, they were gone. Final measurement for Subject 37? 256 centimeters from baseline. 100 inches, 2.56 meters, or 8.4 feet. Final height? 14 feet, 2 inches. The body is physically unable to support that much skin that quickly. Much like a Prince Rupert’s drop, their heads remained intact while everything below had grown and grown and grown exponentially until it was too much and they shattered into bits and pieces from the slightest wind.

We lost our funding pretty quickly after that. The doctor was censured, and I moved onto another research facility. After all, I followed every protocol, and I still had a family to feed. The data was never published. Failures rarely are. But if you are ever out there and you happen to see a super lanky person walking around, maybe they were one of ours. Statistically, at least 6 other people should have had the experimental drug. And humans are strange, so maybe a few adapted to a little S.T.R.E.T.C.H. after all.
