These last few months have been the hardest time in my life. To make a long story short, my wife and I were divorced about six months ago. We had been married for only a few years, had dated for less time than that, but still I thought I’d spend my whole life with her. I won’t get deeper into it, but she betrayed my trust, and because of that we separated. 

Shortly after we had first decided to separate I moved into a new apartment building, where I still currently live, and for the most part I had been quite happy with what I’ve got here. It’s not as big as the house we once had, but it was enough for me. However, after what has happened in the last month of my time living here, I can no longer say that I feel comfortable in this building. 

I suppose I could really say it had been a problem since I first moved into the complex. Taking the elevator down from my apartment, there always seemed to be a sort of… stutter, when it had reached the floor I selected and before the doors slid open, as though they had forgotten their cue. No longer than five, six seconds, but still longer than you’d like when you’re riding the elevator. Eventually I had brought it up to maintenance, and after taking a look at it they had told me the problem I described was not happening whatsoever, though they’d look into it more if other residents reported the same. I didn’t think anything of it after that, until a month ago when the first ‘incident’, as I’ll call them, happened. 

The first incident happened after I was coming home from the courthouse, my wife and I having fully finalized things with the divorce and becoming legally separated. Five months may seem like a long time, but it was actually quite short considering what we had to hash out. If I’m being honest, I gave her more than I should have. I just wanted the process to be done with. 

The elevator rambled upward toward my floor, the fourth floor, and as it always did it paused before the doors opened. The only thing different this time was that, even after those five or six seconds, they still remained shut. Passively I pressed the open-door button a few times, and still no response. If this were any other day I think I would’ve panicked–I’ve always had a fear of being stuck in an elevator–but that day, I just couldn’t care. I stood there, staring at the door, and then I saw something. 

Another door came into view as though it were superimposed over the elevator door. I quickly recognized this door as the front door to the house my wife and I had, the one she now owned alone. That heavy paneled door always stood out on the house, painted a bright red which jumped out at you among the monotone colors that surrounded it. My wife hated it, actually, but I always said we could keep it, that it gave the house character. I wonder now if she’s gotten rid of it, put something more drab to match the rest of the house. 

I stuck out my hand in front of where the door was, but it remained in my vision, now as though it were in front of my hand. I just stood there, staring at the door in front of my hand, the other hanging at my side limply. After another minute or so the elevator opened, and I went back to my apartment, thinking myself in desperate need of sleep. I didn’t leave my apartment for the next three days, ordering delivery when I could eat and otherwise trying to simply sleep the days away. Eventually, though, I needed to go back to work, and that’s when the second incident occurred. 

I was taking the elevator down to the parking garage, which was a subfloor underneath the building itself. My suit hung loosely from my body, my hair disheveled still and week-old facial hair patching my face. I was going to work out of necessity, not out of my own drive, and I was half-hoping I would be sent home for my unsightly appearance. 

As usual, the elevator paused when it reached the floor. I stood there, waiting, and when the elevator chimed and the doors slid open I nearly stepped out before catching myself. There was nothing outside the elevator. Nothing. A pitch black void lay before me. I didn’t dare step off, but the elevator waited for me. Even with the lights from the elevator shining out, it was as though that darkness merely ate the light, leaving no trace of its reach in the confines of whatever floor this was. I pressed the button to close the door and it obeyed, before chiming again and opening back out to the parking garage. I got in my car and made my way to work, traffic a bit heavier than usual. 

When I stepped into the office I was quickly confronted by my boss. 

“Where the hell have you been?” he said. “Why even show up at this point, it’s the goddamned end of the day!” 

I had no idea what he was talking about. I left the apartment at the same time I always did, and I was never late. It had only been an hour since I left, so I figured he was playing some sort of joke on me. 

“That’s a new one,” I said, powering on my desktop. The monitor flickered on and then I saw the time displayed before me: *4:44 P.M.* I looked back up at my boss, his paternal sternness gazing back at me. 

“So, where were you?” he said again. 

“I don’t know. I thought I left on time. I must’ve lost track,” I said, staring at those fours staring back at me. 

“Look,” he said, placing a hand on my shoulder. “I know you’ve been dealing with the divorce. I can only imagine how hard that’s been on you. If you want to take the rest of the week off, get your bearings, that’s fine by me. But you need to call ahead if you’re not going to be here.” 

“Okay,” I said. “Okay.” 

I went back to the apartment that day without incident, and things remained normal for the rest of that week. 

One day, I decided to call maintenance and asked them about any unmarked floors that the elevator may accidentally go to. They quickly told me no, there was no such thing, and probably by then wrote me off as some insane person. I certainly was beginning to feel insane. After some brief searching on the internet regarding the topic, and finding nothing, I decided that it might’ve just been some sort of waking nightmare. I booked an appointment with a psychiatrist to ask about it. 

On the day of my appointment I took the elevator down, and once again when it reached the parking garage it opened up into that nothingness. I was less startled this time and more confused, wondering if I was again having that nightmare. If it was a nightmare, I figured, there would be no harm if I stepped out, to see if anything might happen, and so I did. 

I took a few steps out into the darkness, walking on some invisible solidness that blended in with the rest of that black expanse. The air in there was cool and stagnant, feeling almost like the sterility of a doctor’s office. I walked a bit further, and off in the distance could see a red dot. I looked behind me and saw that the elevator was gone, and so I began to walk towards the dot. 

As the dot grew in my vision, I could see that it was once again that familiar red door, the one my wife hated at the house we lived together and that I figured by now was probably taken down or painted over and no longer of this world. I approached it, walking right up to it, and could see the perfect detail of it. It was an exact replica of that door, maybe even more real than the real thing. I had never seen anything so exact in a dream before. I reached to grab the golden knob of the door, began to turn it. 

“What are you doing?” my wife said. 

Adrenaline pumped through my body at that moment and I quickly let go, turned around, began running back to where the elevator must’ve been but where I could not see. I ran in the darkness toward the darkness away from that red door, shrinking back into the dot that it was far behind me. I ran longer than I ever ran before, and just as I began to lose hope I barreled into the elevator, nearly slamming into the back of it. Rapidly I pressed the button to close the door, and after a moment and the elevator’s gentle chime, it opened back out to the parking garage.

I checked my phone, saw that the time was far after my scheduled appointment, with two missed calls from the psychiatrist’s office. I turned around, stepped into the elevator again, and went back to my apartment. 

I called maintenance again about the unmarked floor, and they told me to stop calling. Again nothing happened with the elevator for the rest of the week.

The fourth incident was the final one, at least for now. It happened one night as I came home from a night of drinking. It was my first time at the bars in a long while, since before my wife and I had ever started dating, and I had felt a bit queasy from a few too many drinks. I had been taken home by an Uber, and so I entered from the lobby and shambled my way back to the elevator, missing the button for the fourth floor and instead hitting the fifth. 

The elevator hummed its way upward, and it opened again to that darkness. Although I was hesitant, I stepped off once more, my alcohol-impaired judgement guiding my way toward the red dot. This time, I had decided, I was going to open that door in spite of what anybody said. I placed my hand on the knob and went to turn it. 

“What are you doing?” my wife said. I hesitated, then fully pulled it open. 

Inside was our house, as it was just before I moved. Dusky light poured in from the windows, the source of which I could not surmise within this infinite blackness. Everything was still. We had already agreed by then that I would let her have the house, and so while her things remained, there were little holes here and there of where my stuff used to be. I left her almost everything, the furniture, the TV. It was really just little things–things barely noticeable–that were gone. 

I looked around for a moment, wondering again at the perfect detail of it all. I wondered how different it was now that I was gone. It was always me who held her back in fully engineering this house into her vision. It was always me who liked those little things like the red door, that made the house just a bit different from the rest. It always clashed with the creative vision that she had, but frankly I never found her vision that creative. With my things missing this house looked like any other. 

As I walked through the rooms of the first floor, I began to hear a faint cry coming from upstairs. Leisurely I continued my tour, naturally making my way toward the staircase before ambling upward, taking the steps carefully as my head still buzzed with alcohol. As I climbed those steps I remembered how I always hated stairs, always joked with my wife that we should install an elevator in the house. I began to laugh to myself as I climbed, made my way to that cry. 

The cry came from the master bedroom, the door of which was closed. I still chuckled to myself as I opened it, and there I found my wife on the floor, on her knees, clutching herself as she gently sobbed. My laughter did not stop as I approached her. 

“What are you doing?” she said weakly, so much so that at first I did not hear her under my laugh. I paused for a moment. 

“What?” I said. 

“What are you doing?” She looked up at me now, the red print of a hand strewn across her face. 

“I came to see you,” I said. “What were you doing?” 

Her sobs grew in intensity, and I kneeled down, grabbing her shoulder. 

“Hey, hey. What’s wrong?” I asked. 

“You know,” she said. “You know.” 

“Right,” I said, standing back up. “I made a mistake.”

“You did, didn’t you?”

“I did. But so did you.” 

“What?” She looked down at her feet, still curled up. I grabbed her shoulder, more roughly this time, turned her around to face me as we spoke. 

“You did too,” I said, looking straight into her swollen eyes. 

“How?” Her tears grew, and she kept repeating “How? How?” 

I threw her down, back on the ground, away from me. That one word repeated from her mouth as I left the room, down the stairs, out of the house, away from the red dot back to the elevator I could not see. It kept repeating even as the house was far behind me, kept repeating so that the word did not even sound real. 

I made it back to the elevator, and closed the door. A moment passed and it chimed a moment later, the doors opening. An older man stood there, hunched over his cane just staring back at me. I looked behind him and realized this was the fifth floor.

“Wrong floor,” I said, hitting the close-door button and pressing for the fourth.
