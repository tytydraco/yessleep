There is a small town in eastern India called Varanpur that does not appear on most maps.

It sits beside a slow river somewhere between the larger towns of Bihar and Bengal. It is known for three things: a cluster of shuttered warehouses, a railway siding that is rarely used, and a bronze bell hanging inside an abandoned temple.

The bell is unusual.

Nobody knows who cast it.

I first wrote about it in my phone notes in October 2015. I was surveying the region for a proposed rail corridor when my team stopped in Varanpur.

The note from that evening read:

\> "The villagers insist the bell rings at night. I heard nothing. They seem completely serious."

Three nights later, I heard it myself.

At exactly 2:17 a.m., the bell rang once.

Not loudly.

Just one deep note that seemed to hang in the air for several seconds.

The next morning, I asked the village headman about it.

He replied:

\> "It rings when someone who should not be here arrives."

I laughed.

That afternoon, an email came in from the head office in Kolkata.

A project officer named Sandeep Verma was coming to Varanpur to inspect the proposed route.

I thought nothing of it.

Verma arrived two days later.

That night, the bell rang again.

Once.

At 2:17.

The following morning, Verma disappeared.

His rental car was found beside the river, the driver's door open. His shoes were still on the floor mat, and there were no footprints in the mud leading away.

His phone was inside, switched on, with no signal.

The police searched for six days.

They found nothing.

Then, on the seventh day, Verma walked into the railway station.

He was completely unharmed.

But he had been missing for eleven years.

The station master assumed it was a joke. Verma insisted it was still 2015.

When someone showed him the date on their phone, 2026, he went pale. He asked why the old ticket counter had been replaced by a machine, and why nobody on the platform was paying with cash.

He then pointed toward the tracks and said:

\> "You haven't built the line yet."

The line was, in fact, already built and running.

Verma refused to explain where he had been.

He gave only one strange account.

He said that after hearing the bell, he had followed a man carrying a lantern into the temple.

Inside, the temple was completely different.

There was no idol.

No walls.

No roof.

Only a railway platform extending into darkness.

And on the platform stood hundreds of people, all silently waiting for a train.

Verma said he recognized several of them.

They were people who had disappeared from Varanpur over the previous twenty years.

Then a train arrived.

It had no windows.

The doors opened.

Everyone boarded except Verma.

Just before the doors closed, an old man grabbed his arm and whispered:

\> "Don't get on. It only takes people who have somewhere to return to."

Then the train left.

Verma said he woke up beside the river.

Eleven years had passed.

Nobody believed him.

Except me.

Because I had kept something Verma didn't know about.

A photograph.

I had taken it on my phone in Varanpur in 2015, shortly before he disappeared.

In the photograph, Verma was standing beside the temple.

Behind him was the bronze bell.

And beside the bell stood an old man holding a lantern.

The same man Verma claimed to have seen on the platform.

The strange part was that the file's timestamp showed I had taken it before Verma disappeared.

And the old man was not a stranger at all.

He was Verma.

The photograph showed him as he would look eleven years later.

After that, I quit the survey.

The temple was demolished in 2016.

The bell was reportedly loaded onto a truck bound for Patna.

But according to the transport company's records, the truck never arrived.

Varanpur itself slowly disappeared from official records.

By 2019, even the digital maps no longer showed it.

Today, there is a village in roughly the same region, but its name is different.

And, so I've heard, there is still a bronze bell somewhere beneath the earth.

People say it rings once every few years.

Always at 2:17 a.m.

And when it does, someone in the village disappears.

Not forever.

Just for eleven years.
