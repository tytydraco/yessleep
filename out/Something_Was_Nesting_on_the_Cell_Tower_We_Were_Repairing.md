The argument started over a hard-boiled egg. Marcus had bought three of them from a gas station that also sold live bait, and Elias was convinced he'd be dead by lunch. We were forty minutes from the job, climbing a narrow road through wet pine woods, when Marcus peeled the second one against the dashboard and told Elias that people his age had weak stomachs because they'd been raised on bottled water and boneless chicken. Elias said that was rich coming from a man who called his wife every time a restaurant put onions on his burger. Marcus said he didn't call her every time, only when the onions were particularly offensive, and I nearly drove us off the road laughing.

Marcus and I had worked towers together for twelve years, through divorces that never happened, vehicles that died at the worst possible time, and one long summer when I thought a sore knee might end my climbing career. He was forty-three, broad through the shoulders, with a nose that had been broken playing basketball and never quite straightened afterward. He complained about everything, especially work that needed doing, and then stayed until it was finished even when nobody asked him to. Elias had been with our crew for eighteen months. He was twenty-seven, a good climber, a better organizer than either of us, and the only person I'd met who could tell Marcus to shut up without starting an argument.

Elias's daughter was seven months old. He'd spent half the drive celebrating her first six uninterrupted hours of sleep. Marcus listened solemnly before asking whether she was available to teach her father how to operate the company expense app. Elias told him he could learn the app himself if he'd stop photographing receipts with half his thumb over the total. That was about when my wife called to remind me our daughter had a science presentation the following morning, and I promised I'd be home in time to help her rehearse. She'd constructed a model of a digestive system that leaked purple water onto our dining table. I told Marcus about it after the call, and he said she had more potential than the engineers who'd designed our truck.

The site was a self-supporting lattice tower, a little under two hundred and forty feet, on a broad rise above a forest access road. The fenced compound contained a prefabricated equipment shelter, a generator enclosure, and enough gravel for the truck to turn around if Elias directed me carefully. We'd been sent to inspect damaged cable protection and replace hardware on an upper mounting assembly after repeated equipment faults. The reports also mentioned debris interfering with one of the cable runs. That wasn't unusual by itself. Birds could make a remarkable mess in a place where nobody disturbed them, and some of the older mounting brackets had enough awkward corners to collect half the forest.

We checked the weather, reviewed the site-specific hazards and rescue plan, and went over the access route before anyone put on a harness. The antenna work had been coordinated with the site operator, and we verified the required shutdown and exposure precautions rather than trusting the note in the work order. Elias read the emergency location from our paperwork into the radio so we all had it. Marcus joked about the formality, then checked his harness and connectors. We used the installed climbing system where it was serviceable, stayed connected when transitioning to work positions, and kept tools tethered. None of that is exciting to describe, although I wish more of what followed could have been prevented by doing it properly.

We found the damaged cable covering almost immediately. Several lengths had been scraped or pulled away, exposing sections of weathered outer jacket that would need inspection and replacement according to the job scope. Above that, near the mounting frame, a gray-brown mass had accumulated between two diagonal braces. From the first angle it looked like a bird's nest packed with insulation. Marcus reached toward it, stopped, and leaned back against his positioning gear. He said it smelled like someone had forgotten a roast chicken in a toolbox.

The smell surprised me because the wind was blowing across the tower, not toward us. It wasn't ordinary decay. There was a warm, yeasty sourness to it, overlaid with something sharp that reminded me of an opened battery, although nothing electrical near the mounting assembly was visibly damaged. The material itself was more organized than a bird's nest. Thick dark strands had been pulled around the braces and wound back through a pale substance hardened into uneven ribs. Small pieces of bone were embedded in it, along with tufts of fur and the torn edge of a blue work glove. I assumed an animal had dragged the scraps up, but that explanation felt less convincing the longer I looked.

Marcus asked whether I wanted him to photograph it for the site report. I said yes and told him not to disturb anything until we understood what was holding the mass in place. The mounting assembly was already exposed enough to examine, so we moved to the other side to check a loosened clamp and the surrounding fasteners. One corner of the debris shifted when the bracket vibrated under our weight. Something pale withdrew from a narrow opening in the material. It was so fast that I saw only a joint, or what I thought was a joint, folding sharply backward.

Marcus had seen it too. He stopped making jokes and said there was an animal inside, which meant we were done working in that area until it could be assessed. I agreed and told Elias to note the obstruction and stand by while we made our way down. Before he could answer, there was a metallic knocking noise from somewhere lower on the structure. It came in three separated impacts that I felt through the brace under my left boot. I thought Elias had struck the steel while shifting equipment, but he came on the radio sounding genuinely puzzled. He asked whether either of us was dropping something.

I told him no. He said something large had come out of the trees on the uphill side of the fence and was moving around the generator enclosure. I asked him what kind of animal. There was a pause, followed by the sound of his boots moving over gravel. Then he said he couldn't tell because it had gone behind the tower legs, and he wanted us to come down as soon as we could. Marcus looked across the latticework at me, and neither of us said anything about the thing moving inside the nest.

I shifted to the descent route and looked down through the open framework. From our height, the gravel pad appeared almost white against the surrounding woods. Elias had retreated toward the truck and was holding his radio up near his mouth. At the western tower leg something pale moved against the steel, only a few feet above the ground. I took it for a strip of fabric snagged on a brace until it wrapped around the leg and tightened. Another pale structure reached up beside it, and an elongated dark mass began lifting between the two.

I told Elias to stay clear of the base and keep moving toward the road. He started to object, then went silent when the mass came fully into view. It was bigger than anything I could have mistaken for an animal native to those woods. The portion I could see was perhaps ten feet long, although the body changed shape as it moved, flattening and drawing up between the braces. There were several sets of limbs, each thick and jointed, with curved gripping surfaces that closed around the angle iron. The upper part of its body was covered in overlapping pale plates, stained the color of old teeth, and darker tissue opened between them each time it stretched.

It didn't climb like a spider. It moved one length of its body at a time, setting three or four grips securely before lifting the next section, and the weight of it produced a dry, rhythmic ringing through the tower. A series of narrow pads along its sides opened and closed as if testing the air or the vibration in the structure. I saw no face that I could recognize. The nearer it came, the clearer it became that the thing did not need to look where it was going in any way I understood. It knew exactly where to put its limbs, even when a brace was concealed behind its own body.

Marcus spoke into the radio first. He told Elias to call emergency services, report an unknown large animal on the structure, and stay away from it. Elias asked whether we were serious. Then one of the creature's limbs reached toward the next cross member and made a sound like a wrench being dragged along a railing, and Elias told us he was calling. I looked for a route down that didn't pass its position. The creature was already occupying the main ladder access below us, and moving out onto the far side of the lattice would have put us in more exposed positions without a reliable path past it. Our best choice was to remain attached and keep the steel between us until we knew what it was doing.

The animal came up through the section beneath Marcus, and the air around us began to smell like the material in the nest. Its limbs were covered with tough, pale ridges that slid against one another when they bent. One gripped a horizontal member, and the whole structure rang beneath the pressure. Another reached across the open space toward Marcus's boot, not striking but probing. Marcus pulled his foot back, trying to turn around the vertical brace while keeping his work positioning steady. The gripping structure closed against the edge of his boot and dragged sideways.

He shouted my name. The next thing I saw was his left foot slipping off the brace and his body dropping out of his work position. His fall-protection system arrested him after a short, violent drop, but he struck the side of the structure on the way down. The sound of his knee or shin hitting steel carried clearly through the tower. He ended up hanging below and slightly outside the brace, one leg folded awkwardly, his hands scrabbling for something solid. The creature withdrew its limb and continued climbing past him.

I called to Marcus, asking whether he could move his hands and whether he felt dizzy. He told me to shut up for half a second and then said he couldn't get his right foot back onto the steel. I could hear him breathing in short bursts. His hanging position was dangerous, and I knew we couldn't leave him in it. I secured myself at a suitable point above him, checked the creature's position, and started working toward a position from which I could assist. Elias was speaking with dispatch below, relaying our situation and the tower's height, and I heard him repeat that the threat was still on the structure with us.

The creature halted at the level of the nest. Several of the pads along its body unfolded, touching the hardened fibers and pressing into the openings. Its upper limbs began working carefully around the loosened mounting frame, drawing strands together. It looked less like an animal trying to defend a territory than something trying to repair a damaged structure, and for several seconds I forgot how close it was to us. Then Marcus made a sound I had never heard him make. It was a thin, frightened gasp, and he told me his right leg had gone numb.

I lowered myself carefully into position and worked with him to get weight back through a foothold and off the harness. He could still move his toes, but the leg hurt badly when he tried to push. It took more effort than I expected, and by the end both of us were shaking. We'd trained for rescues, but not with an enormous unknown animal on the same structure. I wasn't going to drag Marcus across open framework toward it, and I wasn't going to leave him hanging. Between us, with him doing more of the work than he later admitted, we got him into a supported position below the nest and away from the creature's immediate reach.

We'd been sitting there perhaps two minutes when Marcus began picking at the cuff of his right glove. I told him not to worry about it until we could get down, but he shook his head and said something was pinching him. He peeled the glove back far enough to reveal a patch of pale material along the inside of his wrist. At first I thought it was a piece of the nest caught in his sleeve. Then one edge lifted, a narrow joint unfolded from beneath it, and the material took another grip on his skin.

Marcus swore and tried to shake it loose. I caught his arm before he pulled himself out of position, then asked him to hold still while I got a closer look. The thing was about the length of my thumb, thin and flattened, with a flexible central body and six hard little structures arranged along its sides. It had no visible head. Two of the structures were tucked beneath the skin at his wrist, not deep enough to disappear but firmly enough that pulling them lifted the surrounding flesh. I could see the skin rise each time it shifted its grip. The organism's surface seemed to tighten and loosen in time with the muscles beneath it.

Marcus asked whether I could pull it off. I told him I didn't know what kind of damage that would cause, but I'd try to expose the attachment without tearing at it. Using the rounded tip of a plastic cable tool, I lifted one edge while he braced his arm. The thing contracted so suddenly that his fingers closed into a fist. He made a terrible choking noise and knocked my hand away. His little finger stayed bent after the rest of his hand opened.

"I felt that up here," he said, touching his upper arm. His voice was quieter than it should have been, and he kept staring at his hand as if it belonged to someone else. "It wasn't on my skin. It pulled something all the way up to my elbow." I told him to stop touching it and hold his arm comfortably while we worked on getting down. Above us, the big animal was still pressing its limbs into the nest. Marcus looked toward it and asked whether that little thing had come out of the material we'd disturbed. I said it seemed likely, although I didn't know when it had gotten onto him.

We waited while the larger creature moved across the nest, using several of its limbs to draw the loose outer material toward the center. A faint clicking traveled down through the braces as its hard plates opened and closed. It didn't appear to be looking for us anymore. When it shifted to the far side of the upper frame, we began making our way down the route we'd identified, staying attached as we transferred and keeping enough space between us to move without interfering with each other's protection. Marcus tested his injured leg and found he could put some weight through it as long as he kept the knee from twisting. He was angry about how slowly he had to move. I was relieved he could move at all.

The first twenty feet took almost as long as our whole climb had. Marcus's leg shook whenever he put his full weight onto a rung, and twice we stopped so he could catch his breath. His right hand kept tightening around the steel. He would tell me he was ready to move, shift his weight, then find he couldn't let go of the member he was holding. At first I assumed he was frightened of falling again, which would have made sense. Then I watched the tendons in his forearm stand out while he tried to lift two fingers away from the brace. His expression became frantic. He was pulling so hard to open his own hand that his wrist began shaking.

"It's doing that," he said, nodding toward the covered thing beneath his glove. "It's not me holding on." I worked beside him without touching the affected hand, talking him through moving his weight onto his good leg and settling back against his positioning support. His fingers eventually opened, but not because he had relaxed them. I saw a hard ridge travel from the inside of his wrist toward his thumb, and the hand released when the ridge passed. He watched it happen too. For the first time in twelve years of working with him, Marcus said he didn't want me to look at him.

A gust of wind moved through the forest and brought the smell of rain. Elias told us the sky was getting darker to the north, although the weather report still showed no nearby storm. I was trying to work out whether we'd have time to complete a safe assisted descent before conditions changed when Marcus suddenly gripped my sleeve with his unaffected hand. He said the big creature was moving. I looked up and couldn't see it. I asked how he knew. He pressed the knuckles of his right hand against a brace, closed his eyes, and told me there were four heavy movements above us, then a pause, then another two.

A moment later, I heard the steel ring. The sound came in exactly the pattern he'd described. Marcus opened his eyes and looked at me, horrified by his own accuracy. I told him vibration could travel farther through a structure than people realized, which was true, but I'd been standing beside him and had felt nothing until he spoke. He looked at the upper lattice again. "It's over the nest," he said. "It keeps going around the broken part." I asked whether he could see it. He shook his head and whispered that he could feel where it was putting its weight.

We worked down another section with Elias tracking our progress from below. Marcus managed a joke about paying him to carry us, then stopped laughing when he found two narrow ridges moving beneath his sleeve near the elbow. One tightened as he tried to straighten his arm; the other stayed taut, holding his hand half closed. He looked at me, waiting for an explanation I couldn't give, and I said the medics needed to see it as soon as we reached the ground.

"Tell me something," he said after a while. "You know that little shop off the highway, the one that repairs generators?" I said I knew it. He told me he'd interviewed there the previous week. He'd been offered a job servicing commercial equipment, and the pay was almost as good as what he made climbing. I asked why he hadn't mentioned it. Marcus reached up and rubbed his face with his left hand. "Because you were going to make some speech about how old we are and how you'd have to train another idiot," he said. "I had the speech ready to throw back at you, and then I never found a good time."

I told him he was an idiot if he'd turned down work that let him go home every night. He said he hadn't turned it down yet. His son had started answering his messages again, and he wanted to be around when the boy had time to meet him for dinner. He spoke about the interview with the same irritation he'd used to describe the gas station eggs, as if wanting a different life were a minor inconvenience. I asked whether he'd told his wife. He said he'd been waiting until he had something definite, then added that he'd have to find a less embarrassing way to explain why he'd spent twelve years hanging off towers with me. I told him I would write the resignation letter myself and spell everything incorrectly.

He laughed, and for a moment I heard the Marcus I knew. The sound stopped when his right hand tightened around a brace hard enough to make a small metallic click. He looked down at it with the patience of a man watching a misbehaving tool, then the patience vanished. His thumb was bent across his palm and the skin around his wrist had drawn tight. He told me he couldn't feel the little organism anymore, only the pressure of the steel against his hand. When he tried to pull away, a faint scraping came from beneath his glove. We both saw the outer edge of the glove lift, although he hadn't moved his fingers.

We paused again when Marcus suddenly asked whether the small things in the nest were protected from the wind. He immediately sounded angry at himself and told me he didn't know why he cared. I reminded him that one of those things was injuring him, and he nodded, but his eyes kept returning to the unseen nesting site.

Halfway down, Marcus lost his left foothold when his right hand seized without warning. He didn't fall far, but his injured knee twisted as he recovered, and he cried out so sharply that I nearly moved before confirming my own connection and balance. His right hand remained locked against the brace. The flesh of his palm had thickened around it, and I could see a narrow, pale ridge bridging the gap between two fingers. It looked as though the hand had begun making its own gripping surface. Marcus stared at it and started breathing too fast. I told him to look at me, not his hand, and helped him settle while we worked out how to free it without pulling against the new tissue.

He said, very quietly, that he could feel his shoulder changing. I asked him where. He indicated the area beneath his right shoulder blade and said it hurt whenever he tried to stand straight. I reached around his back, keeping my own weight secure, and felt a hard shape moving beneath his shirt. It wasn't a loose piece of equipment. It shifted when he breathed, pushing my fingertips outward. Marcus asked what I'd found. I said there was swelling and we had to keep moving, and he looked at me with an expression that told me he'd heard everything I wasn't saying.

He began crying while we were still suspended there. It wasn't a dramatic breakdown, and he didn't make a speech. He turned his head toward the forest, wiped his face awkwardly with his left sleeve, and said he didn't want his wife or son seeing him this way. I told him we weren't going to decide anything about that until he reached the ground and somebody qualified examined him. He nodded, then asked me to promise I wouldn't leave him alone with the animal if it came back. I said I wouldn't leave him unless staying meant we both died. He considered that and told me it was the most honest thing I'd said all day.

At the next stop, I found Marcus pressing the underside of his right arm against a steel upright. His eyes were closed. For one frightened moment I thought he'd lost consciousness, but he opened them when I spoke and said the pain had stopped. I asked whether the numbness had spread. He shook his head and said he could feel everything, including things he didn't know how to name. Then he placed his left hand on the upright beside the right, leaned toward it, and made a small, involuntary noise of relief. He looked ashamed when he saw me watching. "It feels better like this," he said. "That's all I mean."

We completed the last stretch slowly, stopping twice to check his footing before we reached the gravel. Elias met us at the foot of the tower. He had the medical kit open and had cleared space in the truck while keeping the route to the gate unobstructed. The moment Marcus's boots reached the gravel, he bent forward and nearly fell. Elias caught him from one side while I supported the other, and the three of us staggered away from the lowest braces. Marcus was laughing in a thin, breathless way that made him sound almost drunk. He said he planned to write a detailed complaint about the lack of an elevator, then vomited into the gravel beside the truck.

We helped him sit sideways on the passenger seat with his injured leg supported. Elias had brought water and a clean cloth, and he put both where Marcus could reach them. The adult creature remained somewhere above us, hidden by the tower structure, and for the first time in hours I couldn't hear it moving. I told Elias to keep the truck between us and the tower while I checked Marcus. We had both survived the climb, and the simple fact of having gravel beneath my boots felt so good that I had to lean against the vehicle for a moment. My hands were shaking hard enough to rattle the zipper on my jacket.

Marcus asked whether there was any more coffee. Elias handed him the travel mug, which had gone cold, and Marcus took a careful sip with his left hand. He made a face and said it tasted as though somebody had strained it through a sock. Elias informed him that the sock had cost extra. Marcus told us he was going to take that repair-shop job and spend the rest of his working life complaining about employees who didn't clean the coffee pot. I believed him. Now there was room for a doctor, an ambulance, and the ordinary systems we'd been trying to reach all afternoon.

Then Marcus put the coffee down and said he couldn't move his right shoulder. I leaned toward him, expecting another spasm. He was gripping the steel frame beside the passenger seat, and his thumb had folded so far into his palm that the nail was pressing against skin. A thick ridge traveled beneath his sleeve toward his elbow. I could see the motion in the cloth, like a taut cable being drawn through a narrow tube. Marcus tried to speak and made a short, high sound instead. Something on the back of his shoulder lifted against the fabric of his shirt, then settled into a new position with a muffled crack that I felt through the door frame.

Elias moved toward him with the medical kit. Marcus kicked at him with his good foot, not hard enough to injure him but suddenly enough that Elias jumped back. I grabbed the edge of the door and told Marcus to stop moving. He stared at us, horrified, and began apologizing before I could say anything else. His right hand remained locked around the frame. Beneath his skin, two pale ridges had spread from the wrist into the back of the hand, and I could see the juvenile organism still attached near the base of his thumb. Its body had become flatter and darker, almost flush with him, as though the boundary between the two was disappearing.

"Please," Marcus said. His eyes were filling with tears, and he was trying to pull his hand away from the truck without using his other arm. "Don't let it do this out here. Please get me somewhere people know what they're doing." I told him the ambulance was coming and we'd move him as soon as the medics could assess him safely. Elias stepped back toward the road to flag them in. Marcus watched him go, then looked up toward the tower with a concentration so complete that I knew he wasn't listening to me anymore. He whispered that something upstairs was frightened, and he sounded genuinely worried about it.

I told Elias to bring the truck's first-aid scissors and that we'd cut away the sleeve so the medics could see exactly what had attached itself. Marcus nodded and held his right arm out as far as he could. The cuff had become tight around his wrist, and the cloth was pressed into strange shallow grooves along his forearm. Elias knelt beside him, careful not to touch the organism, and started cutting upward along the seam. Marcus watched the blades and began whispering that he didn't want to hurt anybody. I thought he meant the thing attached to him until his left hand struck the side of Elias's head and drove him against the open door.

The movement was too quick for me to stop. Marcus caught Elias across the shoulders and pulled him partly into the passenger compartment, pinning his arm against the door frame. Elias screamed, and I saw the skin above his wrist turn white where it was trapped. Marcus's altered hand had closed around the loose fabric of Elias's jacket, not his body, but the material was wound between the new hard ridges along his palm. He was pulling with his entire shoulder, turning his body toward the tower as though he meant to drag Elias with him. I grabbed Elias beneath the other arm and tried to get him free. Marcus looked straight at me and begged me to stop him.

I could feel his strength through Elias. The passenger door was against my back, and the three of us were wedged together so tightly that I couldn't get enough room to work. Elias managed to twist his trapped hand sideways, and Marcus's fingers tightened again. There was a small, sickening crack near Elias's wrist. He cried out, and Marcus responded by pulling harder, as though the sound had urged him on. Then he saw Elias's face and made a noise of such naked grief that I will never stop hearing it.

"It's Elias," I said. I was trying to keep my voice steady because Marcus was watching my mouth and seemed able to follow only one thing at a time. "Look at him. You have to let him go." Marcus shook his head violently, then closed his eyes and began forcing his affected hand open with the other. The pale ridges resisted. One tore through the skin at the base of his thumb, and his fingers finally spread enough for Elias to wrench himself free. We stumbled backward together, leaving Marcus alone beside the truck. He looked at the blood running down his hand and whispered an apology that Elias could barely hear.

While Elias crawled clear and held his injured wrist, Marcus climbed out of the passenger seat without standing up properly. His injured leg dragged behind him, and his hands were planted against the door and the edge of the truck bed. There was a rigid shape beneath his shirt, running from the right shoulder toward the base of his neck. He kept trying to lift his torso upright, but each attempt drew another contraction through his back. I took a step toward him and he told me to stay where I was. He said he didn't know what his arms were going to do next, and I believed him.

Then the fabric across his back split. Nothing burst out in a spray of blood, and for a second it was almost worse because I could see how controlled the movement was. Two pale structures had emerged along the ridge of his shoulder, not separate arms but hard extensions built over his existing muscles, pulling the skin tight as they slid against one another. His right shoulder rotated forward, and he gave a long, strangled cry. I saw the outline of his collarbone shift beneath his neck and heard him try to breathe through clenched teeth. The transformation had not made him larger so much as changed where the parts of him could bear weight. He was becoming built to cling to something.

His hand found the open truck door again. The new gripping ridges hooked around the edge of the metal, and the door groaned under the angle of his weight. Marcus looked down at the way his fingers were holding it and began sobbing. He said it didn't hurt when he used them like that. He sounded ashamed, as if relief were something he was choosing. Then he asked whether Elias was all right, and when I told him Elias needed a hospital, he said he'd never forgive himself. A moment later his attention shifted toward the tower, and a look of almost tender concern came over his face. He asked whether the nest was still open.

I told him the larger animal was attending to it and that there was nothing for him to do. He became furious, telling me I didn't understand what was happening up there, then stopped in the middle of the sentence and covered his mouth with his left hand. His eyes went wide. "I can't tell which thoughts are mine," he said. "I can hear you telling me to stay here, and I know you're right, but there's something up there that feels like my kid." He started shaking his head, trying to dislodge the thought itself. "I know it isn't my kid. I know that."

We could hear a siren somewhere along the access road now. It rose and fell between the trees, close enough that Elias began waving his good arm toward the gate. I wanted to get Marcus into the ambulance, let trained people make decisions, and stop being responsible for everything. But he was moving toward the tower, first awkwardly and then with a dreadful kind of efficiency. His feet scarcely supported him. He used the truck's bodywork, a low steel barrier, and finally the gravel itself, taking his weight through the altered surfaces of his right hand and shoulder. He was crawling in the direction of the thing that had hurt him, and I couldn't tell how much of that movement he still controlled.

I stepped between him and the nearest tower leg. Marcus stopped a few feet away, bent so low that his head was nearly level with my stomach. I could see the shape of the juvenile against his forearm, flattened into the surrounding tissue. Its little gripping structures opened and closed whenever he moved. I told him his son was expecting to see him, that he'd wanted the new job, and that none of this had to be decided beside a tower in the middle of nowhere. Marcus looked up at me, and I saw the old familiar irritation on his face. "Get out of the way, Adam," he said. "I don't want to put my hands on you."

I stayed there. He tried to turn around me and his right arm swung outward with enough force that I felt air move against my jacket. I stumbled back toward Elias, and Marcus caught the tower leg with one hand. The sound of his new grip closing around the steel was almost delicate, a series of small hard taps followed by a tightening creak. Then he pulled himself upward. His injured knee hung uselessly, and his left foot dragged against the brace, but the new structures along his arm and shoulder took his weight. I watched a man with whom I'd climbed thousands of feet of steel begin climbing in a way no human body should have been able to endure.

He stopped perhaps ten feet above us. I thought the pain had finally overcome him, but he was looking down at Elias. His face had gone gray with exhaustion, and every breath moved the new ridges beneath his shirt. "I hurt him," he said, and I told him that Elias was alive and getting help. Marcus pressed his forehead against the steel. He asked me to tell his son he'd been trying to come home. Then a shudder passed through his body, his right hand tightened, and he began ascending again.

I could have followed him onto the ladder. My equipment was still on, and the instinct to climb after a coworker in trouble was so deeply practiced that my hand went to the first brace before I knew what I was doing. Then Elias called my name. He was on his knees, struggling to stand with one arm hanging uselessly, and the ambulance crew had reached the gate. One of the responders was shouting questions while another took in the sight of Marcus climbing the outside of the structure. Above them, the pale adult moved around the nest, its weight producing a slow, familiar ringing through the steel. I stayed on the ground and helped Elias toward the medics.

I watched Marcus climb for another minute. At the first wide brace below the upper assembly, he stopped and tried to turn around. He moved one hand from the structure, then the other arm seized and pulled him back against it. He began shouting that he couldn't make himself come down. The adult was no longer moving toward him. It was waiting near the nest, using its limbs to open a gap in the woven material. Marcus pulled himself toward that gap with movements that became smaller and more frantic as he approached, like a person trying to resist walking through an open door. I heard him call my name once, clearly, before the upper framework hid him from view.

The emergency crews didn't simply drive away. They established a safe area, called for specialist assistance, and tried to work out whether there was anyone on the structure they could reach. The first paramedic who saw Marcus climb asked whether he'd been wearing equipment to assist him. I showed her the blood on the truck, the damaged door, and the torn glove still caught beneath the passenger seat. She looked at Elias's wrist and my own scraped hands, then looked back at the tower without saying anything. Later, two responders heard a man calling from above, but neither could locate him through the braces. Nobody attempted a blind climb into the reach of the enormous animal moving around the upper assembly.

I gave statements that afternoon and again the next morning. Elias spent the night at a hospital with a fracture near his wrist and injuries to his shoulder. I had bruises, torn skin on my hands, and a strained back, none of which explained what had happened to Marcus. There were photographs of the damaged equipment, pictures of the altered material, and footage recorded from the ground by one of the responders. It wasn't good footage. The tower was too tall, and the creature was mostly concealed behind its structure, but it showed pale limbs moving where there should not have been an animal at all. I don't know how the people reviewing it explained what they saw.

I spoke to Marcus's wife before anyone else from work got to her. She asked whether he was dead, and I told her I didn't know. That was the truth, although saying it made me feel like a coward. His son came to the house later and sat beside her while I described as much as I could without pretending I understood the rest. He was a grown man with his father's narrow eyes and the same habit of pressing his lips together before he asked a difficult question. He wanted to know whether Marcus had been frightened. I said yes, and I told him that even near the end his father had recognized us and tried not to hurt anyone.

A week later, I returned Marcus's lunch bag to his wife. She opened it while I stood in the kitchen, and inside were a crushed sandwich, two unopened packets of mustard, and a little plastic container full of pickles. She shook her head and laughed once before she began crying. Marcus had been buying those ridiculous pickles for years because his son used to steal them from his lunch when he was little. I hadn't known that. I had spent twelve years listening to him complain about the price of groceries and the state of everybody else's driving, and somehow I'd never asked why he always packed the same thing.

I haven't climbed since that job. Elias doesn't know whether he'll return to the trade, and I don't blame him either way. People have asked me what I think happened to Marcus, whether the creature was changing him into another one of its kind or using his body for something else. I can't answer that. I know the organism on his wrist changed the way his muscles worked, that he began caring about the nest as if the living things inside it belonged to him, and that he was conscious enough to be ashamed of hurting Elias. I also know that the last clear thing I heard him say from the tower was that he couldn't make himself come down.

My daughter still has the digestive-system model in her room, repaired with the glue I bought two days late. Sometimes she'll ask why I can't go back to the job I used to love, and I tell her I'm not ready yet. I could explain the height, or the creature, or what I saw happening beneath my friend's skin, but none of those is the part I have the most trouble living with. It's the memory of Marcus looking down at us from that steel brace, terrified and asking me to bring him home, while his hands kept carrying him farther away. I know what it feels like to stand on the ground where you're supposed to be safe and watch someone you love disappear into a place you cannot reach.
