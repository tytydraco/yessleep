I’m writing this from inside my car in a 7-Eleven parking lot. My son is in the passenger seat, buckled in, wrapped in his Power Rangers blanket. I haven’t been back inside the house since yesterday morning, and I’m not going back.

I want to get this down while I still have the nerve, and because, if you have a child who talks about a voice in the vents, I need you to do what I didn’t.

Let me back up.

My son, Eli, is six. His mother passed away back in 2024, a year after my father, Eli's Papa so it’s just been the two of us. Eli still talks about him as if he might drop by any day, same with his mother. In March, we moved into this little farmhouse to be closer to my work, but far enough out in the country that we wouldn’t be bothered by neighbors. The place was over a hundred years old, with a basement I never used, but it had central air. It was the kind that ran through the walls, with vents low to the floor, maybe a foot across, give or take.

Eli, who refused to sleep without his blanket, sweated like a hog in his sleep. His mother would call him her little piggy in a blanket. The poor child ran hotter than a furnace in a southern summer, but if you even tried to remove his covers, all hell broke loose. So, being the careful and considerate father that I am, I placed his bed next to the vent, and every night I turned the A/C down low to keep him from overheating.

It started in June. I woke up at 2 a.m. to find Eli standing by the side of my bed, blanket in hand.

He wasn’t crying. As long as he had his blanket, he never cried. The last time I’d seen tears run down his face was when I told him Mommy wouldn’t be coming home.

“Eli, what are you doing? How long have you been standing there?”

He looked happy, proud even.

“I’ve been standing here since the clock said one, two—” He thought about the next number for a moment. “Nine!”

He was still practicing his numbers. The doctors had told me not to be too concerned about his development, as trauma can often set a child back, but only temporarily.

The clock on my nightstand said 2:01. He had been standing there for half an hour.

“That’s good, little buddy. Why didn’t you wake me?” I sat up, pulling my sheets high up to fight the shiver building in my bones.

“I wanted to see how heavy a sleeper you are.” He laughed.

“Why?” I was laughing with him. Eli had always been a silly child.

“The new mommy asked me to.” He smiled as my stomach fell.

My eyes, moments ago barely able to stay open, snapped wide in an instant.

“Eli, who is new mommy?”

“I don’t know. I could only hear her.” He shrugged.

I picked him up and looked him over. I turned on the lights and told him to wait in my room until I came back. I locked the door behind me and walked to his room. The lights were off, his door wide open. The windows were locked shut, the closet closed. I walked over and fought all the fear in my body before twisting the doorknob open.

*Empty.*

I walked around the house flipping on all the lights, checking to make sure the doors were all locked. They were. I checked the A/C and saw that I had forgotten to lower it for the evening. I adjusted the thermostat and heard the hum throughout the house, followed by a distant, shallow cough. It was quiet enough that I thought my nerves had gotten to me. I walked back to my room and asked Eli to open the door for me.

“Is everything okay, Daddy?” he asked.

“Yes,” I said, nodding. “Let’s get you tucked back into bed. I’m going to have to wrap you up tighter if you keep trying to wake me up!” I laughed. This child had the craziest imagination sometimes.

I laid him back in bed and noticed him looking at the A/C vent.

“Those things make some funny noises sometimes, huh?”

He laughed and agreed with me. “That’s what the new mommy told me!”

I laughed nervously and rubbed his head.

“Well, tell new mommy I still miss old mommy, and we don’t have room for her right now.” It hadn’t occurred to me that my son might yearn for a mother, but we had both neglected the need for a female touch around the house. It was easy for things to start to fall into disarray with two bachelors living here.

I tucked the blanket under his chin. “Go to sleep, buddy,” I said, the way my father used to say it to me. “Daddy’s right here.”

Over the next few weeks, things started going missing in the kitchen.

It was never anything dramatic: sleeves of crackers, half a loaf of bread, cans of beans, that kind of thing. It was always just enough to leave me doubting myself. I had been working overtime, and Eli had been home with the babysitter. I figured I’d eaten more than I remembered, or that Stephanie had helped herself to some snacks, which I’d given her full permission to do. I thought that for about a month, until one morning I found the pantry door open and the bottom shelf cleaned completely out. Upon closer inspection, I could see a breadcrumb trail leading right to my son’s room. I chuckled. I actually chuckled.

*Eli’s been snacking late. Guess I need to child-proof my snacks…*

But when Eli told me he hadn’t eaten anything—and don’t cross me, Eli’s a good kid and finds lying repulsive—I figured it must be mice. I put on my Sherlock Holmes detective glasses and began inspecting his room for mouse holes. The best thing I could come up with was the A/C vent.

I bought mousetraps and went over the safety rules and the importance of catching the varmints with Eli. I told him he must not eat the food I put on the traps because it was poison. He understood.

Still, in the morning, every trap was sprung. Not a single dead mouse, not a single dab of peanut butter left on the traps. I stood there holding a trap in my hand, thinking that either this mouse had the elusiveness of Jerry from the old show, or I wasn’t dealing with a mouse at all.

That night, passing Eli’s room on my way to bed, I heard humming. A woman, low and muffled and a little off-key, humming the way my wife used to while she folded laundry. For one heartbeat it was her. My whole body knew it before my head could catch up. Then the A/C rumbled on and drowned it out. Pipes, I told myself. Or grief. People hear their dead all the time. I stood there until my hands stopped shaking, and then I went to bed.

Meanwhile, Eli told me everything. Every single day he told me, and all I did was nod my head and smile the way every parent does when their child talks about the bunny who cooks chili dogs or the wiener dog who performs ballet: a child with a very active imagination. I was relieved. His pediatrician had warned me that kids who lose a parent often invent imaginary companions, and I took it as a good sign. It seemed to me that Eli was finally done processing the death of his mother and could move on to normal kid behavior, even if it wasn’t a cooking bunny or a dancing dog. Even if it was the new mommy who lived in the wall.

You see, new mommy was hungry. Another relief: now I knew where all my food had been going. Eli hadn’t been lying about the snacks, I decided. In his head, new mommy was the one eating them. He said her voice was soft and stretched out, like someone talking through a pillow. He said new mommy liked asking him questions, and that when he didn’t know the answer, her voice would get real deep, almost manlike. New mommy would ask when I went to bed and what time I left for work. New mommy even asked Eli to bring her things: snacks, pictures, a spare key.

Mind you, this wasn’t all in one conversation. It was sprinkled out to me over the span of weeks, maybe months.

“Well, it’s a good thing new mommy has a key, because with my hours I’m starting to really let this place go.” I laughed. Eli didn’t. “What’s wrong, buddy?”

“I don’t like new mommy anymore.” He looked down, kicking his feet.

“And why is that?” I asked.

“New mommy said she was happy old mommy had cancer. New mommy said old mommy burned inside out from the chemo, and now she’s burning outside in from the flames where she belongs."

Something cold went through me. I told myself it was grief talking, a little boy giving his worst fear a voice. I paused, got real stern with my boy, and said, “Eli, I think you should stop talking to new mommy. If you hear her voice, just ignore it. If you get scared, you can come sleep with me, okay? And your mom isn’t burning, not anymore. She’s Catholic, remember? She’s with Mother Mary and Jesus.”

He agreed to stop talking to new mommy and went quiet after that. Looking back, that was the day he stopped telling me things. I thought the problem had been solved.

Two days ago, I finally got some time off work and focused on cleaning the house. Since my wife passed, I had really let the place go.

While I was vacuuming Eli’s room, I noticed that something looked off about the vent cover. I couldn’t place it right away, and as I made my passes, I kept thinking about it. It was as if it were watching me vacuum up crumbs and debris. Then it dawned on me: the screws looked brand new! Like I mentioned earlier, this house is over a hundred years old, and most of the hardware is painted over. The screws keeping the vent in place looked almost new. When I ran my vacuum over the vent, it began to make noise, the kind vacuums make when they suddenly have a lot to suck up. I stopped for a moment and inspected further. Metal shavings had fallen from the vent, and the screws looked like they had been loosened.

I feel like an absolute dumbass for not putting the pieces together. I should have called someone at that moment and told them my suspicions, but the idea was too outlandish. Instead, I grabbed my screwdriver and tightened up the vent, chalking it up to the previous owners. I clasped my hands together and admired my handiwork as the raspy breath of the A/C kicked on.

That same night, I woke up around 1 a.m. to get some water. I could hear Eli down the hall. He was talking, very low, very quiet. He wasn’t crying or arguing, just murmuring, the way any child does when talking about their day.

I stopped outside his door. He was kneeling low, his face close to the slats of the vent.

“No, I can’t talk to new mommy anymore. Daddy said so, but I haven’t told him about you, Papa.”

Papa was what Eli called my father. My father had been dead for years.

There was a long pause.

A sound came from the vent. It was a low, wet little huff, wheezing as it cut off. It took me a second to realize what I was hearing.

It was laughter.

I stood there in the dark, shaking, covering my mouth with my hand. I did the most cowardly thing I could have done that night. I walked back to my room, lay down, and told myself it was the pipes acting up again, or that Eli was just using his imagination again and playing both parts of his little game. I probably told myself a hundred things, all lies, as I avoided the obvious, if unlikely, truth. I stared at the ceiling fan, listening to the hum of the A/C, listening to the laughter of the man in my vents, until the sun came up.

Eli didn’t want breakfast the next morning.

He sat at the table, pushing his cereal around the bowl with his spoon, and said, quiet and sleepy, “He says he’s tired of the vents.”

I asked him what he meant.

“He says it’s too small now. He says he was almost out, but you tightened the screws. I don’t know how to help him.” His eyes were red. He had been up all night too. “He says he doesn’t want to scare me like new mommy did. He only wants to be in the house like a family.”

Something in my face must have told Eli that I was all ears, because he began to speed up as he talked.

He said the man had been in the walls since before we moved in. He said the man used to talk to the little girl who lived here before. He said the man sounds different sometimes because he is good at voices. And he said, and this is the sentence that ruptured the skin of denial I’d been wearing, “Sometimes he sounds like Mommy.”

I thought of the humming in the hallway.

I got my keys.

I didn’t pack anything. I picked up Eli, grabbed his blanket from behind the chair, and was halfway to the door when I stopped. I thought maybe I was being irrational. I was letting my fears get to me. I needed proof before I made such a drastic decision. I needed something I could show a cop so they wouldn’t think I was bonkers.

I set Eli down on the couch by the front door, wrapped his blanket around him, and told him to count to a hundred out loud and not to stop until I came back. He started at one.

So I went down the hall to Eli’s room with my phone flashlight on in one hand and a screwdriver in the other. I walked over to his bed and crouched down by the vent cover, removing each screw one at a time.

From the living room, I could hear Eli counting, a little too loud, the way he did when he was scared.

The duct behind it was way too wide.

The metal had been cut and peeled back, enlarged, folded neatly out of the way, and it went back much further than any duct in this little house should, then sloped down out of my light, toward the basement I had never once opened. I inspected the dust at the mouth and saw impressions. Hands. Knees. Elbows. The long smears of someone who spent a great deal of time crawling. I leaned in to my shoulders and swept the light ahead of me.

Eli skipped from nineteen to thirty and kept going.

There was a pile of wrappers back there. Bread bags, cracker sleeves, empty potato chip bags, you name it.

A few feet in, the duct opened into a hollow, just big enough for a grown man to sit up in. In the middle of it, lined up neatly like a shrine, were things I’d lost since we moved in and never thought twice about. My father’s reading glasses. Eli’s baby tooth in a tiny plastic bag. The spare house key. My wife’s hair clip.

The key had been missing since July, and I had never even looked for it. The clip I had looked for. I’d torn the house apart for it in the spring, and I’d sat on the bathroom floor and cried when I couldn’t find it.

Next to the clip lay one thing that wasn’t ours: a faded pink hair ribbon, small enough for a little girl.

Down the hall, Eli had stopped counting.

Suddenly, the A/C shuddered to life. Somewhere deep in the darkness of the cool duct, I could hear raspy breathing. Then, in my own father’s voice, I heard it say, “Go to sleep, buddy.” It was warm, quiet, a little tired, exactly how he had sounded after a long day. “Daddy’s right here.”

Then, in the same voice, much closer:

“Daddy’s always right here.”

I felt the wetness of a tongue slide up my ear.

I shot up and banged my head on the roof of the duct. Something scraped away from me in the dark, fast.

I don’t remember the next ten seconds, not really. I remember flashes. The basement door in the hallway, the one I had never once opened, standing wide, the dark behind it breathing cold air onto my ankles. Eli on the couch with the blanket twisted in his fists, the count stopped somewhere in the forties. The keys already in my hand. Somehow I managed to get Eli, his Power Rangers blanket intact, and myself into our car and down the driveway, listening to the soft crunch of the wheels over gravel.

We’ve been driving since yesterday. I called the police from a rest stop, and they’re sending someone to check the house. The dispatcher was a kind lady, but she clearly didn’t believe a word I said once I mentioned my father’s voice. My dead father’s voice.

So here’s why I’m posting this.

Parents. Listen to your kids.

I didn’t know what to do. I didn’t bring my wallet, so there was no ID and no motel. I’ve been paying for gas with my phone, and last night we slept in the car. I’m shaking because I don’t think the cops are going to find a thing. Eli’s been up for about twenty minutes, quiet, staring out the window. I asked him what was wrong, as if we didn’t have a plethora of things that were wrong. A minute before I decided to type this up, he told me, with doe eyes, as if he were sorry to be the one to tell me. He told me this:

“Dad? He says we shouldn’t have left the house.”

I asked him what he meant and how he knew that.

He pointed at the dashboard. At the vent. It’s turned off. I’ve had it off since yesterday. It’s making a soft sound. It sounds like breathing.
