Okay, since I last checked in with ya’ll some minor events have occurred. One, I’ve managed to get some more accounts together from my colleagues. It seems I’m not the only one looking to blow the whistle on Blackthorn. Two, I may have been slightly careless in how I handled my previous post and subsequent digging into Blackthorn. It doesn’t feel dire yet, but I feel like I’m being watched. For example, when I came back to my computer to begin writing this update it was locked. When I’m sure I shut it down before I left. Little discrepancies like that, but I’m most likely just being paranoid.

After asking around as surreptitiously as possible, I was put into contact with an arachnologist. Understandably he didn’t want his name shared, though he did ask that I refer to him as Simon for the purposes of this post. I’m not sure why, but I saw no harm in it. 

Simon’s first few years at Blackthorn had gone better than mine. His credentials had landed him higher in the ranks initially and he barely had to spend time in the field. When he did, it was in the less dangerous areas of the forest closer to our facility. This also meant that he knew what he was getting into most of the time. As the atypical life in those areas were usually low enough priorities that their files could be accessed by fresher personnel. 

Simon’s account comes from later in his tenure. He was in the lab full time by this point and researching ways to safely control the range of *Theraphosa fecundum*. A large spider that holds the general shape of a Goliath Birdeater, but with some fundamental differences to the tarantula you’re familiar with. The females of *T. fecundum*, or spider-ticks as the higher ranking field operatives call them, are the size of a dinner plate with rough leathery skin in place of the usual hard carapace. They possess no hair and exaggerated fangs. The males are much smaller, mite sized spiders that often live on the skin of the females. They get their formal name from their uncanny life cycle. Having mating intervals that range 7 to 23 days, but under high duress they have been able to shorten this interval to as little as 3 days. As in, the females will mate, gestate, then lay an egg during that interval. Although pushing gestation that quickly has been shown to increase chances for genetic mutations, most often resulting in birth defects. Frustratingly it seems like there is no developmental period for this ability. They are sexually mature after hatching, and begin this cycle immediately. Due to the male’s size, it is currently unknown how the mating process occurs, or even how additional males are generated as all observed eggs contain females. 

They only lay a single egg per mating period, but that egg is nigh guaranteed to reach adulthood. Their eggs prove highly resistant to fire, pressure, impact, and chemical means of destruction. The freshly hatched young retain some of this resilience, but not all. Spiderlings of *T. fecundum* have an incredibly thick carapace opposed to their adult forms. It’s only after their first molt that they obtain the leathery skin that characterizes them. The average time it takes for them to reach that first molt has been deduced to around 3 months. But as of now, they are only vulnerable as adults. The one saving grace that is preventing them from rampant overpopulation is that the hard carapace of the juveniles prevents them from laying their large eggs, forcing them to hold onto their first generation until reaching full size. The adults are also highly preyed upon by other fauna within the forest. Though many predators will choose to only eat their legs and cephalothorax, not wanting to risk ingesting an egg. We are unsure if this is a learned behavior or an inherited one.

Simon explained that the main problem with this species was its possibility to invade regions outside of Blackthorn. It wasn’t particularly deadly, earning its common name from its propensity to feed like ticks. Using its modified fangs and pedipalps to burrow into then drain blood from a host. Due to their size they are able to gorge themselves on up to a pint of blood before detaching. Ending up looking like grotesque balloons before the affair is over. One by itself is enough to make a human woozy or lightheaded. But it wouldn’t take many before they could exsanguinate an entire creature. Although they seem to prefer not to do this, as it removes potential food sources. Doing so only when the creature poses a threat to their colony. Oh, did I mention these things live in groups? They will often seek out each other and form groups numbering up to 20. They form nest-like structures in natural or manmade underground areas such as caves or mines. Venturing out to feed on sleeping animals at night. Then returning once full. 

Simon believed the work they were doing in order to keep *T. fecundum* restrained to Blackthorn was essential. I honestly can’t fault him or anyone for believing the same. It would truly be a nightmare if they were to get out. It seemed his superiors agreed as well. Realizing that their unpredictable nature made them poor candidates for weaponization. So research continued. Materials and properties testing on the eggs and carapaces of the spiderlings yielded little in terms of useful results. The few things capable of taking them out would have been just as if not more destructive to the environment they lived in. Physical barriers were erected around Blackthorn to keep these specific arachnids in. That and careful habits when leaving and entering premises were all they could do to keep them contained. Eventually, testing turned to looking for ways to mitigate damage that they were capable of causing. Specific garments that could prevent one from latching, repellents, anything that Blackthorn could produce (and sell) if their efforts to retain these creatures failed. It was for these efforts that they began human testing.

After some coaxing, I was able to get some of the most notable testing logs from Simon. He did warn me though, something about specific tags on the files that might flag the trackers in our internal computers. I supposed I would be able to explain my curiosity in *T. fecundum* as a possible joint research project for our departments. I’ll transcribe a couple of the logs below.

Item test log #384

Item: Pheromone based repellent for *T*. *fecundum*.

Result: Failure

Experiment and Results:

2 subjects are introduced into the enclosure containing specimen #826. The first is coated in a solution of the experimental pheromone. The second is coated in water as a control. The specimen remains in the corner of the enclosure it had been in prior to the introduction of the subjects. The subjects are instructed to make themselves comfortable in the furniture provided. Subject 1 does so. Subject 2 expresses dissent. 

“I don’t see the point in myself being here if the point is to test the pheromone. Wouldn’t I be a distraction?”

The researcher observing the test replies over the intercom.

“Our experimental design is sound, no further input please. In the event of a successful feeding just stay calm. It physically cannot consume enough blood to harm you on its own. It will only cause discomfort.”

Subject 2 huffs and takes the seat farthest from the specimen, but still within 2 feet of subject 1. 

The specimen begins to investigate the two subjects. Keeping clear of subject 1 while approaching subject 2. It climbs the leg of the subject before coming to rest on his thigh, feeling with its fangs, possibly searching for a suitable vein. Despite this, subject 2 remains uncharacteristically stoic. Staring down the cameras observing them.

Before the specimen can bite, subject 1 coughs. The specimen quickly turns around. Eying subject 1. It scurries to where it heard the offending sound. Becoming visibly agitated once on subject 1. It appears the pheromone serves more to annoy the specimen than outright deterring it. Once it is on his chest, subject 1 loses composure. He attempts to swat at and remove the specimen. Already agitated, the specimen seems to grow slightly before quickly biting into the chest of the subject then just as quickly removing itself and hiding in the corner of the room.

Operatives are sent to remove subject 2, recapture the specimen, and assess subject 1’s injuries. However, 30 seconds after being bitten, subject 1 falls to the floor. Deceased.

Upon autopsy of the subject, it appears that the specimen was able to inject air into the coronary artery. Causing a massive cardiac infarction. 

Conclusion: Further testing of the pheromone has been indefinitely postponed. 

So, yeah. I’m not sure where they’re getting these subjects. I’ve heard rumors that they’re using death row inmates or poor souls who sign up for medical testing. But Blackthorn seems to have this information locked down. So rumors are what we have at the moment. Although, after digging a little deeper into that particular experiment. I was able to find that they were using a pheromone released by these spiders during territorial displays. Which doesn't happen often due to their communal nature, but can occur if one finds a particularly cozy spot within their cave. I’m not quite sure what they thought would happen. Trying to use it as a deterrent. 

I got one more that’s of note.

Item test log #479

Item: Lightweight armor for protection against *T*. *fecundum*.

Result: Conditional success. 

Experiment and Results:

The subject chosen for this experiment was chosen based on his high aggression and likelihood to successfully antagonize the specimens. He is equipped with the experimental armor and a baton before being guided into the enclosure containing the onsite colony. Upon entry the specimens immediately take up a defensive posture. As the subject strikes at the nearest specimen, the entire colony attacks. 18 specimens jump onto the subject and attempt to find purchase through the armor. Their fangs prove ineffective in puncturing the garments.

In defiance of the overseeing researchers, the subject begins smashing the specimens. Most perishing under his baton. Leaving only their undamaged eggs scattered amongst the mess of spiderflesh. Once only one specimen was left, the bloodlusted subject rips its fangs from its cephalothorax before removing his helmet. He proceeds to bite into the creature. Crushing its legs and abdomen before consuming it. 

After the subject was properly restrained he was involuntarily submitted to the medical ward where he could be observed for any ill effects of consuming one of the specimens. X-rays of the subject show that he inadvertently ingested an egg that was held within the specimen. Upon this discovery he was moved to more secure containment. 

After 7 hours in containment, the subject began to complain of stomach cramping and severe nausea. Shortly after the reported discomfort, the cameras in his cell captured \[REDACTED\]. His suffering only ended after operatives were able to capture the spiderling and terminate the subject. As too little of his vital organs remained to justify intensive care.

Conclusion: Product “Dermatector” has been approved for further testing and marketability research. However, ingestion of *T. fecundum* has been and will remain to be both forbidden and extremely dangerous.

After reading that one I think I need to take a shower in bleach. But it might disprove the rumor that these are folks who signed up for medical testing. That behavior seemed more fitting of an inmate. Regardless, it seems like Blackthorn is trying to protect humanity in the event these things escape. Albeit not for the most altruistic reasons. I wonder what they would charge for a set of “Dermatector.” Ugh, I hope they at least pick a better name.

But it was probably a reflection of something behind me. Even if it did, I have the camera taped over in case they try something like that.
