I was sure that at forty-four I had left behind everything that happened in the summer I turned sixteen. But I was dead wrong.

It’s been exactly twenty-eight years, and I’ve never spoken about it with a single soul. I’m hoping that by writing down what happened, someone out there can help me figure out if I did the right thing.

**1998**

If my mother had one superpower, it was making her life unnecessarily complicated. And, naturally, complicating mine right along with it.

She had split up with my dad about a year before. Then, in February of 1998, she fell head over heels for a German guy named Hans who was on a business trip in California. Needless to say, it wasn’t long before he convinced her to pack up and move to his place: a tiny, suffocating village tucked away in Upper Bavaria.

I was furious. I didn't want to leave California. I didn't want to leave my friends behind, no matter how many times Hans tried to reassure me with, "My stepdaughter Katrin and her friends can't wait to meet you."

Naturally, I wasn't surprised at all to discover that Katrin was the quintessential rich, popular mean girl.

And I’m no idiot: I knew perfectly well I was a walking problem for Katrin and her clique. All the guys in their circle couldn't stop talking about "how to hook up with the new American girl."

About a week after I arrived, mid-July 1998, I ran into a blonde girl named Janina. The very first thing about her that caught my eye was her Nirvana t-shirt. Finally, someone who actually shared my interests. I stopped her on the sidewalk to compliment the shirt. Janina turned around; word about my arrival had already made the rounds in the village, so she spared me the awkward intro. She glanced around nervously, looking completely spooked or maybe just hyper-paranoid. (A reaction I wouldn’t fully understand until hours later.)

"You like them too?" she asked, speaking with an English so good it threw me off.

I smiled. "Yeah, big time. Honestly, it feels like a miracle finding someone with actual taste around here."

She offered a small, shy smile. "Yeah. Nobody listens to stuff like that here."

I pulled my headphones off my neck. "Do you guys in Germany know System of a Down?"

Janina shook her head. "No, never heard of them."

I nodded, not surprised at all. "I can lend you the cassette so you can let me know what you think."

I will never forget her expression: a dead-on mix of total disbelief and raw shyness. She took the tape with both hands, like she was being handed a sacred religious relic.

That evening, Katrin forced me to hang out with her crowd. I was bored out of my mind, and the conviction grew stronger by the minute that if I wanted to survive this place, I was going to have to find friends who actually liked the same things I did.

Walking back into the house, I asked Katrin, "Do you know Janina?"

Katrin shot me a sideways glance. "Why?"

I didn't love her tone, but I kept going. "I talked to her this afternoon."

Katrin burst out laughing. "You are way too popular to waste time on a loser like her."

I already knew what her reaction would be, but I asked anyway. "Why is she a loser?"

Katrin shrugged dismissively. "Listen to that noisy, aggressive garbage she's into. Besides, last year one of my friends accidentally stepped on one of her stupid cassettes, and she stood there bawling like a baby in front of everyone."

I won't lie, my stomach dropped straight to my shoes hearing that story. What kind of hellish nightmare of a social circle had I landed in?

I replayed that conversation in my head all night. Suddenly, I understood why Janina had looked around so nervously before answering me: she thought it was some kind of sick prank. And I understood why she took my tape like I was handing her the moon.

A couple of days later, I worked up the nerve and showed up at Janina’s house. Her mom answered the door, a super sweet woman who spoke a little English. She called Janina down, and we spent the entire afternoon hanging out.

While we were sitting on the rug in her bedroom flipping through a magazine, I noticed Janina kept glancing out the window with this dark, haunted look on her face. I asked her if everything was okay.

She dropped her gaze to her hands. "It's just... I'm not used to anyone seeking me out. Usually, everyone at school avoids me like the plague."

I turned toward her, absentmindedly twisting the hem of her hoodie. "Why? Just because of Nirvana and the way you dress?"

Janina shook her head, a bitter half-smile crossing her face. "I wish. It started a couple of years ago. Katrin and her little crew... they decided I made the perfect target. They’d steal my diaries, dump my cassettes in puddles, and nobody in town ever says a damn thing because her family practically owns this place." She shrugged, trying to act tough, but I saw the shine of tears in her eyes. "If you’re not one of them, you don't exist."

A knot of pure, unadulterated rage clenched my stomach. In that exact second, the hatred I felt toward my stepsister multiplied tenfold.

As the days rolled on, Janina and I grew tighter and tighter. I always managed to find a different excuse to ditch Katrin, telling her I just wanted to go out alone.

Then came the afternoon of Monday, August 3rd. That’s when it all started. Or at least, that's when the descent began.

The village where we lived backed right up against a dense forest. The sun was still high, and we decided to wander down one of the trails. I don’t remember exactly how far we walked, but I do remember catching sight of a crumbling, abandoned structure half-hidden among the pine trees.

I stopped. "What is that?"

Janina squinted at it. "Looks like an abandoned cabin."

We stepped off the path, pushing our way through the underbrush until we stood right in front of the rotting structure. I still remember Janina whispering, "This is giving me major creepy vibes, let's get out of here."

I didn't listen to her. It didn't look dangerous. We found a rotting side door that was half-open. I stepped inside, but Janina grabbed my arm, her fingers digging in.

"What if someone's in here?" she asked.

I looked around: aside from peeling graffiti on the water-damaged walls and a few pieces of ruined furniture, it looked totally deserted. I stepped inside, and Janina reluctantly followed. But looking back with the brutal clarity of hindsight, I really should have listened to her.

It turned out to be smaller on the inside than it looked from the street—just a couple of rooms.

I dragged some wooden planks near the entrance and we sat down.

We talked for hours, but what happened at the very end is the only thing that actually matters. Even now, I still can't figure out if that gesture and those words were the spark that set the whole world on fire.

Janina looked at me softly and said, "I never would have guessed we’d end up friends."

I smiled. "Me neither. You’re the only interesting person in this whole zip code, and besides... we're cut from the same cloth."

She whispered a thank you, her voice barely audible. "I thought Katrin had convinced you to hate me."

My heart squeezed. "I'm nothing like her."

And right then, it happened. Like I said, it’s been twenty-eight years and the exact choreography is a bit blurry, but we leaned in. And we kissed.

We pulled back, staring at each other in a panic, equal parts terrified and dazed. In a village deeply entrenched in traditional, small-town Catholicism, something like that wasn’t just frowned upon. it was an abomination meant to be outcast and crushed.

She whispered, "If anyone ever finds out..."

I didn't let her finish. I grabbed her hand, squeezing it hard. "No one's going to find out." 

She held on tight. "I don't want to lose you."

I smiled, trying to radiate a confidence I didn't completely feel. "I'm right here. But I'm not going to pretend we're just friends, either."

She looked at me with deep worry in her eyes. "If this gets out, your life is going to be hell... Katrin and her friends will make sure of it."

I shrugged it off. "I don't care. I can take care of myself."

We left shortly after.

When I opened the front door, the house was pitch black. I thought, *Good thing I brought my own key.*

A few hours later, I heard the front door open. I crept down the stairs and found Hans dragging an injured Katrin inside, her arm slung over his shoulder.

My mother rushed over to me. "We were at the hospital. Katrin took a spill on her dirt bike."

Katrin winced, clutching her arm, and added in a pained voice, "Some guy came out of nowhere. It wasn't our fault!"

An hour later, I was handed the glamorous task of bringing dinner up to Katrin's room. It felt completely over-dramatic; she just had a dislocated shoulder and some nasty road rash.

I set the tray down and asked, "How the hell did you guys miss a guy standing in the middle of the road?"

Katrin huffed, annoyed. "Because the idiot came out of nowhere! He literally materialized out of the tree line and crossed right in front of us without looking. With that thick forest hugging the road, it's impossible to see anyone if they just jump out like that."

That detail hooked a strange feeling in my gut. "Did you recognize him?"

Katrin shook her head sharply. "No. We didn't even get a good look at him. And he took off running the second we crashed."

Later on, talking to my mom, she shared my private theory: Katrin's boyfriend had been trying to show off, popped a wheelie, and lost control of the bike.

To save face, they’d concocted the pedestrian story.

Or at least, that’s what I used to think. Now? I’m not so sure anymore.

Six days later, Janina invited me over for lunch.

After eating with her whole family, we heard the wail of police sirens tearing down the street. Janina's mom peered out the window. "The cops just stopped two houses down."

Janina's dad stood up to go check it out. Boredom and small-town nosiness got the better of me, so Janina and I grabbed our jackets and followed him outside.

As we got closer to the flashing cruiser, a neighbor walked up to us, talking fast in German. I couldn't catch a single word, but the look on his face said it all.

"The family who lives in that house just got back... and found it completely trashed," Janina translated under her breath, her eyes wide. I could tell from the ashen look on the neighbor's face that nothing like this had ever happened in our quiet little pocket of the woods.

Right at that moment, a couple walked out of the house with their teenage daughter. My blood ran cold: it was one of Katrin's close friends.

I took a step backward. "Let's go. Katrin doesn't even know I'm out here," I whispered to Janina. She caught my drift instantly, and we hurried back inside.

Even Janina's mother looked genuinely rattled. "In all the years I've lived here, nothing like this has ever happened. I'm double-locking every door tonight."

I headed home a few hours later. Hans immediately filled me in on the break-in down the street. Then Katrin chimed in with a bizarre detail: "My friend just called me. She said the cops told them nothing was actually stolen."

My mom mused, "Maybe it was a warning."

Hans shot her a dark look. "Honey, things like that simply do not happen here."

The next afternoon, Janina and I went back to that abandoned cabin. We felt safe there, hidden away from judgmental eyes. The second we stepped inside, we found something on the floor.

"A lily," Janina murmured, pointing at the solitary white flower resting near the threshold.

We didn't think much of it and sat down on our usual wooden planks.

A few minutes later, Janina stretched out, resting her head in my lap. As my eyes drifted up the water-stained wall behind her, I froze. There was something new scrawled on the concrete.

It was written in heavy red paint. I couldn't tell if it was fresh or had been there all along, but what caught my eye was the language. It was Latin.

*“Libertas pretiosa est.”*

Janina translated it softly: *"Freedom is precious."*

We laughed. It was almost hilariously on-the-nose given our secret little sanctuary.

Janina had to be home by seven. We left the cabin and started walking back along the trail. But just a few yards before the forest line opened up to the paved road, Janina stopped dead in her tracks.

"Wait. Is something shining over there?"

I squatted down in the dirt. "It's a necklace." I picked it up; it was heavy silver, clearly expensive. A sudden, ugly thought iced my veins: "Do you think this was stolen from your neighbors' house?"

Janina’s eyes went wide. "But they said nothing was taken!"

"We need to get out of here. I'm putting it back," I muttered, dropping the silver chain onto the moss.

I walked Janina most of the way home. Halfway down her street, we crossed paths with a man who looked to be in his early thirties. He stepped right into my path.

"You must be the American," he said.

I nodded, tongue-tied. I had never seen him before in my life. He stared at me with an intensity that made my skin crawl, then added, "A little fresh blood in this boring town is always welcome."

Before I could spit out a single word of a reply, he turned and vanished around the corner.

"Who the hell was that?" I asked, shaken.

"No idea. A relative of someone visiting from Munich, maybe," Janina murmured, looking equally uneasy.

The second I unlocked our front door, Katrin ambushed me in the hallway. "Did you take my silver necklace?!"

At first, I was just annoyed by the sheer aggression in her voice. I shook my head. "No. I didn't even know you had one."

She let out a disgusted huff, rolled her eyes, and stormed off to her bedroom.

Back in my own room, I stopped dead in my tracks. I had literally just held an identical silver necklace in my hand half an hour ago. My brain scrambled for an explanation, and the only one that made any remote sense was that Katrin must have lost it somewhere along the path.

The next morning, I woke up with a jolt: Katrin was screaming at the top of her lungs.

I bolted out of bed and flew down the stairs so fast the room was spinning. Katrin was standing on the front doormat, losing her mind. "What the hell happened to my shoes?!"

My mom bent down to pick them up. They had been deliberately melted and torched with a lighter. I pushed past Katrin to check my own sneakers sitting right next to hers. They had been moved a few feet away, but they were completely untouched.

Hans was furious, his face purple. "What the hell is going on around here lately?!"

"Don't worry, we can drive into Munich and buy you a new pair," my mother said, trying to soothe Katrin.

A few hours later, I overheard Katrin talking loudly downstairs. I peeked over the banister. She was talking to Hans. "It happened to my friend, too. She woke up and her shoes were burned."

The tension in the house was thick enough to cut with a knife. Hans was on the landline calling the neighbors. Nobody had seen or heard anything, but a cold, heavy panic was starting to settle over everyone.

Little did we know, the bottom was about to drop out entirely.

By afternoon, I walked over to Janina’s house. I was terrified someone might have torched her shoes, too. Luckily, neither she nor her family had been touched.

Janina’s mother looked at me with deep concern. "Do you think Katrin got mixed up with the wrong crowd?"

I shrugged. She didn't seem like the type to run deep, but who knew?

Janina and I decided to head back to the cabin one more time. As we walked, I told her, "That necklace we found yesterday? It belonged to Katrin."

Janina stared at me, shocked. "Wait... how is that even possible?"

We lost track of time, hanging out a few minutes too long until we noticed the sun dipping low below the tree line. We panicked, grabbed our stuff, and sprinted out.

Halfway down the trail, we heard the crunch of footsteps. We froze for a single second, and that was all it took. Through the darkening pines to our right, we saw a tight circle of people standing in absolute, dead silence. Not a word. Not a sound.

We didn't breathe until we hit the safety of the village street.

Once we were safely on Janina’s block, she whispered, "What were they doing?"

I answered with all the naive ignorance I could muster, "Were they... burning something?"

"There wasn't any smoke smell," she pointed out, shivering.

Before we could spiral into a panic, a wall of police sirens shattered the quiet. Squad cars tore past us down the main road. I walked Janina to her door, and her mother pulled it open instantly, eyes wide with terror. "What happened?!"

Neither of us had a single answer to give.

I hurried home with long, fast strides, only to find a cluster of flashing police cruisers parked right next door at our neighbor's house. My mom, Katrin, and Hans were standing out on the lawn.

Katrin looked like she’d seen a ghost.

My mom rushed straight over to me. "Are you okay?"

I nodded, my heart hammering. "What happened?"

With a pale, drawn face, my mom dropped the bomb: "The little brother of Katrin's friend is missing."

My brain stalled. "What do you mean, *missing*?"

My mom explained: "Katrin and her neighbor went for a walk with the five-year-old boy in tow. They went out to the spot where Katrin had her dirt bike accident to look for that lost necklace. Somewhere along the way, they lost track of him."

I felt the blood drain from my face. Taking advantage of the chaos, I slipped inside, ran to the phone, and called Janina. Her mom answered and handed the phone over immediately.

"The neighbor's kid is missing," I blurted out.

"What? How is that even possible?" Janina asked, her voice tight with panic.

I relayed everything my mom had just told me.

"If we had just taken that necklace when we found it... maybe none of this would have happened," Janina whispered into the receiver.

"Hey, we couldn't have known," I tried to comfort her, but the truth is, I still carry the weight of that guilt today.

When Katrin finally walked back inside, I caught her eye. "Where did you guys lose him?" 

For the first time since I’d met her, that arrogant, mean-girl armor was completely shattered. "I... I don't know... we were right by the forest," she stammered, shaking.

A violent chill shot up my spine. "Which side of the forest?"

"Right near here, by that trail. We yelled and yelled, but it was useless. We just ran home to get help."

I stood there, paralyzed. Janina and I had been less than a kilometer away from the mouth of that exact trail, and we hadn't heard a damn thing.

The next day felt like time itself had jammed a gear. We all filed down to the village church for an emergency community meeting.

Walking up the stone steps, I spotted Janina and her parents. They looked just as sick about this as I felt.

The adults had broken off into search parties. Everyone was being assigned a different grid of the forest to comb through.

Outside the church doors, I drifted over to Janina. A knot of townspeople had gathered around her family, throwing out wild theories, until a middle-aged man lowered his voice and muttered, "We all know what's really in that forest. It's that damn cult."

Janina and I turned ghost-white, but fortunately, everyone was too wrapped up in the collective trauma to notice. Janina's father quickly stepped in, cutting him off with a harsh wave of his hand. "That cult is just an old wives' tale. Don't talk nonsense."

We didn't say a word. We had seen those people with our own eyes, standing in that terrifying circle in the woods. And we were too terrified to speak up, because doing so would mean admitting we had been hanging out in an abandoned cabin, and in a town like this, two teenage girls didn't hang out in ruins just to look at the scenery.

Two grueling, endless days of searching passed. The search parties even pushed through that abandoned cabin, but they found absolutely nothing, as if the forest itself had curled in on its secrets like a fist. The village was paralyzed with terror. The smug, suffocating peace that had defined this place for generations had been instantly ripped away, replaced by a toxic, choking anxiety.

We dragged ourselves back home after another brutal day of beating the brush.

As we stepped onto the porch, we saw it. Wedged firmly beneath the front door was a folded white sheet of paper, and right beside it caught the porch light was a glint of metal. Hans cursed under his breath, a mix of pure rage and naked fear, as he crouched down.

When he stood back up, he was holding Katrin’s silver necklace.

With trembling fingers, he unfolded the paper and read the rigid, spiky handwriting out loud:

*“You see what happens when you touch someone important.”*

And right below it, a line of Latin that turned the air around us to ice:

*“Proximuseris.”* (You're next.)

Katrin lost it. She broke down in a full-blown, hysterical, screaming panic attack, her body wracked with violent sobs.

"I didn't do anything! I didn't have anything to do with this!" she shrieked through her tears, shaking her head wildly, completely unhinged.

I stayed frozen on the bottom step of the stairs. I couldn't move a muscle. I couldn't even force a word past my lips. My blood had turned to slush in my veins: everything was looping back to the moment Janina and I kissed in that cabin.

My mother flew across the porch, grabbing Katrin and burying her face in her chest, stroking her hair with frantic, jerky motions while whispering over and over that she was safe, that they’d protect her. But her own voice was shaking so hard she could barely get the words out.

Meanwhile, Hans lunged for the landline in the hallway. He punched the numbers with savage force, screaming into the receiver that there had been a break-in and a direct threat made against his family.

The police rolled up twenty minutes later.

Two uniformed officers stomped into the house with heavy, sluggish steps. They looked at the note without touching it, lobbing standard, useless routine questions at Hans and my mom. They asked Katrin if she had any enemies, if she’d been fighting with anyone at school, but she was a sobbing mess, entirely incapable of stringing a coherent sentence together.

The local cops clearly had zero experience dealing with this level of psychological warfare. I would’ve bet my life this was a first for them.

The second the police cleared out, I made a beeline for the landline. I needed to call Janina. Making sure she was okay was my only priority. I thought I was alone in the kitchen.

Before I could even press the first button, Katrin screamed right behind my shoulder.

"Oh, go ahead, call that pathetic loser Janina! Maybe she'll finally crawl off and disappear too... though nobody would give a damn if she dropped off the face of the earth!"

I whipped around. Her face was blazing red, warped by a toxic cocktail of raw hatred and sheer terror. I stayed frozen. I didn't fire back.

I couldn't have known it then, but those would be the last words I would ever hear her say before everything changed forever.

The next morning, I was ripped from a dead sleep by a heavy, muffled thud from downstairs. Then came the frantic patter of bare feet sprinting up the stairs.

My bedroom door slammed open: it was my mom. Her face was paper-white, but the second her eyes locked onto mine, she collapsed against the doorframe, letting out a ragged sigh of pure, shaking relief.

I threw the blankets off and jumped out of bed. "What happened?!"

My mom choked out the words, her voice reduced to a dry rasp: "The front door was wide open... Katrin is gone."

Within minutes, the house was swarming with men in uniform, while the neighbors clustered anxiously out on their front lawns. I had never seen Hans cry before. I honestly didn't think he was capable of it.

Only one of the responding officers spoke English, but I didn't have anything to give them. I had slept soundly through the entire night without hearing a peep, and so had my mom.

From that morning on, the search parameters had to expand: the town wasn't just looking for a missing little boy anymore; they were hunting for another teenage girl.

Days bled into one another, but there wasn't a single trace of Katrin, nor of the boy. It was as if both of them had simply dissolved into thin air.

On the sixth day since their disappearance, the town refused to give up. Search parties were still combing the forest and every dark alleyway of the city deep into the night.

I made up my mind. I needed to go back to where this whole nightmare apparently started: the abandoned cabin.

I went with Janina, and her dad drove us out. We pushed open the rotting door, but everything inside was frozen in place, untouched and exactly as we’d left it.

I stared down at the two wooden planks where Janina and I had sat just days ago, back when our biggest crisis in the world was trying to keep our secret safe from prying eyes.

My eyes drifted up to the wall. There was something new. I glanced at Janina, and she stepped up beside me, reading the fresh red paint aloud:

*“Vestra libertas sanguine empta est.”*

Janina stood completely still for a second before whispering the translation: *"Your freedom has been bought with blood."*

The terrible, suffocating certainty settled over both of us: this wasn't random. The root of this darkness was somehow tied directly to us.

Janina’s father stood by the doorway, looking down at us with a soft, melancholic half-smile, an expression I had never seen on his face before. "There's nothing here... let's head home."

Two years crawled by in the wake of that nightmare. Katrin and the little boy were never found. The fragile marriage between my mother and Hans rotted from the inside out, slowly crushed to death by a heavy, radioactive silence that neither of them had the courage to break.

My relationship with Janina, on the other hand, forged into something unbreakable. She helped me master German, tutored me through my classes, and acted as my human shield against the hostile whispers of the village. Even though the peace of that town was shattered beyond repair, Janina filled my heart with a consuming, absolute love, the kind of feeling I have never replicated since.

When my parents finally divorced and my mother announced we were moving back to America, the news felt like a physical blow.

Back in the States, I slid back into my old life, reconnected with high school friends, and tried desperately to construct a normal existence. But I never told a living soul what really happened over there.

Twenty-eight years have flown by. I’ve built a life here. I have two incredible children and a solid, thriving career.

Three hours ago, my oldest son dropped the mail off at my desk. Buried beneath the usual stack of bills was a plain white envelope. No postage stamps. Just my name, scrawled across the front in handwriting that instantly made my breath hitch.

Naively, I thought it was from my husband’s divorce attorney, we separated a year ago and we’ve been hammering out the paperwork.

The reality, however, was infinitely worse.



*Twenty-eight years have passed, and I don’t know if that summer still haunts your thoughts. I hope by now you understand the exact reason why we had to act the way we did.*

*My group met in that forest three days a week. We aren't evil; the devil isn't evil. He chose freedom, rebelling against a God because he refused to live in a prison. And we refused to live in a society like ours, suffocated by hypocritical chains.*

*To everyone else, we were ordinary people, model citizens. To you and Janina, we appeared the exact same way.*

*Do you remember that day in the cabin, when you thought you were completely alone? I saw your kiss, hidden away in the dust and rotting wood. In that exact fraction of a second, my heart swelled. I saw two pure, rebellious souls willing to fight against the asphyxiating hypocrisy of this small-minded town. You two were the exact answer we had been praying for. For years, we had begged for a single spark of authenticity in this village of the living dead, and our prayers were answered right through you.*

*My comrades and I knew we had to protect you at all costs. We saw the way you were being targeted by your stepsister’s little gang. Let me repeat: we aren’t monsters. We tried the polite route at first, hoping to shake the town awake. But you eventually realize you can’t open the eyes of the blind unless you use brute force.*

The dirt bike crash, the trashed house, the burned shoes, the necklace, that was all our doing.

*When one of my brothers saw that little boy alone in the woods, while Katrin and her friend were sitting nearby running their mouths about you, laughing, speculating that you and Janina had stolen the necklace just to hock it, he decided to act. He took the boy with him; it wasn't hard. We wanted to liberate him, anyway; he was just a rotten little offshoot of that same hypocrisy.*

*But that night, when Katrin screamed those vile things right in your face... well, thinking about it even today gives me the chills. I took matters into my own hands. One of my brothers slipped into your house, knocked her out cold, and carried her away. As you’ve probably pieced together by now, we eliminated both Katrin and the boy.*

*Ironic, isn't it? The town ramped up its prayers, only to realize very quickly that God wasn't listening. They were completely on their own.*

*As the years rolled on, we took profound joy in watching you and Janina spend your afternoons together, totally undisturbed.*

*When you had to pack up and leave for America, it broke my heart.*

*But we never lost sight of you. Your two children are gorgeous (though I can't say the same for your ex-husband). Janina built a brilliant career, had her own family, and we couldn't be prouder of the women you both became.*

*I hope things are crystal clear now, and that this letter can finally put a period at the end of that chapter.*

*There is just one final thing I need to confess. I am sick. By the time this letter crosses the ocean to America—hand-delivered by a trusted brother from our circle—I will already be gone. Don't worry. As long as our group draws breath, we will keep you safe, until the day comes when your two souls can finally be reunited.*

As a father, I only want to say one final thing: thank you. You made my daughter Janina’s life absolute perfection.



I should feel sick. I should be sprinting to the phone to call the police, screaming bloody murder, thinking of Katrin’s father and the family of that little boy, of that hollow, incurable grief, especially now that I have children of my own, I should understand the weight of it.

Instead, my body is paralyzed by a truth far more terrifying: a deep, unforgivable wave of gratitude mixed with total nausea.

I don't know if I'm more terrified of them, or of myself, for wanting freedom so badly that I was willing to accept, even without knowing it, the fact that it was bought with blood.
