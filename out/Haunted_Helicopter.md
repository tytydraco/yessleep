I compare the sound of a helicopter's rotor to the sound of my cat scratching its neck with its hind legs. My favorite sound from the cat I lost three years ago is this one. I never remember it for any of its other behaviors or sounds; the way it used to lie on my lap, its high-pitched meowing that I didn't like, its sudden attacks, the way it would raise its front paws and rub itself against my leg — all of these have a very faint place in my memory. But whenever I'm about to board my helicopter, I think of the way it used to squint its eyes while scratching its neck, and I feel sad.

As for my wife, I remember her most for her stomach growling. Of course, we have dozens of beautiful memories together; after all, we were married for ten years. Wonderful vacations, each unique in its own way; hundreds of dinners, each one thoroughly enjoyable; birthday presents, surprises, family visits, our tennis matches, the concerts we went to, and many other activities involving sophisticated pleasures.

But my one and only ASMR... Nothing could ever replace it. Pressing my ear against her belly and listening to the rumbling sounds would put me to sleep instantly. I could never find this particular combination of sounds in any other sound pattern. They weren't loud, bubbling gurgles. My wife's stomach always rumbled in a delicate, refined, and polite manner.

Today is my last helicopter ride. My pilot and I will fly circles over my wife's grave. She died a year ago, but I've never had the courage to visit her grave. I don't want to talk about how she died, or even remember it. It was a devastating blow for me, completely unexpected. That sentence is still ringing in my ears: "We did everything we could, but we couldn't save her." I remember the doctor's tone of voice, his inflections, every little detail. I remember all of it more clearly and vividly than my first meeting with my wife. It was as if the sentence itself had been a sound effect, like the sound of something falling or a gunshot.

And the helicopter took off. We were on our way.

"Are you sure about selling it, Albert?" my pilot asked. I was watching the scenery below, lost in thought.

"Not entirely, actually. I have some reservations."

"You know what I've thought from the very beginning. I've grown attached to this pile of metal, too, and it makes me sad to think that I won't be seeing you anymore, or that I'll never get to fly this thing again."

"Why wouldn't you be able to see me again?"

"Of course we'll see each other. Maybe we'll grab a coffee now and then. But you go places in this pile of metal four times a week. Come on, Albert, do you really think it'll be the same? Will we have the same closeness, the same conversations? Think about your sister, the one you lived with for years before you married Anna. You got married and moved to another city. Yes, you can go and visit her whenever you want, but is it the same? When you lived under the same roof, you used to just hang out together, doing nothing in particular. You were more involved in each other's lives. You didn't have to plan an activity every time you wanted to spend time together. But is that how it is now? Every time you go there, there's some kind of plan, this and that. That closeness is gone. That's just how it is, whether it's a coworker or a classmate. It's the same with all of them."

"So I shouldn't sell the helicopter just to make you happy, then."

"No no, I'm saying this for your sake, too. How many times have you traveled around with Anna in this helicopter? It holds memories for you as well."

"Don't be ridiculous, Jason. It's going to be sold. End of discussion."

"All right, all right. So, what are your reservations?"

I glanced down. We were approaching the cemetery where my wife's grave was. I took my binoculars out of my bag and looked into the distance, somewhere to the right rather than below.

"We're not there yet," Jason said.

"Do you think it's ethical to sell a haunted helicopter?"

"That again."

"Forget whether you believe me or not. Think about it in general terms. Imagine a house that has somehow been proven to be haunted, with photographs, audio recordings, and so on. Do you think it's right to sell that house without giving the other party any information about it?"

"You're a smart man, Albert. Come on. This is exactly why I don't want you to sell it. You're getting rid of a perfectly good helicopter for such a ridiculous reason. What's the harm in seeing a psychotherapist? You're fucking loaded."

Jason could be really irritating sometimes. I liked him, I consulted him on important matters, and sometimes he'd give me incredibly rational advice. But he was also very stubborn, very persistent. If he didn't like a decision I'd made, he'd keep pushing and pushing, trying to change my mind. Sometimes he'd insist so much that I'd tell him I was the boss and that he needed to shut up already. Later, I'd regret speaking to him that way and have to make it up to him.

"Let's say someone asked you this: would you cut off your little finger if they offered you twenty million dollars? You'd answer that, wouldn't you? An offer of twenty million dollars would be supernatural, wouldn't it, Jason? Twenty million dollars is supernatural for you, right? But you'd still answer the question. Right? So why the hell can't you answer this one?"

"I don't know whether selling a haunted helicopter is ethical or not. No comment."

"I don't know. She's a ghost we know, after all. She's my wife. She wouldn't hurt anyone. What do you think?"

"No comment."

"What if I gave it away for free? Do you think that would make up for the wrong I'm doing?"

"You're going to give the helicopter to a rich person, after all, aren't you? Would this rich person agree to take a helicopter without paying for it?"

"Damn, look how quickly you found your tongue when I said I could give it away for free. You're quite a bastard, Jason."

"You don't seem to have any ethical concerns when you're making fun of me, Mr. Albert."

At that very moment, we were right above the cemetery where my wife's grave was. Contrary to the nature of our conversation, I suddenly felt a pang of sadness, and my eyes began to well up. I placed the binoculars against my tearful eyes and searched for her name among the gravestones.

As I read more and more names, my eyes began to well up even more, and my vision grew blurry. I pulled the binoculars away from my eyes, wiped them, and sniffled. I wouldn't be able to look. I didn't want to look.

Jason was silent. He had noticed my sudden change in mood. Without saying a word, he slowly circled through the sky above the cemetery.

"Start the process tomorrow. We're selling this thing."

\*\*\*

At long last, the wait was over, and I had achieved the dream I'd been pursuing since childhood. In just a little while, my wife and I would be taking off together, and this pile of metal I'd watched flying across the sky as a child had finally become mine. Or rather, my wife's. I'd bought it for her as a birthday present, but it belonged to both of us, really, since we'd be traveling in it together most of the time.

Our pilot asked if we were ready. Of course we were, both of us. But it wouldn't do to let this happiness go by without making a terrible joke. The happier you are, the worse your jokes get. Quite a strange contradiction.

"How about I go take a piss for a couple of minutes, Frank? Or I could just let it fly from up here. Give me a heads-up when we're passing over the sea. All these years, I've never pissed in the sea, out of respect for my parents' advice. But now we've earned it, haven't we?"

My wife rolled her eyes. The pilot didn't laugh.

\*\*\*

We've been in the air for half an hour. It's an incredible feeling. Of course, I've flown countless times before. But this feels different, and Frank is a wonderful pilot, too. He told us at great length about how he first became interested in helicopters. He's a pleasant fellow, and I'll most likely hire him.

We're not above the clouds, and we're not nearly as far from the ground as we'd be in an airplane. It feels as though I've sprouted wings and am flying on my own.

I imagine people in movies who, at the very last moment, manage to reach a ladder dangling from a helicopter and climb aboard. I picture a ladder being lowered from where we are all the way down to the ground, and myself descending it—much like taking a stroll, only instead of moving forward, backward, left, or right, it's a journey from top to bottom.

"Are you hungry?" my wife asked, pulling me out of my daydream.

"What do you mean?"

"Your stomach has been rumbling for the past half hour. If you're hungry, let's land."

"No, you must have imagined it. I'm perfectly full. How could you even hear my stomach rumbling over all this noise?"

"That's exactly what I'm saying. If I could hear it, something must be wrong, don't you think? I'm just saying, if you're hungry, let's land. We can go for another ride afterward."

"I'm telling you I'm not hungry, Charlotte. Good grief."

Once I paid a little attention to my surroundings, I could hear the sounds too. I'd been so carried away by the excitement of the moment that I hadn't noticed either my wife's sullen expression or the rumbling sounds.

"Mr. Frank, have you eaten? We could land and grab a bite if you'd like."

"Thank you, sir. I'm not hungry."

Then came a rather loud sound, as if a large bubble had burst inside a thick liquid.

"You're embarrassing us, Charlie. Mr. Frank, could we land somewhere suitable?"

"I'm telling you it's not coming from me. Don't you understand?"

As we were speaking, we heard a bubbling sound that lasted three or four seconds, starting deep and gradually becoming higher-pitched. It was so loud that Frank briefly turned around to look.

I smiled. I hadn't understood a thing, but I played along with the joke. I didn't want to ruin the moment.

"Which one of us is fucking hungry?"

And I laughed to myself like an idiot. Again, nobody laughed.

The day had gone downhill from there. We made an emergency landing, and after getting off, we went home. My wife immediately started preparing dinner. She was convinced that the rumbling sounds had been coming from me and thought I was too embarrassed to admit it. I was furious. It was unacceptable for her to treat me this way on the very day I'd bought her a gift.

I sat down at the table.

"See? Any noises?"

She turned her head slightly and listened carefully.

"No."

"Well, have I eaten anything? Have I had a hamburger or a bowl of soup to make the rumbling stop? We were together the whole time, weren't we? Is it possible that I came home and secretly gobbled down a kilo of grapes from the fridge?"

"Don't exaggerate, Charlie."

"Maybe I ate a packet of spaghetti from our bedroom while you were preparing dinner. How many times have I told you not to leave pasta on the bedside table?"

"All right, enough. Your stomach was rumbling earlier. It's stopped now. There's no problem."

Her refusal to admit she was wrong made me even angrier. I got up from the table. She didn't even call after me. I went to my study and locked the door. I stayed there until nightfall. She didn't seem to care at all. She didn't even come and knock on the door. She didn't even check if I was alive.

I was quite hungry, genuinely hungry this time. But I didn't leave the room. I listened for her footsteps. Yes, she'd finally gone to bed. I could go out and get something to eat now. I honestly couldn't understand how she could be so indifferent.

I went to the kitchen and ate the roast she'd put away in the fridge without even heating it up. I'd eaten too quickly, and now I was feeling heavy. I sat down on a chair. I thought about the helicopter. Unfortunately, I'd soon get sleepy, and when I got into bed beside her, I wouldn't be able to keep sulking. Once she put her arms around me, I'd forget everything. I was upset about that. I sat there a little longer. I wanted my anger toward her to last just a little longer.

But within minutes, sleep won. Thinking about the helicopter had already relaxed me and made me even sleepier.

I knocked on the door. She answered immediately. So she'd been waiting for this moment.

"Don't you dare come in. Go sleep in the living room."

\*\*\*

I'm not staying home tonight. This really is too much. But I've called three people, and none of them are answering. Typical. Who would answer the phone at this hour? Nobody calls this late unless they need something or there's some kind of emergency. Actually, that's not normal at all. I'm deleting all three of them from my contacts. On the contrary, a phone call at this hour is exactly the kind of call you should answer. Friendship reveals itself when someone calls in the middle of the night. Well, this has been useful. Now I know what kind of friends I have.

I'm walking down the street all by myself. I want to get some sleep as soon as possible. The food I ate is sitting terribly heavily in my stomach. I call a taxi. I hop in. I'm going to my helicopter. To the hangar where my helicopter is parked.

My wife calls me while I'm on the way. I don't answer. She keeps calling, one after another, until I reach the hangar, but I pay no attention. Finally, as I'm getting out of the taxi, I decline the call so she won't think something has happened to me. As I walk toward the helicopter, I check to see if she's sent me a message. She hasn't. I don't know what to make of her insistence on calling when she could have simply sent me a message to tell me whatever she wanted to say. Actually, I wouldn't mind not seeing her for a while.

And now, I'm inside. I wish it could take off on its own and give me a little ride, a few kilometers or so. I should give it a name. Yes, from now on, I'll call it Curtis. My best friend Curtis. It wouldn't be a bad idea to install a sound system in here, either.

The seat isn't very comfortable, and it's not really suitable for sleeping, but I don't care. I still try to find the most comfortable position I can. I close my eyes. Before long, I drift off to sleep.

A loud noise wakes me up. It's hard to describe. I look out the window, but nothing is moving. It sounds liquid somehow. Like the sound of a fizzy Coke.

I keep trying to compare the sound to something, but I can't figure out what. It sounds so familiar. Then suddenly, I hear it right next to my ear. Yes, this is the sound that caused the argument between my wife and me today. The sound of a stomach rumbling, coming from no identifiable source.

It's dark all around me. I'm quite alone in the hangar. The sound keeps appearing first in one ear, then the other. There's no doubt about it: it's a rumbling sound. I pick up my phone to call my wife. The sound never stops. It only pauses for very brief intervals, then starts again in a different form, bubbling or flowing, appearing in one ear or the other. I freeze, phone in hand. My wife hasn't called again since I declined her call.

I can't stand things like this. Fear is my weak spot. I hate being scared.

The sound is in my right ear now. I call my wife and hold the phone to my right ear. The sound moves to my left ear. It's not just how frightening it is that bothers me, but the nature of the sound itself. It's like being forced to listen to music I hate for a long time.

My wife isn't answering. My hands have gone ice cold. I want to scream, but it's so quiet around me that I'm afraid the sound of my own voice would startle me. So I clench my teeth and stay silent.

I scramble out of the helicopter in a panic. I peer inside through the window. There's no movement at all, either inside or around it. The sound stops. I look around. My phone is still inside. I must have been clenching my teeth very hard; my ears are ringing. Expecting to suddenly see something frightening, I move closer to the window and peer inside more carefully. I can't see anything.

This can't be happening. This is unacceptable.

In the silence, I can hear the sound of my own breathing. My breathing is normal when I inhale, but my breath trembles as I exhale.

"Go home," I tell myself. "Go somewhere you can feel safe."

My nose feels cold from all the rapid breaths I've been taking. I look inside again. I need to call a taxi to get home. And I need my phone to do that. I look around, checking both sides, hoping someone might pass by so I can ask for help. There's no one. Suddenly, I'm overcome by the fear of death. Everything is perfectly set up for it. There'll be no witnesses. The only person who knows I came here is the taxi driver.

I see a light inside. My phone is ringing. It must have slipped out of my pocket onto the seat while I was sleeping. It must be my wife calling. I wish I could hear any kind of sound. A car passing somewhere in the distance. A plane flying overhead. Maybe I'd feel a little calmer and find the courage to open the door. My wife's call has made me feel a little less alone. I hope she keeps calling.

I closed my eyes, clenched my teeth, took a deep breath, clenched my left hand into a fist as tightly as I could, and flung the door open.

There was no sound.

I stepped inside quietly. I picked up my phone and put it in my pocket. My wife hadn't called again. I was exhausted. I'd sit on the seat for a minute to rest, then call a taxi right away. To be honest, now that I couldn't hear the sound anymore, I also wanted to convince myself that I wouldn't hear it again. Resting was just an excuse.

But no. I'd been an idiot. The moment I heard the sound, I bolted outside. I tripped and fell to the ground. It sounded like a roar. Like a brutal vocal. But underneath it all, you could still clearly hear the rumbling.

I could still hear the sounds coming from the helicopter through its open door as I lay on the ground.

The sound alone made me feel terribly unsafe. I wished there were something to see as well. Some kind of entity I could run away from. Something I could look back at to check whether it was still coming after me. I lay there where I'd fallen, listening to the sounds. I couldn't move. I didn't want to move. I called my wife. She didn't answer.

The helicopter door closed by itself. The rumbling stopped.

Was that a movement I saw? The helicopter seemed to be rising. There was no sound. The rotors weren't turning, the engine wasn't running. The helicopter rose a little higher. I followed it upward with my eyes and my head. It kept rising, higher and higher. It grew smaller and smaller. It wasn't moving to the right or left, or forward. It was only going up. I felt pressure in my temples. It rose above the buildings and settled into the sky.

And I hear the sound of an engine in the distance. The helicopter starts up, comes to life while it's up in the sky. The rotors begin to turn. The lights come on.

As if saying goodbye to me, as if sad to be leaving, it hovers there for a while. I can't take my eyes off it. I hear footsteps approaching, but I don't pay any attention. I listen to the sound of the rotors.

Then it flies forward.

I am still on the ground. Someone is standing over me, saying something, but I can't hear them. I watch Curtis until it disappears from view.
