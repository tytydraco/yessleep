The first night we spent in Alderwood, the cold didn't just settle outside the windows—it crept beneath the floorboards, up through the soles of my slippers, and straight into the marrow of my bones.

I woke up around two in the morning to a silence so heavy it pressed against my eardrums. Beside me, Ethan was fast asleep, buried beneath two thick wool blankets, his breathing slow and steady. My throat felt parched from the dry heat of the electric radiator humming in the corner, so I slipped out of bed, pulled on my cardigan, and padded quietly down the wooden stairs toward the kitchen.

The moment I reached the bottom step, a sharp, unmistakable odor hit my nostrils. It wasn't the scent of aged cedar or fresh paint we had smelled earlier that afternoon. It was the cold, briny stench of stagnant lake water—thick, metallic, and slightly rotten, like wet weeds left to decay in the dark.

Frowning, I walked into the living room and switched on the small table lamp. My gaze immediately dropped to the wooden coffee table in the center of the room.

The twisted piece of driftwood and the three smooth white river stones Ethan had brought back from Blackwater Lake were sitting right where he had left them. Except they were no longer dry. Dark, glistening beads of moisture clung to the silvery-gray grain of the wood, pooling onto the tabletop in a thin, murky puddle that hadn't been there at five o'clock.

"Did he rinse them off before going to bed?" I muttered under my breath, reaching out to touch the edge of the wood.

The moment my fingertips brushed the bark, I flinched back. It wasn't merely damp; it was freezing, as if it had just been pulled from beneath a sheet of winter ice.

Before I could grab a rag from the kitchen to wipe the table, a soft, rhythmic sound froze me in my tracks.

Scritch. Scritch. Scritch.

It came from the kitchen—specifically, from the lower half of the back door. It sounded like dry fingernails or a wet pine branch dragging slowly across the exterior wooden planks. Then came a low, drawn-out exhale, like something heavy pressing its face right up against the unsealed gap beneath the doorframe, drawing in the warm air from inside the house.

My heart hammered against my ribs. I stood rooted beside the coffee table, staring into the dark doorway of the kitchen. Slowly, I reached into the pocket of my cardigan for my phone—only to remember there was no cell service out here, and the sluggish satellite router was sitting unconfigured in a cardboard box upstairs.
Scritch. Scritch.

Mustering every ounce of rational thought I had left, I forced myself to take two steps toward the kitchen and flicked on the overhead light.

The scraping stopped instantly.

The kitchen was completely empty. The deadbolt on the back door was still firmly locked. Yet as I stared down at the narrow half-inch gap beneath the door frame, I watched a wisp of pale gray mist curl lazily across the threshold, lingering for a heartbeat before dissipating against the warm kitchen linoleum.

"Just a stray raccoon," I whispered to the empty room, though my voice trembled slightly. "Or a branch scraping the siding in the wind."

Still, I didn't linger to pour a glass of water. I hurried back upstairs, slipped into the second-floor bathroom to splash cold water on my face, and paused. The air upstairs felt stiflingly hot and stale, trapped inside by the tightly latched windows Ethan had shut before bed. Remembering how suffocating it felt, I unlatched the small frosted bathroom window and pushed it open just a crack—barely an inch—to let a sliver of fresh air circulate before heading back to the bedroom.

As I walked past the tall bedroom window on my way back to bed, I caught my own reflection in the dark glass. For a fraction of a second—so brief I immediately blamed it on exhaustion—my reflection seemed to lag half a beat behind me, its head turning toward the bed a split second after mine already had.

I climbed under the covers, pulled the quilt up to my chin, and stared at the ceiling until gray dawn finally began bleeding through the curtains.

By seven-thirty the next morning, a thick blanket of fog still clung to the valley floor. When I walked over to the bedroom window to pull back the drapes, what I saw in the yard next door made me pause.

Caleb and Sarah Bennett—our neighbors to the right—were already standing out in their mist-shrouded backyard. Both were bundled in heavy wool coats, holding a long wooden yardstick and a bucket of coarse gray salt. Caleb was crouched down along the neat line of white-painted river stones separating their property from ours, measuring the height of the stones with grim precision.

As I watched, Sarah handed him two more white stones from a canvas sack. Caleb stacked them carefully onto the section of the border directly facing our house, reinforcing the barrier, before scattering thick handfuls of gray salt along the grass on their side of the line. Neither of them looked up at our windows, yet the urgency in their posture was unmistakable. They weren't gardening; they were fortifying a wall.

Downstairs, the smell of freshly brewed coffee and toasted bread greeted me, momentarily pushing away the lingering unease of the night before. Ethan was already in the kitchen, whistling softly as he knelt by the back door with a tube of standard commercial wood putty and a putty knife.

"Morning, sleepyhead," Ethan smiled, glancing up as I entered. "See? Told you I'd take care of it first thing. No more drafts getting under this door."

"Thanks, Ethan," I said, leaning against the counter. "Hey... did you happen to wash off that driftwood last night before we went to sleep?"

Ethan paused, wiping excess putty off his blade with a rag. "No, why? I left it right on the coffee table."

"Nothing," I lied smoothly, deciding not to sound paranoid over a damp piece of wood. "It just looked a little wet this morning. Must be condensation from the temperature drop."

After breakfast, Ethan opened the back door briefly to step outside toward his detached workshop—and stopped dead in the doorway.

"Huh. Look at that," he frowned, pointing down at the wooden threshold just outside the door.

I stepped beside him and looked down. Smeared directly across the top step, stopping right at the freshly puttied line beneath the door, was a thick patch of pitch-black lake mud. It had no distinct paw prints or shoe treads—just a wide, irregular drag mark, as if something heavy and sodden had pressed itself flush against the bottom of the door during the night.

"Must have been an otter or a stray dog wandering up from the lake," Ethan said, though his smile faded slightly as he reached for a broom to scrub the dark sludge off the wood.

Around eleven in the morning, once the sun cleared the pine ridge and the village entered what Ethan jokingly called its "safe window," he disappeared into his workshop to set up his saw table, leaving me alone to unpack the remaining boxes upstairs.

I decided to clear out the small attic storage room at the end of the upper hallway so we'd have somewhere to store our empty moving cartons. The space was cramped, smelling strongly of cedar dust and old paper. While dragging a stack of flattened boxes toward the far corner beneath the eaves, my boot caught on a loose floorboard that shifted with a hollow clack.

Curiosity getting the better of me, I knelt down and pried the warped plank upward.

Tucked neatly into the dark cavity between the ceiling joists was a dented, rust-flecked tin box.

My heart gave a small jump. I lifted the box out, blew away a thick layer of gray dust, and unlatched the lid. Inside were three items left behind by Thomas and Evelyn Callahan—the couple who had vanished from this house four years ago.

The first was a folded, yellowed newspaper clipping dated October 1924 from a neighboring county paper, accompanied by a typed medical journal excerpt from 1968. The headline of the old article read: The Autumn Delusion of Alderwood.

I sat cross-legged on the attic floor and read through it. Nearly a century ago, a visiting reporter had mocked the residents of Alderwood after the entire village abruptly removed or covered every mirror in town, sealed their windows with beeswax every afternoon, and lined their properties with white-painted stones. The 1968 medical note attributed the behavior to "generational mass hysteria triggered by severe geographic isolation and possible ergotism."

A wave of relief washed over me. That explains it, I thought, exhaling slowly. It's a documented local superstition—a shared delusion passed down for generations.

Beneath the clippings lay a hand-drawn surveyor's map from 1894. On the aged parchment, the valley wasn't labeled Alderwood at all. Written across the center in faded brown ink were the words: Hollow of the Glass Water. Along the eastern edge—right where our cabin now stood—someone had drawn a thick red boundary line accompanied by a stern warning: Do Not Build Past the White Stones.

At the very bottom of the tin box rested a cloth-bound journal with Evelyn Callahan written neatly on the inside cover.

I flipped open the first few pages. Evelyn's handwriting was crisp and articulate. Her early entries mirrored my own thoughts almost word for word—she joked about Old Martha's obsession with chalk paste, laughed at Arthur Miller covering his shop mirrors in burlap, and complained about how stuffy the house felt when sealed up at night.

Before I could turn to the middle pages, the heavy front door downstairs creaked open, followed by footsteps echoing in the foyer.

"Clara? You up there?" Ethan's voice called out.
I glanced at my watch. 3:12 PM.

Tucking the tin box under my arm, I hurried down the stairs to show him what I'd found. Ethan was standing in the living room, wiping sawdust from his hands, with the front door still propped partway open to let in the crisp afternoon air before we shut things up for the evening.

"Ethan, look what I just found hidden under the attic floorboards," I began, stepping into the living room. "It belonged to the Callahans—there's an old newspaper article from 1924 explaining why everyone in this town acts so—"

The rest of the sentence died in my throat.

Standing right at the edge of our front porch was Old Martha. Her chest heaved as though she had sprinted all the way from her yard, her small bucket of white chalk paste dangling loosely from one trembling hand. She had clearly come over to remind us again about sealing the house before four-thirty—until her pale eyes locked onto the living room coffee table through the open doorway.

Her gaze fell directly on the twisted piece of driftwood and the three smooth white stones from Blackwater Lake.

The color drained from Old Martha's face so fast she looked like a corpse.

The tin bucket slipped from her fingers, clattering loudly against the porch boards and spilling thick white paste across the wood. She staggered backward two paces, raising a shaking, gnarled finger toward the coffee table.

"You brought it inside..." she whispered, her voice cracking with pure, unfiltered terror. "You brought things from the black water past the white stone line."

"Martha, wait—it's just a piece of driftwood my husband found by the shore," I said quickly, stepping toward the front door with the tin box still clutched against my chest. "Are you alright? Come inside and sit down for a minute—"

"No!" Martha shrieked, flinching away from the open threshold as if the doorway itself were on fire. She refused to set even a single foot onto our porch boards. "It knows where you live now! The threshold is broken!"

Ethan stepped beside me, his brow furrowed in alarm and confusion. "Ma'am, please, calm down. I'll throw the wood back into the trees right now if it bothers you—"

"It's too late to throw it back!" Old Martha sobbed, backing down the porch steps onto the gravel path, her eyes darting wildly toward the lengthening shadows of the pine trees creeping across our yard. "The sun is already touching the ridge! Seal every crack with lime and ash! Cover every piece of glass!"

She spun around to flee back toward her own house, then paused for a fraction of a second, looking back over her shoulder at me with wide, hollow eyes.
"And do not open your door for anyone tonight, Miss Vance," she breathed, her voice trembling so violently the words barely held together. "Not even if the person knocking sounds like your husband."
