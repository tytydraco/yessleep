Governments are very good at hiding things: bodies, weapons, entire towns, and, occasionally, the truth.

I’ve spent almost seven years watching classified reports vanish, evidence get destroyed, and witnesses suddenly develop convenient memory problems. Paper can be confiscated. Servers can be wiped. So when I decided to record what we actually do, I knew better than to put it somewhere the government could reach.

Which brings me to Reddit.

Yes, Reddit. I know how ridiculous that sounds. That’s exactly why I chose it. If someone finds this account, they’ll probably assume it’s fiction written by some bored twenty-year-old with an unhealthy interest in military history and monsters.

Perfect. Let them.

Everything you’re reading is real.

My name is General Eleanor.

Well, no, it isn’t. It’s the name I’m using here. I’m not stupid enough to put my real name on Reddit and then document classified military operations.

What I can tell you is that I’m a four-star general in the military of a very large country that has a habit of involving itself where it probably shouldn’t. For the last two years, I’ve commanded a division that officially doesn’t exist.

Our actual job is simple.

We fight monsters.

Not terrorists. Not insurgents. Monsters.

Things that crawl out of oceans. Things that wear human faces. Things that have lived beneath our soil since before our country existed. Things the government has spent more than a century pretending aren’t real.

I’ve spent almost seven years making sure they don’t reach you.

And I’ve also spent seven years learning that our records are incomplete. Sometimes intentionally. Sometimes because the people writing them died. And sometimes because someone decided the truth was more dangerous than the thing we were fighting.

That’s why I’m writing this.

Every general who takes my position receives the same briefing: forty-seven rules across thirty pages.

I’ve read it nineteen times.

It’s wrong.

Not completely wrong, which would almost be easier. It’s wrong in exactly the ways that get people killed.

So I’m recording everything the official briefing leaves out: what these things look like, how they behave, what kills them, what doesn’t, what they want, and why. I’ll record our mistakes too.

***Especially*** those.

Because after seven years, I’ve learned something important.

Monsters don’t kill most people.

Mistakes do.

I was fifteen when this started. At the time, I was more worried about passing algebra than supernatural creatures and military training.

Mostly algebra.

Now I’m twenty-two. The military has never had a four-star general this young before.

Then again, they’ve never had a monster extermination division before either.

So, future General, if you’re reading this someday, I’m sorry about your promotion.

You’re going to need this.

And if you’re reading it while I’m still alive?

Good.

You’re preparing early.

I like that.

The earlier you’re ready, the better.

Yesterday started like no other day.

Painfully normal.

That should have worried me.

Normal days encourage people to lower their guard, and lowered guards tend to result in paperwork.

At 0530, I woke up. At 0545, I ran five miles, and at 0617, I showered. By 0630, I was in my office reviewing overnight reports while eating breakfast when Major Denver walked in without knocking.

He checked his watch. "I'm three minutes early."

"Exactly."

He sighed and placed a folder on my desk.

Denver was thirty-eight, six feet tall, perpetually tired, and possessed the unfortunate habit of looking at me like I was a normal human being. Most people didn't. They either looked at me like I was a four-star general or like I was a twenty-two-year-old woman who had somehow been given enough authority to order entire battalions into places sane people avoided.

Denver did neither.

No birds or insects.

Not even the rustle of leaves.

But that didn't mean it couldn't see us.

"Move."

I knew what people expected a commander to feel when one of her soldiers died. Grief. Guilt. Anger. Regret. Something.

I had none of it.

Maybe that's why I was good at this.

Or maybe it was why I had been given this job in the first place.

People will probably call that cruel.

They're welcome to.

Cruelty is irrelevant.

Command isn't about being liked. It's about making decisions everyone else is too emotionally compromised to make. Sometimes that means killing a monster. Sometimes it means letting one kill a soldier. And sometimes the difference between those two decisions is only one second.

I had deliberately missed.

I had deliberately chosen where the second bullet went.

I knew exactly what would happen.

And I'd do it again.

Not because Mill deserved to die.

He didn't.

Not because his brother wasn't worth saving.

He was.

**But because the order mattered more than the man who broke it.**

That's the part nobody tells you about command. Sometimes doing your job means making a decision you know you'll be hated for. Sometimes it means making one you know you'll never regret. And sometimes they're the same decision.

I don't expect anyone reading this to agree with me. Honestly, I hope you don't. Remember what I did. Remember Mill. Remember that I could have saved him. Then ask yourself what you would have done.

Because someday, you may be sitting behind this desk, making the same kind of decision. Maybe you'll make the right one. Maybe you'll make the wrong one. Maybe you'll be luckier than I was.

I doubt it.

There is one thing I know now that I didn't know when I was fifteen.

The monsters aren't always the things with claws. Sometimes they're the people making the decisions. And sometimes they're the person sitting behind the desk, knowing exactly what a bullet is going to do before she pulls the trigger. 

That's why I'm writing this down.

Not to justify myself.

I don't need justification.

I'm writing it because someone will eventually sit where I am sitting now, and I would rather they understand what happened than repeat it without knowing why.

The official briefing has forty-seven rules.

It doesn't contain this one.

It should.

# General's Rule #48

**If a soldier disobeys a direct order, deal with it immediately.**

Not tomorrow. Not after the mission.

Immediately.

Because once one soldier learns that your orders can be ignored, others will learn it too. And the next time, you might not have the luxury of being only one second late.

As for Private Mill, there is nothing more to say.

He disobeyed a direct order.

So when the situation changed, I ignored his wish just as he had ignored mine.

Mill died.

The mission continued.

The civilians survived.

That was the equation.

I don't regret the decision. I don't feel guilty about it, and I don't expect either of those things to change.

Maybe that makes me a monster.

If it does, then at least I'm a monster who knows exactly what she is.
