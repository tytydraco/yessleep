I’ve told this story to my family, and I’ve told it to authorities. I’ve told it sober, and I’ve told it drunk. No one believes me, no matter the circumstances. But, I’ll keep telling it anyway—anyway I can, online and offline, with the hope that eventually one of you assholes takes me seriously….

Where should I begin this time? Well, I guess I should start by saying that before I dropped out of Emerson, I majored in journalism. I didn’t take it too seriously as a sophomore, but I took it seriously enough that I did the bare minimum. And we had a big project due at the end of the year that I had been putting off; every student had to play an investigative journalist and create a ten-minute documentary on a topic of their choice.

My more narcissistic classmates sought camera operators from the Film Studies department so they could film themselves giving interviews, but we also had the option to pose our questions from behind the camera, a la Errol Morris, which I thought was the better approach for me. I’ve always hated being in front of the camera. It might be because I pissed myself on camera once on my sixth birthday from drinking too much Mountain Dew, while my friends and family buckled over with laughter. I used to think that was the worst thing that could ever happen me. Turns out I was wrong.

Anyway, I had been putting off this project when I realized I had the perfect subject to interview: my weirdo cousin, Cody. Cody was a freshman who went to a community college in Rhode Island. He was the kind of guy who had a different passion project every three months, like building 3D renderings of abandoned malls or hosting conspiracy theory podcasts. And presently, he was all about chasing UFOs. He even created a club with some fellow nerds called ‘The Lone Gunmen,’ a reference that I didn’t get, which he said I should be horribly ashamed about. Whatever.

So, I messaged him—this was spring time a year ago—and asked him if he had been chasing any UFOs lately. He messaged back saying the correct terminology was UAP, ‘Unidentified Aerial Phenomena,’ as if I was a boomer who’d just said something lowkey racist. He said The Lone Gunmen were hot on the trail of one, in fact, and driving up to Maine for the weekend. I told him about my school project, and he insisted I tag along and that they could pick me up along the way.

This sounded easy enough; Cody and his friends were probably strange enough that I could ask them anything and get ridiculous content for my doc. And, I already had an iPhone Pro that even some directors used on real movies these days. All I needed was some wireless lights and mics that I’d buy on Amazon and then return when I was done. 

My only problem then was Bo.

Bo, my now ex-boyfriend, basically looked like Chadwick Boseman, R.I.P., but with a beer gut. The problem was that he was super possessive, and—go figure—he didn’t like the idea of me going up to a cabin and stargazing with three guys, two of whom I’d never met before. He insisted on tagging along, which was like Charles Barkley attending a Dungeons and Dragons convention. A major buzzkill in other words. So, I told him ‘no’ over the phone. ‘Bo, no!’ was a phrase I used a lot with him. And, I said if he thought I was going to have a sleep away orgy with three unbathed UFO chasers, one being my own flesh and blood, then we might as well break up right now. And with that, I hung up.

I gave Cody my address, and the next day, twenty minutes earlier than planned, this rusted white van double-parked on the street out front of my kitchen window and wailed on the horn. It was the kind of van only plumbers and child molesters used. Then the back door slid open, and Cody was staring in my direction through a set of binoculars. I cursed, spit out my Trader Joe’s O’s, finished packing my bag, and then ran out to greet him. 

Cody basically looked the same as last time I’d seen him, except he’d lost some of his baby fat and now had pork-chop sideburns for some reason. The van had two rows of seats in the back. I sat next to him in the middle row, and he then introduced me to Ryan, who was driving, and Bryan in the passenger seat. Ryan was stocky, pale, and full of freckles, like he recently bailed hay on a farm in Ireland. And Bryan, although Asian, was both the tallest in the group and the only blond, his hair being bleached. He was wearing alien-eye Oakley sunglasses, a metal choker, and had silver nail polish on his fingernails. I wouldn’t be surprised if he had early 2000s rave music as his ringtone too. To be honest, they were both surprisingly good looking, at least for UFO hunters. Maybe Bo had a reason to be jealous—

Before I could even finish that thought, a hand grabbed the van door as I was sliding it shut and jerked it back open. The others visibly jumped—Bryan with his hands in the air as if we were being robbed—as Bo said howdy and jumped into the back seat, introducing himself as my boyfriend/assistant director. He was holding a duffel bag.

I could have killed him. And ‘howdy?’ He never said ‘howdy.’ What felt like five minutes of silence passed after some confused hellos. Ryan pulled onto the road and was driving up the entrance ramp to the freeway when Bo yelled over the van’s rattling. 

“So, UAP?”

This seemed to break the ice, at least with everyone except me. The guys began throwing out nerdy terminology, like ‘plasma orbs,’ ‘biologics,’ and ‘reverse technology,’ while wildly gesticulating, which was a lady-boner killer, if I’m to be honest. Bo feigned interest, or maybe he was actually interested? I couldn’t really tell because I hated him. He squeezed my shoulder, and I shoved his hand away.

“How did you guys meet, anyway?” Bo asked them.

“Reddit!” the others said in unison.

I cringed. 

Cody then turned to us, suddenly serious. “Do you know about the Spinning Man?”

“The what? *Wait,”* I said. I didn’t expect them to be doling out juicy nonsense so quickly. I got out my wireless mics, clipped them to their shirt collars and set them to record. I then began filming with my phone on a gimbal. The van was rocking wildly. I’m sure it looked and sounded terrible, but hey, cinéma vérité.

“Okay, what’s a spinning man? —Wait, introduce yourselves first.”

They introduced themselves. It was adorable. 

“The spinning man was a UAP, an Unidentified Aerial Phenomenon,” Cody clarified into the camera, “captured on camera in Sequoia National Park in 2015.” He pulled out his phone and opened a video. I focused on it, and my smile faltered. The screen displayed a close-up of a white, humanoid shape floating in the blue sky. It was immediately… disturbing. I watched it slowly spin with an almost ultraviolet aura, while at the same time its limbs seemed to phase in and out of existence.

Cody looked at his phone, swiped the screen, and then displayed it to me again. “And *this* is a video Arrow released from their archives last month, but dated back to June 2020.”

Bryan turned towards us in the passenger seat, his gaze invisible behind his Oakleys. “Arrow as in A.A.R.O., the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office.”

I had no idea what they were talking about. This video was black-and-white, maybe night vision, and taken from a distance. The camera began to zoom up and in on a white spot, the gray background becoming increasingly pixelated while the white figure slowly gained clarity. It looked similar to the other spinning man, slowly spinning with one leg out, like a wind-up ballerina filmed by an air traffic controller. The leg melted back into its body. Its body continued to bubble-up and morph. Maybe it was the van’s shitty shock absorbers, but I was becoming nauseous.

“How big are these things?” I asked, my voice weak.

“There’s not really a point of reference, but most people agree they’re six to ten feet tall.”

“Are they... alive?” 

“We don’t know,” my cousin said.

“That first shot was debunked,” Bo interrupted, poking his head between us. “It was a stormtrooper balloon. If you squint, you can kind of see it.”

“Bo, what the hell are you talking about?” I hissed.

“Hi, my name is Bo,” he said and smiled. I realized I had pointed the camera at him and jerked it away. It didn’t phase him. “Star Wars,” he said. “It was a life-sized, helium balloon of a Star Wars stormtrooper. A couple of guys accidentally let it go while goofing around—not too far from Sequoia park—and it shot up into the lower stratosphere.”

“Gentlemen, we have a potential recruit in our midsts,” Cody said. 

“Welcome, brother,” Bryan said.

“I dabble,” Bo said.

“It’s true, the stormtrooper balloon might explain that video,” Bryan said. “But, it doesn’t explain the one from 2020.”

“Another balloon might,” Bo said.

“It might, but we have evidence that it doesn’t.”

“What evidence?” I said.

Cody scratched one of his sideburns and grinned sheepishly. “That will have to wait until we get there.”

“You said that first video was filmed in Sequoia Park,” Bo said. “Where was that second one recorded?”

“It remains undisclosed,” Bryan said. “But, we’re confident it was filmed in the Allagash region of Maine.”

“Why is that?”

Cody and Bryan looked at Ryan, who had been silent this whole time. 

“You’ll see,” Ryan said, cryptically.
 
I stopped recording. I told the guys I’d forgotten to charge my phone and that they should talk about something else while I charge it with my battery pack. I was lying though. To be honest, I was creeped the fuck out. Maybe this was good material for the doc, but I should tell you, I’m one of those basic-ass bitches who loves romcoms and hate horror films. I  was expecting comedic bullshit about flying saucers… not whatever this was. Maybe it was good Bo was here….


* * *


We crossed into Maine a few hours later, the welcome sign saying, ‘Welcome to Maine, the Way Life Should Be,’ which I seriously doubted, considering the theme of our trip. Sometime afterwards, we stopped at a grocery store to stock up for the weekend. It seemed like none of them really drank, but Bo, who was 21, insisted on getting two 30-racks of beer. I couldn’t decide if a cabin full of drunks would give me more material for my documentary or ruin it, so I held my tongue. 

In the parking lot, Bo presented me a pint of Ben & Jerry’s Churray for Churros as a form of apology, which was vaguely offensive considering I was half-Mexican and had never once eaten it in front of him. I told him that him hopping into the van was borderline stalkerish, like a scene out of Cape Fear. Bo explained that he had gotten really high last night and woke up this morning extra paranoid about my trip, which for some reason he thought was a totally valid explanation for his behavior. He then asked if I’d be comfortable if he went off with three women to a cabin for the weekend; and yeah that would fucking infuriate me I conceded, but I still wouldn’t jump into their vehicle as it took off.

We were soon passing weathered farmhouses and barns and the occasional faded American flag hanging limply from a porch deck. I started filming again, knowing I’d need B-roll. The sun was beginning to set, which was pretty, but also a bit eerie. I had lived in cities most of my life and always felt out of place when there was more plant-life than people. I looked over at Bo, who was becoming increasingly bug-eyed, and knew it was worse for him. He had grown up in Brooklyn, and it took him a while even to adjust to life in Boston as a black guy. Now, we were in White Country, USA. I almost gave his leg a sympathetic squeeze, but I still kind of hated him so I stopped myself. 

We followed a wooded river bank, a few canoes tied to a broken dock across the water, and then Ryan abruptly turned into a narrow road in the middle of the woods. There wasn’t even a street sign. The road soon turned into gravel and then into dirt, all the while thinning down to a trail that barely fit one car through, let alone a van. Spruce and fir trees hugged us on either side, and the van’s suspension bucked with every rut. 

“You didn’t tell us it was so secluded,” Cody said to Ryan.

“By the way, it’s secluded,” Ryan said. 

He rolled down the window and lit a cigarette. I didn’t take him for a smoker. Chilly air swept into the van, along with the crunch of the tires and the shrill chorus of tree frogs. It felt more like fall than spring.

“While you all look for little green men, I’ll be looking for men in white hoods,” Bo mumbled.

“You’re good,” Ryan said. “A lot of hippies around here. My aunt’s a lesbian.”

“Well, now I feel better,” Bo said, still frowning.

“I should probably mention we’re going to her farm,” Ryan said. “She’s not there. She’s been in Florida for the past three months, helping take care of a friend with leukemia. I used to come up here as a kid every summer. And well…,” he looked back as I recorded, “I guess I’ll save the rest for when we get there.”

We passed a collapsed shack, made another turn even deeper into the woods, and then the trees abruptly stopped. A vista opened of a cabin sitting placidly on a hill surrounded by an overgrown pasture, last year’s grass still standing in pale clumps. As we got closer, I spotted numerous wind chimes and at least six rocking chairs on a wrap-around porch.

“We’re here,” Ryan said fondly, pulling up to the end of the driveway. “Aunt Rita’s place.”

We stretched our limbs, took in the fresh air, and then lugged all our bags and groceries up the steps. No one besides Ryan had seen the place before. We were a little hesitant entering, and then we began grinning like idiots. The interior was cheesy in the best possible way. I immediately began filming again. It was primo B-roll. 70’s brown furniture filled every corner along with a bunch of hippie shit, like a lava lamp, an airbrushed painting of a wolf howling up at the moon, and a glass sculpture of a caterpillar smoking a pipe. A large wooden chest lay in front of a futon, and I knew 100% there was at least one bong inside. 

“Okay,” Ryan said. “I’ll take her room. Cody, you take the couch in the office. Bryan, you can have the futon. And, you two can have the guest room,” he said to Bo and I. 

“Sounds good,” Bo said. Our eyes met. He offered a nervous smile, and I shrugged, which seemed to cheer him up. He grabbed some lukewarm beers and shoved them into our hands. He cracked his open and held it up in a toast. “To… uh… close encounters.”

I’m not going to lie, it was a good line. I caught it on video. We raised our cans and cheered. 


* * *


After moving our stuff to our assigned rooms, taking turns in the bathroom, and putting our hoodies and jackets on, we went out onto the porch with our beers to admire the scenery. The screen door squealed open and clacked shut as each of us stepped out. The frogs, which Ryan called spring peepers, were so loud that I could feel their chirping inside my chest. 

As the guys shared goofy anecdotes, the stars needled into the darkening sky, the last sliver of sun melting into the horizon behind the treeline. Insects bobbed about the stalks of overgrown grass, and further out two bats swooped down for their dinners. The universe then opened above us. I couldn’t remember the last time I’d seen the stars with so little light pollution. I could count the craters on the moon. This should have all been beautiful, but it unsettled me considering the topic du jour: the spinning man. 

Cody turned to me as if reading my mind. “You better mic us up.” 

I gulped and nodded. I went inside, came back out, and set everyone up.

“Alright Ryan,” my cousin said, “I think it’s time you show us the goods.”

"The goods?" I said.

Ryan grunted. He reached into a backpack and began passing out headlamps. 

Bo held his out as if it was a hand grenade. “This implies we’re going somewhere darker. —Nah, I’m good.”

“Just to the barn,” Ryan said. “Don’t be a wuss.” 

I retrieved the LED panel I'd brought for night shots, and then, turning it on, followed Ryan and the guys through the grass. Moths and gnats flew dizzying circles in front of my camera, and Bo’s headlamp jerked erratically toward every sound in front of us. I tried to be an optimist: this film would be entertaining if nothing else, the all-encompassing 
pursuits of idiosyncratic young men . Maybe I could even enter it into festivals.

A rusted truck was up on jacks in one corner, all of the wheels removed, and some ancient farming tools hung from hooks on the walls behind it, including a scythe. "I wouldn’t bring you all the way out here to murder you, cuz. We have too much history."

“Quit messing with her,” Bryan said to Ryan.

Ellie is my name, I should mention.

Cody and Bryan each carried a bin back to the cabin, setting them down in front of the TV while I continued recording. I asked Ryan to clarify what he meant by the messiah, but he didn’t answer. It seemed to be an annoying habit of his. There was a strange energy in the air, as if we were a bunch of kids looking at something we weren't suppose to be looking at. “He killed himself?” Bo said, his voice trembling.

Ryan pressed a button on the remote, and the screen cut to a recording of the stars at night, the dark trees tops swaying beneath. It was still grainy, but this one was higher quality. “Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you. Cleanse your hands, you sinners. And purify your hearts, you double souled.... If anyone loves me, he shall keep my word. And my Father will love him, and we will come to him, and we will make our dwelling place within him.... Your face, O Lord, I seek. Hide not your face from me.”

It looked like…  it looked an angel descending or like the Second fucking Coming. Cody, Bryan, and Ryan turned to me. I ran over to Bo and shoved my face into his chest. He hugged me with one arm, while continuing to stare at the screen.

“We need to get out there,” Cody said.

“But which way?” Ryan said.

“Won’t we just see it if we look around?” Cody said.

“Pause the video,” Bryan said. “That’s the North Star there. Yeah, see, there’s the Big Dipper. And there’s the Little Dipper. So, if we walk north—”

“You couldn’t pay me to step foot in those woods,” Bo said. “Hell no!”

“Come on, man,” Cody said. “I thought you were one of us, a Lone Gunmen.”

“I said I dabbled, bro. That means watching Youtube clips from the comfort of my well-lit apartment with street lights and a Dunkin Donuts within a one-block radius.”

“This is history in the making,” he said. “This is what separates Werner Herzog from an amateur influencer who’s also a part-time hostess at TGI Fridays.”

I stared at his ridiculous gear. My anger overrode my fear as the words sunk in. “For your information, I quit TGI Fridays four months ago.”

I stood on the porch, overwhelmed again by the cacophony of the spring peepers. I shivered and looked up at the night sky, the moon bright to the west. I gulped and pressed record on my phone. The guys were geared up beneath me, the camera with the huge lens hanging from my cousin’s neck. They were standing in the grass, looking up, and pointing. 

“I’m recording,” I said.

“I don’t see anything unusual,” Bryan said.

“What’s that?”

“A satellite.”

They walked around the cabin towards the woods behind it. I turned. Bo was standing in the doorway, a quilt draped over his shoulders.

“You remember why you came here?” I called up to him. “To prove you were better than them. How do you think this is going to end if you stay in the cabin?”

I turned away, shaking my head with frustration.

“Ellie,” he called out to me. I turned, suddenly hopeful. “Don’t include this scene in the doc, alright?”

“Asshole.”

I caught up to the guys as they entered between the trees. They turned their headlamps on. I tried to position myself so I could record without too much glare. The undergrowth was dense in places, mostly saplings, dead ferns, and thorny brush. Twigs and dry leaves cracked underfoot. This was some Blair Witch shit. The only thing that would keep me calm was if I kept everybody talking.

“Ryan, why didn’t uncle Ron report this, if he had all this proof?”

“A couple of reasons. My uncle used to be a proper hippie, like my aunt, and hated the feds, hated any authority really. Also, before I knew him, he used to be a heroin addict. He eventually swapped this addiction for Jesus Christ. He was kind of annoying about it, always talking about this or that psalm. I think it started the rift in my aunt and uncle’s relationship. Anyway, he started seeing the spinning man sometime after that, and I guess he saw it as a personal blessing or, like, a reward for his faith and not something to advertise to the world.”

“So, your aunt did know about the videos?” Cody asked. 

“I think she sort of knew, but also didn’t want to know. She had already kind of given up on him by then. Like I said, my uncle had lost his mind. He became increasingly paranoid. He kept saying someone was following him.”

“How did he kill himself?” Cody said.

“Bro,” Bryan said.

“It’s fine,” Ryan said. “You’re not going to like the answer though.... He hung himself somewhere in these woods.”

“Fuck,” I said.

We walked in silence, now with equal parts depression and fear, before eventually stopping in a clearing. The guys turned off their headlamps. A chill ran through me as we searched the sky for the spinning man. I knew if I could capture it on camera, it would be incredible, incredible in the general sense but also for my future as a journalist. Deep-down though, I wanted tonight to be as boring as possible. 

A sheet of clouds was rolling in from the southwest as if in answer to my prayers. Maybe it would hide it, if it was actually out there. Something cracked in the distance, and I jumped and cursed out loud.

“Probably a deer,” Ryan said.

We began walking again, Ryan leading the way.

“What do you guys think the spinning man really is?” I said. “You really think it’s a flying alien?”

“I think it’s a four-dimensional being,” Cody said plainly, looking at the camera, the moonlight painting the side of his face. “Do you know about four-dimensional beings?”

“Why would I know about that?” 

“Think about ants,” he said, “or like tiny creatures that can’t see up or down. They can see forward, backward, left, and right. They can spin in a circle, but they can’t see up or down at all. They have no concept of it.”

“Okay.” 

“It’s basically a two-dimensional being.”

“Okay.”

I rolled my eyes. This wasn’t helping my nerves. We had been walking for like fifteen minutes now, and I couldn’t see the cabin at all. All I could see was the trees, bone-white in the headlamps, and the stars pulsing through the black canopy of pine needles overhead.

“Now imagine you poke your finger down right in front of one of these two-dimensional creatures. What does it see? It can’t look up and down. It doesn’t know what that is. It can only see a cross-section of your finger, which to it resembles a flesh-colored wall. Imagine that you push your finger right through the floor of its world. Your finger goes through and then your hand and then your arm. But all this little creature sees is this textured, flesh-colored wall that keeps morphing in length and growing closer or moving further away. It doesn’t understand the full shape of your body. It lacks the ability to see your individual fingers, your hand, or any of you in its entirety. If you were to walk through its world, it would just look like walls teleporting all over the place.”

“You’re freaking me out,” I hissed.

“All I’m saying,” Cody said, “is that a four-dimensional being would appear to us kind of like a three-dimensional being would appear to a two-dimensional being. It would probably look like a blob that shifts in shape and color. It wouldn’t have to follow our laws of space-time. It could float. It could teleport. It could walk through walls... or at least appear to do these things to us.”

“Why would it bother coming here?” I said.

“The same reason we fly to the moon. To explore. To learn.” 

“Or maybe something worse,” Cody said.

“Worse?” I said. 

“Colonization,” my cousin said. “Cortés and the Aztecs.”

“What time is it?” Ryan said.

“11:17—”

A branch snapped. This one too loud and too close.

“Oh shit oh shit.”  

“What the fuck…,” Cody whispered. 

“It’s a deer,” Ryan whispered back. “It’s definitely a deer.”

They turned their headlamps and shone them into the woods. Bugs fluttered into the light. Something moved within the leaves far away, but too small to make that kind of noise.

“What if it’s a bear?” Bryan whispered.

“There are black bear here, but they’re pretty rare. Could be a moose.”

“I don’t want to run into a moose either,” Cody said.

“I better get it out,” Bryan said. 

I recorded him, the gimbal doing its best to stabilize my trembling, as he pulled his backpack off his shoulder and unzipped it. He retrieved a device that looked like a phone with a pistol handle attached to it. 

“Turn your headlamps off,” he whispered. He turned it on. Vivid greens, blues, and yellows filled the device’s screen. “It’s a thermal camera,” he said to me. 

He slowly panned it across the forest, and I tried to focus on it even though my heart was in my throat. Another crack split the air. We all spun around as it came from behind us. I lost focus and then focused again on the thermal camera’s screen. 

My legs weakened. A creature floated in the air with dangling legs, its strange, elongated face watching us. It had to be seven feet tall, the heat radiating off it registered in sickly clown colors. I screamed. I’d never screamed like that in my life. I wasn’t the only one. My legs wouldn’t move. A beam of light swooped down from the sky, burning into my eyes....

“It’s alright,” a man yelled. “It’s alright! I ain’t gonna hurt ya.”

My cousin and his two friends fumbled, swearing, and turned their headlamps back on. The arc of their lights swooped across tree trunks and then steadied on a man deep in the woods, climbing down from some kind of hunter's stand, a small seat ratcheted high up on a maple tree with an attached ladder. He was so high up, I’d confused him for floating on the thermal camera. He was wearing an orange reflective vest and holding a flashlight, which must have been what blinded me. A rifle was slung over his shoulder. He gestured with his giant palms out while walking towards us, as if to convey that he came in peace. The most jarring thing about this scene was that he was insanely tall, like he could’ve played center for the Celtics. Twigs and branches crunched under each of his giant steps.

“Sorry,” he said. “Don’t be alarmed.” He was smiling and blinking, his voice not particularly deep despite his height. “I know, a man in the woods in the dark, very scary. And a tall man? Terrifying! Well, I can’t help being tall now, can I? Ya mind lowerin’ them headlights?”

The guys mumbled and tilted their lamps down. I continued to record. We were momentarily at a loss for words. 

“Who goes hunting at night?” Ryan said, exasperated. His face was completely drained of color, his chest rising and falling. 

“Isn’t that illegal?” Bryan said, equally pale.

“I was chasin’ a coyote,” the man said. “It got half my chickens tonight. The bastard’s been killin’ em all spring. I thought maybe I could spot him from my tree stand.”

“You’re on my aunt’s property,” Ryan said firmly.

“Yer aunt? Who’s that? Rita? Rita O’Reilly?” His Maine accent was thick. He pronounced her name as *Riter.* The man pointed into the darkness. “Rita’s property follows the stone wall.”

“What stone wall? We didn’t see a stone wall.”

“Well, it ain’t much a wall these days. I suppose a farmer stacked it some two hundred years ago, but it’s there if ya look fer it.”

The man lifted an eyebrow, noticing our equipment. “What’re you kids doing out here, anyway?” 

“That's none of your business,” Ryan said.

The man stared down at Ryan and probably could have dropped his giant fist onto the kid’s head and knocked him out cold. Instead, he chuckled. “Yer an O'Reilly alright. Tell Rita I said hi. And a little advice: if yer gonna be playin’ in these woods, ya best be wearin’ huntin’ vests, specially at night. That’s how I know yer a bunch of flatlanders.”

The man turned his flashlight on, pointed it back the way he came, and trudged off. His height was disturbing even from a distance. He looked like Bigfoot dressed in Carhartt. Eventually, the man's light faded and he disappeared through the trees.

“Flatlanders?” Bryan said. 

“Let’s keep going,” Ryan said. “We’ll hit the cliffs soon and have a good view of the sky.”

“But, you heard him. We’re on his property,” I said. I didn't know the rules of the countryside. He could legally blow our brains out with that rifle on the grounds of trespassing, for all I knew.

“Screw that guy," Ryan said. "Nobody owns the trees.”
 
He led the way, and I reluctantly followed. I hoped the phone stabilization was working, because my whole body was shaking. I hated to admit it, but I wished Bo was here, not so much to protect me, but so someone could appreciate my fear. 

“Ellie, shouldn’t you be interviewing us?” my cousin asked.

“Cody, shut the fuck up!”

I wish I could say my school project was all peaches and cream from there, but things were about to get so much worse.
