It began when a construction worker saw a dead man hanging in the dark. Around midnight, walking home from work, the laborer swears he saw a man hanging from a streetlight. The body looked a few days old, its skin blue except for the hands, which were red and livid. He said the body bobbed up and down from the rope. It was dressed in a suit and tie and smelled of rotting flesh. And before he could do anything, it was gone.

Around the same time, another man had been on his way home from a bar when he saw something similar. A hanged man, bobbing up and down, but unlike the laborer's report, this one did not disappear. Not immediately, at least. It was a corpse swinging from a tree, hung with thick, fraying rope, smelling of excrement and fleshy decay. This one, according to the report, was fresh and, yes, still alive. The man was struggling against the taut rope with bloodied fingers. As the witness got closer, the man, with a snap, went limp like a sack of rice as a great force heaved the rope upward, pulling the body into the dark of the trees. There was no body in the morning. In a town this small, everyone heard by morning 

“I've seen this one before, officer,” said one of the drunks in the holding cell, who had overheard me talking to one of the lieutenants about the reports. “It's the Danglehand! The Danglehand!”

I paid the drunk no mind. But Danglehand or not, reports kept coming in. I was heading out of the precinct for a cold night on patrol. As I stepped out, I was met by a long line of families there to report a missing person. Eighteen people or so standing in the frosty night. 

“Please, officer! My son’s been missing for two days!” pleaded one old frail woman, eyes sore and hands shaking at the mention of her missing boy. 

I told her to fuck off in police speak. That is to say, “yes ma’am, I understand. Please take this up with the officers inside.” before taking a long walk to the boulevard for my patrol. That was when I saw it for myself.

It was ten in the evening and the streets were dead, and frozen over. I was alone, sitting on the curb when I saw something out of the corner of my eye. There, under the piss colored sodium-vapor streetlights of an empty street, was a procession of hanged men. 

Five long ropes descending from the sky paraded five bodies, each one blue and red in the wrong places, smelling of sweet rot and dried shit. The ropes seemed to come from high above the clouds, bobbing the bodies up and down like a fucked-up carousel. One of the bodies seemed to bob more than the others though. It was smaller too. It was –he was alive. It was a boy. No older than ten, wearing a red shirt and cargo shorts. Alive. Hanging. There were red streaks on the rope wrapped around his neck, his fingers slick with blood, his fingernails split and dangling on his fingertips by thin hangnails. I watched in frozen horror as the procession passed me by, the boy struggling against the rope with all the frantic terror of a fish on a hook, his eyes full of fear and wet with tears.

I sprinted to the boy, the sour stench of death thick in the air. I cried for help across the streets, my voice echoing amidst the closed stores and quiet houses. No one answered. I was on the kid, my hands like vicegrips against the rope strung around his neck, but it was no use. My hands slipped on the blood that drenched the rope, my fingers digging into the raw gashes on his neck, but it would not budge. I gave up on the rope and held the boy up by his feet, hoping he could catch his breath, but the rope was too tight, and he wheezed terribly against the taut, frayed cord. Then the rope began to rise, slowly at first, and kept rising until his feet were over my head. I held my arms up just to give the boy some leverage, but I could hear his wheeze turn into choked gasping. I kept crying for help, shouting out into the empty boulevard until my throat felt raw and numb. Eventually, he rose beyond my reach, and into the sky, as did the other bodies. 

I ran back to the station, having pissed myself thoroughly, crying, “I saw it! I fucking saw it! Oh Jesus!” None of my colleagues believed me, though, to their credit, they were beyond worried. I wouldn't hear the drunk speak again until later that night.

“It's the Danglehand, I tell you. The Danglehand! Won't be long 'til this whole town's empty!”

“Fuck off!” I cried. 

“The Danglehand!” barked the man through his holding cell, “Listen to me, officer, listen to me! There’s not much time!”

I took that man by the collar and beat him until he went quiet. I was afraid. Someone had to be more afraid. Had to be him. I took my baton and sent it hurtling into his face. It hurt my hand. I sent it down again and again and again, ringing out heavy thuds against his skull. *How about that huh? How about I dangle you from this cell, you fucking dog?* I shoved the baton down his mouth and smacked its butt with my open palm, sending a geyser of blood out his nose.

The beating ended in the early morning once my hands were raw and the man was at the edge of consciousness. Not one of my colleagues said a thing. I washed myself and set out for a long drive. As I did, I saw that the line outside the station had only grown longer. A thrum of people waiting out to file a report, photographs in hand. Worried mothers, worried fathers, thinking that their child was anything but hanging from an impossible rope. People crowded my car as I pulled out of the station. 

“Please sir! My son! Have you started looking?” One woman asked, eyes restless and shaking all over. “He looks like this, see?” She pressed a photograph of a young boy in a red shirt and cargo shorts against my windshield. I stepped on the gas, sending her tumbling onto the street. 

Days passed. Or maybe it was hours. Maybe this all happened yesterday, or –god willing– never happened at all. Hanged men and missing persons were all everyone could talk about. You could tell immediately who was there to report a missing person and who was there to report a hanging. It was in their eyes. There was– is– an indelible mark on the people who’ve seen it.

I still see that boy in my dreams. I see him scratching at his neck and bleeding, struggling against that damn rope. And so I went to see the drunk. It wasn't hard to track him down; drunks are creatures of habit, after all. I found him bent double over a pint of beer, readying himself for another cold night.

“Evening, officer,” he muttered. His eyes were black, his lips busted open, his head wrapped in bandages. I said nothing of our night before and asked him about the Danglehand.

“It's too late. But don't you worry your little noggin, officer. In a few days, you'll stop hearing about hanged men.” He took a long swig of his drink. “They'll disappear. All of 'em.” That last part he muttered to himself.

“Speak normally, drunk.”

“Or what?” he spat. “Gonna shove something *else* up my mouth?”

“You want me to apologize?” I leaned in close. “Well, I’m sorry. Okay?”

“Oh? You’re sorry? For beating me blind?” The other bar patrons were getting restless in their seats, seeing a man in uniform get antsy with a bruised up hobo. “Don’t bother. You’ll be on one of those ropes too, soon enough.”

“I saw it.” I said quietly. “Five of them. A boy, he was alive. The rope was so high and just– just, oh god.” I said feeling terrible fear crawl up my spine. “He was still alive.”

“Alive?!” The drunk leaned in closer, close enough that I could smell the beer in his breath. “Did the boy talk?” he asked. 

“Talk? No.”

  

  

“But you’re sure he was still alive?”

“Uh–”

“Damnit!” He smacked down the table hard. The fellows sitting near us were about ready to run. “Are. You. Sure?”

“Yes!” I screamed, “Yes! The boy was still alive. He was clawing away at the rope around his neck for god sake. His nails were ripped open and his– his– oh fuck, oh jesus…” I said nearly fainting at the recollection.

“Shit.”Hissed the drunk. After a long pause, he said, “Shit! Listen, officer. Out of the kindness of my heart, let me give you some advice: skip town. Especially once you stop hearing about hanged men.” With that the drunk stumbled to his feet and hurriedly walked out of the bar, as I sat there gasping for air, clutching at my chest, my vision blurring to black.

  

I didn’t quite understand what he meant. But that didn’t matter. Work went on, and I was beginning to believe that I was just crazy. But then, one day, the reports of hanging people just stopped.

Once they did, we went back to our usual routine. Of course, the situation was anything but usual. There was a curfew now at six, and at night the streets were deserted, save for the roaming neighborhood watches and the police. People kept disappearing anyway. 

 I was back on patrol on the boulevard when, out of the corner of my eye, I saw it. A child walking alone. It was midnight by then, but that wasn't what I found strange. The kid was familiar. Too familiar.

He ran up to me in a shuffling gait I would not describe as human. His eyes were unsure of where to look, his jaw slack, his joints bending strangely, like a dog walking upright. With a start, he screamed, “Mom! Mom! There's weird people floating on the street!” The boy's arm rose crooked as it pointed to a dark alley. He smelled awful, like sweet rot and dried shit. When I backed away, it began to jump up and down, screaming the same words in the same tone. “Mom! Mom! There's weird people floating on the street!”

I locked myself in the precinct until morning, praying. They found me asleep in a corner, my face dried with snot and tears. “It was that boy!” I cried. “I saw him! It was him, I swear!”

Reports of missing persons kept trickling in. A hundred became two hundred, then three, all in the span of a month. There was a house I often passed on my patrols, a little bungalow that housed a sex offender and his wife. One day their lights went out and never came back on.

The streets were always empty during my patrols. People had become terrified of the night, and in the daytime all anyone talked about was the disappearances. Empty streets offered me no relief. Every night I could see it: ropes from high above, dangling down and hovering over the streets. Or maybe there weren't any. Maybe I was going insane.

I met up with the drunk again. Creature of habit. I was, too.

“Not much I can tell you, officer,” said the drunk as his piss trickled down the urinal.

"But you know what it is?"

“I do.” He zipped up and did not wash. “I'll tell you something from the goodness of my heart, officer: leave this town. There's nothing you can do now.”

He handed me a pack of cigarettes. Inside was cigarette ash mixed with something else, something that smelled like yeast and chewing gum.

“Next time you see it, throw this. You'll see what I mean.”

Days passed, and the streets grew emptier and emptier. The boulevard was quiet even at dusk.

A man came up to me on one of my patrols. An office worker, by the look of him. He looked like he hadn't slept in days.

“Excuse me, sir, can you tell me the time?” he asked.

*Sure. It's twelve past midnight.*

“Can. Time? Tell me. Excuse me, sir.”

*What?*

“Oh my god, what is that outside? Honey, I think there's someone hanging out there. Out there. God. Outside.”

Without another thought, I threw the pack of ash at the man, and that's when I saw it. As the ash settled, I could see that the man was dead, hanging limp. On his neck was a thick rope, and high above, I could see it: a black arm, blacker than the night sky, as long as a highway but as thin as a branch, stretched across the sky and holding a single rope. I dared not imagine what the arm belonged to.

“Outside. Time. God. Outside.” it said, each word coming out in garbled tones. I took out my pistol and emptied round after round after round, the body thudding as bullets thundered into dead flesh, but it kept speaking to me, its mouth gaping open like a fish out of water. *Outside. Time. Oh my god.* I ran. I kept running and running, mad and frenzied, as I laughed and laughed at the funniest thoughts. I could see them in the sky, hundreds of arms spread out like the branches of a tree, each hand a black outline against the night sky, each one holding delicately in its strange thumb and finger a frayed old rope.

Nights went on like this. I could see them now, the arms. There were too many to count.

“You see it too now, yes?” said the drunk. I was with him once again, watching him roll up his tent.

"Where are you going?" I asked.

“Away from all of this. There's nothing more we can do.”

With that, the man put all his earthly possessions into a bag and left. I opened my mouth to say something, but he was already walking.

  

I stayed. I stayed as the precinct emptied out, as the lines outside grew shorter and shorter. I stayed as the arms filled more of the sky each night, as the thousands of ropes began eclipsing the stars. Soon the streets were empty in the mornings, and at night I saw everyone out and about. Everyone. Even that little boy.

This will be the last anyone hears of me.
