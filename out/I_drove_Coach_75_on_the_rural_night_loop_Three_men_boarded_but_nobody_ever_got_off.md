The third-shift rule on the rural transit routes is simple: you call in to dispatch every fifteen minutes past midnight. If you miss two check-ins in a row, company policy says your bus is disabled, and they send county road patrol out to your last known mile marker.  
I was running the late loop on State Route 44 through the western Pennsylvania hollows. It was about quarter to two in the morning, raining hard enough that you could barely read the reflective signs, and the heater in Coach 75 was blowing cold air straight onto my boots. My passenger load was almost nothing. An older woman was sitting three rows behind me, a kid with wired headphones had his forehead resting against the side window, and a young couple sat together near the back doors.  
At ten to two, I keyed the mic to report my position just past the old lumber cut. Dispatch didn't answer, just flat static. The channel stayed jammed, and the needle on the transceiver stayed pegged hard to the right. I hit the dashboard casing with the heel of my hand, hung the mic back on the bracket, and kept driving. Two miles down the road, the bell dinged. The couple had pulled the cord. I swung onto the shoulder, tripped the air switch, and let them off near the dirt access road. That left two people behind me.  
Right at the fork for the pass, three figures were standing directly on the double yellow line. No safety vests, no flashlights, nothing. Two of them had the third by the armpits, dragging his boots through the wet blacktop. Company rules say you don't pick up passengers outside marked zones, but on a two-lane road with no shoulder, leaving people in the middle of a blind curve means a fatal accident. I hit the air brakes, stopped about ten feet off their front, and cracked the doors.  
They walked up the steps without looking in my direction. The two on the outside moved in dead, stiff sync, carrying the dead weight of the guy in the middle. They wore heavy wool work coats that looked old, completely soaked through and smelling of stagnant ditch water. They dragged him down the aisle toward the back bench. Nobody spoke. Nobody tapped a transit pass or dropped change into the fare box. The two men sat on the outside and propped the middle guy up between them. His head hung straight down against his chest, totally motionless.  
I put the coach in drive and pulled back into the lane. The minute I did, the overhead fluorescent bulbs snapped off with a sharp pop. The entire cabin went pitch dark, leaving just the green dials on my dash and the amber headlights cutting through the downpour. I checked the wide overhead mirror. The back row was completely black.  
I grabbed my cell off the dash mount. No bars, just searching. I reached down and twisted the volume knob on the radio, trying to find a clear channel. The speaker crackled once, and then a voice came through over the dead air. It was my own voice. It was repeating the exact words I had said twenty minutes earlier at the lumber cut, completely flat: Coach 75 passing Mile Marker 18, running smooth. Same pitch, same background engine hum, repeating every five seconds on a loop.  
Behind me, the older woman stood up. She walked right up to the front, grabbed the stanchion by the coin box, and started screaming at the kid with the headphones. She said he'd dug through her purse and stolen her wallet while she was looking out the window. Her voice was harsh, loud, and weirdly steady. The kid yanked his headphones off, swore he hadn't touched a thing, and came stomping up the aisle toward us. The woman pointed right at the front doors and told me to pull over right that second and let them out to find a trooper.  
I didn't argue. I hit the brakes, pulled onto the gravel by an abandoned state weigh station, and dumped the air valve. The doors blew open. The woman shoved the kid down the steps into the dirt, walked out right behind him, and turned around to look back at the bus. She didn't look angry anymore. Her face was completely blank. She gave me a short, flat wave to close the doors.  
I shut the valve. The latch locked into place. I turned around and looked toward the back bench to see how the three men in the coats were handling the noise.  
The back seat was empty.  
Nobody had moved down the aisle. There hadn't been any sound of wet fabric or boots on the rubber floorboards. The front doors had stayed shut until I opened them, and the rear emergency door was still sealed tight with the red plastic security tag unbroken. Every slide window on both sides of the bus was latched shut. I got up from the driver's seat, pulled my heavy Maglite off the floor clip, and walked the aisle. I checked under every vinyl seat. There was no water dripping on the floor, no mud tracked down the walkway, and no personal bags left behind.  
I went back to the front, put the bus in reverse, and backed off the gravel. When the high beams swung across the timber line behind the old weigh scale, the lights caught a shape between two hemlock trunks fifty yards back in the brush. A tall, solid silhouette in a dark overcoat, facing straight toward the road. It didn't flinch, put a hand up against the high beams, or move a single muscle. It just stood there until the front end swung around and the headlights pointed back at the blacktop.  
I didn't let off the throttle for the rest of the run. The dash instruments started acting up: the fuel gauge climbed until the needle was pinned past full, and the temp needle dropped down into the cold peg, even though the engine doghouse next to my knee was hot to the touch. The radio stayed dead the whole way.  
At 3:10 AM, I pulled into the maintenance yard in Brookville. The gate was unlocked, and the night mechanic was sitting in the bay office drinking coffee with a clipboard. I parked in Bay 4, cut the ignition, and wrote down the mechanical problems on the daily log: failed cabin lights, broken radio, erratic gauges.  
The mechanic took a flashlight and walked the outside of the bus while I packed up my lunch bag and logbook. He checked the duals, tapped the air tanks, and then stopped at the fuel door on the driver's side. He unscrewed the brass cap, dropped the long metal dipstick into the tank, pulled it out, and held his light to it.  
He asked me where I'd stopped for fuel on the route. I told him I hadn't; we took the coach out on a half tank of depot diesel at the start of the shift.  
He wiped the dipstick with his thumb and wiped it onto the concrete floor under the shop lights. The fluid coming off the metal was dark brown, thick, and sticky. It smelled foul, with zero diesel odor to it. When the shop lead came out and cracked the bottom petcock on the fuel tank into a metal drain pan, the fluid poured out heavy and thick onto the shop floor.  
State Police pulled the highway cameras later that day. The footage from the toll barrier showed Coach 75 passing through at 1:30 AM with four passengers clearly visible through the glass. Nine miles down the road, at the camera mounted on the river bridge, the bus showed up on the sensor recording at 1:52 AM. The cabin was completely dark, the driver's seat was empty, and a figure was sitting upright in the front passenger seat, staring straight out into the dark.  
I handed my route keys to the depot manager that morning and didn't sign up for the next roster. The company retired Coach 75 from service two weeks later, writing off the coach for unresolved electrical harness failures.
