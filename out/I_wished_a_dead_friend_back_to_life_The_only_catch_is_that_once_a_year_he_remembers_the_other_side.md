There was no predicting the days when Dave remembered what it was like to be dead. It wasn’t like an anniversary that I could mark on a calendar, nor did the days start or end with a given time like midnight or sunrise. It was just that at some point during any given calendar year, there would be exactly one 24 hour block of time where Dave remembered what it felt like to not exist in this world. What that meant was difficult to know ahead of time. Sometimes he’d be so sad he would lie catatonic in his own filth for the entire day. Other times he’d be so violent, I couldn’t even get near him. He might barricade himself in the attic and hurl old boxes at me while screaming obscenities. But those were good days because if you could see it coming, you at least had a chance of avoiding the worst.

One morning, not long after it first happened, I picked Dave up for work and as he climbed into my truck he passed me a thermos which I slid into the cup holder. It wasn’t even 6am and it was already hot as hell, and we shot the shit during the long drive to the site where we worked helping pour concrete foundations. He told me all about getting invaded during Elden Ring by some guy called CumSquid, and he said that just seeing the name made him laugh so hard he could barely hold the controller. Even just retelling the story got him bent over with giggles, his head kissing the dashboard, and pretty soon I was cracking up too. Didn’t matter how stupid it was, Dave had a way of making me laugh till my ribs hurt. It was one of the reasons I loved him.

We were about two miles out from the site when he asked what I thought of the coffee. I told him I wasn’t much in the mood for a hot drink, and I had the quiet realisation that the thermos hadn’t been warm when he’d handed it to me.

“Drink it,” he said, and he smiled like someone who’s just given you a dirty birthday card.

“What’s in it?” I asked nervously.

“Just drink it,” he replied while knuckling my shoulder playfully. I figured it might be a prank, so I picked it up and gave it a shake. Something wet sounding slapped around in there.

“What the fuck’s in here bro?” I joked. “I ain’t gonna–”

That was when he undid the seatbelt and rolled out the door. I was doing about 30mph on a dirt road. I cried out but he was already in the rearview mirror, rolling in a dust cloud. David was a big guy, easily 300 lbs and over six feet, but he wasted no time getting back onto his feet and running like hellfire. I realised it was one of *those* days and dread hit me like a punch to the sternum.

He’d covered a surprising distance by the time I pulled the truck over and got out, but I was a lot fitter than Dave. Catching up to him wasn’t the problem. It was bringing him down.

“Don’t!” he screamed as I leapt on his back. He buckled under the extra weight, and went down onto one knee, big hands slapping furiously at me like I was a fly. “Don’t!” He repeated in a harsh bark. “Stop! God! Stop! Jesus fucking Christ Gene, stop! Why? You don’t… you don’t…”

I was pulling at his head. Even hooked a finger in his nose at one point. Not really sure what I wanted to do. I just knew I needed to stop him.

“*It hurts so fucking bad!*” He shrieked, and if you’d been walking past, you would’ve thought I was skinning him alive. “*Oh sweet fucking Jesus it hurts Gene! Oh shit Christ almighty it fucking hurts so bad. Every fucking second. Every last fucking one!*”

He was down on both his knees now, no longer fighting me but just staying there, hands clenched hard around both sides of his head. Squeezing tight like he was trying to push his own brains out his ears. He was turning red. Veins bulging along his neck and forehead. I just kept holding him tight as I could, ready for him to leap up in another explosive escape attempt. His whole body was shivering like an engine about to shake itself to pieces when suddenly all the energy left him. He just relaxed, and his arms fell to his side and his head lolled forward. Like a toy after the batteries go dead.

“Ah shit Gene,” he mewed, drooling blood down the puffy bottom lip he must’ve bitten into at some point. “It ain’t gonna stop is it?”

“It’s just one day,” I said, holding him as tight as I could. “Just one day buddy. Tomorrow you won’t remember.”

He hit the floor and lay there with his eyes open, crying into the dirt. Trusting him not to get up again, I got my truck, then hauled him in like some oversized toddler. I quickly rang our supervisor and explained we were both sick.

Half-way back Dave took out the thermos and emptied it into his hand. There was something little in there. A stillborn thing not much bigger than a thumb, drowning in what looked like blood and oil.

“What the fuck is that?” I cried out in disgust.

“You learn a lot on the other side,” Dave said with a childish sniff. “I know lots and lotsa stuff Gene. I know how to do all sorts. And it’s so easy to do once you know how. You should ask me ‘bout it some time.”

“I don’t wanna fucking know,” I replied, fighting to get a good look and keep the truck steady at the same time. It looked like he was holding a kidney bean with a face that was all scrunched up.

Slowly, it opened its pale white eyes and blinked them one at a time in the early morning sun.

“Yeah… well…” Dave shrugged and clenched his fist just as that abomination began to cry, crushing it into a slimy paste that slid out between his knuckles.

Then he stuffed the rest greedily into his mouth and told me with stained teeth,

*“You will.”*

I brought Dave back because I loved him and because I was young and I hadn’t experienced grief like that before. It wasn’t so bad in the day, but at night I’d dream and it was only then I really got a sense of just how big the pain was. In my dreams I’d go to pick him up to go to a party and he’d never open his front door. Or we’d be back in school and he’d be talking to me about my parents fighting and then I’d turn away and look back and he’d be gone. And then it’d hit me. The knowledge that he was gone forever and it was irreversible and I would have to just keep going. Keep driving. Eating. Sleeping. Shitting. All of it in a world without my best friend. And in the dream I’d start sobbing. Screaming. I’d fall to my knees and start wailing like a banshee. Like an animal. And it’d feel so real that my throat would sting and my chest would ache and then I’d wake up and there’d be a whisper of pain just above my Adam’s apple but that was all. I’d touch my cheeks and they’d be dry. My eyes would be clear. No tears.

The sadness was too big to let out, so it just stayed inside where I’d have to wait for it to erode like a boulder on the beach. And it’d take millennia to break that thing down, but if I ever tried letting it out in the real world, I’d unravel like a piece of twine.

I figured after I’d resurrected him, the dreams would stop. But they didn’t. I had the same ones over and over, every night. Only when I woke up, I didn’t feel any relief knowing Dave was still with us. If anything, the moment I realised what I’d done, I’d be full of fear and that fear would follow me all day, every day, because looking after Dave was a full time job. His parents actually paid me to be his carer cause they loved him and wanted him looked after but couldn’t stand to be around him for longer than an hour or two. Part of that was just the emotional turmoil of seeing him come through the door after spending nearly a week funeral planning. But part of their unease was because of how, one Christmas, he brought the cooked turkey back to life as a kind of parlour trick. It was bad enough seeing the meat on our plates start to wriggle and shiver, but Dave deliberately waited until after we’d started eating.

I think if I’d ever asked Dave *why* he did these things, he would have told me. But only if I asked which I never did. I didn’t want to know what was on the other side. Maybe that was selfish. Maybe we could have shared the burden, but I’d seen what happened to people Dave spoke to during his episodes and it scared me shitless. Dave never killed anyone. He just told them things, but that was enough. It ate them from the inside out, or sometimes it ate the people around them. There were times I’d think how can *knowing* something be so bad for you, and that would almost make me curious. But that curiosity never lasted long. Every time I got close to knowing, I got too scared and looked away.

Dave took a little girl from a shopping mall once. We were at the food court and he went to the toilet and I didn’t even realise he’d been gone too long until I’d finished my burger and fries and started sipping my coke. Told myself he probably just had stomach trouble when I saw a couple cops gathering in one corner. They were with this woman who was rubbing her damp cheeks with a curled first. I couldn’t hear what she was saying but she spoke quickly, and over time more cops arrived until eventually, one of them stood on a table and cried out,

*“Has anyone seen a little girl? She’s about nine years old, yay tall, brown hair. She’s wearing a yellow sweater–”*

A sinkhole opened up inside me, and when I snuck out and checked each stall in the foodcourt toilet and saw that Dave wasn’t in any of them, that hole opened even further and it was as if bits of my mind went tumbling into the dark.

I joined the search, and I felt guilty as I went about looking. Me and a dozen other people poking our heads into shops and doing laps of the parking lot. I thought maybe I should say something, but what? What *was* Dave, exactly? What would I tell them? How could I explain it?

*He knows things. He wouldn’t do anything. Not like that. Not like you’re thinking. But we don’t want him talking to your daughter for any longer than is possible because his brain is full of poison.*

I wasn’t looking for more than an hour, but it was the longest hour of my life. Under cars. On roofs. Storage areas. I finally found them in the cinema. An empty screening of Finding Dory playing at 10am on a weekday. To the right of the screen was a fire door and it was open, which I found suspicious. It led me into a long and dusty corridor meant to only be used as a fire exit. Halfway down it, beneath a flickering fluorescent, Dave stood with his back to me. I felt relieved to have found him but it was clear something was wrong with him. I walked, then ran towards him, crying out his name. But no matter how loud or desperate my pleas were, he didn’t turn to face me until I finally reached him and put a hand on his shoulder and spun him round myself.

He was drooling and staring vacantly at a chalk rectangle he’d drawn on the wall.

“Dave,” I said. “What the fuck did you–”

The wall moved.

It was as if the chalk rectangle was a door swinging on a hinge. It shouldn’t have been. *Couldn’t* have been. It was just a drawing. But something Dave had done meant that it was also a doorway and it opened, and I remember looking through it and feeling like I was looking down off a tall building. But that’s all I remember. The sensation of great height and depth. Of a whole world laid out at my feet and I was tilting down down *down*, looking at it and feeling gravity starting to pull at my hair and my neck.

Before I could slip and fall down into that hole, Dave hit me. Hard. And when I woke up I was lying on a stretcher with a bandage round my head. Dave was smiling and talking to some police, and a little girl was crying in her mother’s arms.

“Well it’s lucky you found her when you did,” one of the officers was saying, and Dave quickly replied,

“Thank my friend. He’s the one who heard her calling for help. And he was the one who got the door open. Ran into it as hard as he could. Just a shame he took such a knock to the head doing it.” 

I touched the bridge of my nose and felt it was broken. All around me everyone was laughing and patting each other on the back. The girl’s mother was crying but smiling, and the girl was smiling too. And while all the grown ups around her were busy describing their relief, she looked at Dave and smiled admiringly. And when Dave knew that no one else was looking he smiled back.

Both their hands had chalk on them.

Two years later, that girl’s house burned to the ground and they only found the parents’ bodies. It was a big story in the local area, and I remember walking past that house out of some kind of morbid curiosity. I don’t know what I thought I might find. By the time I got there a couple months had gone by and the house had been demolished, although they hadn’t finished clearing the rubble away.

Standing for a moment on the curb outside, I found that something about the ruins made me want to try and get a closer look. The gate was locked, so I did a quick circle of the barren lot. And I briefly considered jumping the fence since there was no one around on account of the rain. But that urge to go poking round died real fast when I saw that among the patchwork masonry and charred timber beams, there stood a single wall.

Someone had drawn a door on it using chalk, only this one wasn’t blank like the one I’d found behind the cinema. There were two little child-like figures inside it, and for all that I wanted to run I stayed for a few terrifying seconds. Staring. Transfixed by their smiles. Their knowing expressions, and the sinister intent in the crude but effective drawing of their cartoonish faces. A little boy and a little girl smiling and holding hands.

When I finally pulled my gaze away, I looked over my shoulder instinctively feeling as if someone was standing right behind me. Of course, no one was there.

But when I looked back towards the wall, the two figures were no longer holding hands. They were now facing me and waving. And the longer I stared in that terrible, heavy downpour, the more I could’ve sworn that the drawings moved in the flickering rain. Their hands waving back and forth. *Back and forth.*

And around the door, there grew a slither of light, as if behind it glowed some great light and the door was slowly opening.

I fled before I could see anything else.

March last year Dave had an episode, and he spent most of it in his room while muttering obscenities and occasionally sobbing. He’d done this before, so it wasn’t that strange. If anything it was a relief to have him signpost the upcoming episode by screaming like a stuck pig at 8 am and barricading his door with the wardrobe. All I could do was sit outside on a chair and keep him company best I could until the clock ran out the next day. When that happened he finally fell asleep and woke up eight hours later a little confused about why his room was a mess, but otherwise he was back to himself.

After fifteen years, we’d fallen into a rhythm. Dave understood he had blackouts. He’d even asked me about it once. Asked me why sometimes, once in a while, he seemed to sleep the entire day away.

“Maybe it’s a type of epilepsy,” he said. But he didn’t sound convinced, and who could blame him?

“Maybe,” I said.

“Maybe I should start journaling or something,” he said. “ A diary. Might help me keep track of any lost time in case it ever gets so bad I gotta see a doctor.”

“Why not?” I replied, just hoping the conversation would end.

After that, he didn’t ask and I didn’t tell. Every time he had an episode, we’d just clean up together and then keep on living our lives. By this point there wasn’t much to them. Our friends didn’t stick around and why would they? The last to go was Lucy, who was Dave’s on and off again girlfriend since he was 20. She was the one who’d found him dead from anaphylaxis. A bee sting of all things. He hadn’t even been allergic before then, but sometimes the body just decides it doesn’t like something. I guess she felt guilty because despite everything, she still tried to help out when she could. But in the end even her patience ran out.

The last day I ever spoke to her, it was to guide her as she sobbed into the arms of a weary EMT.

“It can’t be true, Gene” she wailed, hiding her face in her hands. “What he says… Jesus Christ. It can’t be true. *It can’t be fucking true!!”*

I haven’t spoken to her since. Her father, who I have as a friend on Facebook, posts a couple times a week asking for us all to pray for his daughter, hoping she finds her way back to God. I can’t quite bring myself to like the posts. I certainly don’t pray. But I do feel guilty. Prior to that day Lucy didn’t even smoke tobacco. Now, her father tags her long dormant profile in reposts from pages that push clean needle initiatives.

There were no more friends left after that. And Dave was too old to keep going to raves and clubs and buddying up with the latest cohort of freshers. One thing when you’re a college student yourself. It’s another when you’re a thirty year old drop out.

Dave and I had become a team of two. Jobs were pretty thin on the ground for him, and it wasn’t great for me either. I never knew when I’d get some signal telling me I had to stay home, and more than once I bailed from important gigs that left my bosses furious. In the end, I opted for freelance work as a copy writer which wasn’t great, but Dave’s parents also sent some money our way too which helped. And to keep busy, Dave started up an Instagram where he posted some photography. He was good, and he even sold some pieces including a photo of some endangered bird that got put on the wall of a local municipal building.

It was around September that we went camping. Dave had had his episode in March, so I didn’t have to worry until New Year's rolled around. And he’d gotten all worked up about taking some photos of this old growth tree he’d read about online. One of the oldest trees in the country, he told me. So old and tall that its very existence was kept a secret by the rangers, but others had found it before and that’s how Dave knew it was out there.

It seemed as good an excuse as any to get out the house, and we hiked for a few days to try and reach it. It was nice. Kind of. That part of the country was beautiful at that time of year and I often got lost in my thoughts just admiring it all. The exercise was good as well. Dave struggled but I loved it.

But there was a tension between us. It’d been there a while. Maybe it started after there was this fire in Dave’s bedroom which started because he summoned something using bits of old roadkill. A human arm longer than my body came out and I eventually managed to stop it. But when Dave woke up the next day, he was in hospital with smoke damaged lungs and no memory of why or how. And he got real suspicious when he finally went home and found my lighter buried amongst all his burned up clothes.

But there were probably a hundred other times he got suspicious too. He asked after Lucy but I had no answers. Asked after his parents, but I had nothing to say. I’d felt all of this mistrust accumulate over the years, and I was helpless to stop it. But it was only when we were among the trees, left with only our thoughts and the chores that come with camping, that I appreciated just how big the wall standing between us was. We laughed a bit. Joked too. But only a bit.

Nothing like we used to.

And I guess for some reason I can’t put my finger on, I started to wonder about Dave. He was leading us. He’d spent ages sorting out the navigation and I trusted him. Mostly. I had a satellite phone he knew about, and a GPS tracker he didn’t. And I’d provisioned for us to stay out there an extra fortnight if we had to. So I didn’t feel in danger letting him point the way, especially since he stuck to the trails for the first two days. But then, on the third, we broke away from the marked routes and I felt that first real pang of anxiety.

The way he moved through the trees, sidling down steep bankings and climbing over tangled roots. It struck me maybe a good six hours after we’d left the closest trail that Dave was acting like a man with a mission. That wasn’t like Dave. Least not the one who was around 364 days a year. And if he hadn’t had an episode earlier in March, I would’ve been convinced he was having one then.

“You want a break?” I cried out a couple times. And each time he’d smile and look my way and he’d be red as a beetroot and covered in sweat, but he’d shake his head.

“Nah man I’m cool. This is good, yeah? You enjoying this?” 

He wouldn’t even wait for my response before marching on. And as the hours ticked on his pace picked up and up and up until I swear there was a couple minutes where he was lightly jogging across the rugged forest terrain.

“Dave for fuck’s sake!” I cried out when he eventually took a tumble. “Dude you gotta slow down. You okay?”

“Yeah yeah yeah,” he stammered anxiously while getting back up. But then he looked at me and the woods around us and seemed to calm. I thought that he must’ve gauged how scary the place was in the failing light and how dangerous it was. Part of me was hoping that maybe Dave was just having an artistic moment. Creative types are renowned for being a bit weird and Dave could go all in on a hyperfixation when he wanted to.

Maybe seeing the *deep* forest in fading twilight had finally broken him out of that fixation. The trees were crowding in around us, getting denser with every step we took. It wouldn’t be hard to get lost out there, even with all our provisions and safety gear. They say the woods are as cold and dangerous today as they were a couple hundred years ago, but it’s not something you realise until you’re actually out there and find yourself wondering what good the GPS tracker in your backpack is gonna do if you take a trip and break a leg so bad you’re left bleeding out with just minutes to live.

“Shit man,” Dave said while panting and rubbing his hands clean on his shorts. “Maybe we should pitch up for the night?”

“I think that’s a good idea,” I replied and breathed a deep sigh of relief.

That night we cooked, ate, and talked a little. Dave nursed a bottle of beer almost the whole evening, which wasn’t like him. And then he called it a night around 9pm and I was left alone, sitting by the fire and trying to wrap my thoughts around the strange but unmistakable unease that had rattled me to the core.

About an hour later I went to sleep myself and for the first time in fifteen years, I dreamed something different to the usual.

I dreamed of the day he came back. I dreamed of the woman who came to me in my own home and offered me an impossible deal. I remembered it so vividly, the wet hair clinging to her face, like she’d just climbed out of a storm drain. Her sad and quiet warnings. The way my head hurt if she made eye contact. I remembered the things she did to prove to me her powers were real. The way she me read me back my own intrusive thoughts. Her finding a dead bird and bringing it back to life.

Dave could come back, she promised, and she’d even clean his mind the best she could, but it couldn’t be perfect. There had to be some release. Some outlet. One day a year, she told me. That had worked out before. It was tough, but others had lived full and happy lives. And what was one day? I thought. Nothing. Nothing at all if it meant having my best friend back.

I took the deal, and she told me I needed to drive to the morgue and quickly because Dave would find it hard to wake up back in this world. I had to be there to comfort him. And when I got there I found out she was right. The world was rewritten. The rules had changed and nothing would ever be the same again.

Dave, who’d been lying there dead for over a week, waiting for an autopsy as part of some bureaucratic bullshit, was now sitting quietly in the corner and crying. And when I put clothes on him he sobbed and told me what he’d seen. I think, in real life, that I blacked out real quick after that. Although I can’t be sure. I don’t think he got very far telling me those things, at least that’s what I always figured because if you’d put a gun to my head and screamed at me to remember more, I don’t think I could have. I would have told you that Dave mentioned something about how it was a bad dream. How humans were given free reign in an infinite universe but everything we needed was on the same rock as us, and everything else was too far away and it was just all fucking hydrogen anyway.

“Just squint a little,” he said. “And it’s like the low poly backdrop in a videogame.”

And if I’m honest, that alone was more than I ever wanted to know.

But in the dream, I didn’t pass out. *Dave kept going,* and I heard every word\*.\* And I still couldn’t tell you what he said, because as soon as I woke up it was all gone out of my head. But in the dream it felt like someone pouring acid in my ear. Like being vivisected. Like every cell of my body was being tortured. My eyes bulged and my chest burned and it didn’t stop until I finally woke up screaming, my hands covered in blood from where I’d raked fingernails across my bony chest.

Sweat covered and near-delirious from the nightmare, I switched the light on and stumbled out into the absolute dark of the woods at night. There was only the chalky beam of my flashlight to see by, but I soon discovered that Dave’s tent was wide open and he was not inside.

It wasn’t empty though. Dave had left something lying on his bedroll, poorly hidden beneath the blanket.

It was a book.

*His diary.*

I took it and flicked to the final entry and what I read there reduced the world around me to a kind of papery hum. I no longer felt the pain in my chest. Instead I could only hear the hammering of my own heart and the blood rushing through my ears. There were pages and pages of rambling overlapping spidery notes and sketches. Shapes and words and runes that spiraled and danced across the pages in a labyrinthian mess but the same few sentences were repeated over and over and over…

*He is lying to you.*

*I can help you to remember.*

*We can stop his lies.*

*You will remember everything he’s done.*

*It’s not too late to fix this.*

The final page in the diary was just a crude sketch of a strange looking tree and a set of coordinates.

For a moment I stood there in total silence, nearly overwhelmed with helplessness. Dave had abandoned me in the woods. Manipulated me. And not the *other* Dave. The one with a head full of poison. *My* Dave. The one I looked after. The one I lived with and spent all my time with. My friend. My *only* friend. The one I’d paid a terrible price to save from death.

I checked and the coordinates he had written down weren’t far from where I currently stood. Less than an hour’s hike.

I grabbed his diary and went after him.

What’s the oldest tree you’ve seen? Or the biggest? I’m gonna be honest and say I’d never really given it much thought. I marched through the darkness of the woods with the tracker clutched in my hand like it was the only thing keeping me alive because I knew, with some certainty, that if I lost it I’d never leave those woods alive. I couldn’t navigate my way through that maze. If you’d been born in that forest and never left it, you might live your whole life not knowing there was even such a thing as open sky. Just a crawling mass of vines and branches that filled the air all the upwards, forever and ever.

Old growth forest isn’t the kind of thing we’re used to as humans. And it's impossible to truly imagine until you cross the dark woodland floor and feel a sudden kinship for the fish that scuttle along the sandy bottom of the Challenger deep. In that place, you’re just another small thing trying to live under the oppressive weight of an entire world hovering unseen, overhead.

I’d never felt so small in my entire life. At least, not until I found the tree.

I wasn’t out there long before I reached it. And I didn’t need coordinates to know what I’d been looking for. The tree owned the space around it like a spider owns its web. Nothing else grew within fifty feet of its trunk because the ground was a knotted mess of tangled and stony roots that grew out and over the Earth like a suffocating nest of snakes. I recoiled as my flashlight’s beam climbed the trunk upward. Its gnarled and twisted branches, devoid of any leaves or greenery, made me think of bone cancer and fungal protrusions. I felt violated on the Earth’s behalf. Just seeing it made me want to shake like a dog and start scratching at my skin. A tick the size of a skyscraper, made of iron-hard wood and unable to die.

*Off off get it off!*

And it was hollow at the base. A small triangular opening that resembled the kind in a tipi. The bark around it was grey and flaky, and the wood nearly crumbled at the touch. I thought the tree must be diseased and dying, but on some level I knew that by the time that tree finally ceased to exist, a satellite photo of the Earth would be unrecognisable to a modern human. It would outlive me. It would outlive us all. And it was old enough that it had once belonged to an entirely different continent. And yet still, it was *dying*. Both things were true in a way that would have made my head hurt if I took the time to dwell on them, but I didn’t.

I had to find Dave and I could tell from the prints left in the pale ashen floor that he was somewhere inside. So I crawled in after him and found myself in a room larger than most community halls. And in the centre was a stone block about the size of a desk. Seeing it immediately made me understand why the tree had been hidden, and why it would *stay* hidden. Anyone stumbling across it would not have stuck around to take selfies or set up camp. They certainly wouldn’t have the guts to start up a chainsaw or contemplate cutting it down. At best a scientist might make note and think they should come back for future study, but they never would.

The block in the centre of that tree was inimical to human thought and life. It resembled a harsh and unfinished slab of concrete, except its edges blended perfectly with the calcified floor. It gave off a kind of energy that worked its way under the skin. Maybe it was the proportions. Something in the mathematics of its lines and shape. Or maybe it was something to do with the material, which was not quite bone, stone, wood, or concrete, but something else that had qualities of all four. Or maybe it was whatever had once been placed inside, and I was *quite* sure there was something inside.

*I hated it.*

I hated being near it. Hated the way it felt like it’d been placed so deliberately in that exact spot on the ground because if it’d been anywhere else, nothing about it would work. And some part of my brain told me it was *working* but what that was, I didn’t know. It wasn’t a cell phone or a computer or an engine or a pulley. It didn’t belong to the mechanical workings of a world made by humans. It simply existed. And it was *wrong*, and its wrongness was made all the worse by the way it stood brazen in my flashlight. Something like that should be afraid of the light. It should hide itself away. It should be ashamed of its own existence and it should shy away from the light but this thing didn’t.

It just stayed there. Immutable, undeniable, and utterly impossible.

In an instant, I saw it for what it was. A sarcophagus. The knowledge came to me the way perception turns a bunch of lines into a word with semantics and meaning. I don’t know how else to put it. In fact, the thought felt quite alien. Quite otherworldly. Like it belonged to something else. But yes, it was a sarcophagus. Something had been buried in it and the tree was part of the burial in some strange way.

When Dave spoke, I didn’t jump or startle in any way. I simply realised that he’d been standing in one of the dark corners of that empty space, where the tree’s foundations began to swirl into the enormous roots thicker than a man. But I had no energy to be afraid of him. Not anymore. Not with that *thing* lying so close.

“Do you remember?” he asked.

“Dave,” I whispered. “I think we need to go.”

“I thought this place would give me answers.”

“We need to go!” I hissed, unknowingly stepping backwards towards the only exit.

“I knew you’d fucked with my head,” he said in a dull, low voice.

“It isn’t…”

“I knew you’d found some way. I knew nothing was right. Nothing. Not since that night.”

“Dave,” I pleaded. “I will explain everything as best as I can. But can we please leave this place? It isn’t right. I’m scared.”

“I remember,” he said. “I remember everything.”

Even with fear coursing through my body like lightning, my heart sank as I heard the words that confirmed I’d finally failed.

Dave would never be the same. One day a year was bad enough. In fact, it’d been a fucking nightmare, exposing me to the kinds of things that had wore me down and taken years off my life. What could I do with him like that all the time? He couldn’t exist. *Shouldn’t* exist. The Dave I knew. My friend. The laid back stoner who had a bit of a special interest for Scooby Doo and introduced me to a new anime at least twice a year. That guy was dead. Gone forever. Now I was just left with the Dave who couldn’t be trusted unsupervised around a drink.

“Gene,” Dave said with a whimper, and I realised that his face, obscured by the shadows, must be streaked with tears. “Gene, I’m so sorry.” His voice, distorted by the desperate fight against all-out tears, was grotesque and uncomfortable. A grown man speaking like a furious, grieving toddler. “Gene. Don’t you remember? This thing. The energy. That’s what we’re feeling. This whole fucking time we’ve been feeling it. Did you know? I didn’t. We’re here to wake it up.”

Every hair on my body stood on end.

“*I don’t want to wake anything up*,” I said. “Dave let’s just get the fuck out of here.”

“If they’d put the knowledge inside one person’s head,” Dave said. “It would’ve killed them. So they did the next best thing. They split it.”

The tree groaned as its twisting sickly fibres strained under some unseen force. The sound was terrifying. A deep and guttural sound that felt as if the Earth itself was trying to wake up from a bad dream.

“Dave please just walk towards me as slowly as you can.”

“Gene,” he said. “I’m so sorry. I thought you were trying to trick me.”

Dave burst into tears and collapsed to his knees. He seemed helpless in that moment, and without wanting to take another step further into that horrible place, I still ran towards him.

“Dave we gotta go,” I said as I tried to pull him to his feet. “Come on we gotta–”

Weakly, he moaned:

“I thought you were just having another one of your episodes.”

And the blood in my veins froze. My arms went limp and without meaning to, I dropped him to the floor.

“One of *my* episodes?” I whispered.

“But it wasn’t you,” he cried, slowly pulling himself up onto his feet. “Not *just* you. The things you did. Oh God the awful things you did. I thought the missing time was just part of it. Some vile *trick* of yours. And our friends. Our family. None of them can stand us. I thought it was you. *Always you*. But it wasn’t. It was *us*, Gene. They couldn’t stand *us* because of the things *we* did. One day, me. One day, you. Each of us blaming the consequences on the other. Watching our lives shrink down into nothing, and always blaming the other.

“We were tricked, Gene. The knowledge necessary to wake this *thing* would’ve killed a single person. So they split it and put it in both our heads. And now it’s too late.”

The world spun around me.

*One of my episodes…*

“Dave,” I said, my voice a stammering mess. “Dave what the fuck are you going on about?”

“I remember everything now.” He smiled like a brain-damaged toddler. “I can’t help it.”

“We need to get out of here before whatever’s inside that fucking thing wakes up,” I hissed, determined to push any thoughts irrelevant to survival out of my head.

“What are you on about?” Dave cackled and pointed behind me. I turned and saw that from where I now stood, where Dave had been standing the whole time, there was a gaping hole in the side of that terrible stone block.

*“It’s been awake this entire time.”*

My skin went cold. My eyes focused. I looked around and for the first time I saw *it*. A shape. A person. A thing. Standing at the far end of the empty hollow. I was overcome by the bone deep knowledge that we were not alone. I think it must be what a mouse feels after waking up and looking into the eyes of a cat that’s dragged it inside for sport. I was paralysed, quite literally, by the terror. Millions and millions of years of inherited instinct instilled in me the powerful urge to stay *perfectly* still.

“It’s only alive because we are,” Dave groaned. He was a spluttering mess on the ground. “Kill me,” he mewed. “Jesus Christ just kill me and put it back.”

He gripped my ankle and looked up at me with pleading eyes.

“We can’t let it out, Gene. It’s wrong. So *so* wrong. Only one of us has to die. And then it’ll be back inside its hole.”

I looked at it once more and my mouth filled with blood. It wasn’t yet real. Not quite. But it was getting there. Getting close. Coming through. Man-shaped, for now. And looking at me with a kind of eagerness that made my skin crawl. It was excited. Curious. The way it looked at me felt like a kind of lust but not for sex. Something else. Something vile. And I felt it reach into my head and unlock something terrible and at last, *I remembered.*

I remembered dying, and I remembered coming back. It had been before Dave, which surprised me on some dim level of consciousness. I’d died first. And he’d brought me back. The same woman who came to me had come to him.

Jesus Christ.

I’d only been sixteen.

But that didn’t matter. I didn’t matter. Nothing mattered. Not compared to the knowledge of the other side. There is no time there. The suffering is so great that if you took all the pain in the world right now, it’d be just a grain of sand being compared to the Sahara of what awaits us after death.

And a piece of *that* was coming into our world.

Blood and mucus erupted out of every hole in my body. The knowledge in my head was making nearly every membrane in my body break down. Even just a few more minutes in that place would’ve killed me. Time was running out.

*“Please. Please please please please… you’ve gotta do it. I’m not strong enough. You’ve got to kill me.”*

Dave lay at my feet sobbing.

And when I made my final decision, I was sobbing too.

\-

Three days ago he came to visit.

Outside of the tree, all our knowledge of the other side seemed to sort of… blot out. Like soaking movie film in acetone. It just *dissolved*. Of course we still get episodes. Twenty four hours where we do remember and nothing can stop those. Working together and with the help of his diary that was full of notes, Dave and I figured out a way to predict our episodes in advance. And because no one else alive would ever understand, we agreed to help each other through them.

But those are the only times I see him. He couldn’t accept the choice I made. Couldn’t accept what I left to fester inside that horrible place because I refused to kill him.

“I saw enough to know it won’t do anything for at least another thousand years,” I told him. “Whatever came through is patient. Global warming is more likely to kill us first.”

“It’s like letting a serial killer into a kindergarten,” he replied. “And telling me it’s okay because they’re taking their time getting everything ready.”

We still can’t bring ourselves to see eye-to-eye on any of it.

And at night I still have my dreams where I scream for what feels like almost the whole night. Only now I know why I still grieve him.

Dave and I are close, but I don’t think he will ever call me a friend again.
