In this house, Halloween is a religion; my wife and I its benevolent gods, our two children our acolytes. Every year all the leftover craft supplies from Lauren’s teaching job are “sacrificed”, and we scatter the remains in the front yard in the form of homemade decorations and little games for trick-or-treaters. Hell, one year I turned an old wire spool on the side of the road into a “Wheel of Doom” for kids to spin for various candy-related prizes. We go hard in the pumpkin. 

Even this year, we managed to scrape something together despite moving to our new house the first of this month. I repurposed some old fishing gear and a kiddie pool full of red-dyed water; Lauren painted gills on the kids’ necks and green scales on their faces, and we were ready for our first Halloween in our new neighborhood. 

“Hey, did you see the house across the street?” I asked my wife.

“Yeah, no way that’s real paint, right?”

“I hope not,” I said, chuckling. “Probably something that’ll wash off whenever it rains.”

“What is it?” Olivia, my youngest, asked. 

Lauren and I looked at each other for a moment longer than we needed, reading each other's expressions. Olivia sensed our tension and fidgeted with her hands. We turned to her and motioned for her to step closer, to lean in. As she got within arm's reach, we both grabbed her and yelled, “It’s blood!” while tickling her. 

Not ten seconds of giggling later, Olivia's older brother barreled down the stairs to protect his sister. I managed to catch and lift him clean over her, at the expense of my lower back. He’d be too big to lift and swing around soon, especially when he was so adept at flopping about like a fish in my arms.

“C’mon, Oliver, we gotta get ready,” I said to him. 

“*Finally*,” he said, “I’ve been ready forever!” 

“You too, missy,” Lauren said, taking a still giggling Olivia into her arms. “Pond stocked?”

“As it’ll ever be,” I replied. Oliver did his best to unbutton every pocket he could find on my fishing vest as I carried him out the front door, including the clip holding my keys, which landed in a soft patch of dirt. An unusually warm wind carried a dozen or so dry leaves into our home through the door behind me. “Ah, *shit*.”

“Shit, shit, shit!” the kids chanted in unison, sharing whatever telepathic ability kids have to know when they shouldn’t repeat a word. Lauren shot me a look and attempted to hush them both. 

Outside, the sun just barely hung above the horizon, the first sign of a pink sunset highlighting the few clouds in the sky. I’d seen the neighbors setting up their decorations as the weeks passed, but it was still a shock to see them all in full bloom. Every property on the street, in either direction, had some sort of elaborate, creative Halloween setup. In comparison, our dingy kiddie pool of blood and candy looked like macaroni art set between a dozen Picassos. 

The house next door had blood-splattered walls, and three bloody cars in the yard, each with fake limbs poking out from underneath them. One home down the road had a skeleton two stories tall made of what looked like paper mache, and another had a massive stretch of fake webs sprawling between each of their trees, complete with spiders made of thin metal wire. I think I even saw someone with a whole corn maze in their yard, somehow. 

A few little ghouls and vampires prowled the streets already, with the smallest ones followed by their parents or an older sibling. While I made sure the fishing poles were set with their magnets, Lauren gathered the kids and let each of them pick some candy as payment for their work. Half now, half later, just like every year. Whatever candy we didn’t give out tonight, after the dad-tax, was all theirs. 

“What’s that?”  Frankenstein's monster asked, walking up our driveway. His mother waited at the end, watching with a gentle smile.

“’Tis a fishing rod, matey. Take it, and catch yerself a bite, if ye dare…” I said, shooting for my best Captain Ahab impression. Likely I sounded more like Captain Hook. 

Lauren and the kids were still chatting quietly a few feet away as the trick-or-treater stepped up to grab the rod. With a mighty heave and roar, he swung the rod as hard as he could. The tiny magnet on a string swung clean over the pool and landed in the grass.

“You have to get it in the water, sweety,” the woman at the end of our driveway said.

“Oh,” the kid said, a faint shade of red visible under the green face paint. Instead of reeling the line in, he started walking backwards until he dragged the magnet through the grass and over the edge of the pool. With a soft *plink,* it dropped in the water and connected with one of the magnets inside.

“Aye! Ye got a bite, lad, now reel it in!” I said, making the motion with my hands to show him how. 

Looking down at the rod in his hands, he frantically started cranking the reel. Out of the red water of the pool, a small Ziploc bag with a candy bar and a small magnet emerged. The little monster started hopping and laughing as his mother recorded with her phone. By this time, the sun was dipping behind the houses in the distance, and a few other families were wandering the street. 

“What a catch, lad! Let me unhook ‘er for ye.” I unstuck the two magnets, pulled the chocolate out of the bag, and handed it to him. “Hark! Step forward, lads and lasses, if ye *dare!*”

A few parents chuckled at my accent as they nudged their kids forward. Oliver and Olivia ‘swam’ around them, guiding them to the fishing poles and making whatever it was they thought were fish noises. Lauren walked between the parents as I helped the kids fish, passing nips of sweet booze, hand warmers, and pricier chocolates to any accepting adult.

As expected, it didn’t take longer than fifteen minutes for a line to get tangled. Some Batman who didn’t pay attention started crying as I took the rod from him. Lauren and Olivia entered damage control mode right away, consoling the kid while I turned around so no one could see me pulling a knife out to cut the line. Oliver continued to be a fish.

Restringing the rod and tucking the knife into my belt, I said, “Look, me matey, good as new! The White Whale’ll be yers, yet!”

Sniffling, the kid accepted the rod with a confused look. I motioned with my hands once more, guiding him to fling it into the water. As soon as he saw the king-sized peanut butter cups he reeled in, the tears vanished. 

“Look, Dad, it’s me!”

I turned with a smile, expecting to see Olivia standing next to another fish-person. Instead, I saw a little girl wearing a mask that looked like my daughter's face. 

“What the fuck?”

Every parent present, including Lauren, looked at me with shock and disgust. Apparently sharing the same trait as my own kids, a few children exclaimed, “Fuck, fuck, fuck!” repeating after me. One of them was the girl dressed as my daughter, right down to the pink jacket with a fake-fur hood.

“Can you watch your language, please?” one parent asked. The girl walked up to me and wordlessly outstretched her hands for a fishing rod. Lauren and I stared at the girl, looking over her dark brown hair, faded blue jeans, and pink Skechers. If it weren’t for my daughter's face paint and the girl's bulky mask, the two could have been twins. 

Lauren didn't look pleased about my swearing, but she was still visibly uncomfortable. Apparently, we were the only ones who found it weird. Seeing mob mentality at work, Lauren returned to handing out treats to the parents. I passed the rod to the girl and heard her giggle as she stepped to the pool. It sounded just like when we’d tickled Olivia.

“And who might you be, little lass?” I asked, struggling to maintain my accent.

She turned to me and exclaimed, “I’m an Olivia!” 

“An… Olivia?”

“Mhmm!” she responded, twirling the rod. The magnet landed in the pool with another *plink*. She slowly reeled the line in, feeling it connect with another magnet. Without instruction, she cranked the reel, slowly dragging out a baggie holding a box of chocolate-covered caramels. The girl looked back to her father at the end of my driveway, holding the candy on the line like a trophy catch. 

“Oliver, can you help her out, please?” I asked. He made a few *glub glub* noises and swam over to take the candy out of the bag for her. I walked over to the father I saw her showing off to, hoping I didn’t look as mad as I felt.

“Hey, man,” I whispered so the kids couldn’t hear me, “what the hell is that?”

“What’s what?”

“Your *daughter*! Why is she dressed like mine?”

The man looked offended at my question, and the parents beside us stepped back. “She said the new girl at school looked pretty, so she wanted to dress like her. I’m… sorry? I guess?”

An irritatingly reasonable answer. “And the mask?”

“3-D printed it at the library.” The man raised an eyebrow at me, like I was acting strange. He motioned to a family down the road and said, “Not sure about that kid, though.”

I looked down the road and saw a small family of skeletons, two parents and a kid wearing the Spirit Halloween special. All except for their youngest son, who was wearing a mask bearing a striking resemblance to my boy. Complete with a navy blue sweater, cargo pants, and Crocs, the resemblance was uncanny. 

“Hey Dad, help!” a young voice called. I turned, and only after a few steps did I remember she was not my daughter.

I turned to look back at the girl's father, but he just shrugged and said, “I think she means you.”

“I’m not her dad.”

“Sorry, man, but it’s Halloween. Can’t you just play along for a bit?”

Accent long forgotten, I grumbled all the way to the girl. Olivia had wandered over and was currently playing a game of tug-of-war with the girl wearing her face. Oliver was helping the two kids *actually* dressed for Halloween, taking their candy from the fished-up bags and handing it to them, before refilling it with new candy and tossing it back in the pool. I saw him try to sneak some candy in his pockets, but I didn’t have time to call him out on it.

“Olivia fished this, so that means it’s mine!” my daughter said, tugging on the bag. The plastic stretched between their hands, red-dyed water dripping onto the grass. 

“Dad, help, she’s stealing my candy!” the neighbor’s girl exclaimed.

“Olivia, drop it, you know it’s just a costume,” I scolded. Olivia let go with an exaggerated sigh, and I turned to look at the girl in the mask. “Sorry, lass! I guess you-”

I stopped talking. Through the mask's eye holes, I saw the kid's eyes underneath. A shade or two darker than my daughter's, and wet with tears. She sobbed gently, like she was trying to hide it, and ran back to her father, candy bag in hand. He met her with open arms and leaned down to hear her say something in his ear. 

For a second, I thought he was going to walk over to me, like I had done something to her, but he just whispered into her ear and led her away. Trick or Treat was in prime time at this point, the street full of kids running around excited to visit the “good” neighborhood, the one with full-sized candy bars. Lauren walked over to me, her basket finally run empty.

“What’d he say?” she asked, nodding her chin in the direction the father walked off with his fake Olivia.

“Some bull about his daughter thinking the new girl was pretty.”

She scrunched her face. “And him?”

Oliver was currently admiring the mask his lookalike wore before loudly challenging him to a wrestling match. “No idea.”

“Freaky ass neighborhood…” I heard her mutter as she walked to our porch to restock her basket. 

I made my way back to the pool. As sure as I was that my son could kick his fake’s ass, I was not about to let it happen in front of the entire neighborhood. I tried not to look at what costumes the new kids were wearing, and paid attention to the ones fishing for candy. The accent, refilling the bags, untangling the lines, it all went well for a while. The moon was almost full tonight, lighting the small crowd in a silver glow so haunting and beautiful I was sad to see the street lights turn on.

Lauren maneuvered the families like the professional she was, used to the demanding needs of parents trusting the care of their children to another. Two dozen-ish parents and older siblings formed a horizontal line across our yard, and as she worked her way down it, I dropped the rod I was holding. I saw them before she did, and I stood gawking in stunned silence. Nobody else seemed to care that an entire family was dressed like us, as she stepped in front of a man and woman dressed *exactly* like her and me, with a little Olivia and Oliver standing beside them.

My body moved unconsciously, taking a step forward. As the man stretched his arm out to shake, I swear, unmistakably, the chatter of the crowd died. Even the children stilled, as if they too wished to listen. I heard the man clearly introduce himself to my wife as me. Then the crowd picked up again, the children resumed their laughter, as if I’d only imagined the silence.

I took long strides over, anxious to reach them. A hole felt punched in my gut, not dissimilar to the feeling of getting a call from school while I was at work. As if I was anticipating something dreadful and irreparable, when in reality the situation was likely much plainer. Lauren, ever coy, took his hand and shook it firmly. 

“Why, it’s a pleasure to meet you, sir. What lovely children you have!” she said. 

“Aren’t they?” the woman dressed as my wife said.

I stepped beside my wife, uncaring of the scowl I wore. Every second that passed, I could feel my blood pressure rising. He was still gripping Lauren’s hand. The man looked at me, only releasing her when we made eye contact. She stepped back, behind me. In the yard, I could still hear the drag of fishing line cutting through children's giggling. None of us spoke for a moment, but if the parents surrounding us noticed the tension, they didn’t care. I puffed my chest a bit and did not stop staring into the man's eyes. The knife clipped in my pocket felt noticeably heavier than usual.

“Trick or treat?” the fake Oliver asked.

“Ahh, I’m so sorry, we’re actually running out of candy. It’s getting a bit late, I think we’ll be calling it a night soon,” Lauren said behind me, tugging on my sleeve. 

“Oh, already? It’s not even eight yet,” her double said. Both the kids, as well as a few others in earshot, groaned with disappointment. 

“Yep, sorry. Long day today, early morning tomorrow. You know how it is,” I said quietly, bluntly. The man stayed silent, maintaining eye contact.

“Of course, we’d hate to be a bother,” the woman said. “Moving can be so difficult; you must be exhausted. We just wanted to say welcome to the neighborhood, right, dear?”

I was almost surprised to see the mask's mouth, *my* mouth, remain still as he spoke. “Absolutely, sorry we haven’t had a chance to pop in to say hello yet. Welcome to the neighborhood, I’m sure you’ll love it here.”

Then, with not much more than a grumble from their kids, the family left. A few others who heard our conversation left with them, leaving our yard still half full. 

“Why don’t you go inside with the kids, babe? I’ll take care of the rest,” I said. 

“Thank you,” she whispered. I kissed her on the cheek and gave her hand a squeeze, but she sucked in a sharp breath. “Ow!”

Her pale hand had obvious finger marks across it, as if the man who shook it did so with an iron grip. She shook it out and gave me a thin-lipped, placating smile. “I’m fine, I promise, just a bit freaked out. I’m opening a bottle of wine; want a glass?”

“Please, a full one. I’ll be inside in a few.”

Lauren walked over to our kids, whispering in their ears that the night was over, likely telling them it was time for their candy so they didn’t put up a fight. I walked over to the kiddie pool and, unceremoniously, lifted it to spill its contents into the grass. 

“Look, get it while you can!” I shouted, pointing at all the bags of candy. For a minute I stood by and made sure no one got hurt fighting for the sweets, before waving at all the parents watching and shouting, “Have a great Halloween, everyone, and good night!”

Lauren and the kids had already grabbed the massive bag we had been restocking the pond from and brought it inside. Guess Oliver and Olivia would have a hell of a haul this year. I followed them inside and shut the door behind me, turning off the light on our porch. Universal trick-or-treat language for “go away.” After that, I could see from the glass pane in our front door all of the families slowly wandering off our yard, and I breathed a heavy sigh. I locked the deadbolt and, ignoring the fragments of dead leaves scattered in our foyer, stripped my fishing vest and shoes. I could clean tomorrow.

“Honey, you okay?” I asked, stepping into the living room. The little fishes were haggling with each other on the floor, trading candies like little brokers. Lauren had two glasses already on the coffee table, both full to the brim with Pinot Noir, the bottle already visibly empty. 

She stood and wrapped her arms around me in a tight hug, taking a shaky breath. “Yeah, I’ll be fine. Thanks for finishing up. I can clean the yard tomorrow.”

“You have work in the morning,” I said, chuckling. I leaned back to brush some hair behind her ear. “I have off; let me take care of it.”

“Fuck that,” she whispered, before stepping away to pick up her glass. “I already texted my boss I can’t make it tomorrow. I need an *us* day.”

I leaned in for a kiss, but felt a tug on my pant leg. Olivia looked up at me with her bulging, painted fish eyes and asked, “Weentown?”

Groaning, I said, “Again, sweetie? You watched it twice yesterday.”

“Hotel Transylvania!” Oliver yelled, raising his arm like he was in class. 

“We can do Halloweentown first, then Hotel, okay?” Lauren said. “Daddy needs a shower, so he can skip most of Halloweentown.”

She gave me a quick peck on the cheek and said, “You are absolutely *covered* in red dye.”

“That works, join me?”

“And let them eat candy until they throw up? Maybe tomorrow,” she said with a wink. 

I smiled and lightly smacked her ass while the kids were busy fumbling with the TV remote. “Keep my glass safe for me.”

Our master bedroom had its own master bathroom, away from the sickly sweet stench of bubble gum-flavored toothpaste and no-tears shampoo. One of my favorite parts of our new house, honestly. Not having to close the bathroom door, or even not having to bring clothes in with me, is an oddly liberating feeling. Even if I did have to shut our bedroom door. While the hot water ran over my body to the tune of Halloweentown quietly in the other room, I still couldn’t get the thought of that family out of my head. 

Seeing Lauren freak out in front of our kids set off some kind of fatherly instinct in me, like I had to remain calm and cool, but alone in the shower, the pit returned to my stomach. No one, not a single person out of the dozens who visited our house, thought it was odd. No, apparently I was the weird one for even noticing.

For the past month it was picture-perfect. Upper middle-class suburbia, exactly what we’d been saving our money for. No HOA, good schools nearby, close to Lauren’s mom but not so close she’d visit often, nice lake for fishing down the road; it was great. But tonight, Lauren said it best. Freaky ass neighborhood. 

The water running over my feet faded from a dark red to a dull pink as I scrubbed as much of the dye as I could from my skin. My hands would be blush for days, and I’m sure if it weren’t for her stress, my wife would gladly remind me she had warned me to use gloves. I stayed under the hot water as long as reasonable, enjoying the peace, before turning it off and stepping out. Hopefully Halloweentown was almost halfway over, at least.

Completely nude and mostly dry, I started digging through the dresser for some sweatpants. That glass of wine was calling to me, but not as much as the second I’d have when the kids went to bed. I stopped. The movie wasn’t playing. Paused? Bathroom break, maybe? If Olivia had to go, she’d demand fiercely that the movie be paused. No matter how many times she’s seen it, she wouldn’t want to miss a moment. 

I stuck my ear to the door to listen. Not a peep. Even left alone, Oliver was seldom quiet. Had Lauren put them to bed? I knew I was psyching myself out for no reason, but maybe I was more rattled than I realized. I tucked a towel around my waist and cracked the door open, bracing myself for the kids to leap out and scare me. Nothing. No creaking of floorboards, no crinkling of candy wrappers. Dead silence.

With a painful paranoia, I grabbed the knife from my dirty pants pocket and stepped outside the room. The hallway attaching all three bedrooms to the living room was still with anticipation. I walked down it, straining my ears to each room I passed. Nothing. I could see half of the TV from the hall. The movie was paused. 

The living room was devoid of activity, my children and wife gone. My full glass of wine was spilled, staining Oliver's pillow sack full of candy a deep, deep red. The kitchen light was on, flooding the living room with a pale yellow sheen. A yellow light with a small shadow obscuring it, wiggling and squirming out of sight. Silently. I crept towards the kitchen, pocket knife cold in my clammy hand. 

“Lauren?” I called out. Nothing. 

I tried and failed to convince myself they were just waiting to scare me, to leap out and pay me back for earlier today. But Lauren, freaked out as she was? Spilled wine? Something in me found it hard to believe she would want to pull a stunt like that, not after today. I walked to the kitchen, as stealthily as I could, and as I walked, I finally heard something make a noise. From down the other hall, inside the foyer, I heard the crunching of leaves under someone's shoe. 

I whipped around and strode forward, looking towards the front door. Standing awkwardly in the foyer, leaves underfoot, was Olivia. At least, she looked like my daughter. The girl wore pink pajamas and a porcelain smile. A mask. This girl was not my daughter. 

Before I could shout out at her, rapid footsteps approached behind me and kicked the back of my knee. I dropped immediately, and a hand wrapped around the side of my head. It bashed my temple into the wall once, twice, again and again. I waved my arm widely, desperate to catch whoever was behind me with my knife. I felt the blade scrape and catch something before another hand grabbed my wrist. The hand was wet, sticky, and with blurred vision, I think I saw it was red with blood. 

“Hold still,” the man holding me said before pressing his weight on my back and pinning me to the ground. 

My ears rang and dark spots coated every other inch of my sight. The floor underneath me felt like a carousel, careening far too fast for me to hold the contents of my stomach in. In a dizzy haze, I retched, and the man holding me lifted my head by my hair and slammed my face into it. The scent of bile mingled with the copper aroma of the blood pouring from my nose. 

With one hand holding my head and the other holding my wrist, the man held me down while the fake Olivia scuttled over and began to peel my fingers away from the knife. I wasn’t able to put up much more of a fight. I felt his knee dig into the small of my back as he leaned down into my vision. Somehow, I was unsurprised to see my own face meet my eyes.

“You ready?” he asked. It was impossible to focus; his mask was swirling around two or three duplicates as my brain struggled to process what my eyes saw.

“What?” I mumbled.

“Are you ready?”

“Where are my kids?” I slurred. “Laur-”

The man slammed his fist between my shoulder blades, sending an electric shock of pain through my limbs. I yelled out in pain, gurgling through the contents of my stomach stuck to my lips and nose. Wrapping his arm around my neck, he tucked it into the crook of his elbow. I pushed, feeling adrenaline course into my wobbling limbs, driving me back onto one knee. It was no use. He flexed his bicep and pulled my throat in close, and after a brief struggle, everything went black.

I gasped for air, coughing as I inhaled drops of blood and stomach acid. He loosened his grip, dragging me back to reality. Into my ear he hissed, “Are. You. Ready?”

“Ready… for what?” I managed to squeak out. The girl wearing Olivia's face giggled, twirling the knife like it was a toy.

Maintaining his arm around my neck, he dragged me to my feet. My legs shook, and I almost fell with every step as he led me to the kitchen. On the cold tile floor sat my family, bound in rope. Each of them wore a mask of themselves on their face, and a heavy set of earmuffs over their ears. Standing behind them, backs against the stove, were the woman dressed as my wife and a little fake Oliver. Waiting, patiently, for me to come.

“We’ll let you go,” the man holding me said. “And you can take one.”

My heart beat so fast I thought it would burst, and I’d drop dead before I could respond. The room still spun, and without consciously thinking of doing it, I leaned on the man wearing my face. I opened my mouth, feeling the sting of a split lip slowly oozing blood. Then I closed it, opened it again, and closed it once more. I was in shock.

“What?” I managed to mumble again.

“You can leave, and we’ll let you pick one to take with you,” fake Lauren said. My wife's kind smile mocked me, hiding the woman's cold eyes. 

“I don’t… I can’t… why?” 

The man's arm was still around my throat, and I felt his hand bleed onto my bare shoulder. At the sight of my family, my beautiful wife and children, bound on the floor, the fight left my arms. Once more, the man whispered into my ear, “Don’t need to know why. You just need to choose.”

I couldn’t choose; I didn’t want to. How could I? I heard Olivia sniffle under the mask obscuring her face. Could she see me? Did she see how weak, bloody, and beaten her father was? Oliver was trying his best to put himself in front of his sister, to obscure her. The faint line of his chin, barely visible under the mask, was wet with tears. Out of all three, he was the only one with visible blood, running down the middle of his shirt. Lauren was still as a statue, and twice as quiet. Of the three, she sat with her back held straightest. The only one of us with fight left.

The man dropped me in a heap on the floor. I coughed and sputtered as they watched me grasp my bearings. For half a second I considered turning around, attacking again, defending my family, even if it cost me my life. Hell, I did turn around. I took one step and crumpled again. My head pounded, and I saw the man wearing my face grip my knife tightly in his hands. I could die fighting, or I could hope they really were letting me go, with one of my family. I was gutted, ashamed, downright humiliated. I made the most painful decision of my life. I decided to trust the people I was sure were about to kill us. I saw no other option. 

“I’ll take Ol-”

“No, no no, wrong,” the man interrupted me. “Don’t tell us who you’ll take. Take the earmuffs off, talk to who you won’t.”

Stunned, I stared at him. If his face changed expression under the mask, I couldn’t see. The four of them stood steady and unmoving, waiting for me to act. With my head sunken into my shoulders, I crawled on my knees to my wife and took the headphones off her ears. 

“Bill?” she hissed.

“Yes, honey, I-”

“No, no, no, no! The kids, you have to take one of them!” she cried, thrashing her body against her restraints. 

“Shh, shh, Lauren, I am taking one of the kids,” I croaked.

“Wh-what? But they said…”

“They told me,” I said, through tears, “to talk to the two I’m not taking with me.”

Lauren’s body relaxed, like she released all her tension. “Thank God, Bill. They just said they’d let you take one of us; I didn’t know if… Please, won’t you take this mask off? I want… I want to see you again.”

I looked at the invaders, and when they did not move, I reached around her head to take my wife's mask off. Underneath, she seemed aged. Worn and tired, like the only thing she wanted in the world was to sleep. She was the most beautiful woman I’d ever met. The tears flowed freely, and I wasn’t sure which of us shed more. She said to me, “I love you.”

“I love you, too, Lauren. I love you so much,” I said. She leaned forward, pressing her forehead against mine. We kissed, one last time. She tasted like salt and iron. When I pulled away, the woman wearing her face leaned down and picked up the mask, holding it out to me. She didn’t speak, but I understood. Taking one last longing glance into my wife's eyes, I placed the mask back over her face and the earmuffs back over her ears. 

I took a long time to make my way to my kids. They sat next to Lauren, only a few inches away, but the journey felt like a marathon. The throbbing pain in my head was gone, replaced by a deep ache in my chest. I’d love to say it was a difficult choice, to say I didn’t play favorites. To grant some insight into what should have been the hardest decision any man could ever face. I have none. In spite of how deep it cuts my soul to admit, I was never going to take Oliver with me. It was always going to be his sister. I lifted his earmuffs and mask, and faced my son. 

“Dad?”

I said nothing at first, just pulled him in close. I kissed the top of his head and pressed him into my chest. His nose was more crooked than mine, wet blood still trickling down it, and the skin around his eye was dark and swollen. My son's resolute pride crumbled as he sobbed into me, and I told the hardest lie I would ever tell. “It’s gonna be okay, Oliver. You’ll take care of your mother, won’t you?”

“Is she okay? Olivia?” he asked, muffled by my embrace. 

“Of course she is, buddy. Her big brother protected her, didn’t you?”

He didn’t respond. My son sobbed and heaved into me, wailing with a mourning no boy should possess. I let him, for as long as I was allowed. After the longest minutes of my life, the man behind me leaned down and picked up Oliver's mask. He held it up for me to take. 

I pushed Oliver away. He tried to lean in harder, to stay with me, to cry into my shoulder more, but I knew we were out of time. I pushed him away. He looked up, seeing the man wearing my face, before shutting his eyes hard. I placed the mask and earmuffs back over his head.

It was almost over. That’s what I kept repeating to myself, that it was almost over. I turned to Olivia and saw she was shaking. That tremble that runs through a child's body when they’re trying their best to hold back feelings they have no business carrying. As I reached for her earmuffs, the man stopped me, grabbing my wrist. 

“Pick her up.”

Nodding, I got off my knees and onto shaky feet. From underneath her armpits, I lifted her up and into my arms. I hoped she somehow knew it was me lifting her. Told myself she didn’t struggle not because she had no fight left, but because she knew the feeling of her father's arms. As I was guided out of my own house, I kept telling myself that. Somehow, Olivia felt lighter than usual. Like I’d left a part of her behind.

Outside, gilded by moonlight, was a small crowd of people. Adults and children alike, all dressed like my family. They were pin-drop silent, watching me with the devout attention of a Sunday church service. The whole neighborhood formed a line, single file, leading directly to my car. Someone wearing a mask of my face held up a set of keys, *my* car keys.

I took the mask and headphones off Olivia when we were a few miles down the road, out of sight, but she still never spoke a word. She curled into a ball in the passenger seat as I drove, sobbing quietly to herself. I didn’t speak either; I had no idea what to say. I doubt any words at all could have helped us. Lauren’s mother wasn’t home, and I had nothing on me but the towel around my waist and some emergency cash in the glovebox.

About four hours later, around one in the morning, I stopped at a motel a few cities over. I was too tired to drive further. Olivia had fallen asleep long ago, and I struggled to keep my eyes open. Countless times I fought with myself internally. *Go to a damn police station*, I told myself. *Get help!*

But I just kept driving. My head felt like it was full of every thought I’d ever had, crowded and struggling for dominance all at once, and yet I couldn’t focus on any one of them. All I felt was tired. So, so tired. Not like I expected help from the police anyway. The entire neighborhood's word against mine, right? No, I needed sleep first. I carried Olivia into the motel room and set her on the bed. I lay next to her, refusing to let her out of my sight for even a moment, like she would disappear if I wasn’t looking. But the silence was painful. I refused to be left with my thoughts, not tonight. I turned on the TV, just in time to see an emergency broadcast.

“-eved to have murdered both his wife and son before escaping with his daughter. He is suspected to be both armed and dangerous. If you see this man, please call the police immediately.” The news splashed a photo on screen, a photo of me. Taken from my front yard, it showed me, wearing nothing but a bloody towel, pressing my daughter into my bare, vomit-stained chest. Seemingly cropped so the masked neighbors in my yard were out of frame. The only mask in the photo was the one my daughter wore, of her own face, in stark contrast to my own bare visage. I looked like a beast, coated in wet blood, with the eyes of a wild, desperate animal.
