The smell of formaldehyde doesn't wash off. It stays in your pores, under your fingernails, and follows you home to ruin your dinner. After years in this basement, you stop seeing people. They just become a checklist of tasks: weight, fluid volume, structural damage, cause of death. Paperwork. Next body.

I don't fear the quiet ones on the steel tables. They are predictable. It's the living ones outside these walls that make me lock my doors at night.

It was late, somewhere around 3:00 AM, when they rolled him in. An old man, unmarked, found on a park bench. No ID. Just another John Doe for the county to bury.

I snapped on my gloves, adjusted the overhead light, and grabbed the scalpel.

The first incision is always routine. You trace the Y-shape from the shoulders down to the sternum. But the moment the blade pierced the skin, the resistance felt wrong. It didn't slide through fat and muscle. It crunched. Like cutting into dry parchment.

There was no blood. Not a single drop spilled onto the steel table.

I peeled back the skin, expecting some bizarre post-mortem drying or a rare medical anomaly. Instead, my breath caught in my throat.

His chest cavity was hollow. No heart. No lungs. No ribs holding up the frame.

It was packed tightly, edge to edge, with thousands of small, folded slips of white paper. They were crammed into the space where his life used to be, compressed so hard they looked like a solid block of wood.

I stood there for a full minute, listening to the hum of the cooling units, wondering if the fumes had finally gotten to my brain.

With my forceps, I carefully pinched the corner of one note and pulled it free. The paper was cheap, torn from school notebooks and receipts. The ink was smeared.

I unfolded it with my gloved fingers.

The handwriting was jagged, frantic: "He's sitting outside my window again. The police won't come. Someone please help me."

My heart gave a heavy thud. I dropped the forceps and reached directly into the cold chest, grabbing a handful of notes. They rustled against my latex gloves.

I unfolded another one. A different handwriting, shaky and faint: "Three days without food. The baby stopped crying an hour ago. I can't get up."

A third one, written in red ink: "He promised he wouldn't use the belt this time. He lied."

Every single slip I opened was the same. Hundreds of them. Raw, terrifying screams of agony, terror, and despair. These weren't notes from history. Some of the dates written at the top were from last night.

Thousands of people, currently alive and suffering out there in the city, had their secret miseries stuffed into the chest of a dead man on my table.

I backed away, my boots squeaking against the linoleum. The pile of opened notes fell from my hands, scattering across the floor like autumn leaves.

I should have called the supervisor. I should have called the police. But I couldn't move.

Then, the draft from the ventilation intake hit the open body.

The thousands of remaining papers inside the chest began to shift. They rubbed against each other, creating a dry, raspy friction that filled the silent morgue. It didn't sound like paper anymore. It sounded like a collective, breathless whisper rising from the steel table.

"You are the only one who looks."

The papers on the floor stirred, flipping over.

"Help us."

I stumbled backward, my hip slamming into the tool cart. The scalpel clattered loudly against the floor, bouncing into the shadows.

The whispers grew sharper, a chorus of dry throats scratching against the metal walls:

"Help us become like him."
