I was born on an island far north in the Atlantic, in a village scarcely found on any map. It was a quiet place nestled among the inland fields, with a view of the coast to the east and a suffocating forest closing in on every other side. Back then, its lack of presence to the world was thanks to our isolation. Now it is because that place no longer exists.

My birth was nothing extraordinary. It was as every other; I was born cold, shivering and crying in a tiny hut where our doctor lived. For a time, I was assumed to be just as plain as every other child born within the same season. That changed upon the new moon of my sixth month.

The story goes that my mother was roused from her sleep in the night by strange dreams, and when she rolled over to check on me in my crib, she was aghast to find that I was nowhere in sight. It was late, the fire in the mantle nothing but a scatter of embers, and the door was open, peering out into a dark, consuming night.

Her first thought was that something had taken me; perhaps a wild dog or unknown beast of the woods. The crib was not destroyed, however, and there was no chaos or crimson to be found scattered about the hut. The only footsteps that were there in the dust of our stone floors were that of an infants. Two feet. No hands that indicated crawling.

She woke the neighboring huts with her panicked cries, and soon, torches and lamps were lit, scouring the fields for wherever I had wandered off during such an all-consuming night. People began doubting my mother's claims and returned to the theory that something must have taken me. After all, a child so young could not possibly scale their crib, open a door whose latch is twice their own height, then walk off on their own.

Where they found me was not a place that was expected. On the edge of the village, within a storage hut for festivities and celebrations, I was found sitting on the floor. Once again, a door far too tall for me had been opened, and I sat in the center of the room. Somehow, I had gotten a flute carved of antler into my hands, and I was teething the object.

The person who had found me moved forward with relief to pick me up, but froze with horror when they heard a sound. I wasn’t just gnawing on the instrument. I was blowing. My lungs heaved enough wind to create a steady tune, and what's more was that my chubby, clumsy hands were working the reed to plug holes and create a varying rhythm.

At first they thought it had to be a coincidence, but as they listened longer and longer, I never strayed in my pattern. It was the same notes played at the same tempo over and over in maddening repetition. The man had been too stunned to interrupt or leave, and by the time the rest of the village caught up to him, he was slumped in the doorway, laughing wildly to himself. When my mother found me, she clasped her hand to her mouth and wept, though no one knew if this was because of relief, or from amazement at witnessing such a miracle. My father could not bring himself to react. Only stare in dark awe.

My upbringing diverged heavily from that day. Where I might have ended up as all the other children, I was hailed as a miracle child. A divine gift from the spirits that watched over us. I had been born to hone and use the craft I had been gifted—my flute playing—and from that day forward, it would be what I spent every waking moment doing.

When I was old enough to finally attend lessons, I learned that I had not been the first.

“One like you is born only once a generation,” the elder told me, “It is a great honor. You have a gift that nobody else receives.”

“What gift?” I asked, “My flute?”

“In a sense. The flute is only your tool. The gift is your talent; one that, someday, will lead you away from this island.”

It was something that both scared and elated me. My village was one heavily steeped in tradition and isolation, and it was a rarity that anyone made the venture beyond the shores at all. To have an excuse to leave was coveted in secret by many, and though I should have mostly been grateful, I found myself overwhelmed by the prospect.

“Why do only ones like me leave?” I asked.

“Because you have something to offer,” The elder replied, “and we do not dare ask the spirits for passage unless we have something to offer.” We were also a culture steeped heavily in superstitions.

“Did the others play a flute like me?” I asked.

“Nay. All the gifts are different. Some were also musicians. Some artists. Some craftsmen. But all were gifted young, like you.”

I looked around the empty hut where, normally, fellow children would be listening. We would have all learned together, and talked about things other than me and my flute. I did not feel special having to attend lessons in isolation. I felt like an oddity.

This was none more apparent than around my fellow village youngest, Olav and Espen. Having been born in the same season, they were almost siblings to me, and my closest friends. When we were babes, we were free to play and run free together like the other children in the village. While I still had to practice my flute playing, it had not yet taken on the intensity that it would upon reaching age seven.

After that, the isolation began. It was when responsibilities were imparted to us as we were coming of age, and while theirs consisted of plowing fields for crops or tending the cattle, my duties remained only to practice my flute.

I didn’t see the need for it. Even at such a ripe age, I was already creating melodies that could make listeners weep with melancholy or dance with fervor, depending on the tune. ‘Prodigy’, they called me. It was a nickname that at first chased that feeling of self-imprudence away, but that was until one day Olav approached me using it mockingly.

“There are you are, *Prodigy,*” he jeered, his boots covered in a thick slurry of slop and boar feces and his clothes soaked in sweat.

I had been sitting in the village center upon a large stone, playing my flute for the last few hours as instructed. Espen followed close behind, also having just finished his chores for the day.

“I was wondering why I did not see you in the fields,” Olav continued, “I should have guessed you were here as always.”

“It’s where I’m told to be,” I responded.

“It’s unfair. Why must we get covered in filth while you get to sit around and do nothing all day!”

“I’m playing constantly, Olav,” I told him, “It’s rarely nothing.”

“There’s nothing unique about your music,” he grumpily accused, “There are plenty other musicians in the village, and even they must work. Why do *you* get out of helping?”

I did not know how to answer, so I regurgitated what I had always been told, “I am special. This is my duty.”

Espen looked sadly to his soiled boots, “Does that mean we are not?”

“You cannot play like I can,” I pointed out, “Or paint or whittle.”

My answer did not sit well with Olav, and he bared his teeth, “You’re spoiled is what you are! We all think so! You never have to do anything hard, and everyone treats you as if you’re an elder when you’re just a brat that can’t do anything but blow into a horn!”

 Before I could respond, Olav stormed off, and though I looked to Espen hoping for him to invalidate the accusations that had been made against me, his eyes found his muddied boots once more, and he sauntered off in tow.

I did not feel like continuing to play, but I knew it was my duty, so I carried on though my notes were not as sweet as normal.

When I arrived home, I told my mother as she prepared a stew that I did not want to play the flute anymore. I told her that I wished to work in the fields with the other children or join our neighbor Sigurd and the others on trips into the woods while they hunted.

She froze as if I’d just spoken a curse and set her knife down carefully before kneeling before me.

“My dearest, you cannot stop using your gift—this is your duty. It’s more important than any other work you could possibly do. What has sparked this thinking in you?”

I tried to convince her that it was all my own desires, but she could sense the shame within me, and finally I caved and reluctantly told her about what Olav had said to me.

She chewed on my words behind pursed lips for some time before finally laughing and pinching my cheek, “My darling, is that all? There is nothing to worry about. What Olav feels is not justified, it is jealousy. Those without gifts will always covet what they don’t have. Your work is more important than all else. Do not let the sharp words of those who envy puncture your skin.”

It made me feel a better, but that night I still slept restlessly, guilt tight in my stomach over the luxuries I had that my friends did not. That was, until, during my playing the following day, Olav approached again. His clothes were just as muddy, and I once again expected ridicule, but he wore an expression of sorrow this time rather than anger. He seemed to have a hard time lifting his gaze from the floor, and the one time he managed to, I noticed that there was a large bruise across his cheek below his eye.

“What happened?” I asked him.

“I fell,” he told me quickly before shuffling on his feet. His eyes flickered to the edge of the square, where I saw his father watching the situation under a stern gaze. He hastily continued, “I wanted to apologize for my words. I know that you are not spoiled. I was just upset at my new tasks.”

I sat taller on the rock, “That’s okay. I wish I could help.”

“No. You must play,” He told me, “That’s what’s important.”

I didn’t like that he was talking to me in the manor of the elders and other adults now. Trying to find the familiar Olav beneath the bruise, I asked, “We’re still friends, right Olav?”

He nodded, but still couldn’t find my eyes, “Of course.”

It was the last thing he said before walking away, and it was enough to lift my spirits once more. All seemed okay after all, and my playing returned with a confident melody. What I soon found though, was that Olav didn’t talk to me much anymore. Espen either. Anytime I waved their way or made an effort to find them in my free time, they always seemed to have an excuse to avoid me.

The loss of my close friends didn’t have time to weigh heavy on me, however. At that point, I became much busier. My lessons grew in intensity. Till then, training had been a trivial matter. Playing came as easy to me as it had the night I’d allegedly crawled out of my crib by some supernatural force. Looking back on my young life, there was no moment that I could recall not having melodies swimming through my head. Even fewer times that I recalled my instrument ever leaving my hands.

That simplicity changed when I reached age 8. No longer were my concerts held in the square for all to hear. Instead, I was forced to play in the hut of one of the elders, with my teacher as the sole listener. I was told that, for the duration of my six-hour lessons, I was to keep playing and to stop for absolutely nothing. There would be no breaks; only rests between melodies for me to take a drink of water should my throat get dry.

Discomfort was the goal. On top of playing out of my element, the room was kept dim, lit only with a single candle in front of me. With such low light, I could barely see the elder, let alone my own fingering on the flute, though that hardly mattered to my muscle memory. What *did* falter me was the sounds.

In random intervals—sometimes only minutes apart, sometimes hours—the elder would make a noise. The first time, it was her dropping a wash bin onto the floor with a thunderous, rattling crack.

It startled me out of my playing, and I looked to where I heard it. There was no words that followed, only a silence so strong I could hear the flicker of the candle’s flame.

“Elder?” I called out.

“Your instructions were quite clear at the beginning,” she called back, “Do not stop playing no matter what.”

I was confused, but cautiously, I raised my flute to my lips and began again. It was a rocky start trying to recover my rhythm, but eventually, my playing found its consistency, and I began stringing together new melodies in perfect flow.

*“GRAAHHHH!!!”*

Behind me from the dark, mere inches from my ear, a scream jarred me so badly that I spun from my seat, clashing with the table and knocking the candle over. The flame extinguished, leaving the hut in darkness save for the light that peeked in through the crack in the door.

“Elder!?” I whimpered, “W-What’s happening? Is something—”

“Do not stop playing.”

“I-I don’t understand? What is going on; I—”

*CRACK!*

I saw stars against the darkness as a hand swept unseen through it, striking my cheek and making me lean harder against the table. My flute clattered on the floor, and I held my face.

“Do not. Stop. Playing.” The elder warned again.

I was still in utter confusion, but my very simple task had been made dangerously simpler. Hastily, I clambered to pick up my flute again, then placed it to my lips. Over the next few hours, the elder made more noises. Sometimes she only whispered a word soft enough for me to barely hear, other times, she crashed around dishware. Every time, though, I knew better than to stop, the sting on my cheek persisting in the now constant darkness.

My notes wavered or even paused for a moment, and my playing never reached the certainty again that it once had, but those mistakes didn’t cost another strike on the cheek, and I was soon released.

When I got home, I told my father and mother about the change in lessons. Till that point, I had never been treated poorly or harmed in any way, and I was certain that if he found out what the elder had done to me, there would be repercussions. My father didn’t even look at me as he brought his axe down to split a log of wood.

“Your lessons are your lessons, child. No matter how difficult.”

I turned to my mother who sewed nearby in her chair, but even she didn’t meet my gaze. That was the end of the conversation.

Until that point, even with my life being ruled by it, I had still loved playing the flute. No matter how many hours I played, I never grew tired—in fact, it almost felt like I *needed* it; like the air I breathed through the reed was the only kind that could satiate my lungs. From that point on, however, I grew uneasy over it. If this was what my gift was always leading up to, then where would it head from here?

My talent wasn’t the only thing imparted to me at birth. As long back as I could remember, I had strange dreams at night. They were always the same. It would begin with claustrophobia. Cold, damp and coarse walls pressing in on me from all sides, and the smell of earth heavy in my nose. The taste was always sap.

I’m stuck inside the trunk of a tree.

My breathing picks up, and I start to squirm, but my body is so tight in its hollow that I might as well be a part of the roots attached to the tree. My breathing is fast, and each time I exhale, I can hear the shrill notes of my flute squealing out, like it’s lodged in my throat.

All I can do is stare forward out a small crack in the bark of the tree that looks into the shadowy woods I’ve become a part of. The light is a dull periwinkle as the sun somewhere off to my right prepares to rise. An ocean crashes against a shore some distance away.

Ahead of me, only a few feet away, another tree sways in the soft morning breeze; an open hollow staring at me like one of the openings in my flute. As soon as it senses me, two glowing dots appear within—eyes glowing like an animals on the edge of a fire. There’s someone in that one too.

I can just barely see the pale contour of their cheek through the opening as the sky catches ablaze, staining everything in vibrant orange. Their eyes remain glowing as if it's dark.

“Prodigy,” they whisper with a quivering excitement, “It’s time to learn to swim.”

My breathing is still heavy, but I try to quell it and ask them what they mean. It yields the same effect of only squeaking out frantic notes. My eyes turn downward through the crack to see a branch extending into my view. Its length is drilled with tiny holes. A flute. The instrument isn’t just crammed into me, it’s grown inside and out. All around me. I have become it.

“Swim away, little prodigy,” the person in the tree across from me says.

Suddenly a noise so thunderous and loud that it rumbles the earth I’m affixed to breaks the air—something like a celebration thrown into chaos. The orange radiance coming from the rising sun bleeds into a sickening red, and its rays finally crest an unseen horizon, slashing spears of sinister light through the space between us.

Then, I would wake up. It never mattered how many times I had that same dream, I always woke with my heart racing and covered in sweat.

The nights after always made lessons the next morning difficult. The tension and unease combined with hours of playing in one spot made me feel like the nightmares were going to come true. That I might sprout roots where I sat, and that the flute in my mouth might become an extension of it and fuse as one.

I was no longer allowed to take breaks, and my lips would become chapped—my mouth painfully dry. I hardly noticed. Focus and discipline had been so thoroughly drilled into me that I played on without pause.

I never had any reason to enter the woods, and rarely did. Even so, the nightmares gave me an aversion to them. I feared that there truly might be bodies within the trees. That if I drew too near, they’d grab me and pull me into the trunks. Having grown up with tales about the spirits of the forest, my young brain began to believe that maybe there was merit to them.

Of course, I had a much more rational reason to fear the woods as well. The only ones who ever traveled into them were the hunters and foragers of the village, among them, my neighbor Sigurd. Sigurd was a kind and deeply loving man, one who looked after everyone as if family and never removed the woven bracelet his wife had made for him as if it were a part of his flesh. He was always kind to me, and never wary like some others.

In the morning, I’d often pass him on my way to my lessons, and he’d call out with a smile, “Prodigy! I could hear your playing through the elder Tuva’s walls yesterday. Curse it all—I’m going to scare the prey away humming the tunes you’ve gotten stuck in my head!” Sometimes, he’d even offer me a strip of jerky to eat on my walk.

One day—the only one I had off during the week—I did little else than sit outside our hut and whittle away. I had been working on carving a new flute from an antler Sigurd had offered me from one of his hunts. On his way by to join his fellow hunters at the trees' edge, he remarked on how it was coming along, and that he couldn’t wait to hear me playing it. I couldn’t wait either.

The compliment invigorated me to work more furiously on the instrument, hoping that by tomorrow, I would be able to fulfil Sigurd’s wish. Time even slipped away from me, and before I knew it, the sun was setting low in the sky. The flute was finished, and for my last few moments of daylight, I tested its merit, letting a peaceful tune drift off over the village. Mother eventually called from inside to pack away the tools and come in, to which I began to oblige, but just then, a ruckus from the trees caught my attention.

There was yelling—lots of it, and I could hear cries of agony. They spread like a disease among the huts as the source came closer, inspiring panic among the occupants as they witnessed whatever horrific scene was unfolding.

I moved around the edge of our home and looked between the alleys. Rushing back into the village was the group of hunters who had set out that morning, but they were not joking jovially with one another like normal. It took me a moment to realize the mangled, blood-soaked body they were carrying wasn’t a fresh kill—it was Sigurd.

His body was splayed against his comrade, head lolled forward so that I could not see his face as drool and snot poured from it in messy strings. His legs barely carried him along, and he had to be dragged, but it quickly became clear why.

His entire left side was missing. His arm up to the shoulder was gone, in its place a mess of stringy tissue and flesh that leaked blood like a cascading stream. Strange strings of pale flesh like long, tall grass flapped uselessly in the breeze, sputtering blood everywhere, and down his chest to his ribs were exposed. I could see the bone, but whether it be the distance or my mind imagining things, I swore that they were curved and twisted in ways that looked like the messy bramble of a blackberry bush. I knew little about anatomy, but I knew it was all wrong.

“Our breath is not ours—n-nothing is…” Sigurd gurgled in a way that made my stomach churn like a violent tide. “It wants *all* of it—every part…”

Nobody seemed to pay his rambles any mind, and instead, the nearest elder rushed forward and spoke to the man holding him up, “What is this?! What happened?!”

“He split off formation to pursue a deer,” the hunter responded, “We lost him for hours and then found him stumbling like this!”

The elder did not respond, only gestured for them to take him to the doctor's hut. Another elder had joined his side and looked around, seeing all the prying eyes and shooing with his hand, “All of you! Inside! Now!”

I was so frozen in shock and horror that I could not move, but I was forced to when my mom grabbed me and yanked me inside. I remember the next day being a blur as I waited to see what was going to happen with Sigurd. I knew little about medicine and naively hoped that he would somehow survive his injuries.

The next morning, my neighbor was dead.

“Killed by a wily buck,” The doctor explained, “Before he passed, he was able to tell us that he had pursued the deer straight into its mate, and it gored him fiercely.”

The explanation was bought without argument from anyone, but even to me at such a young age I did not buy it, and I knew deep down no one else did either. I even pointed out to my mother one day, “Sigurd's wounds—they did not look normal.”

She did not look at me, but I could sense her unease, “Have you ever seen such gore, my dear?”

Images flashed back into my head, and I worked to chase them away, “No, ma’am.”

“Well, that is what it looks like. Never how you’d quite expect.”

I did not press further, but the questions never left me. Neither did the sight of Sigurd. It certainly made my nightmares worse. In the backgrounds of the trees, I could always see my neighbor, bloodied and panting in pain, his eyes glowing like the person in the log across from me.

“Learn to swim, little prodigy,” they urged, “Or you shall drown in the earth with us.”

I was mortified upon hearing of Sigurd's passing. Never before in my lifetime had someone in my village died, and the concept felt foreign and haunting that a member of our already small number was permanently removed.

Though, I suppose that wasn’t correct to say. There was one other death within my short years that had occurred prematurely, but I was too young to recall it. The man who had found me the first night of my flute playing had died too. Allegedly, he had strayed too close to the coastal cliffs one night shortly after and accidentally slipped to his death, smashing his head on the rocks below. At least, that’s what the consensus was.

If you asked the right folk, however, they’d claim otherwise. Some elders said it was the spirits of the woods.

“If you don’t respect the boundaries laid out for us, it invites bad affairs such as these.”

Others had a more logical opinion. That it wasn’t an accident at all, and the man cast himself off the cliff. The reasons varied, but I couldn’t help but notice the wary glares I got whenever the topic was brought up. I tried to not dwell on it for too long.

Luckily, my lessons had not only made me resilient in my playing but also strengthened my mental fortitude. I was a master at keeping my emotions restrained. I had to be by that point. No longer was I allowed to stumble and pause for an interruption from the elder without being struck. If I so much as wavered in my playing, I would be punished, and forced to start again with even higher standards than before. Even when a live chicken, clucking and screaming, was brought in during one of my sessions, then silenced so close to me that it’s blood spattered my face, I did not waver in my playing.

I almost was one of those trees from my nightmares now. A motionless, unmoving thing that only whistled out the wind blowing through it.

By now, I did not understand what the point of my playing was. If it was supposed to be a gift, why was I now playing hidden away from everyone, and if it was mine to use, why was I forced to play exactly as the elder commanded?

I began to covet the promise once made to me long ago—the final step in my journey the day that I turned sixteen. Escape. The ability to finally leave this island and village behind so that I might see the world beyond it. I knew very little about it all, but by then, I knew that it had to be better than where I was. Rarely anyone in the village spoke to me personally anymore, and even my own father seldom glanced my way, though, I suppose he had always had a cold demeanor towards me.

The only exception to this was my mother, who had the opposite reaction. She heaved love upon me any chance she could, always gushing about how proud she was and that she would mourn the day I had to leave her. Despite my affinity for her, I was sorry to say that I would not be mourning. I was ready to go.

It would be time once I reached my sixteenth birthday, and one week before I did, for the first time in nearly eight years, I had a lesson that varied.

The Elder had taken a knife and began to carve a small cut into my arm with it, but that was not the oddity, and I played through it with expert control over the pain. This was a tactic she had done several times in the last few years, and it was no worse than any other at this point.

She made a noise of satisfaction at the flawless melody, then spoke, “You may stop playing.”

I knew better than to do so, or maybe I had been so broken that I couldn’t.

“Suit yourself,” She shrugged, “I know you think me cruel—you may see all of us that way—but understand, young one, that all of this was necessary for the steps that follow next.”

She stood and moved to the window, opening the shutters and letting the sunlight break through. I squinted out of reflex, but did not falter in my playing.

“The time is coming for you to leave. Do you recall long ago in your infancy what I told you? About why it is you who goes?”

I nodded. Only I had something to offer the spirits, and we did not make passage without such a gift.

“In a weeks time, you will make the journey to the far shore of the island,” The elder told me, “Past the boundaries of the spirits and into their land. You must play the whole time, just as I’ve trained you, and you shan't stop for anything. If you do, you will anger them, and you will never be seen again. Once you reach the shore, wait and continue playing. Your guide will arrive from there to take you away.”

She turned to me and moved closer, reaching out and placing a hand over my flute to silence me, something she’d never done. I tried to pull away to keep playing, thinking it a test, but she held strong.

“It is as simple as that. Do this, and you’ll be free of this place. Do you understand me, child?”

There was a graveness to her tone, and I swallowed to clear my dry throat. Till that point, I had always seen our island’s isolation as a choice. We got by with the resources we had, and lived in relative abundance and joy, celebrating and mourning as any other culture would. In that moment, though, I remember suddenly gaining the awareness that maybe all of that was out of necessity. Maybe this place was a prison, and those among my age had never bothered to question that fact.

“Yes, Ma’am.” I finally told her.

The beginning of my birthday ceremony was odd, and not at all like any before. The night before, I was prepped for my journey, then woken early on the morning-of, before the sun had even risen. My mother laid out new garbs of fine woven wool for me to wear for my journey, and had prepared me a hearty breakfast. Knowing that I was leaving, she could not cease her crying, and she kissed my head as many times as she could sneak in.

“I’m so proud of you, my little prodigy.” She told me.

My father did not share the same warmth as her, but I could him trying to hide eyes full of tears as he watched me get ready.

“I’ll be okay, papa.” I told him with a faint smile.

He nodded gratefully, but did not return the smile.

Normally, ceremonies were held at night after everyone’s work was done and merriment could be unapologetically had, but that day, everyone was already up and waiting for me. One by one, I made my way through everyone saying my goodbyes, and soon it was time to go.

Fitted with only my flute, my new clothes, and a bag of supplies, I was guided by my teacher to the edge of the woods.

“Do you remember your instructions?” She asked me, nodding at the only trail in and out of our village.

“Don’t stop playing,” I replied, “Just like aways.”

The elder nodded, then stepped aside before saying something that I never expected of her, “Thank you for your gift, child. You have no idea how much joy it will bring future generations.”

Cautiously, I looked back at my village one last time, the sky turning a dull shade of blue as the earth began to face the sun. They all stood gathered at the edge of the last houses, watching and waiting for me to start my journey. My father held my mother at the front who cried into his shoulder, and I gave a wave to her before turning and leaving.

The flute was to my mouth and playing out a steady concert for the rousing birds before I was even in. I had been training for this day my entire life, and I had an entire library of composed songs at my fingertips ready to fill the silence that lay between my village and the crashing waves of the shore. All there was to do was follow the path forward, an easy enough task even with the overgrown path before me.

It was still dim out, and would be for nearly another hour, making the whole forest bathe in that eerie blue existence between the night and the dawn. I had never been one to be frightened of the dark, having grown up in such a remote location, but the atmosphere of the woods began to weigh on me the deeper I went. I hadn’t realized how much the tales of spirits had affected me until I couldn’t see more than a few meters into the surrounding trees.

And it was *them* that seemed to press in the hardest; the trees, almost alive with their perfect stillness. A council of spectators, shocked stiff by the trespasser in their midst. My dreams were called back to my mind as I marched and played, and suddenly, each hollow I saw in their dark bark was host to a pair of eyes peering back at me. The familiar anxiety of the visions manifested in my gut, but it did not muddle my playing. Something so simple never could.

After an hour of walking, the path now thick with foliage, I began to hear the crash of the ocean up ahead, and the trees roused with its breeze. Ahead, I could see a clearing, and breaking through into it, I was surprised by what I saw.

The space was not the wild, untamed wilderness that I thought only existed out here. Instead, I stood on the edge of a rocky, coastal cliff side with waves breaching against the rocks below. Before me, carved away in the stone, was a large circle akin to the village square, among which were several carved wooden totems. Most of them were of average height and depicted featureless human figures holding their arms out, but one in the center towered above the rest.

It stood nearly five meters tall, and its weathered surface made it difficult to tell what it was depicting. It was an oblong shape, and there were many grooves and appendages that suggested some sort of animal. Near the top, a hole about a meter across was carved into its surface to suggest a gaping maw, and a lump crawled into my throat as I couldn’t help but wonder if it went down and inside like the hollow of a tree.

That lump did not halt my playing, however. Despite the unsettling nature of the place, I had arrived where I was instructed, and I assumed that it had simply been crafted by the elders long ago as a ceremony site. After all, some of our ceremonial garbs looked just as strange as the totems. I cautiously moved forward to the edge of the cliff, staring over the water as somewhere far beyond, the sun prepared to awaken.

Looking down, I did not see a boat or person waiting to take me away, but the elder had told me to wait, and someone would arrive. This had been my test, and they must have still needed to see if I would pass.

So I remained there, playing a melody that rang out over the sea while the waves acted as a drumbeat against the rocky shore. A few minutes passed, without event, and eventually, the ocean grew tiring to look at.

I looked to either side of me along the shore to see if I could spot anyone coming yet, but I was still entirely alone. As I did, the trees caught my eye once more, and that lonely feeling dissipated with a bitter taste. My eyes fixed in on one tree that was closest to me, at the edge of the circle. All the trunks were smooth for most of their length, only sprouting branches near their tops, but this one oddly had a branch low to the ground, just about level with my head.

I focused in on the thing, its shape strange with its odd roundness and the way it began skinny before turning club-like at its end. The bark seemed gnarled and sharp at its tip. The strangest part was near its base where it emerged from the tree. There was a perfect ring of lichen that grew around it.

I kept playing, but now I was transfixed, the oddity and the pressure of the forest making my heart beat rapidly. I scanned the coast again, but there was no boat or guide in sight, and unable to help it, I turned back to the tree. My flute played almost on its own now, my mind feeling detached from my body as I tried to solve why the branch irked me in such a peculiar way. That was when I regarded the shape at a new angle than before, and my blood became as chilled as the morning ocean air.

It looked like an arm that fused into the trunk of the tree.

As soon as the thought dawned on me, so did the sun. The sky burst from blue to orange with a radiant light, and it illuminated the grove. My eyes, still fixed on that wooden arm, saw the ring of lichen spring to life with color, and my breath hitched in my lungs causing my playing to cease.

It was not moss that circled the branch, it was a colorful bracelet of woven threads. The same colors that Sigurd’s wife had made his bracelet with.

In the absence of my playing, I suddenly heard a wooden creak behind me, and in the raging terror that I felt in that moment, I neglected every lesson I’d been given. I did not keep playing. I turned around.

There, from the hole in the center totem, something had emerged. Pale skin caked black with dirt and muck covered a tangle of twisted arms fanned atop one another like the petals of a flower. They met in the center at a body like a serpent, its core a gaping black maw that allowed me to see inside. Yellow putrid mushrooms grew around the rim that leaked a viscous red pudding, and within the dark I could see dozens of eyes glinting from the sun, desperately prodding at me. Echoing groans began to rouse within once they knew I’d seen them.

The thing that contained them hungrily thrashed its form, tugging itself further from the hole, and the arms stretched toward me, a prize that was just out of reach. Angrily, it whipped its head along with its mane of limbs, and a sound poured from it that I recognized. In my dream, I could never quite make it out, but now it was clear.

It was the sound of hundreds of voices crying out, mingled with wooden clanging chimes and instruments of all sorts. There were boys and girls alike in the choir, and the horrifying part was they all sounded young. My age. Even more paralyzing still was that with the sharpened ear I had toward my instrument, I knew with certainty that there was no flute within the chaos.

In the face of the unfathomable that I was presented with, I still found time to realize one last single horrifying thought. Never had my elders told me that I would be leaving the island for the mainland. I was only told that I would be granted passage to some place else.

On pure instinct, I backed away, but I had forgotten how close I had been to the ledge of the cliff. The ground fell out from under me, and in an instant, I tumbled away from the beast in the totem. As my feet fell backward over my head, and I faced the sun upside down, I saw it become choked with a blistering crimson, and the creature above me unleashed another burst of tormented voices. I heard the might of the earth as it began to rumble, but that’s when I collided with it, and my vision blurred. I clattered even further down the rocks before the frigid embrace of the ocean took me, icing the fresh blows and numbing me into a hazy sleep.

I’m unsure how I survived. Had it not been for the bag on my back, the fall alone would have killed me. I was found somewhere an untold amount of days later drifting in the Atlantic by a passing boat of fisherman, and was barely clinging to life.

From there, I was taken to the mainland and, through a miracle, was saved. Once I was able to talk again, I attempted to explain what had happened and where I was from, but anyone I told the stories to, whether police or doctors, only looked at me with pity and as if I were insane. They would believe the stories of my island, but in regards to the totem and strange creature living in it, I learned fast to simply stay quiet about it all.

Growing up was hard from then on. With no way of knowing where my island was, I had to grow up in the mainland, which was where I wanted to stay regardless. At age 16 with no knowledge of the real world and hardly any education, I struggled as I never had, but eventually, I found my way. I was no longer a ‘prodigy’ but I was more than okay with that.

I tried not to think much of my home, or what they had tried to do to me, but it was hard not to when I was talked to one day by an investigator who had been working on my case. After hearing my story, the authorities had gathered surveyors to search the sea and find this strange mystery colony that I had spoken of. Based on where they’d found me, there was only a handful of islands I could have been talking about.

There was only one that turned up with possible signs of civilization in their search, but they claimed that it couldn’t have been the place I had spoken of. It wasn’t an island, it was more of an archipelago separated by vast, harsh chasms, and the only village they found upon it were rundown huts amidst an overgrown forest covered in ivy. They claimed that to be that way, it would had to have been abandoned for decades.
