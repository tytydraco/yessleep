It was a tall paper bag in dull gold with a ribbon-twist handle and a spray of tissue at the neck, and I had not looked inside it, not once, in nine weeks.

Her handwriting was on the little tag. I knew the loops of the letters without having to read them.

I sat down on the edge of the bed with the chair in front of me. The stove ticked. Outside, the light had gone the colour of pewter, and somewhere beyond the glass a wind was getting up, I could hear it feeling along the eaves.

I told myself I was only going to put it somewhere. On the table, say, or on the shelf by the bed, where I’d be able to see it. That was all. It wasn’t a decision. I would lift it out, set it down, and leave it alone.

I stood up and leaned over the chair and put my hand into the holdall, and my fingers closed around the gold twist of the handle.

I lifted the bag out of the holdall and set it on the table, between the binoculars and the notebook. The tissue gave a dry whisper as it settled.

The tag was turned towards me. I had taken the loops of the letters for hers all the way from the hallway, and I had been wrong about that for nine weeks. It wasn’t a name, and it wasn’t English. It was a line of small, close characters, unhurried and dense, the strokes joined in a way I couldn’t follow. They looked like something that had been grown rather than written. I thought: she must have bought it somewhere. A shop. A market. She’d always loved a thing with a story.

I had not, until then, wondered where.

I told myself it didn’t matter. I put my right hand into the bag, past the tissue, and my fingers touched card.

The box was smaller than I expected, and heavier. It was covered all over in the same script, close and even, from corner to corner, with the lid and the sides and the edges all dark with it. Under my palm it was smooth, like a pebble from a river. It was also cold.

That’s too weak a word. It was cold in a way that did not feel like temperature. It went into the heel of my hand and up through the wrist before I could take it away, and then along the forearm, bone first, and I felt it settle in the elbow and the shoulder and down the whole right side of my ribs. I knew that side. It was the side the van had taken her on, the first side, in the half-second I’d watched. For one stupid moment I thought: it’s showing me.

I tried to let go.

I told my hand to open, and my hand didn’t. The fingers were curled round the box as if they’d been moulded there, and nothing from my head reached them. I pulled back, and the box came with me, out of the bag and into the air, and the tissue fell away from it in a soft spill of gold.

“Let go,” I said aloud, to my own hand.

The script moved.

I’m sure of that, though I’d have given a great deal not to be. A line at the corner of the lid lifted off the surface, very slightly, the way a hair lifts in a draught. Then another. The whole of the writing seemed to draw breath, and begin to flow, not in any direction I could name, but gradually, patiently, like ink in water. It slid down from the lid and across the card toward my knuckles. It was dark, and it was wet, and where it passed the surface gave off a thin sweet smell that I knew the instant I met it.

It reached my thumb. I felt it as a delicate, deliberate pressure, no more than a fingertip’s weight, and then a slow slick warmth where the cold had been, and then it was moving over the back of my hand.

I should have screamed. I think I opened my mouth to. What came out was a short, dry breath.

The script spilled off my fingers and onto the table. It crept across the wood, writhing in fine, patient threads, and wound itself round the foot of the thermos. It curled up the leg of the chair. A thread of it laid itself against the edge of the notebook, and the cellophane over the cover went dull and then blind. It was reaching, I thought, with a sort of horror that arrived all in one piece. It wasn’t spreading at random. It was reaching for things I’d touched.

The stove ticked. Outside, the wind had risen to a long steady note along the eaves. The window was the colour of slate.

I stood in the middle of the room with my arm out in front of me and the box clamped in my hand, while the writing moved across my skin like something very small that had finally found its way home.

The script stopped, as if it had heard something.

Every thread on the table lifted at once, in the same unhurried way it had left. It drew back across the wood, off the foot of the thermos and down the chair leg and out from under the edge of the notebook, and as it came I saw that it was not retreating. It was going home. The threads ran together on the boards, gathered into a dark line, and the line came up the leg of the table, over the edge, and across the back of my hand.

I felt it settle. There was a faint, ticklish pressure, then a slow tightening, like a glove being drawn on by someone gentle. It ran over the knuckles and along the tendons and up past the bone at the base of my thumb, and it stopped at the wrist. It sat there in a band of fine dark characters, close and even, as if they had always been there.

The cold went out of my arm, and warmth followed it in a long, loosening wave. My fingers uncurled from the box without any effort of mine, and the box dropped four inches onto the table and lay there with a dull knock.

It was bare. The lid, the sides and the edges were all plain, a pale, matt, finely grained surface like unvarnished wood or old bone, and there was not a mark on it. The tissue lay torn beside it, and the gold bag sagged on its side. The tag was still there on the handle. I looked at it, and it had gone blank.

I stood for a while breathing through my mouth. Then I lifted my right hand and turned it over in the grey light from the window.

The writing lay under the skin, or on it. I couldn’t tell which. It was dark and fine and perfectly steady. I pressed the band with my left thumb and felt only my own wrist, warm and ordinary, with the pulse going hard beneath it. I tried to rub it off with a thumbnail. It did not smudge. I flexed the fingers and the hand did what I asked, and everything in me that had been braced for pain found nothing to brace against.

I washed my hands. I stood at the basin for a long time with the cold water running over my wrist, and the writing neither faded nor shifted. It lay there as though the water was something that happened to other people.

Then I dried them, and went to the stove, and found the kettle.

It took me ten minutes to make tea. I did it properly. I found the tin and the cups and a spoon, and I filled the kettle from the tap and watched the surface shiver as the heat came up. I stood with my back to the table. I don’t remember deciding not to look at the box, only that I didn’t.

The tea was dark, and strong, and too hot. I carried it to the armchair and sat down with the mug held in both hands, my right wrist resting on my left, and the steam rising into my face. The stove ticked. The wind along the eaves had settled to a long, level breath. For the first time since the airport I felt something close to calm, and I distrusted it at once.

The mug was warm against my palms, and I was warm.

Somewhere behind me, someone said something.
