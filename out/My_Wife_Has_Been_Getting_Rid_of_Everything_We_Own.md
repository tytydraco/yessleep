The smell started near the garage.

Lauren noticed it one morning while we were leaving for work. She was halfway down the driveway when she stopped and looked toward the bushes beside the house.

"Do you smell that?"

"You know I don't."

"Right. Sorry." She covered her nose with her sleeve. "Something died over there."

I lost my sense of smell after a sinus infection six years ago. Lauren still occasionally forgets, usually when something smells particularly good or particularly awful. She once asked me to smell a candle she'd been considering buying, then laughed so hard at herself that she dropped it.

"Probably a mouse," I said. "I'll check when I get home."

She said she'd already looked. Whatever it was, she couldn't find it.

I spent twenty minutes searching the bushes that evening, including the narrow space between the garage and our neighbor's fence. There were some dead leaves, an old tennis ball, and a plastic grocery bag tangled around a shrub. Nothing that explained the smell.

Lauren complained about it a few more times that week. She described it as rotten meat with something sweet underneath, and said it was especially bad in the mornings. I offered to call someone if it continued, but by the following weekend, she wasn't mentioning it anymore.

Around the same time, she suggested we have a yard sale.

We'd spent nearly $6,000 replacing our furnace and air conditioner that spring, which had taken a substantial bite out of our savings. Lauren had been looking for ways to build the account back up, and clearing out the garage seemed like an easy place to start.

We'd accumulated an embarrassing amount of junk in the eight years we'd lived there. Half-empty paint cans, boxes of Christmas decorations, exercise equipment neither of us used. We also had a spare bedroom full of furniture left over from our first apartment.

Lauren handled most of the organizing. She photographed things for online listings, made a spreadsheet of prices, and spent a couple of evenings sorting through the garage while I helped carry boxes.

We made a little over $400 that Saturday. More importantly, we could finally park both cars inside again.

She was pleased enough with the results that she suggested selling a few more things. I didn't object. There was still plenty we could do without, and the extra money was welcome.

The first disagreement came over our dining room table.

We'd bought it secondhand shortly after getting married. One of the legs wobbled, and the finish was worn through in places where we'd set down hot plates. Lauren had been talking about replacing it for years.

I came home one Thursday and found the dining room empty.

She'd listed the table that morning and given it away to a woman who could pick it up immediately.

"You gave it away?"

"It wasn't worth much."

"Then why get rid of it? We still eat there."

"We can use the counter until we find something else."

I reminded her that the whole point of the yard sale had been to save money. Giving away furniture we would eventually have to replace didn't accomplish that.

She acknowledged that I had a point and apologized for not asking me first.

We ate dinner at the kitchen counter that evening. It was awkward without the table, but Lauren seemed happy with the extra space. She talked about putting a smaller one in there eventually, something that wouldn't make the room feel so crowded.

I assumed we'd start looking for one over the weekend. Instead, she began clearing out the living room.

A bookshelf went to her sister. She donated an old cabinet, then asked whether I'd mind moving the television upstairs. She said she wanted to see what the room looked like without so much furniture.

I helped with the television but drew the line at the couch.

"That's staying," I told her.

She laughed. "Okay. I wasn't going to get rid of everything."

For a while, the changes were easy enough to live with. The house looked unusually bare, but I could understand wanting a fresh start. We'd been talking about repainting anyway, and Lauren had always enjoyed rearranging furniture.

The thermostat was harder to explain.

She'd always been the one complaining about the cold. Now I kept finding the air conditioning set to 62, sometimes lower. When I asked about it, she said the house felt stuffy and she was having trouble getting comfortable at night.

I suggested opening the windows in the evening. She didn't want to because the air outside was too warm.

One morning, I woke up shivering and found the thermostat set to 59.

We argued about it over breakfast. She insisted she'd only turned it down because she'd been uncomfortable during the night. I pointed out that we'd just spent thousands of dollars replacing the HVAC system, and running it constantly wasn't going to help our finances.

We agreed on 67.

I bought a small locking cover for the thermostat a few days later, after she changed it again. Lauren was irritated when she saw it, though she didn't make much of an argument. She asked for the key, and I told her we could discuss changing the temperature together if she was uncomfortable.

That evening, I found her sitting in the garage with the door open, reading a book. It was cooler outside than in the house, and she'd brought a blanket to sit on.

"Just getting some air," she said when I asked why she wasn't inside.

She seemed perfectly content, so I left her alone.

A few nights later, I woke up and found her sleeping on the garage floor.

She'd laid out a blanket beside the car and curled up on it without a pillow. The garage door was closed, and the concrete was cold enough that I could feel it through my socks.

I woke her and asked what she was doing.

"I couldn't get comfortable upstairs."

I told her I wasn't going to have her sleeping on concrete because she wanted the house colder. We argued about the thermostat again, and eventually I agreed to remove the cover if she'd stop sleeping in the garage.

We settled on 65.

For a few days, she kept it there. Then I started noticing the temperature dropping again, usually a degree or two at a time. By the end of the following week, the thermostat was back down to 60.

I considered putting the cover back on, but Lauren had started spending her evenings in the coldest parts of the house, and I didn't want to find her sleeping in the garage again. I wore a sweatshirt indoors and kept an extra blanket on the bed.

She seemed so much more comfortable in the cold that I began to wonder whether something was physically wrong with her.

The first humidifier arrived the following week.

It was a large commercial unit she'd found secondhand. She said the air conditioning was drying out her skin, which wasn't unreasonable. We already had a little humidifier in the bedroom that we used during the winter.

But this machine was considerably bigger, and she ran it almost constantly.

Within a few days, condensation had collected along the windows. The house felt damp even with the air conditioning running, and the cold air made my clothes feel clammy whenever I came downstairs.

I bought a humidity meter and showed her the reading.

Eighty-one percent.

She seemed surprised, but not particularly concerned.

I explained that we'd end up with mold if we kept the house that wet. She agreed to turn the machine off.

When I got home the next day, it was running again. I unplugged it and carried it into the garage.

That evening, Lauren asked where I'd put it.

"In the garage. We're not running that thing anymore."

She wanted to know whether we could compromise by using it for a few hours each day. I told her we could talk about getting a smaller unit if she was having problems with dry skin, but I wasn't willing to risk damaging the house.

She didn't argue. She just looked disappointed.

I was beginning to feel like every conversation we had was about something she'd changed while I was at work.

There were still normal evenings. We cooked together, watched television upstairs, and made plans to visit her parents for Thanksgiving. She was doing well at work and had recently been given responsibility for training a new employee. Nothing about her suggested she was struggling to function.

But the house had become an obsession.

I came home one afternoon to find the couch gone.

She hadn't sold it. A man from a local charity had picked it up that morning.

That was the first time I really lost my temper.

I'd specifically told her I wanted to keep it, and she'd agreed. Now it was gone, along with several hundred dollars' worth of furniture she'd donated over the preceding weeks.

Lauren listened while I explained how much it bothered me. She apologized, but when I asked why she'd done it, she couldn't give me a satisfactory answer.

"I thought we'd have more room," she said.

"For what?"

She looked around the empty living room.

"I don't know. I just like it better this way."

I asked whether she was unhappy with our marriage, whether she was worried about money, or whether something had happened at work that she hadn't told me about.

She said no to all of it.

Then she started crying.

She told me she knew I was upset and that she hated fighting with me, but she couldn't understand why I was making such a big deal out of some old furniture.

I held her for a while. Eventually, we agreed that neither of us would make any more changes to the house without discussing them first.

I believed she meant it.

That weekend, I called her sister.

I explained what had been happening and asked whether she'd noticed anything unusual about Lauren.

They'd had lunch together a few days earlier. Her sister said she'd seemed happy, maybe a little distracted, but otherwise completely normal.

When I told her about the furniture, she was surprised. She offered to come over and talk to Lauren, though we agreed it might be better if I approached the subject first.

I started reading about compulsive behavior and mental health conditions that could cause sudden changes in personality. I didn't know what I was looking for. Lauren wasn't behaving impulsively at work, and she hadn't shown any interest in spending money on things unrelated to the house. If anything, she'd become more cheerful and patient.

The following Monday, I asked whether she'd be willing to talk to her doctor.

She wanted to know why.

I explained that I was worried about how much her behavior had changed.

She thought about it and said she would make an appointment if it would make me feel better.

Two days later, I found her attaching something to our bedroom wall.

It was a thick brown material that looked like an enormous roll of doormat. She'd removed the pictures and shelves, patched the holes, and was working her way across the wall behind our bed with a staple gun.

She called it coir matting.

Apparently, it was made from coconut fibers and used in landscaping to prevent soil erosion.

"Why are you putting that in our bedroom?"

"I thought it would look nice."

I ran my hand over the rough surface. Fibers came loose against my palm.

"Lauren, this is gardening material."

"I know."

"It's going to shed everywhere. And what happens when you take it down? You've put hundreds of staples in the drywall."

She assured me she'd repair the holes eventually.

I asked her to stop until we could figure out what she was trying to accomplish.

She agreed, but the next evening, another section of wall was covered.

I took the staple gun away. She followed me downstairs, asking where I was putting it. When I told her I wasn't comfortable with her continuing, she asked me to reconsider. Her hands were trembling, and she kept glancing toward the stairs.

"Why do you need this on the walls?" I asked.

"I don't know how to explain it."

"Then try."

She was quiet for a moment.

"It feels better with it there."

"What does?"

"The room."

I asked whether she meant it looked better.

She shook her head.

"It's just better."

I told her I was keeping the staple gun until we'd spoken to her doctor. She looked toward the stairs again, but eventually agreed.

The following morning, I asked whether she'd made the appointment.

She admitted she hadn't.

I sat with her while she called the office. The earliest available appointment was the following Wednesday, and I asked her to tell them about the sudden changes in her behavior. She did, although she sounded embarrassed explaining it to the receptionist.

I was relieved that we finally had a date.

By then, the house was becoming difficult to live in.

The temperature stayed around 60 degrees, sometimes lower, and the humidity had crept back up despite my efforts to keep the humidifier turned off. Lauren filled bowls and shallow containers with water and left them near the vents. I found damp towels hanging in the empty dining room and a spray bottle beside our bed.

The windows remained fogged throughout the day. Water collected along the sills and ran down the walls beneath them, leaving dark streaks in the paint. The hardwood floors had begun to swell along the seams.

Lauren kept the blinds closed, even during the day. She said the sunlight made the rooms feel too warm. She'd also taken down the curtains, explaining that they were collecting moisture.

The coir matting covered most of one bedroom wall. When I pulled a loose section away from the drywall, the material behind it was wet.

I took pictures of everything to show her doctor. Looking through them on my phone, I realized how much had changed in just a few weeks. The rooms were nearly empty, the curtains were gone, and the walls had developed dark patches that hadn't been there before.

I moved into the office with an air mattress.

Lauren was upset about that, but she understood when I explained that the bedroom was too damp for me to sleep comfortably.

The office became the only room I could tolerate. I closed its vents, kept the door shut, and used a small electric heater to take the chill out of the air. I left the blinds open during the day, partly because the rest of the house had become so dark.

She offered to move the humidifier out of the hallway. When I reminded her that I'd already put it in the garage, she admitted she'd brought it back inside while I was at work.

I carried it out again, wondering how many more times we were going to have the same argument.

The air mattress was uncomfortable, and I was waking several times each night, usually because I could hear Lauren moving around downstairs. Sometimes I'd find her standing in the dining room with the lights off, looking at the walls. She'd turn toward me when I spoke and ask whether I needed anything.

I considered staying with my brother for a few days. But I was worried about leaving her alone, particularly after finding the bedroom wall wet enough that the drywall had begun to soften. I also thought about calling her sister again, though I wasn't sure what I expected her to do that I hadn't already tried.

Lauren still brought me coffee in the mornings, asked about my day, and apologized whenever she realized she'd upset me. She seemed genuinely concerned about how tired I was, even while she continued making changes that kept me awake.

By the end of that week, she'd covered the remaining bedroom walls and started on the dining room.

I hadn't seen her working on either room. She'd apparently been doing it while I was asleep or at work, using another staple gun she'd bought without telling me.

On Friday evening, I found our mattress leaning against the garage wall.

Lauren was in the kitchen, sorting through a box of household items she'd collected for donation.

"Please tell me you're not getting rid of our bed."

She looked embarrassed.

"I was going to ask you."

"After you moved it into the garage?"

"A woman said she could come look at the mattress tomorrow. I haven't promised her anything."

I went upstairs to look.

The bed frame had been dismantled, and nearly every surface was covered in the brown matting. There were damp patches on the floor where water had collected beneath the material.

The room was freezing. I could feel the cold coming through my socks, and the damp air made it unpleasant to breathe. With the blinds shut and the walls covered, there was barely enough light to see the opposite side of the room.

I went back downstairs and told her we needed to leave the house until we could figure out what was happening.

She asked whether we could wait until the next morning.

I refused. I told her I was afraid she was having some kind of medical problem and that I wasn't willing to spend another night in the house the way it was.

She sat beside me on the kitchen floor and apologized. She said she knew things had gotten out of hand and promised we'd find someone to talk to in the morning.

I asked her to come stay at a hotel with me that night.

She didn't want to.

When I pressed her, she became increasingly upset. She wasn't angry with me. She kept saying she needed to stay home, but she couldn't tell me why.

I finally agreed to wait until morning, on the condition that we leave together first thing, regardless of how she felt. We would go to urgent care instead of waiting for Wednesday's appointment, and we wouldn't return until I'd had someone assess the damage to the house.

She agreed.

I didn't feel good about leaving it there, but I was exhausted, and it was the first time she'd agreed to leave at all.

Later, we ate dinner at the kitchen counter and talked about ordinary things. She told me about a coworker who'd accidentally forwarded an email complaining about her manager to the manager himself. We laughed about it, and for a little while, she sounded like the Lauren I'd known for eight years.

After dinner, she washed the dishes while I dried them.

"I don't think I'm going to sleep tonight," she said.

"Neither am I, probably."

She smiled.

"No, I'm too excited."

"About what?"

She put a plate into the cabinet.

"I think tonight's the night."

"What night?"

She considered the question.

"I'm not sure."

"Lauren, what are you talking about?"

"I don't know." She was still smiling. "I just have a really good feeling."

I reminded her that we were leaving in the morning.

She agreed, kissed me goodnight, and went upstairs.

I lay awake on the air mattress for a while, listening to the air conditioner cycle on and off. I'd been sleeping badly for several nights, and the conversation had left me feeling more helpless than angry.

I must have fallen asleep shortly after eleven.

A dragging noise woke me sometime during the night.

It sounded like somebody moving something heavy across the driveway. There was a long scrape, a pause, and then another scrape, closer to the house.

I listened from the air mattress, trying to determine whether it was coming from outside or downstairs.

The sound moved along the wall beneath the office window.

I raised one of the blinds.

Our motion-activated floodlight was on, illuminating the driveway and the bushes beside the garage. The driveway was empty, but the scraping continued directly beneath the window.

Something pressed against the siding hard enough to make the frame creak.

A thick, folded surface shifted across the lower corner of the glass. It was wet enough to reflect the floodlight, with deep ridges running through it and a series of dark openings that narrowed and widened as it moved.

It was too close to the window for me to see its shape.

The surface slid out of view, accompanied by a slow dragging sound that continued toward the back of the house.

I backed away from the window. I couldn't think of an animal large enough to make that noise that could also move along the side of a house.

I went downstairs and checked the doors. Both were locked.

Through the kitchen window, I could see the deck and part of the backyard. The motion light there had come on as well, but whatever I'd seen was already out of view.

The scraping continued somewhere beyond the kitchen wall, then gradually faded.

When it didn't return, I went upstairs to check on Lauren.

She was lying on the blankets she'd arranged in our bedroom, sleeping on her side. The room was dark, and the coir matting made the walls look almost black.

I considered waking her but decided against it. I didn't know what I'd seen, and she was finally sleeping.

Back in the office, I kept the lights on and sat with my phone in my hand. I thought about calling the police, but there was no sign of anyone trying to enter the house, and the noise had stopped.

I stayed awake listening for it to return. At some point, I must have fallen asleep again.

The next thing I remember is waking to the sound of running water.

I assumed Lauren was taking a shower, but the sound grew louder as I reached the stairs. Downstairs, water was spreading across the kitchen floor and soaking into the hallway carpet.

The back door was standing open, and our garden hose ran across the kitchen floor and into the dining room.

Lauren was standing in several inches of water, spraying the walls.

She'd changed into an old T-shirt and shorts. Her hair was tied back, and she was barefoot.

The coir matting had darkened where she'd soaked it. Water streamed down the fibers, collecting on the hardwood and spreading toward the kitchen.

"Lauren, turn that off!"

She turned toward me.

"Oh, you're up."

"Turn it off. You're flooding the house."

She lowered the hose.

"I'm almost finished."

I crossed the room and twisted the nozzle shut myself. Water soaked through my socks.

"Look at what you've done."

She glanced down at the floor.

"I know it's a lot of water."

"The floor's ruined. The walls are going to be ruined. What are you doing?"

She seemed confused by my reaction.

"I wanted to make sure everything was wet enough."

"For what?"

She looked toward the wall behind me.

Several swollen shapes were attached to the matting near the ceiling.

They were roughly the size of fists, though their outlines were irregular. Some hung in loose folds while others bulged tightly against the fibers. Their surfaces had the color and texture of uncooked chicken skin, with darker tissue visible beneath the thinner areas.

Water ran over them and dripped from their lower edges.

As I moved closer, one of the shapes tightened. A crease formed across its surface, deepened, and slowly relaxed.

More of them clung to the matting farther along the wall, ranging from grape-sized lumps to swollen masses that protruded several inches from the drywall.

One had a narrow dark line running through its center. Something shifted beneath it, pressing outward before settling back into the mass.

I thought about the sound outside the window and the folded surface moving along the siding.

Lauren came to stand beside me, smiling as she looked up at the wall.

"Did you put these here?" I asked.

She shook her head.

I took her by the wrist.

"We're leaving."

She looked down at my hand.

"Why?"

"Because something was outside our house last night. I saw it. I don't know what those things are, but we're not staying here."

She glanced toward the wall.

"They're not hurting anything."

"You don't know that."

I tried to lead her toward the kitchen. She resisted, not violently, but enough that I stopped.

"Lauren, please."

Her smile faded.

"I don't want to leave."

The largest mass contracted again, revealing a long, narrow shape pressing against its surface from the inside.

I released her wrist.

"I'm going to get help. Please don't touch anything."

She called after me as I hurried through the kitchen, but I was already grabbing my keys.

Outside, the morning was bright and clear.

Across the street, Mr. Donnelly was arranging furniture in his driveway. He'd lived there since before we bought our house. He was retired and usually spent Saturday mornings washing his truck.

Today, his driveway was covered with boxes and furniture. Two dining chairs, a coffee table, several boxes of kitchenware, and a television sat beside a handwritten YARD SALE sign.

He saw me and waved.

Farther down the street, another neighbor was carrying a recliner out through her front door. There was a yard sale two houses beyond hers, and another at the corner.

The garage door of the house beside ours was open. A man I recognized from the neighborhood was unloading a large machine from the back of his SUV.

It was an industrial humidifier.

Beside it were three enormous rolls of coir matting.

He noticed me watching and raised a hand in greeting.

Farther down the street, a woman was carrying a mattress onto her front lawn. Two children were arranging boxes on a folding table while their father brought out a bookshelf.

Everywhere I looked, people were carrying things out of their houses. Some had already started carrying other things in.

I got into my car and drove.

Twenty minutes outside town, I pulled into a gas station and called the police.

I told the dispatcher that my wife had been acting strangely for weeks, that she'd flooded our house, and that I'd found something growing on the walls. I explained that I was worried about her safety and needed someone to check on her.

She asked whether I thought somebody had broken into the house.

I told her about the noises I'd heard during the night.

She said she'd send an officer.

While I waited, I called Lauren.

She answered on the second ring and sounded perfectly normal.

I begged her to leave the house and meet me somewhere. Anywhere she wanted. I told her I'd come get her.

She said she was fine and asked when I'd be home.

Before hanging up, she reminded me that somebody was supposed to come look at our mattress that afternoon.

An officer called me back about forty minutes later.

He said he'd spoken with Lauren and that she seemed perfectly fine. There was no indication she was in immediate danger, and he hadn't found any evidence of a break-in.

I asked whether he'd gone inside.

"Yes, sir."

"Did you see the dining room?"

"I did."

"Then you saw what's on the walls."

He hesitated.

I waited for him to tell me what they were, or to say he'd contacted somebody who could identify them.

Instead, he said, "Your wife's done a really nice job getting everything ready."

I hung up.

I don't know what's happening, or how far it's spread. I just know that whatever came to our house has been visiting others.

Stay away from Ashford.
