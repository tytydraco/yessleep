Hey y’all, I was the Pilot In Command of Flight 411 and I have a strange problem. The problem is that everybody thinks I’m dead. 

Flight 411 was reported to have crashed into the ocean over two weeks ago about half way into its flight, killing everyone on board, however that’s a lie.  

The incident craft in question was a Boeing 737-800, more infamously known as the “Max 8”

For some of you, that name probably sounds familiar for all the wrong reasons. To be brief, a few years ago Boeing rolled out this bad boy which was longer and could hold more passengers than other 737s. The thing is, making the plane longer meant it would fly differently, while not a massive difference, this meant that pilots would have to get specific training to fly this aircraft.

That would have cost lots of time and money. So Boeing decided to build in a fly-by-wire system that would adjust the pilot’s inputs to account for the new aerodynamics. Thing was, they didn’t tell the pilots that, and they didn’t build in a way to turn it off. 

Normally fly-by-wire isn’t an issue, Airbus planes use it all the time, as do most modern fighter jets. But this one was shitty and had a habit of freaking out and sending the plane into a nose dive. Since the pilots never really had control, there was nothing they could do. 

3 crashes and lots of lawsuits later they eventually fixed the problem but nobody trusted the damn thing anymore, passenger or pilot. 

Except me, of course. 

Now, why was any of this relevant? Because whenever there was anything even remotely wrong with a Max-8, the news exploded with coverage, always blaming that damn fly-by-wire system. Hell the IFE could have gone out mid-flight and someone would have still blamed it on the fly-by-wire system. 

So when Flight 411 was lost, they blamed it on the fly-by-wire system, said that's what caused it to crash into the ocean.

But the loss of Flight 411 was not caused by a fly-by-wire system, and it did not crash into the ocean. My proof? I was the Captain of Flight 411, and as of me writing this, I am alive. 

So if Flight 411 didn’t crash into the ocean, killing everyone, what happened to everyone else? 

Flight 411 was a once-a-week trans-pacific redeye route that functioned as a low-priority route for the airline, servicing a rural hub in the Midwest. The route was strange, not just for the fact that I didn’t even know this hub existed until I was scheduled to fly it, but because it always had the same passengers on each flight, give or take a few. 

It flew out of PHNL, where I was based, and to this hub. Most airports, well all major civilian airports, are supposed to have a four letter code to identify them with. PHNL for Daniel K. Inouye Intl. in Hawaii, for example. 

Except for this hub location. Instead, we had to fill the ICAO field with four Z’s and provide the coordinates of the hub. 

Truthfully this didn’t bother me much, though as a major commercial airline pilot who wasn’t exactly on a short-haul route these non-standard filings should have been an anomaly for me. It probably helped that because of how identical each flight was in terms of passengers and load, even down to the seating arrangements, I craved the slight deviation in the norm. 

The night of the incident was no different.

I arrived at the airport about 3 hours before the flight, a bit earlier than I usually would to account for the Labor-day weekend traffic. The weather was fair, optimal conditions for flying. Clearance delivery was a bit slow, likely due to all the outbound traffic. 

My First Officer, a dependable man named Jason Higa, and I chatted as we waited at the gate for pushback and start clearance. We talked about this Pho place they had at the terminal in this hub that, by both of our accounts, was the best damn Pho on the planet. You could practically taste it from memory. Whenever you’d step off the plane it would be the first smell that worked its way into your nose. Not the kerosene, not the aging linoleum paneling, and certainly not the musty carpets.

The mind controlling aroma of the herbal spices, star anise, cinnamon, and the unmistakable calling of that perfectly simmered bone broth would pick us up by our ties and drag us down the concourse right to the counter. No matter how much we had eaten beforehand, or how we might have felt, we always had room for their Pho. 

I’m sad I won't ever get to experience that again.

When it finally came over comms that it was our time we began our startup procedure. A task we had each performed easily over a thousand times by this point. Our hands glided over the overhead control panels, over rows of bleed air valve controls and electrical bus switches, sending compressed air from the APU into the engines to start them up. 

The tug pushed us back from our gate as our engines roared to life, and once clear we began taxiing to our takeoff runway under our own power. 

If you don’t know, PHNL is famous for its 8R/26L runway. It’s a massive artificial island built just to the south of the main airport structure, connected by a narrow artificial land bridge taxiway. During the day you get to take off surrounded by the beautiful ocean on either side.

During the night, however, it’s like the world just stops existing outside of the marked areas. No beauty, and no margins if you mess up on takeoff. 

But Flight 411 was not destined for the ocean, not destined to overrun the runway and crash into the shallows just past the localizer array. Flight 411 was destined to disappear mid flight, by a means of which I still cannot explain.

Oblivious to our fates and the fates of the other 140 souls on board we departed Runway 8R at 9:34 PM. 

I’m not the NTSB so I won’t drop the word-for-word flight plan, nor will I go into excessive detail about every correction and adjustment we made before handoff, but I will mention one thing. 

Much like the highways you drive on with your car, there are air corridors that are marked by GPS coordinates that all organized air traffic flies along with little exception. This is doubly so for ocean crossings. 

While SOP for our airline typically has us follow these corridors, tonight we were instructed to fly a different, and to my knowledge, unmarked air corridor. This was discussed well before take off with the company and the FAA. ATC was informed of this decision but would not be able to provide coverage during our crossing as a result, as they did not have any record of the corridor on their end and thus no real means of keeping track of us. 

This was not entirely unusual, military aviation has almost an entirely separate ruleset they work with whenever they aren’t interacting with commercial air infrastructure.  However for a commercial airline with no special clearances beyond the norm, that was unheard of, as well as the fact that this was the first time in flying this route that such a flight plan had been filed.

Beyond the odd level of secrecy and different hands involved in this flight plan, nothing stood out to me as being dangerous or even worthy of note. Forecasts for the area were clear, no major storms that we couldn’t navigate around, no TFRs or potential conflicts that we knew of. 

We still had our transponder on, and while outside of radio and radar coverage for a majority of the crossing we still had GPS coverage if anything urgent came up. 

After about an hour or so we heard a knock at the cabin door, that meant the chief steward had brought our meals. After 9/11 we are supposed to use the intercom to confirm before opening the cockpit door for anyone but this had become such a routine I could swear I’d hear her footsteps before she’d knock. 

Much to my surprise, and further proving why we should have really enforced that protocol, it was not the chief stewardess I had grown accustomed to after all this time. Instead I was greeted by a much younger woman with long, red hair that was tied up in a loose ponytail. Uniform standards stated that hair needed to be either short or tied up into a bun, but I didn’t really mind that much, figured she was newly assigned to this route and might have been a fresh graduate from the training program. 

I thanked her for the food and closed the door behind her. An uneasiness in my stomach began to grow as the flight continued. I thought maybe I was paranoid, but the notion that on my alarmingly routine flight there would be not one but two notable differences was impossible to ignore. 

I brought this to the attention of my co-pilot, who agreed that it was highly unusual given the prior routine and suggested doubling up on our in-flight monitoring. Feeling satisfied with that resolution we made sure to double up on the fuel calculations and route tracking. I doubted, that with everyone in attendance while flight planning, we would have forgotten to add the right amount of fuel for the new route that same confidence had been the cause of crashes before. I very much intended to land safely and taste that sweet delicious Pho yet again. 

At what I could roughly call the half-way mark was when the incident began. 

At first I noticed the hair on my arms began to stand up, indicative of a build up of static charge. This was followed by St. Elmo’s fire which started to form and lick at the cockpit windows. Normally St. Elmo’s fire only really developed on the support tracks for the flaps and other “sharp” surfaces, and really only

In active weather systems. 

There was one instance I could recount where the severity of St. Elmo’s fire was such that it formed the way it had now.

British Airways Flight 009. 

The aircraft in that incident had been flying through a plume of ash from a recent eruption in Indonesia, at that time nobody knew that ash could choke out engines and turn a modern airliner into a gliding brick. This was the incident that taught us what we know now. 

But before the engines were choked out by the volcanic glass that began melting into and gumming up the engines, the pilots reported seeing St. Elmo’s fire rippling across their windshields. 

Jason apparently had the same revelation as I did as we both began scouring radar and charts of the region. 

Though, both of us knew it was impossible yet it was reality. Either a volcano had spontaneously erupted and rose out of the ocean below us, or we were flying into a region that had somehow obtained a massive static electric charge, a dangerous level of charge. 

With the sun still well below the horizon and the new moon offering no reprieve we had only our instruments to help us navigate out of this potentially catastrophic situation. While planes are designed to be resistant to lightning strikes, with many having been directly struck mid air before, there has yet to be a scenario involving a static charge as severe as the one we believed to be facing. 

As the minutes ticked by, the flames of St. Elmo’s fire only grew, eventually covering the entire windshield in dancing waves of heavenly luminescence. 

We descended rapidly, hoping to break out from whatever storm our radar might have missed. We altered our heading in just about every conceivable way in an effort to try and break free of whatever this was. Yet the fire continued to grow. 

I made the decision to declare an emergency and we began vectoring for the nearest airport. Radio and Radar coverage was still an hour away at this point, but it was all we could do. 

I briefed the flight attendants on the situation, informing them that while the plane appears fine for now that we should make preparations to ditch the aircraft in the event we lose engine power. 

Shortly after the briefing I heard a scream from the cabin, followed by more screams. If I had to guess I’d say they were freaking out as they watched the wings and body of the aircraft become engulfed in St Elmo’s fire. This guess would likely have been the case as I saw flashes of light followed by muffled popping sounds from behind the instrument panels. 

Before I had time to even say anything I heard an unfamiliar voice begin shouting the words that no one, pilot or passenger, ever hopes to hear. 

“BRACE BRACE BRACE, HEADS DOWN, STAY DOWN, BRACE BRACE BRACE,”

In no time at all the rest of the flight attendants began to chant the mantra in perpetuity, up until the very last second. 

But the plane was not in free fall, nor did I give the order to prepare for an emergency landing. I specified the possibility, but maybe the new stewardess misunderstood it. Regardless, it’s better to have them focus on their own survival than continue screaming again. As loud as the chanting was, the rhythm was soothing to me. 

The last thing I remembered from that flight was looking over at Jason as he became enveloped in that same heavenly glow that had wrapped itself around the plane, eventually overwhelming my senses in a sea of blinding sea of white. 

When I woke up I was in a hospital bed, back in Honolulu. When I asked about what had happened to me the nurse had said someone found me unconscious on a beach in Waikiki. She specified that I was not dragged out of the water but instead was passed out under a massive tree, they thought I was a homeless John Doe. Apparently all I had on me were the clothes on my back.

When I tried to tell the nurse who I was she didn’t believe me. Said that Flight 411 crashed in the ocean weeks ago. When I finally got a hold of a newspaper I saw Jason, but standing next to him was someone I didn’t recognize. Someone who was wearing my uniform but looked nothing like me. Every photo with my name in it that had been put out in relation to the story featured a man who looked nothing like me. All of these photos were ones I remembered being taken, moments I remember in full, but it was as if they’d plastered some stranger’s face on top of mine.

If I find out more about this I’ll be sure to put it out there. Right now I’m at the state library doing my best to reclaim my life from a government that declared me dead. Wish me luck.
