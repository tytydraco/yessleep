When I was about 14 or 15 years old, I had a girlfriend who lived roughly a 30-minute walk from my house. One night, after walking her home, I started making my way back along the same route I always took. Nothing felt unusual at first. It was late, but not particularly dark, and I had walked that road countless times before.

There was, however, one part of the route that always made me uneasy.

Near the top of a hill, where the road curved toward my neighborhood, sat a small cemetery. Growing up, I was always told to pass by it as quickly as possible. People in the area often talked about hearing voices there late at night, and there had supposedly been a number of accidents near that corner. Whether I believed the stories or not, I always crossed to the opposite side of the road before reaching the cemetery.

That night, though, I wasn't paying attention. My mind was elsewhere, and before I realized it, I was already walking alongside the cemetery's stone wall.

The wall stood about shoulder height and ran all the way to the entrance gate. It was a clear night with a half moon overhead. Between the moonlight and the streetlights farther down the road, I could make out several gravestones and mausoleums inside the cemetery.

As I walked, I noticed a small flickering candle in the distance among the graves.

That wasn't unusual. In Mexico, it's common for people to visit the graves of loved ones, even at night, so I didn't think much of it and kept walking.

As I got closer, I began to hear faint sobbing coming from the same direction as the candlelight.

Again, I told myself it was probably someone mourning.

Then I saw her.

A woman was kneeling beside a grave, bent over as if she were crying. A chill immediately ran down my spine.

Without really thinking about it, I started walking faster. I kept my eyes fixed ahead on the nearest working streetlight and refused to look directly at her. The further I walked, the louder the sobbing seemed to become.

When I reached the cemetery gate, curiosity got the better of me.

I turned my head for one last look.

The candle was gone.

The woman was no longer kneeling.

She was standing.

Her arms were stretched out toward me.

I froze.

Not because I wanted to, but because I couldn't process what I was seeing.

I couldn't make out a face. Long black hair completely covered it. She wore what looked like a tattered dress that hung loosely from her body. Even now, years later, that's an image I can still see perfectly in my mind.

The moment I snapped back to reality, I picked up my pace even more. For some reason, I couldn't run. My body simply wouldn't do it. All I could manage was a fast, almost panicked walk.

As I moved farther away, the crying grew louder.

And louder.

And louder.

Then, suddenly, it changed.

The sobbing became laughter.

Not normal laughter, either. It sounded mocking, as though whatever it was had been toying with me and found the whole situation amusing.

By the time I reached the streetlight, the sound stopped.

Only then did I realize something that still bothers me to this day.

From the moment I entered the cemetery's vicinity until I reached that streetlight, there had been no other sounds.

No wind.

No insects.

No distant cars.

Nothing.

The only things I could hear were my own footsteps and that woman's cries.

When I finally made it home, I told my parents and my aunt everything that had happened. Without hesitation, they all said the same thing:

"You saw La Llorona."

I'm not here to argue whether that's what it really was. Maybe it was, maybe it wasn't.

All I know is that I'll never forget the sight of that woman standing among the graves, nor the sound of her cries turning into laughter.

Even now, every time I tell this story, I can still hear it as clearly as I did that night.
