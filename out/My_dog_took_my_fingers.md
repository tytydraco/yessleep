Our dog got out of the side gate on Monday morning. Daisy, four-year-old golden, friendly to the point of being useless. I put flyers on every pole between our house and the elementary school and my son cried at dinner two nights running. My wife is on nights at the hospital this month, so it was me and the kids and the flyers.

Wednesday I fell asleep on the couch with the TV going. The porch light was off.

I woke up to somebody beating on my front door. Not knocking. Beating on it, over and over, hard enough that I felt it in the couch, and screaming over the top of it, and crying so hard the words were coming apart.

I got to the door and put my hand on it and looked through the peephole, because the little window beside the door has a curtain and I didn't want to move it. It was the kids from the corner house, the girl who's about eight and her little brother, five maybe. The girl had snot down to her lip and was hitting my door with the flat of both hands, with her back to the driveway, and she never once looked behind her. The boy was behind her with his mouth open and nothing coming out of it that I could make into a word.

And behind them, sitting on my driveway under the streetlight, was Daisy.

I saw my dog. She was sitting on the driveway with her tongue out, wet, looking at the door. I figured she'd been out a couple of days and come home.

So I opened the door.

They came through the gap, both of them, and the girl was screaming not the dog, not the dog, not the dog, and I thought she'd been bitten. I had the door in one hand and I leaned out and said, "Daisy. Come."

She stood up. She came up the walk at a dog's pace, and I remember being relieved about that, that she could walk. I kept the door in my left hand and crouched to get her collar with my right. I put my hand into the fur at her neck.

There was nothing under the fur where her neck should have been. My hand went in and kept going, and something closed on my wrist.

The girl hit the door with her whole body. It slammed on my forearm and I screamed and pulled, and the fur slid up my arm, and my hand came out of it. Most of my hand. She locked the deadbolt before I was off the floor. Eight years old.

I didn't understand what I was looking at for a second or two, because there wasn't any blood yet. My ring finger and my little finger were gone at the knuckle. The ends were flat, like they'd been pinched off, and there were four marks around my wrist above them, parallel, not punctures, lines, like I'd been held by something with four fingers and no thumb. Then there was blood, a lot of it, and I was yelling at the girl to get a towel from the kitchen, and she did.

I called 911 from the floor. I told her the two kids from the house on the corner were at my door screaming and that something had taken two of my fingers, and I gave her their address. She said police and an ambulance were on the way and to keep the door locked.

I got everybody into the kitchen, checked the back door was locked, and got the towel around what was left of my hand and squeezed it in the other one and held it over my head like they tell you. The boy sat on the floor against the dishwasher with his knees up and didn't talk. He still hasn't, to me or to anyone. The girl told me what happened.

Daisy came to their back door after dinner. They let her in, because it was Daisy. She went into the kitchen where their mom was. Then the coat came off in the kitchen.

I asked her what she meant and she looked at me like I was the child.

That's when my own kids came down. Maddie is nine, Ben is six. Ben went straight to the little window beside the front door, because he'd heard the word Daisy, and pulled the curtain back. He looked out and he screamed. The same scream. The exact same one the girl had been making on my porch, out of my son.

Maddie looked, went white, and pulled her brother back by the shirt without saying anything.

I turned the porch light on and looked. I had my good hand on the door. The deadbolt was locked, I knew that, and I still couldn't make myself take my hand off it. Daisy was sitting on my mat. Wagging. Looking at the door.

"Guys," I said. "It's Daisy."

Four kids on one side of that sentence and me on the other.

I got angry. I was two fingers down and bleeding through a dish towel, my dog was on the porch, and two children were screaming at me that she wasn't and two more couldn't get a word out. I said to the girl, fine. Tell me what you see.

She told me in kid words. I'm going to give you her words because mine don't work.

The legs are too long. The head's not on right, it's slipping. There's a line down its front. Her eyes are shut. It's looking out of her mouth.

I looked again, with my hand on the lock. A wet dog, sitting on my porch. I looked for a long time. My dog.

She'd been checking the lock every couple of minutes since she'd locked it. She went and checked it again, and leaned to look out the little window with her hand still on the deadbolt.

Her face changed. It went slack, like relief. "It's Daisy," she said, in a different voice, a smaller one. Then she took her hand off, and looked at her hand, and looked at the window. "It's not."

She did it twice more while I watched. Hand on the lock: Daisy. Hand off: not. The third time she started crying again and I pulled her away from the door.

Then I did what she'd done. I took my hand off the door, and I stepped back from it, and I looked.

The girl had it right. The legs. The line down the front. Daisy's eyes were shut, and something was looking at me out of her mouth. I stood there until I couldn't, and then I put my hand back on the deadbolt because I had to know, and it was Daisy, wagging. I took my hand off. It wasn't.

That's the rule, as far as I can tell. If your hand is on the way in, you see the dog.

The police car came, and when it turned onto the street the driveway was empty. The officer came in, and the ambulance behind him, and they dressed my hand and I signed the form that says you refused transport. The girl told the officer about the dog and he wrote it down. He asked me where the dog was now and I said I didn't know.

A woman from the county came for the kids. The girl looked back at my front door the whole way to the car. After that it was blue light on the kitchen ceiling from three doors down, and the three of us with the blinds shut.

I called Sarah at the hospital and told her all of it. She said she was leaving now, in the voice she uses on people in shock.

I heard her car as it was getting light. She pulled all the way up to the garage, where I couldn't see her from the little window. I heard the engine stop. Then nothing.

I stood at the window with my hand on the door and waited for her to come around the corner of the house, and she didn't, and she didn't. I thought she was on the phone. I thought she was crying in the car, which she does sometimes after a bad shift, before she comes in. I'd started saying her name.

Then she came around the corner of the house and up the walk, in her scrubs, with her bag on her shoulder, and she looked up at the window and found my face and smiled, and I had the deadbolt turned and the door open before I'd thought about it, because it was my wife, and I'd been up all night, and she's a nurse.

I let go of the door to get hold of her.

It was wearing Sarah.

The scrubs were the same. The bag was on its shoulder. Her eyes were shut, and her mouth was open in a way hers doesn't go, and something was looking at me out of it from a long way back. It was standing on my mat with the door wide open and it did not come in. It stood there with my wife's bag on its shoulder and waited for me to let it.

I got the door shut. I don't remember doing it. Ben says I did it with my bad hand.

Her car is still in the driveway. I went to the ER in daylight with the kids in the back seat, and they called it a crush amputation and asked if it was the dog, and I said yes, the dog. They wanted to keep me and I didn't stay. The lines around my wrist have gone dark under the dressing in a way the doctor didn't like. I have two fingers and a thumb on that hand. It has the other two somewhere. The police have had me in twice about Sarah. I told them the truth and they wrote it down the way the first officer did. It's never there when they come by, and I don't know where it goes. The corner-house kids are with their grandmother in another town, and the boy still hasn't talked.

It's there every night, at the edge of the porch light, in her scrubs. Ben asks when Mom's coming in. Maddie doesn't ask. Last night I found her at the front door with her hand flat on it, looking out the little window, and she was smiling.

She's nine. She can reach the handle.
