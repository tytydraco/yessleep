  
I should say up front that I don't do this because I like it.  
I used to be a contortionist. Circus, then a hotel residency in Macau, then a fall from a hoop that I won't describe, because the part of me that remembers it is still in physical therapy. The debt stayed after the career left. This is the one job left where my body is worth money.  
A builder makes the compartment. I test it. I get in, pull the panel shut, and stay for as long as the client's paying for. If I can breathe, if the latch works from inside, if the seam holds up under a flashlight dragged across it, they get an invoice. If I can't, the builder gets a phone call. The builder I work with is an old man named Furuya. He puts a folded towel under where my knees will go and has never once said anything about it.  
The men who hire us call me Hako. It means box. I stopped correcting them.  
I've done forty-one of these. Under a kitchen floor. Inside a piano. Behind a water heater that wasn't hooked up to anything. I'd been in the wall of a man's bedroom for three hours once while he slept eight feet from my face. That was the most frightening job I'd ever done. I'd like that on the record.  
The house was in the hills outside Osaka. Half-basement, new drywall, a cavity behind a false furnace panel. Furuya had left the towel. I got in, exhaled, pulled the panel to, and started counting.  
Around minute four the lights came on in the room outside.  
The seam gave me a view about as wide as a pencil. Five men came in. Four of them I'd seen before. The fifth was the boss, Mr. Sone, who has never spoken to me directly. He's small and neat and wears reading glasses on a cord. Between them they had a man in a gray suit, walking on his own.  
Nobody was holding him.  
I want you to understand that nothing about this was loud. They stood him in the middle of the floor and laid out a blue tarp. One of them brought a folding table. Another one bowed to the man. The man bowed back. Then he apologized to them, for the trouble.  
I thought it was an accountant situation. I've seen those. I know what that room smells like.  
Mr. Sone rolled up his left sleeve.  
On his forearm was a tattoo of a hag. Cartoon green, hooked nose, one wart, a little pointed hat. It looked like something off a 1990s lunchbox. The fingernails were drawn long, longer than the hand, curling past the wrist almost to his elbow, and sharp as paring knives.  
I have the same hag on my ribs. I got it at nineteen, from a guy who worked out of a laundromat in Busan, because I thought it was funny. I've never told anyone here. I'm telling you.  
Mr. Sone flexed his fingers, and the nails on his arm moved first.  
I've tried a few ways to describe what happened next and none of them are right. The best I've got is a diagram. You know the one that comes in a furniture box, where the bookshelf is drawn with every piece pulled apart and floating, spaced out evenly along the lines it would slide in on? That, but the man.  
He didn't scream. He did look. Each piece of him lifted off the one underneath it in order, skin, then what was under the skin, then what was under that, and hung there with a hand's width between each layer, perfectly still. His eyes were in the middle of the arrangement, and they were moving, and they were still the same eyes. He said "thank you." I heard it. I don't know where the air came from.  
The men laid out the tarp under him as the pieces came down. Rows. Nothing touched. One of them wrote something on a clipboard.  
I counted. I always count. It took eleven minutes, and nobody hurried.  
At minute nine my ribs started to itch, the left side, where the hag is. It wasn't pain. It felt like someone drumming their fingers against my skin from the inside, slowly, in time with Mr. Sone's hand.

When it was done, Mr. Sone looked around the room. He looked at the walls. The new drywall. The false furnace. He looked at the panel, right at the seam, where the pencil-width of light was coming through.  
"Nice work," he said, to nobody.  
Then they left.  
I stayed another forty minutes, because the contract said to stay until someone opened the panel. Nobody did. Eventually I let myself out. The tarp was gone. The floor was clean. The smell was bleach and something like hot pennies.  
Furuya paid me double the next morning. He wouldn't look at me. In the envelope was a measurement written on a slip of paper, not for a build I'd done before.  
The next one is deeper in the room. Better view.  
I took the job. I'm not going to explain that, because you already know why. The money is good and the debt is real, and I've started to think the first one wasn't an accident. I think the compartment was never to hide in. I think it was to watch from.  
I'm typing this from one now. Phone on airplane mode, almost no signal, brightness at the bottom so the screen doesn't leak through the seam. I've been in here for two hours and nine minutes. The towel is under my knees.  
My ribs are itching again.
