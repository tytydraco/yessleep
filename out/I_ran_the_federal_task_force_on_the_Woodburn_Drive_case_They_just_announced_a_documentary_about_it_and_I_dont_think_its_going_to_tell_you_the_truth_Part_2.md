A few of you sent me DM's asking whether I'm allowed to be doing this, and the answer is no. And one of you wrote something about the white dress that made me put my phone face down on the kitchen counter and go for a very long run. You're closer than you know.

Here's the rest of it, or most of it. I'm going to tell it the same way I told the first part, in the order I learned it, and I'm going to try not to get ahead of myself. That's harder this time, because this is the part where I start getting things wrong in a way I can't explain away as a judgment call.



That storm I saw coming in over the gas station hit the town around ten that night, and it didn't let up for most of the next two days.

By the time my partner and I got back to the Khoury house, Anna was on the floor of Jenny's bedroom in a full-blown panic attack. I've seen a lot of panic attacks. This was a bad one. She was on her side beside the bed, clawing at the front of her shirt like she was trying to peel the feeling off her chest, breathing in on top of breaths she hadn't let out yet. She couldn't seem to blink. Jenny was on her knees holding her, one hand cupped around the back of Anna's head, the other rubbing slow circles on her back, saying nothing at all, which was exactly the right thing to say.

Blake and Karen stood in the doorway with their heads bowed, praying. At one point Karen peeked up and caught me not praying with them. She looked hurt, and then she looked embarrassed for looking hurt, and then she closed her eyes again.

I'll tell you what I believed in that moment, standing in that bedroom with rain coming sideways against the window. I believed Ryan Millington was alive, and close, and that he'd just sent us a valentine. And I believed it was my fault, because I'd put the one person he wanted most in a house across the street from his own and then put that house on the evening news.

I was half right.



Later that night I stood in the Khoury kitchen with a file under my arm and told Blake that I wanted to move his family to a safe house, and Anna separately, until we got the situation stabilized.

I've had a lot of people react badly to the words "safe house." Blake's reaction was a new one for me. He smirked at me, and then he asked me who I thought I was, coming into his home with my buzzwords and my tactical jacket. He looked me up and down in a way I'm very familiar with and told me I didn't exactly scream authority, that I screamed something else entirely. Then he poured himself two fingers of whiskey and drank it standing up and asked me whether I'd been fast-tracked through Quantico to fill a quota, and how many men who'd beaten me in the mile I'd been promoted over.

I didn't answer him, because there wasn't anything worth answering.

Then he walked over to a cabinet beside the refrigerator, unlocked it with a little key on his keychain, and showed me a pump-action shotgun. He told me he was a card-carrying member of the NRA and that this was all the protection his family needed.

I said something to him then that I've thought about a lot since. I said, "You think he's going to knock? Give you a second to rack that thing and draw down like it's a fair fight?" I told him that the man we were looking for didn't just kill people, he tortured every single one of them, and that if he saw Blake first it wouldn't matter how loud his gun was. I meant every word. I was describing Ryan Millington, and I was describing him accurately, and I had no idea that I was also giving a very good description of somebody who was asleep upstairs.

Blake didn't move his family that night. I doubled the cars out front and told him to get some rest.

On my way out I looked back through the kitchen window. He was standing at the counter with his money clip out, looking at a business card. I couldn't see whose. I found out later. It was the local news anchor's.



Before I left the house that night, I went up and sat with Anna for a few minutes.

She was on the edge of Jenny's bed by then, breathing evenly again, holding the bunny in both hands. Jenny was beside her. I took the chair by the closet, not too close, and told her I wasn't there to do a formal interview. I asked her if she felt safe in that house.

She thought about it for a long time and then said, "I feel watched."

I asked her whether she trusted us to handle it if someone dangerous was still out there. She looked at me then, really looked at me, and asked whether I'd ever stopped someone before they did the bad things.

I said we try to.

She repeated the word "try" back to me, flatly, the way you'd repeat a price you thought was too high. And then she asked, "Do you wait? Or do you fix it yourself?"

I didn't have an answer for her, and before I could come up with one my partner knocked on the door to tell me Blake wanted us out of his house and was starting to make it a thing. When I stood up to leave, Anna had already looked away. She was stroking the bunny's ear between her thumb and finger, staring at the floor, very calm.

I wrote "do you wait or fix it yourself" in my notebook in the car and underlined it twice. I thought it was a question about whether she could trust us. I thought it was a trauma survivor asking whether she was safe.



I didn't hear about this next part until much later, from Jenny, so I'll keep it short. After I left that night, Jenny told Anna a secret. She told her that before she came home from college she'd been pregnant, by a professor who'd made her his teaching assistant and then stopped listening to her when she said no, and that she'd ended the pregnancy alone, and that no one in her family knew. Jenny told me Anna held her afterward, and that it was the first hug from Anna that didn't feel like Anna was learning how to hug while she did it. She said Anna whispered something into her hair.

"Don't worry. I'll save you."

The next morning, Anna woke up in Jenny's bed and saw a shape standing in the gap of the bedroom door, watching them sleep. It turned out to be Blake. He said he'd thought he heard something. Jenny told me Anna stared at him with a look she'd never seen on anyone, like she wasn't in that house anymore, and said, "You looked like him."

After Blake left, Jenny told Anna more. She told her she was pretty sure her father was cheating on her mother. She'd seen him around town with women who weren't family friends or coworkers. She'd found a long blonde hair in the bathroom sink once, and her mother's hair is black. She thought he paid for it sometimes. She said the worst men are the ones who parade themselves as good, family men and church men and yard-mowing men, and that behind closed doors they rot things from the inside out.

Jenny told me Anna lay there staring at the ceiling and listening to every word, and then asked, very calmly, like she was asking about the weather, "If someone like that keeps hurting people, what do you usually do about it?"

Jenny didn't answer her.

I'm putting this here, in the order it happened, and not in the order I learned it, because I think you need to see it where it sits. I didn't get to.



Over the next few days, the town came apart.

National news vans showed up and parked in a line along the town square with their satellite dishes up. The hardware store owner on Main Street started pulling his gate down before sunrise and locking his door three times. Two marked cruisers and an unmarked SUV started idling at the elementary school drop-off every morning, and the teachers held the doors open with tight smiles and watched the sidewalk like it might stand up. Nobody walked their dog after dark. Nobody let their kids ride bikes. That town had always been the kind of place where nobody locked their doors, and I'd said as much in a briefing, sourly, about why we didn't have a single doorbell camera on that whole street. Within a week you couldn't buy a doorbell camera within fifty miles.

And the podcasts started.

I'm not going to name the one I'm thinking of because I don't want to send it a single download. It's hosted by a man who holds the microphone too close to his mouth and says "correct" when he agrees with himself. One of his first guests was the cameraman who worked for that local news anchor, a big guy I'll just call the Cameraman, who told the host with total authority that the Feds had no leads. Then he said he'd heard on another podcast that most serial killers are never caught, that they're just out there, surviving, and we never even know. The host nodded and said "hunting," like he was finishing a prayer.

My partner listened to that episode in my car, with an AirPod in, eating a tamale out of the foil. When I got back in with my dry cleaning, he pulled the AirPod out, looked out the window for a while, and then told me he'd had a thought. He said that if everyone already thought we were flailing, maybe we should lean into it. Use the silence and make it look like we were lost, so Ryan would feel safe enough to move.

He called it feigned incompetence, or tactical vulnerability. He was very pleased with himself.

I told him it was good, and it was good. It was also, I figured out about a minute later, almost word for word something the host had said in the episode he'd been listening to. And then he said, "How many of these guys are out there that we never even hear about?" and I recognized that one too. I didn't say anything. I let him have it.

I'm telling you this because about a week later I was standing in front of a wall of evidence about to put a twenty-year-old woman in the woods at night as bait, and I said the words "feigned incompetence" to my partner like it was a plan. That's where that came from. A podcast host with a nicotine pouch sponsor. I want that on the record too.



I was watching the Khoury house more closely than Blake knew.

There was a security camera on the house by then, ours, and for two nights in a row it caught Blake stepping out his front door at 3:14 in the morning in a t-shirt and jeans, lighting a cigarette, and just standing there in the yard staring at the tree line across the street until it burned down. Both mornings he told my agents he'd slept straight through.

I watched that clip over and over at a folding table at two in the morning while my partner told me that if I dug deep enough into those files I'd find a motive for everyone in town. He said people lie to their spouses about smoking all the time, and that didn't make Blake an accomplice to anything. I told him I knew that. I told him what bothered me was the way Anna had talked about being watched. She hadn't said watched by Ryan, she'd just said watched. And that I didn't like the way she looked at Blake.

He told me that wasn't a profile, it was a hunch.

He was right that it was a hunch. It was the best hunch I had on that entire case, and I pointed it at the wrong person.



The next night, the Khourys had a fight at dinner.

Karen told me about it afterward, much later, in a voice so flat it sounded recorded. She said she'd been talking about azaleas, trying to fill the silence, and Blake had announced that they weren't going to any safe house, and Jenny had started to cry and then told them everything: the professor, the pregnancy, the clinic she drove herself to.

Anna was at the table. Karen said Anna asked what an abortion was, and Jenny told her it was when someone decides not to have the baby, and Blake stood up very slowly and said that no, it was when someone kills a baby before it has a chance. He told his daughter she'd thrown her child away like trash. He told his wife not to pretend she was the victim. Then he grabbed his coat and told them all that if they wanted to run, they could go ahead and see how long they lasted out there, and he slammed the screen door on his way into the yard.

That's when he found my sergeant.

He was twenty-nine years old, and I'll call him Tiller. He'd been on overnight detail outside that house for three shifts, and on his first one, a veteran officer had told him to keep his car running, stay off his phone, and keep his music low, because something about this one was strange. Tiller had said "Yes, sir" to everything. He had a buzz cut and a uniform so starched it still had the creases from the package.

Blake found him in his cruiser at the curb with the driver's door hanging open and the dome light on. His throat had been cut from one side to the other. He'd never touched his radio.

The anchor's news van was coming up the street at that exact moment, for reasons that were not about news, and she got Blake on her phone, sprinting back into his house with his face the color of paper, shouting at his wife to get inside. That clip ran nationally the next morning. It's in the teaser. It's the shot right before the one of me.

I got the call at my folding table. I'd spent that whole night going frame by frame through body cam footage from a patrol officer who'd stopped a hooded man near a park on the edge of town for curfew. The man hadn't stopped walking. For about six frames he turned his head, and I ran the face through every piece of software we had, and enhanced it, and froze it, and set it side by side against Ryan's license photo. There was a crescent-shaped scar under the man's left eye. Ryan didn't have a scar. I closed the window and wrote "NOT RM" on a clipboard and sat there feeling stupid and tired. A minute later my phone rang and told me a twenty-nine-year-old was dead in front of a house I'd promised to protect.

The Khourys moved into the safe house that night. Blake didn't argue anymore.



I want to stay with the safe house for a minute, because the documentary is going to make it look like a fortress, and it wasn't. It was a rented split-level at the end of a gravel road with beige carpet and a ceiling fan in every room and a clock in the kitchen that ticked so loudly you could hear it from the bedrooms. The woods came right up to the back of the property.

That first night I sat with Blake and Karen in the kitchen. Blake had stopped calling me names. He asked me, very quietly, what kind of man watches out a window while someone else decides how to keep his family alive. I told him it was the kind of man whose family stays alive. He asked me if I thought the place would hold. I told him long enough. He told me I seemed calm, and I told him I wasn't calm, I'd just had more practice hiding it.

Then I told him something I've regretted saying ever since, because of how right it was and how little good it did. I told him that what he'd been doing to his family wasn't protection, it was control and shame, and that he still had time to be the man they needed if he stopped trying to prove he'd been right.

He didn't say anything. He looked out the window into the woods.

Then I went down the hall to the girls' room.



Jenny was lying in the bed by the window with her back to the room, and Anna was sitting cross-legged on the other bed, staring at nothing. I asked if we could talk. I told Anna she didn't have to protect him anymore.

She said, "I'm not protecting him. I'm protecting me." And then she told me that Ryan Millington was her father.

I didn't react, because I've been trained not to react, and I'm very good at it. I asked her if she was sure. She said he'd told her so her whole life. I asked about her mother, whether she remembered anything, a name, anything at all. She said she had memories but sometimes didn't trust them. Then she said, "Just her voice," and I put my hand on her blanket and left it there.

I told her we'd find him and end this and make sure no part of him followed her past it. I believed that sentence while I was saying it, which I think is the worst thing I can tell you about myself.

I asked her if she'd told anyone else. She looked over at Jenny's back, rising and falling slowly, and said, "Yes. All of them. But they're all gone now."

I didn't know Jenny was awake. Jenny told me later she'd been lying there with her eyes wide open the entire time.



Two days after that, the anchor ran a story on the eleven o'clock news, from the curb in front of the Khourys' empty house. She said there were new questions about the identity of the girl found in the basement. She said some reports suggested Anna had been more than a victim, possibly a witness and a forced accomplice to more than a dozen murders, and that sources hadn't confirmed her identity or her relationship to Ryan Millington. She ended by asking, and I'm quoting her, "Who is Banana Anna?"

Banana, on the eleven o'clock news, a word I had never once put in a report. My partner found out from that broadcast that Anna was Ryan's daughter, because I hadn't told him, and he was quiet the whole next morning in the car until I asked him why. He said we were partners. I told him we weren't partners, that it would be a break of protocol to call us that, and he said he knew, but that it felt like I was flying solo, like it was me against Ryan and everybody else. He said he wasn't half as good as me and no one outworked me, but that I had to let them help.

I told him I was sorry. I told him I wasn't used to being wrong.

I'd like to point out that at that moment I still didn't know I was wrong about anything. I was apologizing for not calling him.

That same evening I picked up my dry cleaning, a day late, from the two sisters with the duct-taped calculator. When I pulled out my wallet a folded piece of paper fell on the counter. It was a photocopy of the Jeep picture, Jenny and her friends with Ryan behind them in his driveway, and the woman in the white dress. For the hell of it I slid it across the counter and asked if she knew him.

She said, "Yes, of course. Ryan."

He'd been coming in for years, she said, all the time. A very nice man.

I showed her my badge and asked if he still had anything on the rack.

I'm going to stop there on the dry cleaner, because what was on that rack belongs to the end of this and I promised myself I'd tell it in order.



The morning after that, on the highway, my partner spotted Blake's SUV going the opposite direction from the safe house, fast. We put a car on him. When we got to the safe house, Karen told us Blake had insisted on going in to his church office to "get his head on straight."

Jenny was alone in the girls' room, and Anna was gone.

Jenny told me she'd woken up and Anna just wasn't there. I asked her if that was true. She crossed her arms and said it again, the same words in the same order. I've thought about that a lot. I'm still not sure what the answer is.

The agent I'd put on Blake radioed in twenty minutes later. Blake hadn't gone to his church. He'd gone to a motel on the edge of town, and the local news anchor had gone in after him, and about forty-five minutes after that, the anchor had come out, fixing her hair. And then Anna had walked out from behind the building, crossed the parking lot, and gone into Blake's room and closed the door.

She'd ridden there under a blanket in the back of his SUV. He never knew she was in there.

I got to that motel door as fast as I've ever driven anywhere, and I knocked hard and shouted my name, and it took Blake a long time to open it. His shirt was buttoned wrong. The room smelled like perfume that wasn't Karen's, and Anna was sitting on the edge of the unmade bed with her hands folded in her lap, entirely composed, like a girl waiting outside a principal's office. She didn't say anything. Blake said she'd just shown up. Both of those things were true.

I put her in my car alone and shut the doors.



I'll be honest about what I said to her, because I'm not proud of it. I told her I was done with the bullshit. I told her she was too smart to have spent her whole life in a cage and that she should stop pretending. I told her that if she lied to me, I'd put her away myself. I told her I was not the person to lie to.

Her shoulders started to shake. She turned to me with her eyes full, and she said, "I killed Ryan."

She said Ryan had told her Jenny was next, and she'd gotten to him first. She'd hurt him badly. She'd been sure he was dead. And then when the police came, he wasn't there. He was just gone.

Then she said, "There's more than one, Sherry. There always was."

I asked her if she meant Ryan had been part of something bigger, and she nodded. She said she didn't think it would ever stop until it got to her. And then she looked up at me with something building in her face that I read as courage, and she said maybe we should let him come and find her. She said it was coming for her one way or another, and this way we could stop it before it did something bad.

I told her it wasn't an option.

I'd like you to notice that this was the second time in this story that I said something wasn't an option and then made it one.



I didn't go back to my hotel that night. I stayed in the war room and built a timeline.

I mapped every victim we had, best estimates, first known to last recovered, eighteen to twenty-three years of it depending on how you counted. It had a rhythm to it, almost seasonal. And then there were gaps, three years in one place and five in another, long silences where nobody went into those lockers at all. There were no prison records to explain them. Underneath it I ran a second line, a single line, with a single label: ANNA, BORN APPROX. 2005, HELD SINCE BIRTH. The victims on the first line changed. He had a type at the start, and then he didn't: an older woman, a blonde with a pixie cut, the four men at the very end. Nothing stayed consistent except the line underneath. Anna was the one constant under everything else.

When my partner came in that morning with a coffee and found me still there, I told him that. I told him Ryan had raised her in the shadow of murder and trained her to survive it. Then I pinned Tiller's academy photo at the end of the timeline, smiling, and told him Anna had said there was more than one.

Then I told him she'd offered herself as bait, and that I hadn't said yes, and that I hadn't said no either.

He asked me if I'd seriously entertained putting a survivor back in front of her abuser. I said only if she insisted and only if we could protect her every second. And then I said "feigned incompetence," like it was a strategy, like I hadn't heard it on a podcast.

He asked me what the play was, and I told him.



The safe house was quiet that night in the way places are quiet when everybody in them is listening. I had more agents on that perimeter than I've ever had on anything, in the yard, on the gravel road, and out in the tree line in camo with suppressed rifles and thermal. Jenny went in to sit with Anna before it started. Jenny told me later she told Anna she didn't have to do it, and Anna said she had to. Jenny made her promise to be careful. Anna said, "I promise."

A promise is a promise is a promise.

At about one in the morning, Anna slid her bedroom window open, climbed out with the bunny, and walked into the woods.

I watched it from a surveillance van on a night-vision drone feed with my partner leaning over my shoulder. She looked terrified, and she moved through the trees like every step was a gunshot, flinching at twigs, spinning around at nothing, clutching the bunny against her chest. My partner said she looked scared. I told him she needed to, that if he was watching he had to believe this was real.

My vape blinked three times and died. I remember being irrationally furious about that.

At 1:22 in the morning a man stepped out of the dark about fifteen feet in front of her. On the drone feed he was a pale smear, and then the camera pushed in and I saw the face: white, with a weeping mouth frozen open. It was the tragedy mask.

Our audio caught him saying two words to her, softly. "Found you."

Then there were red dots all over him, half on his forehead and half on his chest, and he looked down at himself like he didn't understand what he was looking at. And then the team in the trees fired, all together, and he went down in the leaves and didn't move.

I was out of the van and on the radio asking for status before he hit the ground, and nobody answered. I asked again, louder, and then the voice came back.

"Target neutralized. Anna's secure."



I ran into those woods. I found her on her knees beside him, sobbing so hard her whole body was shaking, and I got down in the leaves and wrapped my arms around her and held her and told her he was gone. I told her he wasn't going to hurt her anymore. I told her it was over.

Over my shoulder she could see him. One of our rounds had gone straight through the nose of the mask, and there was still smoke coming off the hole. His eyes were open behind it. Somebody put a blanket over his face, and Anna closed her eyes and said, "It's over."

I told her we got him. I told her he wasn't coming back.

I meant it, and I need you to understand that I knelt in those leaves with my arms around her and meant every word, and that she let me.

Behind the van, the news anchor's cameraman was standing in the road with a controller in his hands, landing a drone of his own at his feet. They'd gotten every second of it. That footage is in the teaser too. And the anchor told him she was going in for a closer look and walked into the woods by herself, and he called after her and she told him to shut up, she'd be right back.

We had a DNA rush on the man in the mask, and the lab told us thirty-six hours. Everybody told the Khourys they'd be home Monday.

Anna didn't wait thirty-six hours.
