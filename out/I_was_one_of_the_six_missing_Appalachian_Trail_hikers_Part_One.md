***Part One***

If the title caught your attention in any way, then to some extent, you know what happened already. You’ve surely seen the reports, the press conferences, or the interviews with the other surviving members. I’ve done my best to stay out of the limelight for the most part, but you’ll come to find out who I am soon enough.

I don’t know why I feel that now is the time to share my story. The news has long since broken, the investigations are underway, and every bit of commentary and speculation from the bottom-feeding reporters and podcasters has been squeezed from the dry sponge of my experience. Perhaps I want the truth revealed, to tell the first authentic and desensationalized side of the experience. Or perhaps I want to rid myself of the memory entirely, my soul cleansed of those torturous months that have haunted me ever since. Either way, I know that I will have no hope of moving on until I make sure my perspective is known. 

Or maybe it’s guilt, I suppose.

I’ll begin with a fact about myself.

Every night, the second my head hits the pillow, I dream of burning. It’s a complete sensory image of my entire body engulfed in flames, flesh scalding and melting. It’s so vivid, I can taste the chalky ash and feel the acrid smoke grasping my lungs. I can feel the flames dig their talons under my skin, tearing and shredding me to pieces. I’m suspended in midair, floating in some dark, abstract expanse that is almost entirely obscured by the searing heat in my eyes and the tears bursting from their ducts. The pain is as real and agonizing as I can possibly imagine.

The nightmares aren’t always short, either. The dreams can extend my torture beyond what I can fathom just as they can quicken it to a fraction of a second. Every time I drift off to sleep, I’m unsure of whether or not I’m about to enter an eternity of endless immolation, inflicted on me by my own mind. 

The night before I left for the trail, the dreams were particularly bad. After an unquantifiable amount of time spent on fire, I was saved. The sound of my alarm echoed in the distance of my nightmare, like a freight train barreling towards my charred corpse, clinging on to life. The impact finally ended my suffering as the sound reached me, and I awoke. 

I sat ramrod straight up in my bed and wiped the beads of sweat dripping down my face as I fumbled for my phone. I could feel the cold drops running down my back. My brain, still acclimating, imagined them extinguishing the fire from my dreams. I wheezed out ragged, dehydrated breaths. Finally, my mind slowly returned to reality after my seemingly endless burning.

This is my bed, I’m in my room; in my apartment. I’m safe. 

I slowly kicked my feet around off the side of the bed, letting the Earth’s gravity and the soft feel of my carpet coax my inner ear away from the lingering sense of floating. The blue glow of the early stages of sunrise peaked through my blinds,and my eyes finally adjusted to the dark room. I patted my sweat-soaked pillow, wiping my hands on the bed in disgust.

I cautiously limped into my bathroom, every step precise as I nursed the waves of nausea that threatened with the slightest movement. I splashed some cold water on my face, another tactic to stave off the trauma of last night’s torment. I flicked on the light behind me and stared into the mirror. My skin was pasty white and my sweat-soaked hair looked even thinner with the moisture. And are those bags under my eyes superficial, or have my eyes sunken in from my years of restless sleep?

I looked horrible. 

My dry eyes were finally baptized by my saline-soaked contacts, and I downed a glass of tap water, figuring my stomach would now let me. I instinctively reached for my toothbrush, only to realize I had already packed it in my bag the night before. Oh well. I just won’t stand too close to anybody. 

After washing up, I rushed to my kitchen to pour myself a quick bowl of cereal. I checked my watch as I rummaged through my cabinets, looking for a box that hadn’t expired. The closest I could find had a date of December 16th, 2023. Six months ago. It would have to do. I was already running late and my mother would be here any minute to pick me up. I poured the milk and sat down. After forcing down a few stale, bitter bites, my phone rang. My mother. 

“Hello, mother,” I said, praying she was almost here. I could hear the whooshing of her driving through the phone.

“One moment, I’m putting you on speakerphone,” she replied. After a minute of the violently loud sounds of her fumbling with the phone, she spoke again. “Okay, can you hear me?”

“Yep. Are you close?” She was silent for a few seconds. 

“Not particularly. I just left my house,” she finally says. I winced in frustration. 

“I thought we said you’d be here at 8,” I said, trying to hide my annoyance.

“No, *you* told me to leave at 8, Elijah,” she replied in that familiar authoritarian tone that sent a chill down my spine. “And so I did. My GPS says I’ll be there at around quarter of ten.”

I was sure that she was wrong, but it didn’t matter. I sighed and pinched the top of my nose, rubbing it as I felt the murmurs of a headache. 

“No worries. I’ll see you when you get here.”

“Are you all packed and ready to go?” she asked. I glanced back at my twenty pound hiking bag leaning up against the wall.

“Uh, yeah. I think so. My apartment kinda looks like that model unit now. The one they have downstairs,” I hazarded with a chuckle. I don’t think she got the joke. 

“Just to be sure, why don’t you double check that you have everything. I don’t want you losing a leg or an arm just because you forgot your first aid kit. Lord knows my conscience can’t handle that.” 

“I will, don’t worry.” I replied, “Call me when you’re here.”

“Yes, yes. Just be ready.” She hung up before I could answer.

After dumping the rest of my cereal in the trash, I glanced in my bedroom to do a quick visual sweep. I took one last look at my bed, already mourning the comforts of it. Despite my tumultuous relationship with sleep, waking up in my own space was one of its few saving graces. And now, I would have to go without it for half a year. I took a solemn moment of reverence, then shut the door.

My mother had me when she was in her mid-forties, something that she described as nothing short of a miracle. She and my father struggled to conceive ever since their marriage, and it was only after he walked out on her one day that she realized she was finally going to have a child. She told me that instead of accepting the hardships that came with being a single mother, she chose to adopt the mindset that God had rewarded her with a son in the face of the trials of her marriage to my father. To her credit, she never faltered. Despite being in poverty for my early years, she always made sure I was fed, clean and had a warm place to sleep every night. She never quite elaborated on what was so hard about her life before my father left however, despite my many attempts to get it out of her.

“Your father had a lifestyle that I was roped into tolerating,” she would always say, “and it was only after I found Jesus that I realized how much of a prisoner I was to it.” She left the rest up to interpretation. 

Sitting with her in the car that day, I realized that she had shrunken even more than the last time I saw her. She hunched over the steering wheel, her head barely clearing it. Her worn, wrinkly hands gripped it tightly as she drove a consistent five miles under the speed limit. She wore a plain beige dress and a white bandana over her hair, a look not uncommon for her. I used to tell my friends that she dressed and talked like a pilgrim, like she had walked straight off the set of a living history museum. 

As I watched dozens of cars whoosh by on my left, the thought occurred to me to pull out my phone. I started dialing a number as my mother glanced over.

“Who are you calling?” She asked.

“Ilanna,” I replied, “should probably tell her and the group that I’m running late.”

“Oh, there’s no need, I already talked to her.” I looked up at her from my phone as my fingers hovered over the call button.

“You did?” I asked. Why was she talking to Ilanna again?

“Yes, right after our phone call this morning. It seemed the polite thing to do. She’s very excited to see you,” she said, the corners of her mouth ever-so-slightly curving upwards; the most she’d ever let herself smile. 

“Oh,” was all I could say, unsatisfied with her answer. I put my phone away. The car was silent for a few minutes.

“We both think this will be good for you,” she remarked, “getting out in nature, clearing your head. Experiencing His creation in its purest form.”

“Yeah, no… definitely.” She glanced over at me again.

“You look sickly, Elijah. Gaunt. Are you still having those nightmares?” she asked, ever-so delicate with her words. She felt my cheek with the back of her hand.

“Uh… no,” I lied, “Just couldn’t sleep last night. A bit of anxiety for the trip, maybe?”

“Ah, of course,” she replied. “Well that’s natural. This is a very big thing you’re doing. But you’re in good hands with Ilanna. I’ve heard she’s become quite the experienced hiker.”

“Yeah, so she says,” I said. My mother nodded. I hadn’t seen Ilanna in years, and had only started frequently calling her as we made our plans for the trail. “I guess she’s been going with that group a lot. Kinda makes me feel like the make-a-wish kid they’re gonna be dragging along with them.”

“Elijah, that’s a horrible thing to say. Ilanna and the rest of them are happy to have you. She’s very sympathetic to your troubles,” she replied. Troubles… I hated when she used that word.

“I’m sure they are. It’s just that um…” My voice trailed off. Do I say it? “You know that I haven’t seen her since Nico died.”

I watched my mother physically tense up. It was rare that her domineering and commanding demeanor slipped. I could almost see the calm yet stern mask that she wore melt off of her face. I analyzed the micro-movements in her jaw muscles as she dug for the right words to say. 

After a few sighs and quiet “ums”, she blurted out, “Yes, well… it will be good for you two to catch up.” And that was that.

We didn’t say much for the rest of the car ride.

I certainly don’t remember it, but my mother says that I’ve known Ilanna since I was a newborn. She had grown up in the same area as Ilanna’s parents, becoming quite close with them after my father skipped town. They were always bringing my mother food and supplies when she wasn't financially on her feet. She would always tell me that the Antonious helped to raise me just as much as she did. Ilanna and I were only born about forty-eight hours apart, and this serendipity that my mother called a “cosmic coincidence that only the Lord could create” led her to always assume that we would become inseparable. Whether it was out of genuine connection or the expectation of one, I couldn’t tell, but I did get to know Ilanna pretty well. We remained friends all the way through every year of North Star Christian School, eventually joined by her kid brother, Nico. We’d spend hours being dragged into the woods by Ilanna behind her house, searching for cryptids, dead bodies, and weird bugs or plants. There had been a couple of times where we’d lost track of both time and our bearings, which always ended in my mother screaming into the woods as the sun set, thinking we had been kidnapped. Her panicked cries would always reveal to us that we weren’t ever more than five hundred feet from the house. Upon our return, I’d be dragged to the car and given a raucous earful that only she could muster. 

Given how we spent our summers, I suppose what we’d major in at college was a foregone conclusion. We both attended the University of Maine, and we’d spend hours in the Raymond H. Fogler library together, studying for our ENVIS exams, fueled by the local pizza joint down the street that had the flat soda and always made their crusts too thin. We’d occasionally go to the campus Christian fellowship club, keeping our promises to our parents. We’d host movie nights in our dorm suites with a group of friends, and Ilanna would sometimes tutor all of us on our math classes. (She always had a good head for numbers.) On the weekends, we’d roadtrip home to have dinner with her parents, Nico and my mother. We’d talk about our studies and catch up with how her brother was doing in middle school. 

During the last year of college, after Nico passed, we drifted. I moved further up north to Ashland, while Ilanna did the opposite, getting a cushy apartment and an even cushier job in Boston. Our environmental science degrees did a lot more for her than me. She ended up as some sort of soil-testing consultant, making what was surely at least six-figures. Meanwhile, I bounced around from car dealership to car dealership as a basic mechanic, with some less-than-reputable Jiffy Lubes in between that would make Erin Brockovich prematurely roll over in her grave. 

I couldn’t tell if the idea to hike the entirety of the Appalachian Trail was first floated by mother or Ilanna, but it certainly wasn’t me. Ilanna first mentioned it last year when she called me on my twenty sixth birthday. She said that she and a group of her good friends from work were already planning it, and that after talking with my mother, she wanted me to join them. 

My initial answer was a resounding no. I was a self-proclaimed homebody, and I was nursing a healthy dose of depression anxiety that was only worsened by my daily nightmares. Despite the way I spent my summers as a kid, I was never the biggest outdoorsman either. I did enjoy a good camping trip or hike every once and a while, but I wasn’t the “roughing it with nothing but a pocket-knife and your wits” type that Ilanna was. Plus, hiking the A.T. didn’t exactly seem like a natural progression from the occasional overnight at a campground. But after some rigorous convincing from the two and the assurance that it would be a gratifying experience, I eventually caved. Save for hypnosis and ketamine, a change of scenery is one of the few things I haven’t tried to cure my endless nightmares, and ultimately, that was what convinced me. 

After dozens of phone calls with Ilanna, extensive research on the routes, what gear to pack and some practice hikes, it seemed surreal to finally be pulling into the lot for the Hunt Trail at the base of Mt. Katahdin. The dry air seeped in through the cracked windows and my mother’s squeaky Sedan kicked up clouds of dirt as it rolled in. I peered out my side, eventually spotting Ilanna and four other people: three men and a woman. They had their packs open, and were pulling various items out, passing them about and talking in what I assumed to be some sort of verbal checklist. Ilanna, sporting a modest tanktop and a baseball cap, spotted my mother’s car and waved. After my mother parked and shut off the car, I braced myself to meet the woman I’ve known my whole life yet who I felt like such a stranger to. My mother opened her door and got out, waving back at the group.

“They’re waiting, Elijah. They’ve been here for hours already,” she said scoldingly. I wondered if the irony of that statement was lost on her. I sighed and opened the door. 

The second I stepped into view, I could immediately see Ilanna’s eyes light up. She tapped the other woman’s shoulder and pointed to me, and her friend waved. As my sneakers sent up clouds of dust with each step, I felt like I was walking straight into a tornado of social interaction, ready to rip me apart with its awkwardness.

“Well that confirms it! It was actually you I was talking to these past months,” Ilanna said in her bubbly, high-pitched voice as she walked towards me. She must’ve seen my confusion on my face as she added, “The Eli I knew would never agree to this. Wasn’t sure if you’d been kidnapped by aliens and replaced with a robot mimicking your voice.”

Before I could respond, she threw her arms around me and gave me the tightest and friendliest hug I’d experienced in my entire life. I chuckled. “Nope. Not a robot. At least I don’t think so,” I replied. 

Ilanna let go and stared up at me. At six foot one, I towered above her, but the heavy, suffocating weight of talking to her for the first time trumped any physical advantage. After she sized me up, I could see that she finally noticed my ghastly appearance. Her tan Greek complexion twisted and contorted as I watched her try to hide her horrified face in futility. I must’ve looked like I was ripped straight out of a zombie flick. It was right then and there, after seeing her face like that, that I wondered again if this wasn’t a mistake. Even if she didn’t know it, I don’t think she was ready to see me, and I certainly wasn’t ready to see her. She patted me on the arm.

“It’s good to see you.” She said those words like she never thought she’d get to say them to me.

I managed a smile through my growing regret. “Likewise,” I said, scratching my head. I saw Ilanna’s gaze shift to someone over my shoulder. 

“Ilanna, my dear,” my mother said, craning over my body like a crow begging for seeds, “How are you?” She stepped out from behind me to give her a quick side hug. 

“I’ve been great!” Ilanna said, “It’s been a whirlwind with all of this prep, but I’m good. Tired good.”

“Oh, I don’t doubt it,” my mother replied, her eyes gleaming, “And how are your folks? They must be elated for you.”

“You know, still kickin’ around, but they’ve been good too, ” she said, “A little nervous for me, as I’m sure you can relate to.”

“I absolutely can,” my mother replied with an uncomfortable level of conviction. “Now, are they still going to that church up on Highland?”

“Oh yeah, every Sunday still,” Ilanna replied.

“Lovely. Well I must say, Elijah has been so excited for this,” my mother said. I don’t quite know what gave her that impression.

“Me too! I’m excited to finally show this guy the real outdoors.” She gestured towards me. 

“I’m sure you are. He has been quite cooped up in that apartment lately,” my mother replied, nodding at me. “He’s been having the most awful time with his sleep.”

Ilanna turned to me with concern. “Really?”

I shrugged, eager to change the subject. “I don’t know. It’s nothing really, just some insomnia. Is this the group right here?” I pointed towards her gaggle of friends, who watched us and talked amongst themselves.

“Yeah!” she replied, “They’ve been wanting to meet you! I’ve told them all about you.”

“Not everything, I hope,” I chuckled as I nervously rubbed my ear. She probably didn’t hear me as she walked towards them. I followed. I quickly glanced back at my mother, her watchful eye following my every move. 

“This is Kayode, and his girlfriend, Ava,” Ilanna said as she gestured to a man about my height. Kayode, whose name sounded like “coyote,” pushed his long hair off of his dark complexion as he went to shake my hand. Ava, who was just as tall as him, sporting a bandana over her buzz-cut, gave another little wave. They seemed like quite the unlikely couple. 

“We are glad to have you join us, Elijah,” Kayode said in a thick African accent, looking me dead in the eyes. Despite his imperfect ESL, he spoke evenly and calmly. There was a certain intensity about every one of his deep vocal inflections, like he didn’t have a single doubt about any word he said.

Ilanna turned to the two other men. 

“This is Danny,” she pointed to the man wearing a tank top, “and Brent.” Brent’s man-bun made it so that he was almost as tall as Danny, and he wore a baggy t-shirt that featured the name of a local Boston coffee shop. I got the impression that they were pretty good friends or maybe even brothers, given the way they smiled at each other. They both had that look on their face, that sort-of smug grin where you couldn’t tell if they were just happy to meet you or if they knew something you didn’t. 

Handshakes and greetings were exchanged, and we spent the next ten minutes or so going through all my gear and the rest of theirs. We all had the basics: tent, sleeping bag, first aid and mess kit, toiletries, and enough food to get us through the Hundred Mile Wilderness, which was an eleven day trip from the start of our hike.

However, what I quickly realized after comparing my supply to everyone else’s, was that I had woefully overpacked. I had brought a solar charger for my phone, a mini battery-powered space heater, an overabundance of clothes, and other things that I (but mainly my mother) had deemed essential, but the rest of the group seemed to not need. 

“Good luck carrying that thing for six months,” Danny said, lifting my bag and straining. He grabbed his pack and handed it to me. Admittedly, it was a lot less heavy. “Always pack light, man, ‘cause you’re gonna be hating life a few days into this hike,” he said through gritted teeth as he did some mock bicep curls with it. 

I forced a chuckle. “Yeah well, we’ll see,” was all I could say.

“Hey guys,” Ava spoke up, pack already on her shoulders, “we should get a move on. We need to get to the campground before sunset.”

“Yeah, true,” said Ilanna. “Brent, when’s sunset today?”

Brent thought for a second. “‘Bout 8:30, give or take,” he finally said. 

“That’s ten hours. Cutting it close,” Ava said sternly, briefly glancing in my direction.

“We’ll make it, Ave, I’m not worried,” Ilanna said. I could tell that her positive attitude hadn’t faltered a single bit, even given past events. I couldn’t decide if that was admirable or ignorant. Kayode nodded in agreement and rubbed Ava on the shoulder, but I couldn’t tell if she was convinced.

“Has anyone ever pitched a tent in the dark?” Ava asked no one in particular. No one answered as they started to zip up their bags. 

I was fighting an ever-growing pit in my stomach, fueled by my regret. My regret over how things ended with me and Ilanna, and my regret over my lack of experience. I felt like an outsider, a piece of dead weight that would be chained to these people’s ankles for the next six months on one of the longest hikes on the planet. 

“Just give me one sec, guys,” I said, walking back to my mother. All of my instincts were telling me to ask her to drive me back home, to my only space where I felt safe. But I knew that would let her down more than anything in the world, and her disapproval would be tougher to swallow than anything else. So, the only thing I could say when I reached her was..

“Guess that’s it. See you in six months.”

My mother nodded, and I could tell she was fighting back her emotions. 

“Yes… okay, then. Be safe, listen to what Ilanna says, and don’t forget to call me whenever you have cell service,” she said as her voice ever-so-slightly quivered, “keep your wits about you.”

“I will,” I said as I went to give her one of those emotionally sheltered, obligatory half-hugs.

I wish I had held her for longer that day. 

But I didn’t, instead letting go after a few seconds and walking back to the group. I figured the less time I spent looking at her, the less of a chance I had of bursting into tears. My stomach sank further and further as I heard her car door open and shut, the engine ignite, and the screech of her tires and the squeak of her motor belt get ever more distant. My hearing strained to catch even the faintest whisper of her presence until there was nothing but the sound of the soft breeze and chirping birds. I never looked back, keeping my head forward as I walked to the group. I was alone now, facing an unknown part of the world and an uncertain path for me and Ilanna’s relationship. Ava watched me like an impatient hawk as the group stood around her, backpacks on and ready to set off.

As we walked towards the trail entrance, I watched the tall trees that stood tall over it. They formed a foreboding shape, like a jaw, ready to chew me up with its unforgiving terrain and harsh conditions. I looked down at my feet. They felt heavy. They moved independently of my discomforts and my fears. I watched them reach the end of the dirt parking lot, the last whimpers of civilization, my last chance to change my mind. I paused and looked ahead at the pine needle-ridden trail. The clean air that carried their scent made me sick to my stomach. Once I crossed this line, the demarcation between the comforts of the modern world and the unpredictability of the natural one, I’d be committed. But as I watched Ilanna and her friends traverse the edge without pause, I was forced to follow. It was too late now.

I entered the trail. There was no turning back now.

When planning to hike the A.T., it was well known that the first two or so weeks were the most strenuous. The inaugural day would be spent hiking up and down Mt. Katahdin. At a four-thousand-foot elevation, this would be no easy task, especially while lugging heavy bags with all of your necessities - mine in particular. Once we reached the bottom, after another four and a half hour hike, we’d pitch tents for the night at the Abol Bridge Campground. The next day, after a quick pitstop and last minute restock at the campground store, we’d be off into the Hundred Mile Wilderness. The name is pretty self-explanatory: no roads, no houses, no civilization for one hundred miles. That’s ten days where we are completely and fully alone until we hit the town of Monson. There’d be no places to restock, so we all had to pack everything we’d need for this stretch and then some. Conservation was key.

The six of us were known as “SOBO” or south-bound hikers: we begin in Maine, at the base of Mt. Katahdin, and end at the southern terminus at the top of Springer Mountain in Georgia. The best way to describe the Appalachian Trail was grueling. Six months, fourteen states and almost 2200 miles of terrain filled with bears, injuries, ticks, and poison ivy that only one in four hikers see to the end. It varied in its levels of difficulty throughout, but the idea of spending half a year sleeping out of a tent and living on dehydrated meals and ramen was not exactly appealing. Some hikers were known to take breaks, sleeping in motels for a few nights here and there. But Ilanna, ever the purist, wanted to see this hike to the end through and through: no breaks, every night spent in the Trail corridor. Not exactly an easy feat. I was probably the least optimistic about our chances of finishing, although the only person I could really get a read on was Ilanna, who not ten minutes into our hike asked the group…

“So, after we make it to Georgia, first day after being done with the trail, how are we celebrating?”

“A little premature, no?” I said chuckling. Ilanna, who was ahead, turned her head to grin at me.

“Doesn’t hurt to have something to look forward to,” she said.

“Me and Brent are gonna get shitfaced,” Danny hollered from the front of the pack, “we got a few buddies flying down to Georgia. Gonna rent an AirBNB. You’re all welcome to come.”

“I’ll just be content to be home by then, I’m sure,” Ava chimed in, “sleep in my bed, see our dogs.”

“The modern comforts of AC and food delivery will be plenty enough for me,” Kayode added. Ava, by his side, nodded. “What about yourself, Ilanna?” he asked.

“Haven’t worked it out yet,” she answered, “that’s why I’m asking you guys.” She turned to me again. “You?”

I watched the sun shimmer as it peeked through the trees that covered the sky, the shadows and shine dancing on Ilanna’s smiling face. I thought for a second.

“A big seafood dinner. Maybe some cod or some salmon. But it has to be from the best restaurant in the area. Maybe some wine too,” I said, “I think that’d be nice.”

“So,” Danny said, “your idea of a celebration is to eat like a fifty year old woman?”

That got a few chuckles. It was funny I suppose, but I didn’t exactly know what warranted it. 

“You’re such a jerk, Danny,” Ilanna said, stifling her own laugh, “you trying to scare him off already?”

“You don’t take anything seriously, do you?” Brent asked Danny, to which he shrugged and shook his head.

Ilanna rolled her eyes. She looked back at me again and nodded in my direction, slowing down her pace and motioning for me to do the same. Once the group was a decent distance ahead, she turned to me as we walked.

“So how *have* you been, Elijah?” she asked more seriously than I was used to her being. I shifted my pack on my shoulders, the weight already straining my trapezius muscles. I stared ahead at the treeline in the distance.

“Well… you know,” I mumbled, “Not too bad. Just working.” She raised an eyebrow.

“‘Just working’?” she repeated, “No other news? No vacations, crazy stories, girlfriends?” I snorted.

“Not on my end,” I said, “although it sounds like you got a lot going on. Since I last saw you, at least.”

“It definitely has been a while. I mean I don’t think I’ve seen you since we graduated… or was it since the funeral? I can’t remember.”

I tensed up at her bringing up that painful memory so nonchalantly. Although it was more painful for her, I’m sure. “Uh… yeah, something like that,” I replied, “You been liking your big fancy job in the city?”

She laughed. “It’s hardly big, *or* fancy. Soil testing is not as exciting as it sounds, believe it or not.”

I chuckled. “No, no… nothing about researching dirt sounds boring at all,” I ribbed, “Must pay pretty good, though.”

“Shut up,” she said, grinning, “But, yes. The pay is not too shabby. Except for the fact that I’m living in Boston, so I’m basically house-poor.”

“Well, if you ever want a taste of the real ritzy part of New England, come on up to Ashland,” I said.

“Oh yeah?” she asked, snickering.

I nodded. “The nightlife there… unbeatable. I mean, where else can you find a town with not one, not two, but three bars,” I said, “It’s almost too many.”

As our awkward laughter died down, the momentary distraction faded. My immense guilt returned as quickly as I had forgotten it.

“Look, uh…” I stuttered, “You really didn’t have to invite me along with you. You and these guys all seem like pros. I kinda feel like I’d just be weighing you down.”

I saw her face shift. A wave of sadness rippled across her countenance, 

“You’re not weighing anybody down, Elijah,” she said in that painfully understanding tone, “You’ll fit right in with us, I promise.” She gave me a smile as she touched my shoulder. I almost flinched. “Plus,” she said, looking down and kicking a rogue rock, “I missed you and all that stuff.”

Those last words stung more than they had any right to. I wondered how much of it was true. I looked in her eyes for any trace of anger or resentment, but found none. If she was still harboring a grudge, she had buried it deep down. I sighed, pathetically attempting to smile back at her. 

“Me too,” I mumbled.

We were silent for a minute as we continued on the path. The sun was slowly creeping up in the sky, getting brighter and hotter by the minute.

“But be honest, how much of this was my mother’s doing?” I asked.

Her wide grin returned. “Your mom… may have had a bit of influence,” she said, “but only because I thought there wasn’t any world out there where I could get Elijah Simmons to hike the A.T. with me.”

I gave a half-chuckle. “I honestly can’t believe I’m out here myself,” I said, looking up at the hazy pine trees. “So you work with all of these guys? I don’t really have them pegged as the dirt-analyzing types.”

“Oh, and I am?” she asked.

“Oh, yeah, you’re definitely a dirt-analyzer. Born for it.”

She laughed. “Yes, I work with them, except for Brent. He’s Danny’s brother. I’ve met him a couple times before this. Super smart guy. Pretty much the polar opposite of Danny.”

I nodded. Called it. I knew they were related

“You hear that, Danny?!” she yelled to the front of the pack, “I said that Brent’s smarter than you!”

“Wouldn’t be the first time I’ve heard it!” Danny yelled back. “Our dad used to say that when he got drunk!”

Ilanna rolled her eyes as we kept walking. I wondered if her and Danny were a thing, given the way they talked to each other. I made a mental note in my head to keep tabs on it.

As the day rolled on, the trees became more sparse, and the dirt path slowly morphed into boulders and rocks that became more and more steep and jagged. My legs burned from the increasing incline. By noontime, it had gotten to the point where we were practically scaling them. We reached a landing, where we all wordlessly agreed to take a break to catch our breath. 

Kayode watched me as I panted and wiped beads of sweat from my forehead.

“It will be worth it, Elijah,” he said, “The view from the top of the mountain is truly magnificent. My breath is taken every time.”

“You’ve been up this mountain before?” I asked through ragged breaths.

“Oh, yes,” he said, “Me and Ava, all the time. It is one of our favorite spots.”

“Well, it’s definitely making us work for it, that’s for sure,” I said as I stared at our path ahead.

“Indeed,” Kayode answered, doing the same. “But much of this life must be worked for, no?”

I nodded, pulling my water bottle from my pack and taking a few sips. 

“Should only be another hour or so to the top now, right?” asked Brent. Kayode nodded. 

“We’ll only have a few minutes to stop and take in the view, though, okay?” Ava said to Kayode, “We gotta haul ass down the mountain.”

Kayode nodded. He seemed disappointed. “Ah,” he said quietly, “of course.”

“I’m serious about getting there on time,” she added. She seemed angry.

It took him a second, but Kayode nodded and forced a smile, putting his hand on Ava’s shoulder.

“Let’s go,” he said to all of us, shouldering his pack and heading up the path. I shot Ilanna a “what was that all about?” look, but she just shook her head and rolled her eyes. I’d have to ask her about it later.

Admittedly, the view from the top *was* quite beautiful. The mountain ranges poked through the ground, painted with the dark green forests that ran as far as the eye could see. A hawk soared through the valley as the sun made its dancing reflections on the string-thin rivers that flowed into the little ponds that seemed to be puddles. In the distance, the pure white clouds gave the illusion of taller, snowy mountain peaks far, far away. It felt like looking back in time. I pictured the glaciers that would accompany them, carving and slicing up the planet at will, then disappearing in a cosmic second, thoughtlessly leaving behind the unforgiving terrain as we puny humans attempted to conquer it. I pondered how futile the notion was that we could ever hope to have control over our unforgiving planet. I thought about how indifferent nature was to our lives: our triumphs, our struggles, and everything in between were as trivial to the Earth as a leaf falling from a tree. It felt cruel, and I had never felt smaller.

Danny slapped his hand on Kayode’s shoulder.

“Damn, man! You weren’t lying!” he said, “This view’s killer!”

The five of us stood shoulder to shoulder as we gazed over the top. Ava impatiently tapped her foot, already taking a few steps further down the path. Kayode nodded, but his eyes were fixed on something else. 

“Ilanna, do you see those spots?” he asked as he pointed down the mountain, “Right there, in the valley.”

Ilanna looked up from the unfolded map in her hands. She scanned the area.

“Those?” she asked, pointing to a few different spots in the forest we overlooked. I had noticed them too and hadn’t thought much of them, but Kayode clearly did. He nodded. It was five small areas, scattered across the land, each of them miles apart. Each one was a large circle where not a single tree had grown. They couldn’t have been more than one or two thousand square feet in diameter, but the near-perfect roundness of them was offputting. The grass and plantlife that would normally be canopied by the tall pines had died, turning the ground a dull tan. They looked unnatural, manmade, like someone had carefully carved holes in the valley, revealing the dead and dry land underneath.

“They were not here the last time Ava and I were on this mountain,” Kayode said, his even keeled tone wavering a hair. Ilanna analyzed the circles for a long time, scratching her chin.

“Forest fires maybe?” she said, seemingly less concerned.

“In such a small area?” Kayode countered.

“Yeah, I guess they really aren’t that big,” Ilanna said, turning to Brent, "Brent, you brought those binoculars, right?”

Brent nodded, pulling them out of his pack as he and Danny had now sidled up beside me to look.

“They kinda look like crop circles,” Brent said, handing the binoculars to Ilanna.

“Yeah, if the aliens were fucking stupid,” said Danny.

Ilanna looked through the binoculars for a long time, moving her head from circle to circle. Kayode stood behind her, following her gaze.

“What can you make out?” he asked.

“Not much else,” she replied, “They’re too far away.” She handed the binoculars to Kayode, who held it up to his face.

“Guys!” Ava called from even further down the path, “Can we get a move on?”

“We’re coming,” Ilanna said back, walking towards Ava. Kayode dropped the binoculars down to his side, sighing in confusion. I studied the circles harder as Danny and Brent left to follow Ilanna. I couldn’t explain it, but something about them unnerved me, like it tapped into a very specific yet unknown part of my brain that sent waves of chills up my back. I almost found myself fighting off a shiver as I turned away from them to Kayode, who was already starting to walk behind the group.

“Kayode,” I asked, “Have you ever seen anything like that before?”

“I have not,” he replied, shaking his head, “It is very strange.” He turned and headed towards the group. “Very strange,” he repeated to himself. I took one last quick glance at the circles. I attempted to shake off the bad feeling they gave me. I had already had plenty of that to go around. I rubbed my eyes and followed Kayode down the path.

I often think back on this moment. I should’ve left. I should’ve walked, no… ran away at the very first uneasy feeling I got. My instincts were screaming at me in every way possible, pleading with me to get out of there. But I ignored them. I wonder if I was a fool for listening to my mother, for believing for one second that this journey would somehow change my mental state. I wonder if I had ever had it in me to turn around and leave, or if my fate was predetermined, my soul condemned to the cruelty that awaited it in the depths of the Appalachian Trail. 

***End of Part One***
