I don't know how to start this. I've written this post four times and deleted it four times. But my wife is asleep in the next room and I can't sleep, so I'm going to type this out before I lose my nerve.

I need to tell someone what happened in my house.

15 years ago, my wife gave birth to our son. I'm not going to use his real name here, so I'll just call him Ray.

The first two years with Ray were the happiest of my life. He laughed a lot. He slept well. My wife and I used to sit on the porch at night and just look at him and feel like the luckiest people alive.

Then something changed.

It started small. He would pull the wings off flies. Kids do that, right? I told myself that. Then it was the neighbor's cat. We found it behind our shed, and I don't want to describe what it looked like. We told ourselves a fox got it.

By the time Ray was six, teachers were calling us every week. He pushed a boy off the monkey bars and broke his arm. He said the boy "made a funny sound." He set fire to a trash can in the school bathroom. When the counselor asked him why, he smiled and said, "I wanted to see."

We took him to doctors. Psychologists. One of them, a quiet older man with glasses, pulled me aside after a session and said words I will never forget.

"Your son doesn't feel what other people feel. I don't think he ever will."

We didn't want to hear it. We loved him. We told ourselves love could fix anything.

By the time Ray was twelve, love wasn't fixing anything.

I won't go through every single thing that happened over the years. Some of it is too much. But by the time Ray was fifteen, we were locking our bedroom door at night. We had cameras in the hallway. My wife stopped leaving knives out on the counter.

Our son, our sweet baby boy who used to fall asleep on my chest, had grown into something that looked at us like we were furniture. Like we were things he was still deciding what to do with.

And then my wife found out she was pregnant again.

I remember the night she told me. I didn't feel happy. I felt sick. All I could think about was Ray's face the day we brought our daughter home from the hospital. He didn't smile. He didn't cry. He just stood in the doorway of the nursery and stared at her crib for eleven minutes. I know it was eleven minutes because I was standing behind him counting every single one, too scared to move, too scared to say anything that might set him off.

We named her Lily.

For the first year, nothing happened. Ray mostly ignored her. We started to relax, just a little. Maybe he didn't care about her at all. Maybe that was a good thing.

Then one night I woke up at 3 a.m. and Ray's door was open. His bed was empty.

I ran to Lily's room so fast I don't even remember moving my legs.

He was standing over her crib. Just standing there. Watching her sleep.

I grabbed him by the shoulder and he turned around slow, like he had all the time in the world, and he looked at me and said the words that have been stuck in my head every single night since.

"I'm the only reason she's still breathing."

I didn't understand what he meant. I still don't, not fully. But I dragged him out of that room and I didn't sleep again that night, or the next, or hardly any night after.

Two weeks later, I found out why he said it.

I was cleaning out the garage and I found a box shoved behind the water heater, taped shut, hidden under a tarp. Inside were photographs. Old ones. Photos of our street. Photos of our house taken from outside, through the windows, at night. Photos of Lily's nursery, the crib, the window above it — the window that faces the woods behind our property.

Photos I never took.

Photos my wife never took.

I brought them to Ray and I asked him where they came from, and for the first time in years, I saw something on his face that wasn't emptiness.

It was fear.

He told me there was a man. He said the man had been coming to the edge of the woods for almost a year, watching the house, watching Lily's window. He said he saw him first, months before we ever put in the cameras. He said he never told us because he knew we wouldn't believe him — because why would we believe the boy who hurts things, the boy who scares people, the boy the whole town already thinks is broken.

He said the only reason the man never got inside was because Ray had been sitting outside Lily's door every single night since she was born, waiting.

I felt the floor tilt under me.

I asked him why he never just told us the truth.

He looked at me with those flat, empty eyes I had spent years being afraid of, and he said:

"Would you have believed me? Or would you have just been more scared of me?"

I didn't sleep that night either. I sat in the hallway between both kids' rooms with a baseball bat until the sun came up, feeling like the worst father in the entire world.

We called the police. They found footprints in the mud behind the fence. Cigarette butts. A pair of binoculars wedged in the fork of a tree, aimed directly at Lily's window. They're "investigating." That was eleven days ago. No arrests yet.

Ray still doesn't feel things the way other people do. That part is real, and I'm not writing this to pretend it isn't. The doctors were right about him in a lot of ways. He still scares me sometimes. I still lock my door at night, if I'm honest.

But three nights ago I got up to get water at 2 a.m. and I walked past Lily's door, and it was open again.

And Ray was sitting on the floor outside it. Not inside her room. Outside the door, in the hallway, back against the wall, eyes on the window at the end of the hall the whole time.

He didn't even look at me. He just said, very quietly, "Go back to bed. I've got this."

I stood there for a long time before I finally did.

I keep telling myself this is a good thing. I keep telling myself maybe I was wrong about my own son this whole time, that maybe there's something in him after all, something that just looks different than it does in other people.

But last night I couldn't sleep, so I went back through those photographs one more time, the ones from the box in the garage.

And in the very last one, taken from the tree line, aimed at Lily's window at night, there are two figures visible through the glass.

One is my daughter, asleep in her crib.

The other one is Ray. Standing over her. In the picture, taken from outside, in the dark, at an angle no one in our house could have taken it from.

The photo is dated the night before Lily was born.

I haven't shown my wife yet. I don't know what it means. I don't know if it means anything.

I just know Ray is sitting outside that door again tonight, and I don't know anymore whether he's keeping something out, or something in.

If anyone here has dealt with anything like this, please, tell me what to do. I don't think I'm going to sleep again until I understand what that photograph means.
