I have for a long time run from the List Man. He appeared everywhere I went. I suppose I should commend him for his determination; there he was, around every corner, carrying with him a meticulously written outline of my demise. Any one of his ploys may have worked if not for my abundant cowardice. 

Many people have recounted their experiences with the List Man on Reddit – though they never call him by name. I admire novice horror stories, and I consumed them ravenously as a teen. I thought he was fiction; he was from the internet, a place ripe with the fruits of fiction. I couldn't have suspected he was real until I was confronted directly by his handiwork. 

I decided to move out of my parent's house when I was freshly eighteen. That's what grown-ups did, be independent, I thought. I browsed all manner of website and forum and community cork boards in my search for an apartment. I found one: a single bed, an individual bath, and a shared kitchen and living room. The landlady was a short woman of eighty-five years. Her name was Edith.  

When I met Edith, she told me this deal was unusual. I asked her in what way. 

"There's a list of rules," Edith croaked, "that you must follow." 

"Okay." 

"First – you can never go in the basement." 

"Alright, well, I don't like basements. Everything's here on the ground floor, anyhow." 

"Secondly, you must barricade the closet door before midnight." 

"You know, Edith, I'm having second thoughts – I don't know if I'd be the right fit. I wish you luck finding a tenant. You seem like grand old dame!" 

I moved in with my grandmother; it was much cheaper that way. She needed a hand around the house and I needed a place to stay without a list of strange, foreboding rules. Few things in my life would seem to come without them. 

After that it was dog-sitting for my auntie's mutt, Princess – the rules she'd laid out were bizarre and flagrantly ominous. I said no, I'd earn grocery money elsewhere. Then it was the job at the corner store; never let the big hand tick past twelve! I rescinded my application. I wanted to reward myself for writing my final exam of freshman year, and booked a remote cottage with a lake view (never open the door if it knocks six times). I drove the three hours home in silence. 

I have avoided him that way for ten years. Look the other way and don't look back. When fear drives you it sometimes steers you in the right direction; it did for me. I always knew what the List Man was up to and I always knew when to run. 

Evening had not yet fell when the man dressed in a black collared shirt and khaki slacks stumbled through the brush and staggered through my backyard. Some hoodlum, I thought, to be dressed in business-casual attire; I noticed he carried a clipboard and a pen. He was disheveled and headed for my porch door. 

His appearance frightened me. I knew a trick when I saw one. I went to the supply cupboard and picked out my Granddaddy's red rubber mallet, a big, heavy thing with a long wooden handle. The list had never come to me before. The man and I arrived at the screen door at the very same moment. 

"Mr. Jones?" he asked, exasperated. 

"That's me." 

"Oh, thank goodness! I have been just about everywhere in this neighbourhood searching for this house – is this, is this your backdoor? Oh my, I'm so sorry. Wow, I am an idiot," he panted. 

"Alright." 

He caught his breath, and continued: "After I knocked on Mrs. Elliott's door she said I could just take that little path through the woods to your place, that it wasn't far at all. Boy was she wrong! I thought I might die out there. Coyotes and all." 

"Yup." I held the mallet limply at my side, the head skimming the linoleum floor. 

"Well, now that I'm here, I wanted to tell you about the good news and ask you to possibly make a donation. For the parishioners at Saint Theodora's?" 

"What are the rules?" 

"Pardon me?" 

"If I were to make a donation, what are the rules?" My grip on the mallet tightened. 

"No rules, sir. You just write the amount here and then you sign your name. Let me tell you about the church, sir, and about our pastor – " 

He droned on about that for several many minutes as I thought intently. No list. Well, okay. My Grandma had gone to Saint Theodora's; they were a nice bunch, I knew, because they'd helped me pay for her funeral when she passed. I didn't go there (the choirboy had said there were rules for sitting in the pews), but they were kind to me all the same. I could pay them back now that I'd made something of myself. 

"Okay. I'll make a donation." I slid the door open warily. 

"Oh, my, thank you! Wow, thanks! That's the second one I've got today – I've knocked on thirty houses. Mrs. Elliott's was the first, of course, she always does. Oh, Father Jubb will be so excited!" he exclaimed. He hurriedly thrust the clipboard and pen into my free hand. I set down the mallet and wrote on the lines, leaving a signature and making it out to the amount of five-hundred dollars. I passed it back to the man, and he smiled. 

And chuckled, then chortled, and coughed and hacked in raucous enthusiasm; he looked beyond insane as he brayed like a mule, tossing his head and heaving his shoulders. 

"You've finally done it! Oh, didn't you read the fine print? Was that all I ever had to do to get you? That was so simple, so simple. I knew I'd get you!" he screeched, his voice grating and high-pitched. He looked monstrous now, as the sun set, his features garbled beyond recognition. He grew warty and wrinkled and hairy – he was pulsing with a foul aura, like a wound oozing a sickly puss. 

"It's me, me, the List Man! And I've got you, finally, finally I have you! Now I've done it proper!" 

"What are the rules?" 

"Light," he giggled, "light can never touch your skin – it'll turn you to ash. Ashes!" He danced around happily, kicking his crooked feet and clapping his gnarled hands as he waltzed in awkward circles. 

"You're a dolt." 

"What?" 

"I said: You're a dolt. You're not smart at all." 

"Hah, you say, but when day comes, what'll you do, boy? I've got you!" 

"You haven't read it." 

His movements stopped abruptly. His grip on the clipboard tightened; I heard his long nails squeak against the plastic backing. He read, and his eyes filled with horror, and he could not lift them from the page as I brought that red rubber mallet down on his head. One time, two times, three, four, one time for every time anyone had every said to me there are some rules and then some more. I bashed him until he was not more than a pile red sludge and I washed him off my porch with a garden hose.  

All I'd done was sign John Smith, because really, I was too afraid to fork over a full five-hundred bucks of my hard-earned cash; I'd thought maybe I'd go back later, and make a fifty donation, while apologising for the mix-up. The List Man hasn't pestered me since, and nor should he pester anyone else ever again.
