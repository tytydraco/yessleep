Tonight I was reminded of the night that ended my childhood.

Halloween, 1994.

Before that night, the worst thing I could imagine was waking up to my birthday that nobody could remember. Time also moved differently then. Slow. So slow the second hand on the classroom clock looked like it might reverse course if you watched it long enough. I thought girls were gross and had cooties. I would be caught dead if I was ever talking to one. Most importantly, there was never enough candy. And Halloween was the one night a year that promised to fix that.

My friends Jack, Toby, Skylar and I had planned the whole thing for months. Costumes first, then the route, street by street, the ones where the houses handed out the good stuff and didn’t short you. Something about the nineties made Halloween feel bigger than it had any right to. Kids and teenagers came pouring out of houses dressed as sports heroes and monsters and whatever else they’d thrown together, running wild through the neighborhood. Not an adult in sight. Just the noise of it. Scares and pranks and trick-or-treaters everywhere you looked. On every corner kids stood around comparing what they’d got, trying to figure out which house was giving out the full-size bars. You could hear the girls screaming when the boys jumped out at them, and over that the nerds telling their stories, the ones that always ended with “this really happened.”

“So it’s settled,” I said proudly. “We go straight for Little Dove Drive before they run out of candy. Then turn right on Running Bull Boulevard, hit up every house there. Remember we avoid Chief Black Hawk Road since the driveways are so long despite the houses being bigger. We go straight for Moon Flower Way and hit up every street after that. Alright guys?” I folded up our crudely drawn map of the neighborhood, the one we’d made on a torn sheet of notebook paper with the spiral-bound frills still on it.

“Guys, were you even listening?”

Jack was listening. Toby was eyeballing my baseball card collection and Skylar was working through my Rubik’s Cube, the one I got for my birthday that had never shown a matching face since.

“Dude, I’ll trade you Derek Jeter and Michael Jordan for your Mickey Mantle.”

I gave Toby the same annoyed look I’d given him the first hundred times he’d asked about that trade. “Okay, okay, how about two Derek Jeters?”

“Put Mickey down,” I said sternly. Toby proceeded to slowly slip it into his sock, making eye contact with me the whole time. “On the counter, you Butt-head!”

Toby was the troublemaker. Any time our little group was caught doing something we shouldn’t, it was Toby’s idea. His dad was a cop, so maybe he thought he could get away with anything. In a way, he did because every time I asked him about his punishment after I got my ass spanked or grounded for the week, he would always say that he just got his tv time taken away that evening. I was always jealous of that little shit. Even as an adult he had it on easy street. College paid by dad, top of the academy, and a career in law enforcement that guaranteed a promotion every year. He had a good life but I had better baseball cards.

“If we stick to my shortcut through the kiddie park, we will have plenty of time to hit every house on Moon Flower twice before 9,” said Skylar as he adjusted his glasses not once looking at the map.

“That shortcut better be good. The last thing I need is everyone thinking we hang out at kiddie parks,” said Toby as he attempted to tape the bolts of his Frankenstein costume onto his neck.

“Perhaps if we rehearsed the route like I suggested you wouldn’t be doubting my shortcut like how I’m doubting your costume choice.”

“I wanted to go as robbers but you all wanted to dress up like monsters. And I still don’t know how I ended up as Frankenstein when you should have gone as his bride since you have the girl name, SKYLAR.”

Skylar frowned and rolled his eyes as he wrapped the last of the bandages completing his mummy costume. He quickly noticed the Crazy Glue next to my model paint collection.

“Here, use this,” he said as he handed Toby the glue. “I read that this was an old movie prop trick for those bolts.”

“Really?”

“Oh sure! It will come off with just a little warm water.”

Toby looked around the room for validation but no one wanted to even look his way. He was way overdue for some painful punishment.

Skylar smirked as Toby thanked him. That was probably the first time Skylar did something clever in his life. The kid was known to be an idiot. He would recite the most random facts that he read from the National Geographic magazines that he probably read that morning. He tried so hard to be the poindexter that he came off as a dork rather than a nerd. Just trying to fill his older brother’s shoes, who was actually the real brainiac, accepted into college at 13.

“Just make sure you ask your dad for help before trying to remove them yourself,” said Jack, placing his webbed fish monster hand on Toby’s shoulder.

Skylar scowled at Jack.

“I told my mom that I’ll be home by 9:15, so wherever we end up on this map at 9:00, I’m leaving.”

“You’re so lame Saint Jack, so lame,” and he shook off Jack’s empathetic hand.

Saint Jack was what we all called him. He was the good one. Always did his homework. Always finished his chores. Always brushed his teeth. He was polite and genuinely caring. He was the best friend any kid could ever have. He was perfect in every way. Every teacher made sure his mother knew.

His mother had it rough. His dad died from an illness no one talked about before Jack was even born. Jack and his mother were close. They only had each other and he was her pride and joy. Too bad she never saw what an amazing person Jack could have been because after that Halloween night, no one saw Jack again.

The night began at the threshold of my parents’ house. The A-Team: Toby as Frankenstein, Skylar as the mummy, Jack the fish monster, and myself the vampire. We were ready and determined to have the greatest Halloween of our lives. The priority was to obtain enough candy that would last us until next Halloween.

“Where do you boys think you’re going?” my father asked from the living room. He was sitting on his La-Z-Boy, staring at the TV. Commercials were on, so he was more aware of his surroundings.

“Dad,” I said nervously. “It’s Halloween. The guys and I are going trick-or-treating.”

The old man knew darn well what day it was. We all did because of him. He made sure that the only TV in the house was all his. He had to be the biggest horror movie fan I knew, and my mom hated that fact. She would let him watch those movies, but he was never allowed to buy them. She didn’t want those “wicked mediums” in her house. So my father would tear pages out of the TV Guide, circling the scary movie he would be watching that week. When one of those pages went up on the fridge, we all knew not to disturb Dad. This Halloween was special for him. They were running a horror movie marathon of pretty much all of his favorites. And this year, he was trying to tape all of them without my mom knowing.

“Take your sister.”

The dreaded words that ruin every boy’s plans. The guys looked at me angrily, telling me to get out of it without using their words.

“But Dad…” I pleaded, but was interrupted by my mother.

“Honey, don’t argue with your father. Take your sister or you will be staying indoors for Halloween.”

I reluctantly agreed.

So there I was, at the porch with my friends dressed up as a vampire, Frankenstein, a mummy, with my sister dressed as a fairy. Our scary vibe was instantly killed.

“This better not affect our candy numbers,” complained Toby.

“I’m going to assume about 25 percent less than our initial estimate,” Skylar explained.

“Just shut up, everyone.” My parents would have murdered me in front of all of you if I even tried to put up a stink.

“Well this stinks now!” cried out Toby.

I felt I was about to be jumped by my own friends before Jack came to my defense.

“Fellas, this can actually work. Grownups love little kids trick-or-treating, especially when they’re as young and cute as McKenzie.”

My little sister blushed and curtsied to Saint Jack.

“Kiss ass,” Toby mumbled.

I grabbed my little sister’s hand and pushed the fellas off my porch as I said, “Let’s just go. We have a night ahead of us!”

Immediately things didn’t go as planned. Jack’s costume was the biggest hindrance. His mother’s painted snorkel fins made up his webbed feet. The costume looked great but it was impractical. The first street took twice as long to go through as planned but fortunately my sister’s “cuteness” factor was scoring us extra candy so Toby and Skylar weren’t that upset but I wanted to keep pushing. By the next street, Jack would take off his flippers before we would approach the house then put them back and hobble to the doorway to collect his candy.

Running Bull Boulevard ended up being a good run but we were way behind schedule. Skylar’s shortcut was now crucial for this night to be as successful as we wanted it to be. It so happened that his shortcut wasn’t a secret. Every kid who was brave enough to walk through a park with no lights was taking that route. Jack was taking off his last fin when we found out Skylar’s shortcut may also not be the best route.

An older kid named Evan, dressed as a dark ghost with shackles, was sitting over the ledge of a brick wall of one of the corner houses. He was eating a full sized candy bar which obviously generated a small crowd of curious kids seeking the source for the king sized bars.

In that crowd was another older kid named Stacy and she was with her little sister. The only reason why I know her is because our sisters were friends. It was always an awkward “Hi” when I bumped into her when I had to pick up my sister from class. She would always try to initiate small talk that I would always try to skirt out of. I treated her as the big girl with the hoodie that was always friendly to me. That night, she wasn’t wearing a hoodie. She was dressed as a pirate. She wasn’t a big girl and her corset made everyone aware of it. 

“Where did you get that?!?” Cried out Toby with a murmur of other kids joining in.

“One of the houses on Moon Flower.” Said Evan.

A collective praise among my friends.

“But I wouldn’t cut through the park. It’s haunted!” Bits of candy flew out of his mouth.

”What do you mean, haunted?” Nervously asked Skylar. “My research did not pull up anything haunted in this area.”

“Because you’re an idiot! Why do you think all these streets have all these goofy names like Moon Flower, Running Bull, Souring Ravine? They are named after the Indians that died when we fought for this land. They say, the kid’s park is where the mass burial happened after the last battle. That’s why they didn’t build over that lot. Thousands of bodies were thrown into a massive pit. Some were still alive who were able to curse the land above them with their final breath. At night, you can still hear them whisper their curses.”

”Bullshit!” Said Toby. Stacy immediately covered her sister's ear.

“Toby!” She scolded.

I also covered McKenzie’s ears and also shouted, “Toby!” Anything to score more points with Stacy. Maybe her and I can complain about Toby the next time we bump into each other.

”Really, Dude?” Toby looked at me with a combination of disgust and disappointment to remind me that I broke the Bro Code.

”Remember last year when the Riley Brothers disappeared?”

”Everyone knows what happened to the Riley Brothers. They ran away because they were responsible for the break-ins last Halloween.”

“That’s what everyone thinks happened to the Riley Brothers. But I saw them enter that park over there,” Evan pointed at the kiddie park. “I followed them to see what they were up to. They were smoking cigarettes. Then I heard the whisper from below my feet. And they heard it too. I ran out of the park. I looked behind me and the Riley Brothers were gone.”

Everyone was listening intently. He was proud of his work and with every masterpiece he closed it with a, “this really happened.”

The group of kids instantly started talking about the story they just heard amongst each other, debating if they should enter the park. Everyone agreed to take the long way to Moon Flower.

”Are you going in there?” Stacy asked me with a concerned look.

“I have to. My friends are counting on each other to make this shortcut work.”

“I wish I was as brave as you.”

”I’ll save you a king sized bar for you.” I smiled at her but couldn’t keep eye contact.

She touched my left arm and I instantly melted inside. I never felt this way with anyone let alone a girl.

”See you on the other side.” She said.

The entrance of the kiddie park looked different at night. During the day, everything was so colorful. Red swings, a big yellow slide, blue rocking horses, and an orange seesaw. As if the colors of the rainbow were the only palette used to design the park. The park was small with all the amenities centralized in the dead center. However several long and windy paths led in and out to different neighborhoods. Each path was covered with thick vegetation so you couldn’t see directly into the park.

”You guys, are you sure about this?” Asked Jack. He was asking what we were all thinking. This little kid park looked absolutely menacing under the moonlight. The park had no lights and some sections of our path had bushes so high that it covered the little light we had.

“I’m scared.” McKenzie said softly to me as she squeezed my hand.

”We aren’t chickens. There are king sized candy bars waiting for us on the other side,” proclaimed Toby. “But which path do we take?”

”Umm.. I’m not sure.” Said Skylar nervously.

“What do you mean you don’t know!? You did the research, you dork!”

”There were only a couple paths when I looked at the map. Look,” pointed Skylar to a flight light at the center of the park. A cartoonish sign, ripped from a Dr Seuss book, had arrows pointing to different directions. “There we will know what path to take!”

“Let’s push through.” I said. The imagination of me giving Stacy that candy bar I promised is all I can think about. So fear just became an afterthought.

The walk to the sign was more of a speed walk. Each of us was trying to walk faster than the other without running. We all knew in the back of our minds that a single skip or jolt would cause a panic and we were trying our absolute best to keep it cool.

A cold breeze flowed through the narrow pathway as if the park was warning us not to enter. As we made our way to the center of the park a low and cold fog awaited us. Now it was freezing and our teeth were chattering as we attempted to speak.

”Moon Flower,” pointed Skylar, “That way.”

Just as we were about to go down the new path we heard a surprising cheerful sound coming from Toby!

”Hey look you guys!” He knelt into the light fog. “Candy! Someone left their bag here and it’s all for the taking!”

”Toby, that candy doesn't belong to you.”

”Listen to Jack,” I explained, “Some kid probably got scared and took off without it. He’ll probably come back in the morning for it.”

”And leave it for the raccoons? This kid is definitely not getting his candy back,” Toby grabbed handful after handful loading up his candy bag. “Come on guys, get in on this.”

”I’m cold and scared.” My little sister was about to cry.

”We need to move.” I began my brisk walk with my sister in hand. Jack and Skylar followed quickly.

“Your loss!” Toby chuckled.

We weren’t even halfway down the path when the breeze turned into a strong wind. Plants were shaking so loud I couldn’t hear my own footsteps. What I did hear next was even louder.

”Oh my God! Run!” Screamed Toby. Next thing I knew he ran right past all of us in a dead sprint.

We all took off running and screaming as fast as we could through the path. I could feel my heart pounding through my chest. At the end of the path I was so out of breath I could have passed out if I didn’t hear someone laughing hysterically. Toby stood under the streetlight on the far side, bent double, laughing so hard he could barely stand.  
All of our hands were fists. Even Jack looked ready to clobber him. Toby straightened up, still grinning, then the grin slipped. “Oh shit. Where’s McKenzie?”

The anger went out of me so fast it left me cold. She wasn’t beside me. She was still in the park.

“We need to go back for her.”

Toby shifted his bag to the other shoulder. “I, uh… I ate too much candy before I took off. I feel sick. You don’t want me in there. I might throw up.”

Skylar wouldn’t meet my eyes. “And someone should wait here. If it gets bad, who calls for help?”

“Sure. Thanks, guys.” The words came out flat. “Guess I’m going in.”

Jack stepped up beside me before I finished the sentence. “You’re not alone. Let’s get McKenzie. She probably just tripped after Toby’s stunt. She’ll be fine.”

His voice was steady. I could still hear the shake under it. If I had to walk back into that park with anyone, it was him.

The wind was gone when we crossed the entrance but it was still cold. Every step sounded loud on the path. The bushes didn’t move.

“Do you see her?” Jack whispered.

“Jack, this is the one place nobody should be whispering.”

“Sorry,” he whispered again.

“Jack!”

After that, we heard the small sobs. I knew the sound. McKenzie was under a bench a little way down the path, curled tight with her fairy wings bent around her like a blanket. I didn’t ask how she got there. Jack and I pulled her out and started back the way we’d come, fast, her hand locked in mine.

Toby and Skylar were still under the streetlight when we came out. The relief on their faces was clear. I guess they were my friends even though they were chickens.

 I almost missed it. It was thin, right at the edge of the bushes behind me as I was leaving the park entrance.

“Goodbye.”

Just that. Soft enough I could have told myself I imagined it. I didn’t. The word stayed. There hasn’t been a day since that I haven’t heard it again, usually when I’m not expecting it, in the same small voice.  
We stood there a minute longer than we needed to. Then Toby cleared his throat and pointed down the street.

“Moon Flower’s still open. King-size bars don’t hand themselves out.”

Nobody argued. We started walking. The bags felt heavier. Nobody mentioned the park again that night.

So we trick-or-treated every house on that street. Eagerly waiting for the prized candy to drop into our sacks which never happened.

“It's 9 o clock, guys, that’s it for me.” Said Jack.

”But we aren’t finished until we get that stupid king sized bar!” Said Toby. 

“Skylar, are you sure we hit every house? We skipped a few.”

”We skipped them because they didn’t have a pumpkin out or the lights were off.”

”I don’t remember trick-or-treating that house!” Toby pointed. It was the first house we walked by since we left the park. That house was overgrown and underkept but a lone jack-o-lantern sat flickering on the porch.

“I know this house.” Said Jack. “No one lives here, I think. My mom and I walk this everyday for her morning walk. This house never has the trash cans outside on trash day.”

The front yard of the house was walled off with shrubs that overtook the original rod iron fence so you couldn't see through it. But you can see looking through the courtyard entrance a lone lit jack-o-lantern sitting on the porch. 

“That was definitely not there when we first got here.” Said Skyler.

”Probably because the owners were probably trick-or-treating too.” Said Toby. “And now they are back.”

”But no one lives here.”

”Must be new neighbors! Let’s go introduce ourselves” Toby said excitedly as he entered the property line. He stopped right at the threshold.  “Whoah! Wow, guys, you got to check this out!” 

Skyler immediately ran for Toby. Jack looked at his watch then looked at me with concern because he always keeps his promise especially with his mom.

“One more house,” I said. I took McKenzie’s hand. Jack sat on the curb and forced the flippers back on.

The yard opened up past the bushes. A big tree stood in the middle, branches reaching over the fence and onto the attached garage. Ghosts and witches hung from the branches, but they weren’t flying. Each one had a noose. The sheets and striped socks turned slowly when the air moved. Hay bales and broken sections of white picket fence filled the rest of the space. Pigs, sheep, and a small calf lay on their sides and backs with the guts pulled out. Standing dolls that were supposed to cover their eyes had been laid on the ground instead, dressed like trick-or-treaters, knives and forks stuck in their backs, candy and little pumpkin buckets scattered around them.

Everything faced the same direction: a throne of hay bales. A farmer figure sat on it. The head was a pig’s. The eyes glowed red. It held a pitchfork and did not move.

“This is the house,” Toby said. “These are the people with the good stuff.”

He headed for the porch. The rest of us followed slower. I kept watching the pig head. It never blinked. Jack was still catching up, flippers slapping.

“Jackpot!” Toby stopped in front of a big bowl next to the jack-o’-lantern. It was full of king-size bars, different brands, wrappers shining. On top of the pile sat a piece of paper in thick capital letters: TAKE ONLY ONE.

Toby’s hands were already in the bowl.

“Take only one,” Jack said. “More than that is stealing.”

“Don’t start, Saint Jack. We went through hell for this.”

“The owners aren’t here,” Skylar said. “It could mean one per group.”

I had my eyes on the pig man until Toby started pitching.

”Clearly these are new neighbors who practically worship Halloween. I mean, look at this place!”

I quickly looked back at the pig man forgetting that my gut was telling me that our lives depended on it. Even if he did move, we were screwed because the only way out of this yard would be through him.

“This is their first Halloween in this neighborhood and they went all out for us. And look,” Toby points and the bowl filled to the brim with candy. “No one has been coming to this place. If we don’t convince these poor people that their house wasn’t  a huge hit by taking all their candy, they are going to stop doing this so say goodbye to these king sized candy bars for good!”

”He has a point,” added Skylar. “Even if we are the only trick-or-treaters that come here, we have established a loot source for future halloweens.

”Check out the big brain in Skylar. I wasn’t even thinking that far ahead.” Toby placed the biggest handful of king sized candy bars in each of our sacks including McKenzies. He poured the rest of the bowl into his own bag as he said, “Trick-or-Treat!”

The most powerful creak imaginable cut through the yard. I swore the weight shifted on that wooden porch. I looked back at the throne. It was empty.

Streams of terror followed as the pig man took another step closer and entered the porch from the side. The red eyes were looking down at us now. The pitchfork in his hand.

What I experienced next was a fever dream of sorts. My head was spinning. The yard tilted under my feet. My teenage body felt like it was having a heart attack and a stroke at the same time. Guilt for stealing, the fear that had been building all night, and the sudden knowledge that I might die all hit at once. None of that changed what I was already doing, running.

We all ran. Jack managed maybe three steps in the oversized flippers before he went down hard and pulled Skylar with him. Something grabbed McKenzie’s ankle. One of the prop trick-or-treaters had come to life, the knife still stuck in its back, and it yanked her off her feet. I turned and saw the pig man closing on Jack. Jack had already ripped the last flipper free and was scrambling up. Then I heard Toby slam into the side of the garage. One of the witches hanging from the tree was thrashing hard, sheets and striped socks whipping, and it had kicked him mid stride. The other ghosts and witches were moving too, nooses swinging.

I kicked the prop trick-or-treater until its hand opened and McKenzie’s ankle came free. Jack was already there. He grabbed her other hand. We made a break for the exit, the three of us, while the porch boards kept creaking behind us and Skylar’s voice cut through everything else.

“Help me, please!” Cried Skylar as the pig man places his boot on Skylar’s back to stab him with the pitchfork with a single blow.

“No!” Jack and I both yelled. Toby shoved between us and kept going. Jack braced toward Skylar to try to help, but I wrapped both arms around Jack and used my weight to drag him toward the gate. We ran down the middle of the street screaming. People came out of houses. 

My father’s arm out of nowhere hooked me hard enough to lift me off my feet.

Our dad was frantically trying to figure out what happened. He and my mom had been looking for us. Apparently I never heard them tell me to have my sister home by 8:30. The map never made it back into my pocket, and they used it to track us down. I wished they had found us sooner. Skylar was still at the house.

Toby’s dad was on patrol that night in the neighborhood and stepped in. None of the adults could believe what we were saying. When they went to the house, there was no jack-o’-lantern, no hanging ghosts or witches, no dead trick-or-treaters or farm animals, no pig man, and no Skylar. The yard was empty and overgrown, the same way it had always been.

They thought we had gone crazy. They accused us of eating laced candy. It didn’t matter what they believed. Skylar was still missing. That night turned into a neighborhood-wide search for Skylar. By the next day they were looking for two boys. Jack’s mom worked at the local diner on Halloween. When she got home early the next morning, he wasn’t there.

The rumor was that Jack felt so guilty about Skylar that he ran away or worse. I knew Jack. He wouldn’t leave his mother like that unless he thought he could still fix it. I think he went back to the house for Skylar. Whatever took Skylar was still there, and it took Jack too.

Halloween was never the same after that. Toby and I stopped hanging out. My sister may have forgotten the whole night. I never brought it up to her, even after we were both grown. A couple of years later I went back to the house. It wasn’t always empty. People moved in and out. For whatever reason, every Halloween the house sat vacant.

Halloween 1996, I was sitting on the curb across the street with a gas can between my feet. I was crying so hard I could barely see. I wanted to burn that place down. What stopped me was the thought that if I did, I might never see Skylar or Jack again.

A hand settled on my head. Fingers moved through my hair. I knew exactly that touch. Stacy knelt beside me. She was crying too. She pulled my head against her and held it there. For the first time in a long while I felt like I could breathe.

I married her. She was the only person I could tell about that night without someone looking at me as if  I was crazy. I owed her my life and I gave it to her. We stayed in my parents’ house. They retired onto cruise ships and treated the place like a hotel when they came through. We had a son, Francis. He was polite, careful, and smart. He brushed his teeth, ate his vegetables, and said his prayers every night. Sometimes I would slip out and call him Saint Francis. Every time I did, he wiped the tears from my face and thanked me for naming him after someone kind.

It’s now Halloween 2026. Francis loved this day the way I once had. He had a map of the streets he wanted, just like I did. Most of the old routes were quiet now. The kiddie park was now a parking lot with a trunk-or-treat and he still had a good time. Not once I heard the cursed words that haunted me for years.

We ended up on Moon Flower. The corner house was still overgrown and unkept. The single jack-o’-lantern was lit on the porch again. I froze. Stacy had to tug me forward. She saw it too, so it was real. My shock had left Francis alone at the gate. He was calling for us the way Toby once had, excited.

I felt the old panic start. Stacy placed her hand on my chest. The touch still worked. I could think.

“Come on, Mom! Come on, Dad! You have to see this,” he said.

“This is our last house, okay?” Stacy called back.

“I don’t think we should do this.”

“This is your son’s Halloween,” she said. “Do it for him. Do it for Jack.”

We walked in slowly. Stacy kept my hand the whole way. Francis ran ahead like he was in a museum, looking at everything. The same decorations that had lived in my head for years were still there. The tree. The hanging figures. The animals. The dolls on the ground. And the pig man with his pitchfork. I did not look away from him.

“Trick-or-treat,” Francis said at the door. He saw the bowl before he could ring the bell. King-size bars. The note on top read TAKE ONLY ONE.

I caught his wrist as he reached.

“You see the sign?”

He took one bar and held it up. “Here’s yours.”

“No,” I said. “That’s for you. This is your night.”

He nodded and put it in his bag. I checked the throne. The pig man had not moved. We walked out at a steady pace.

“Time to go home,” Stacy said. She squeezed my hand. I think she was telling me she was proud of me.

Francis took my other hand on the sidewalk. “I know you didn’t want to go in there, Dad. It was pretty scary, but it was worth it. Thank you!.”

I looked at the dark yard behind us, the gate was still open. The jack-o’-lantern still burned on the porch, and the pig man had not moved from the hay. Nothing had followed us out. Then I looked at Francis. He was watching me the way Jack used to, waiting to see if we were really done, the prized candy bar already safe in his bag. The streetlight caught the side of his face. He just looked like a kid who had gotten what he came for and was ready to go home.

“Anything for you kiddo. Happy Halloween.”
