I was eight years old when my family moved into that decrepit old cul-de-sac. Twelve when my father shot our next-door neighbor in the chest. I was the oldest out of my siblings to remember the day that it happened. The police made me tell them three times.

Once in the aftermath, amidst a blurry red and blue glow. Then again at the precinct. The third time I recounted all that I saw, it was while staring down my father’s bloodshot eyes from my perch upon a witness stand. They gave him thirty years. I never talked about what happened again after that.

Yet the details were burnt into the fronts of my eyes, a gnarled film strip lit by loud words and the flash of gunfire. I knew my father wasn’t violent, but he was known to shove back at the people who pushed him. A proud man with a solitary weakness, I should have never said a word.

“Allie-May!” The subtle heat of a quilted blanket made it hard for me to want and wake up. It was a Saturday morning in late December, and the remnant tatters of old Christmas decorations did little to harbor a holiday spirit now that the day itself was over.

I didn’t need to hear my name a second time, as a full house rarely allotted for any excess in resumed sleep. Unburdened feet stomped up and down a short flight of stairs meanwhile my younger brothers had started arguing over something in the basement.

I’d offer a short argument in the form of a frustrated groan, clenching my covers close before accepting that no one, not even my mother, was going to grant me the chance to seek shelter in so much as five more minutes of shut eye.

“Why do the walls have to be so fucking thin!” The words I offered were sharp, and my mother proved quick to chide me. “Language, Allie-May. I don’t need the school calling me because you’ve been teaching your siblings how to swear.” She kept her voice level, but I knew we were both starting the day set at each other’s throats.

Our relationship hasn’t been easy since everything that happened. It didn’t help that my mother internalized her daughter’s testimony as the reason she now had to work two jobs just to keep a roof over our heads. “Get dressed and come downstairs, I need your help with breakfast.”

After finally acquiescing, I rolled out from the tangled nest I’d weaved into the layers atop my ratty mattress. “And by help, I assume you mean doing it all by myself.” The remark was needlessly hostile. I was acting out because I’d spent the night struggling to get any sleep while both my little sisters showed signs of getting too old to share a bed.

Had I wanted to maintain this bratty attitude, I should’ve never turned and looked my mother in the face. It was there that I effortlessly saw a cruel reflection. She was just as exhausted as I was, though I couldn’t begin to discern the many overlapping reasons as to why.

So, I let my glare stagger then transition into a sheepish avoidance. “Just give me a minute, ma. I’ll be right down.” She’d nod while keeping her voice low. “Thanks, love.” I’d sit up in bed while the door clicked shut and the hurried progress of my mother’s feet fled back into the master bedroom.

I resisted the lure indulging bare limbs to quiver and pulled open the flowery curtains obfuscating a bedroom window. It was settled between both beds, overtop a rickety nightstand that could hold most things but was never allowed to host drinks.

An outward glance showcased the better part of our snow-capped cul-de-sac. Ten houses, constructed with a layout that left every two conjoined at the hip. A trio of oaks loomed large along the circular scrap of land settled at the center of the street, which coerced most vehicles into following a clockwise rotation if they ever found the inclination to leave.

It was the most any of us really had as a legitimate claim to being a part of any real community. We were situated along the furthermost corner of what had at one point been a promising neighborhood. I heard mom talk about it with an older lady three houses down.

Behind us was nothing but one hundred and twenty acres of wide-open fields, all mapped out with an express intent of being developed into the second half of a small-town suburbia. Hundreds more homes with several parks and recreational spaces, as well as a new community center and library. Old Missus Haggerty swore that it would have put our small town on the map.

But then the factories closed and the jobs that they promised were sent overseas. It stalled out construction and drove housing prices down. Which was the only reason why we even moved out here in the first place. My father was already struggling with finding employment back where we used to live, and had it set in his mind that so long as his family could live in a house that provided an ounce more comfort compared to the two-bedroom apartment we were crammed into, he had no problems commuting to other towns so long as they offered half decent work.

He’d take on the brunt while my mom found something that was part time. I’d watch after my siblings with the promise that it would get easier as they all got older. It would have still been tough work, but we would have been happier. This was before my father noticed that old extension cord.

Our house shared an odd layout with the one directly beside us, and perhaps there had been the intention to increase the size of our backyard so that it wrapped all the way around. Instead, the front was met with a cul-de-sac. The west side was enclosed with a six-foot fence.

An east side transitioned into the garage which then connected to our neighbor’s western wall, all while the backside of our home was left exposed to the large empty field. Just as well, no windows pointed out that direction. For all intents and purposes, it was just a flat wall with nothing to offer but a single, two-headed outlet.

That was where the problem started. The rest of our house was surprisingly secure. The backyard fence was both tall and excessively reinforced so to endure the high winds that often traveled over the surrounding plains. What trees we had were planted inside the fence line and had grown sturdy enough to provide decent coverage during the summer.

There was a gate, of course. But after the first incident, my father made a point of moving the latch down several feet so that way no one could easily reach over and open the gate from the outside. This meant that whenever me or any of my siblings wanted to go play in the tall grass, we couldn’t close the gate behind us otherwise we would essentially be forced to walk around to the front.

After the second incident, my father covered the eastern wall of the garage with several layers of sheet metal. After which, he lined a blockade of cinderblocks in front of it, then installed six heavy cabinets side by side and filled them with a lot of heavy, useless junk.

Our house was essentially L-shaped, with the longer side pointed out towards the field. The shorter end was protected by the backyard, which traded a quarter of its fence line with the back wall of our other neighbor’s garage. The fence would inevitably pick back up where the wall ended and corner a second augmented gate which exited out into the front yard.

Along the crux of an oblong shape sat a heavy, wooden front door. It had a small stoop with high walls which were peppered with the windows that peered into the boys’ and girls’ rooms. My brothers shared a small room in the basement, which made their windows too small to really open. But my sisters and I had the room right above them, along a second floor.

After the third incident, my dad had installed a four-foot-tall fence that outlined the outer edge of the stoop and capped it off with a strong metal gate that could only be locked from the inside. By the time he finished, our home was practically impenetrable.

For a time, it must have felt that way. My mom got a job at a small grocery store in town. They liked having pretty faces at the helm of each register and were happy to employ her part-time since it meant they didn’t have to give her full benefits.

My dad found a job at a refinery two towns away. He worked twelve-hour shifts but got to have three days off. We didn’t see him much throughout the week, but during the days he had off there was always an attempt to make up for lost time.

School wasn’t too far away so as we each became eligible to attend the appropriate grade, I became responsible for making sure all my siblings made it to and from class while my mom took the youngest to daycare.

Then the last incident occurred, and my father was arrested. A deeper breath clung like lead to the back of my throat, and I had to turn away from the window in order to recollect myself. I’d stand motionless inside a shared bedroom for a moment more before finally acquiring the capacity to get dressed then head downstairs.

The ground floor was a series of rooms connected by a roundabout path. The center of our house was nothing more than plaster walls and a built-in fireplace, and it was an ever constant that my mother had to chide my siblings for running in endless circles until something broke due to them being dizzy.

Toys littered the floor from where a prior interest had been summarily lost, and I had to watch my feet so as to not impale a bare sole on an abandoned building block or diecast car. The television downstairs as well as the one in our living room was on, and their contrasting volume drowned my ears beneath the sounds of violent video games and Saturday morning cartoons.

Frost bathed the windows and an overworked heater plied what cold it could manage from out the crisp morning air. Everyone was awake and causing each their own specific brand of chaos, from both my brothers still arguing downstairs about being too close meanwhile my sisters complained about the other being too far while playing separate games in the playroom.

The youngest sat upright on the couch, that thousand-yard stare pointed directly at the tv while she chewed the rubber buttons off the remote without thinking. Any attempt made to turn off her show would be met with immediate violence, despite her having the most adorable face.

I pointed myself towards the kitchen, which was often overcrowded even when no one was in it. I pulled two plastic bowls from out the running dishwasher and washed a few more off within the sink. Then I pulled the only box of cereal out from the cupboard, noticing far too late that we were out of milk again.

My mom was already bounding down the stairs as I contemplated substituting milk with water again. And though a prior interaction had placed us on opposite sides of a constant brewing argument, she still pulled me into a passing hug and pecked the top of my head. “I know, I know. I’ll grab a gallon after work.”

She was telepathic, I wondered if I’d also adopt such powers if I ever decided to have my own kids. It wasn’t likely, but I also wasn’t sure if there was a price too high for x-ray vision. “Can you grab something for dinner too? The pantry’s practically empty.” I posed the question without an ounce of hostility.

“Of course, love. We just need to make it two more days and then we’ll have more food stamps.”  She’d fill up a thermos using the lukewarm kettle on the stove, then grace me with a particularly sobering mention. “I’ll be taking everyone to go see their dad tomorrow. We have to leave early, so maybe that’ll let you sleep in?”

She was offering me restitution for what was already a sour deal. She’d taken my siblings to go see dad twice every month since he was transferred to prison. I was never allowed to go. Which made sense, I broke his heart by telling the truth. I would live with that sin for the rest of my life.

Without matching my mother’s eyes, I begin placing bowls of dry cereal along each seat of a kitchen table. “So, I’m still not allowed to come?” My throat was taut. I wanted to beg her to bring me along. I wanted to tell my dad that I was sorry. That it was suffocating to know he refused to see me.

“Not yet.” My mother replied. She held no malice, but I could tell that she felt the same way as my father. I tilted my chin. “Okay.” It wasn’t so much agreement as it was a curt affirmation. I wouldn’t see him tomorrow. Perhaps ever.

Tomorrow heralded the first full year since last he’d called me his little girl. It was the night before that final incident. Before I put my own father in prison for what could be the rest of his life. I should feel lucky that I was merely barred from joining the rest of my family in going to see him. Had my mother possessed the capacity, she could have disowned me for what I’d done.

It all started with that very first incident. The incredulous looks upon both my parents’ faces as they stared at the number boldly stated on a sleek utility bill. Nearly a thousand dollars. They struck down harshly after cobbling together enough money to cover the cost.

At first, there were limited showers. No flushing overnight. The heater could only run for an hour at the start and end of each day, then came the bill a following month. Again, a hearty grand. No more hot water. We had to ask permission first before turning on a light. Every electronic was unplugged when not in use. We spent the second month of winter without any heat.

Then the third month. Another grand. My dad was busy unscrewing the bulbs from every conceivable source. This brought him outside, along a broad side. This was where he found an extension cord sticking out from the exposed plug along the back of our house.

The first day, he unplugged the cord then bought locks for the outlet. The next morning, he found them chewed through with wire cutters and that damned cord plugged back in. The second day, my dad ripped out the entire outlet. The very next morning, he found a new one installed.

On the third day, my father ripped out the outlet, buried the wires, and bathed the hole with liquid cement. He cut a two by four to fit the shape of an exposed wound and screwed it into the wall, covering it as well with an excess of expensive sealant.

After all that, he located the proper switch along the circuit breaker in the garage which cut off power to the outlet. As soon as he flipped it, nothing else happened. And when the bill came in the following month, an otherwise massive toll had been reduced to barely a hundred dollars.

My father kept an eye on the back wall after that. He checked it almost every single day like clockwork when he came home from work. The rest of that winter went without issue. The summer was a lull. Then came a full year since moving in.

One morning, mid-December, my father woke us up while throwing a fit. Utilities were nearly a grand again, but after checking, there was nothing installed out back. A second month brought us all back to just how bad it had gotten the year before, but my father didn’t blame us.

It wasn’t until February that he discovered a new source, only this time, it brought with it a host of hostility aimed at our connected neighbor. While scouring the garage, my father found a hole drilled directly through the east wall. Wide enough for a man to crawl through.

An extension cord was threaded through the hole and plugged into one of the outlets inside the garage. The hole would then be blocked using the same piece of drywall that had been cut out. My father had words with our next-door neighbor. But I don’t think the man agreed to stop. My dad then buried the far wall of the garage behind a mess of different things. And for the rest of the winter, everything returned to normal.

The third winter was much more direct. But it barely affected the utility bill this time. My father would quickly notice what was happening because each time it was aimed at him. Throughout the day my mom would keep watch, but it was only at night whenever my dad would return home that he’d find an extension cord plugged into the outlet beside our front door.

He’d argue with our neighbor more and more as the months wore on. It was like clockwork before my dad finally installed the gate, yet the hostility carried itself well into the summer. We weren’t allowed to play out front anymore, and both my mother and father agreed that it would be best to stop plugging holes and instead set up several cameras.

Calls made to the police were useless, given it was all hearsay and my parents lacked any legitimate proof. My father started talking about looking for another line of work, something that could keep him at home even if it cost him a substantial pay cut.

My mom was all but willing to take on additional hours and become full time at the supermarket should it help. But before any real plans could be followed through, the final incident occurred and my father pulled that damned trigger.

I’d call up my siblings for breakfast and watch in silence as my mother left for work. It was around this time that the snow began falling again. I cleaned up the table after everyone ate what all they wanted, then I went about cleaning up the house while negotiating every argument with the threat of telling and getting each one of them grounded.

I’d watch television with our youngest, omit to playing dress up with the twins. My brothers were far less agreeable with me being the one left in charge, but they’d calm down after I relegated myself to getting beaten over and over throughout subsequent matches of couch co-op.

The day was proving longer with every inch of falling snow, but after emptying the dishwasher and preheating the oven for whatever mom brought home for dinner, the constant chaos came to a head once we all noticed that loud set of headlights brighten the frostbitten face of our living room window.

I’d have my ass chewed out for being too stern, I surmised. My siblings were not without want to hold onto every grudge given my firm sense of judgement sourced itself from the image I had of our father. The snow was still falling, only slower now. The moment our mother entered through the front door, entirely exhausted, I knew it was as good a time as any to breathe.

While she was assaulted with questions and praise, I headed back into the kitchen. The oven kept the short space warm, and it helped with easing what cold found leverage throughout the odd cracks lacing our aged house. Eventually, my mother entered the room, noticing my presence, and merely offered me both a gallon of milk and frozen slab of prepared lasagna.

It would be another two hours before we could be fed. Another late night but perhaps this was what my mother wanted. I’d shed the family sized meal of its cardboard packaging then place it inside a hot oven. My siblings were earnest in their resolute displays of affection as well as countless accusations aimed in my direction.

My defense was that I proved stern, yet understandable given an inquired context. It wasn’t my fault that I wasn’t allotted any respect given the responsibility I’d been tasked with. My mother disapproved, as always, and told me to be gentler with my siblings before excusing herself from the ensuing situation.

She would go take a shower while I persisted in wrangling my siblings and preparing them for bed. It was hard enough convincing my brothers and sisters that there was a necessity in sleep. The night grew long as dinner was cooked, and it was only by a miracle that I’d situated everyone into a set of clean pajamas before the oven timer went off.

Twenty minutes were then spent setting the table. It was an excuse I’d employed so to let the lasagna cool well enough that I wouldn’t be asked a thousand times over to blow on somebody’s food. I was spent just as soon as our mother came out from the shower. She adorned her own nightgown before joining the rest of us to eat.

Like pigs to a trough, some stuffed their faces while others spent the better part of their meal asking for the specifics in terms of ingredients. It was some shared form of child politics. Never mention onions, garlic or spice. Ensure the heat was because it had just come out of the oven, remind the twins that they’ve always loved cheese.

Then came the madcap brushing of teeth while I cleaned up the table. A moment was spent rinsing all the plates before packing full the dishwasher and turning it on. There were still a handful of dishes that’d need cleaning in the morning.

“Thank you, love.” My mother’s expression of affection had become more than merely robotic. I wasn’t stupid, just lonely and sad. She’d take the youngest and recede into the master bedroom. She’d watch an episode or two of her show before passing out amidst the act of feeding my littlest sister.

As for the others, they fought, much like always. Bets were made to try and switch beds, but I never relented. I refused to ever allow one of my brothers to lie in bed with a sister. They would always fight, no matter how much they swore to do the opposite.

After putting every last sibling to rest, I’d then wait until the inevitable calls for my mother surfaced. They always waited until they assumed I was asleep before overruling my decision with doe eyes aimed at our mother. I’d respond and they’d give up. I was exhausted. This kept up until nearly midnight.

Then the final mouth proved wide open amidst the loudest yawn. I stood in the living room with groggy eyes. I’d think the same thing that I always did, that I couldn’t keep this up. I’d always change my mind come morning.

A short sweep of our home had me tightening the latches of any open windows. And while yes, it had been snowing for most of the day, it was still easy for my siblings to presume a change in temperature as being mandatory.

Then I shut off the needless lights. I left the televisions on inside my siblings’ rooms. They wouldn’t go to sleep otherwise. Every other fixture was put out, except for the strategic selection of an overhead kitchen sink and stain-glassed living room lamp.

I shut the front porch light off and locked both sliding glass doors. The storm was ugly, yet it wasn’t until I traveled downstairs to make sure my brothers turned off their own light that I heard a careful knocking. This perked up my ears, given the location of such sound.

Three walls could reassure me with several windows, but the repetition sourced itself from the other side a featureless backside. I couldn’t comprehend why there was any amount of knocking from opposite a wall that staired out into a field, but I grabbed my mom’s coat anyway, wrapping it around my slender body before heading out into the storm.

Thick flakes stuck to the front of a sliding glass door, a harsh frost creeping up the finger-stained glass. The world outside was much different from the temporary calm I’d left behind, heavy snow bowed the dead branches belonging to both the trees looming high above me.

I left a purposeful imprint along the untouched snow as I crossed the short distance leading from the backdoor to a locked gate. I considered the ramifications before unlatching the metal lock. The door swung open and I was met with an endless contention. A sea of snow below the jutting void of pitch-black night.

The world was much larger from this perspective, and I’ve never felt so small. I clung to the side of the house as I looked to identify where the knocking had come from. Halfway down the snow spackled wall, a hole had been produced. I noticed it only after having spotted the soft orange glow that persisted far off amongst the tall grass.

A thick, yellow extension cord. The outlet my father had buried was seemingly reinstalled and working. I stared out at the glow before glancing towards the nearby fence line belonging to our neighbor. A pair of eyes watched from overtop the worn wooden planks. Something inside me grew sick.

But I didn’t run. Instead, I turned and approached the glow. It wasn’t too far out for me to prove frantic, yet it still stuck itself behind the weight of a frost tipped expanse of untrimmed overgrowth. As soon as I realized what the thing was, I could only stare in utter amazement.

A simple space heater. Stuck out along the snow. It lacked a purpose in this place, and perhaps so did I. I didn’t hesitate, however. With one foot, I toppled the heater over before rushing back to the wall. I wrenched the cord free from its socket, then threaded the thing as my dad taught me between both thumb and elbow.

I was shaking, but not from the cold. The curse was harrowing, it was persistent. I knew not to act meant allowing what remained of my family to suffer the fate my dad fought tooth and nail against. It was in that moment I steadied myself against tears. He had only been trying to protect us, what was my reason for wanting to destroy that?

I kept the cord but locked all the doors. I didn’t sleep at all that night. Instead, I stood in vigil. Settled along the bottom step leading both up and down into a trio of occupied rooms. I listened closely until the storm passed and a cacophony of birds chose to punctuate the dawn with a simple mating song.

The youngest proved first to wake up. She next brought my mother to stand within the threshold of her bedroom door. “Allie-May?” Her voice was both hoarse and restless. She could probably never find sleep so long as my sibling clung close to her breast.

I stared back at her with puffy red eyes. “Please, let me see dad.” The question alone brought her resolute figure to falter. “Alright.” We traded places and I found the time to drift headlong into maybe three hours of sleep. My mother didn’t betray me. She woke me up when it was time to leave.

Perhaps it was foolish to not try and tell her what I found the previous night, but I held close the worse experience right up until we all entered one after another into a cold sterile room. My father sat empty along a chair in opposition to our approach. At first his features glowed, until his eyes fell over me.

“Allie-May.” I winced as he whispered my name. I didn’t try to say anything because my intent was not to ruin this visit with my presence alone. He noticed and was grateful, my siblings got to engage with him much like they all had before.

It hurt my heart to see him showcase a love so voracious towards every one of my siblings. I blinked away tears and faked a smile before finally overhearing that a visiting hour was nearly over. This was progress, I’d cope, before hearing something that froze me in my step. “Allie-May, wait.”

I’d turn back towards my father, still feeling my mom gripping one of my arms. “Let me talk to her, alone.” A burdensome grip would relent and with what few moments remained for us to speak, I found myself standing alone before my father.

“I didn’t mean to hurt you…” I’d start, but the man merely hugged me. His hold was so warm, and it broke me down entirely. “I know.” That should’ve been it. I should’ve been happy knowing that my father didn’t blame me, but then the man chose to keep speaking.

“I’m sorry I couldn’t protect you.” His broad form shook as he emptied those words from out a spoiled belly. I stared up at him with curious eyes. “Protect me?” The offer of ignorance shook the man where he stood. “You still don’t remember.” What does that mean?

I applied a mere bit of distance. “Remember what?” It was an innocent ask, one which charred the words as they spilled forth from my father’s mouth. “The day I shot Harold. You were still in shock.” Parts of a bridled memory kept me clinging to the act of not knowing.

“There was never any reason, the bastard simply hated us. He liked his solitude, and we took that by moving into our home.” With effort, I steadied my chin. “He wanted us gone.” This was true, my father nodded. “And we didn’t make it easy. He tried burying us in debt, plugging in a heater then leaving it on full blast behind our home.”

I nodded. “But you stopped that.” This was also true. “Then he tried doing something similar by cutting a hole leading into our garage.” Once more, I nodded. “You stopped that too.” My father’s frame shook. “Do you remember what happened the following winter?” His voice was as fragile as brittle. “He took advantage of the outlet set right beside the front door.”

“And the winter following that?” This time I froze. The final incident. “He came into our home.” The words he spoke offered me to clasp at quivering limbs. “You shot him because he wouldn’t leave us alone.” My father shook his head. “I didn’t shoot him because he refused to stop siphoning our power. I shot him because he assaulted my little girl.”

Something clicked in me just then, and I remembered the night everything fell apart. After all my dad did to try and keep our neighbor from kicking us out, the man managed to climb in through the unlocked window settled between a pair of twin beds.

He plugged in that damned extension cord but didn’t leave when he should’ve. Instead, he saw me curled up, fast asleep. The man did what he wanted because there was ample opportunity, I simply refused to remember because it was far easier to forget.

Then my dad found me, wrists bound tight with a thick extension cord. He was supposed to carry me outside so to meet with the approaching ambulance. But our neighbor stood along his front porch with a smile pasted upon his leathered face.

What I remembered was my dad going back inside to grab his gun. In the ensuing argument, he shot our neighbor in the chest. This brought more than one ambulance as well as the police. “But they didn’t believe me.” I was never even checked. Instead, they took my impaired word for it, and now my father was in prison.

I damned the wrong man to an unrelenting penance. Of course he wouldn’t have wanted to speak with me. He must have told my mother, his wife the truth. Hence why she herself hadn’t forgiven me. Time lapsed before I could pry further answers from my own father. My mother was accompanied by a prison guard while she came back out to drag me away.

I should’ve said something more, but I was still shocked. My father simply dipped his chin before relenting to the other officers that led him away. I knew the whole truth now, and it was nearly enough to make me lame. I blamed myself in lieu of everything. It was my fault things went the way that they did.

Next thing I knew, I was back home with the rest of my siblings, our mother choosing to take the rest of the off day so to watch television in her own room. A back and forth followed while I struggled to keep saving face. Did any of them know the truth? I was too afraid to ask my own mother.

Instead, several hours passed and I was inevitably met with another long night. The storm was over, but the inches it built left large drifts looming outside our home. A collective effort had been made by other neighbors to keep the street out front all clear, meanwhile the field behind us succumbed to the moonlight and left a blanket of snow wildly aglow.

I didn’t sleep again. My head was far too busy to want and find a moment’s rest. I sat in silence while the rest of my kin proved busy in their snores. It wasn’t until the early morning that I heard a knock again. I was more aware now. Our neighbor hadn’t died. He was shot in the chest, of course, but he’d only returned to his home less than a month ago. I could see his front porch light from where I sat.

But I didn’t remain seated for long. As soon as I heard that clear commotion, I tacked on a coat and slippers then left through the backyard. I opened the gate then ran towards the outlet, that same cord had been plugged in. There was that same orange glow out amidst a full faced moon.

But what I hadn’t been prepared for was the escalation. Something hard hit the back of my head, and I collapsed in a heap amongst the snow. Heat kissed at the front of my chin, but I didn’t stir as the blackness took me.

I didn’t know how much time had passed, only that my head hurt and I was freezing. My bare hands slipped amongst the ice accumulating outside the breadth of a fiery glow, but I knew that my attempted death wasn’t meant to be the only.

I spilled over onto my side and saw the lingering shadow brought on by an ample glow leading out into the backyard. The lights inside were on. I staggered to both feet without noticing the blood trickling down the back of my neck as each drop leapt towards the snow.

There weren’t screams as I was expecting, just sorrowful voices as I stumbled beyond the threshold of a sliding glass door. The damned thing had been left wide open, an unlatched gate having left our neighbor with the quickest route inside.

I already knew that it would be within his best interests to head directly upstairs. I stole a short detour towards the kitchen, grabbing a butter knife from out the closest drawer. Then I followed the water left behind by warmed footprints.

It should’ve ended with me. My mind raced with the sort of exceptions strewn from the idea that my suffering was all that needed to be elicited. Yet I noticed the melted path leading from the top of the stairs into my mother’s room.

He knew my father wasn’t here. I kicked myself for being so weak. I slipped beyond the cracked door and noticed the shadow of a man standing before my mother’s bed. She was sobbing, clutching our youngest to her chest while our neighbor brandished his own vicious firearm.  

Without pause, I lunged at the bastard. He heard me coming and twisted his posture. His gun went off just as soon as I buried the full length of a butter knife into the side of his neck. Crimson bloomed along the belly of my snow spattered shirt.

The man dropped whilst contending with the effective severing of a blatant artery. I curled onto my back because for god’s sake, I was twelve, the gunshot crippled me. Panic was quick to strew itself across the room just as soon as my mother leapt off the bed. Our other neighbors heard the gunshot and were already dialing the police.

It was impossible not to hear the gurgle as our neighbor flailed along the floor. He proved impatient in dragging the knife from out his neck. This would prove the death of him. Meanwhile, I sobbed into the chest of a paternal figure. The pain was prominent, and like a child I wailed for momma.

I wasn’t expecting to pass out from the trauma, but perhaps it was better that I did. I awoke, later that morning, in a hospital surrounded by my family. Each sibling was present. My mother was still in shock. It didn’t take too long before the investigation brought better details to light and my father’s sentence was reduced.

We never found anything else plugged in throughout the years following everything that happened. I was twenty-three when my father was finally released. The house beside us found itself condemned in the years after our neighbor breathed his last, but despite his lasting absence, I still spent every night doing the rounds throughout our home.

The day my father walked through the front door, he spent the subsequent moments hugging and kissing every one of my siblings. I held back while my mother also got her share of affection. It was only after everyone else sought to pull a decent reunion into the living room that I found the briefest moment to meet my dad eye to eye.

“I kept them safe.” I simply smiled, owing to the sudden influx of gentle tears as my father fought to stifle the mess reaching along his flustered face. “That’s my little girl.” He stuttered, before dragging me into a hug.
