“You seen him?”

​

Her voice barely registered with me for a moment as I tried to sort the keys in my hand. Car, mailbox, storage, laundry, work- what time is it?

​

“You seen him?” Midge asks again, a bit louder and rougher.

​

I jump, my hands squeezing tightly and letting the brass dig into my skin. I had taken an extra half shift and got home at 3 AM. God knows exactly why I’m awake, but why was she? The hunched, short, older lady with loud makeup reached forward and handed me a flyer, her eyes narrowed in an annoyed squint as she waited a second too long for me to take it.

​

“I- uh-” I took it, glanced at a picture of an orange cat, and shook my head. “Haven’t seen Oscar yet, sorry, Midge.”

​

“Randy.”

​

“Pardon?”

​

“Randy.”

​

I looked at the flyer in my hand and realized the poorly lit orange was instead a gray-brown.

​

“How long has it been?” I asked her, trying to make light conversation between distant noises in the disgustingly damp hallway. She looked at me again, still annoyed, as if I should have known how long it had been since her cat went missing. All it says on the flyer is his name and that there would be a reward, not even how much the reward would be.

​

“Three days.”

​

“Three-” I was taken aback. “And you’re just now making the flyers?” I asked jokingly, rubbing my tired face. Her frown turned into a well-deserved glare, and I folded the piece of paper, tucking it under my arm as I pursed my lips and nodded. “I’m sure Randy and Oscar are gonna turn up soon; they’re probably getting into trouble with some neighborhood- you know- cats and-"

​

The glare worsened.

​

“I’ll keep an eye out.” I finished.

​

Midge grunted and then turned to walk away from me, leaving the flyers on the floor mats of every apartment she passed. I watched as she made her way down the dimly lit and poorly carpeted hall, passing through the dark spot where the lights had died, cursing as she bumped into something, and then entering the light again on the other side to turn the corner. The lamp that hung above buzzed and flickered; every few seconds the darkness expanded another few feet before lighting back up.  

​

I pushed into my apartment, making a mental note to try and tell the landlord about the growing dark, but realized that- if he didn’t care the first five times, he probably wouldn’t care the sixth. Carefully, I slid my keys and the flyer across the countertop, trying to keep as quiet as I could to avoid waking my sleeping wife and daughter. I grabbed a can of soda from the fridge and sank back into the living room sofa, taking a long cool sip before handing it to my daughter so she could take one-

​

“Fu-” the swear word almost made it past my filter. My daughter, bright blues ringed with tired purple, looked up at me with an eager grin. I grinned back at her. “What are you doing awake?”

​

“I heard him again!” the seven-year-old squealed in a whisper, taking another sip of cola before I gently snatched it out of her hands. That was already too much sugar and caffeine at 3 am. And it was mine.

​

“Why aren’t you in bed?” I scanned the apartment, only now realizing the lack of snoring sounds. “Where’s mom?”

​

“At work, but dad- dad, I heard him again! He sounds closer-”

​

“Dang it-” I finally read the text messages on my phone from hours ago, telling me that she had to rush off to work due to an ER emergency at work. Lynn, the little monster, was supposed to be dead asleep. I forgot to inform her that I had to take another half shift- she’d kill me if she found out. “Wait, Lynn, when did you wake up?”

​

She smiled up at me.

​

“You stayed awake.”

​

She nodded. “I wanted to wait for Mr. Owl!” She dragged her sentence out.

​

“But you need to try to sleep on time.” I dragged mine beside her.

​

“But dad! I heard him really close this time! I wanna see!”

​

“I don’t hear any-”

​

On cue, the unmistakable muffled call of a great horned owl echoed into the night, followed by a jump and a gasp from Lynn as she raced to the window, eyes immediately in her binoculars, breath already fogging up the glass. She was right; it was closer.

​

The first few nights we heard the owl, I stayed up with her. We listened, and there it was. Somewhere in the treeline beyond the apartment buildings came a quiet hoo-hoo-hoooh of a horned owl. She grabbed her binoculars and sat at the balcony window until midnight before she fell asleep, and I carried her off to bed. As the calls steadily became louder, I assumed the owl had made its way over to some of the overhang roofs of the apartment complex to hunt for the mice- a job usually left to the, what I assume to be, dozen cats belonging to Midge.  

​

Lynn’s restlessness grew with the increasing volume, always complaining that she could hear the bird, but never see it. I told her that it’s just how it is. The harder you look for the owl, the more he hides himself. You’ll never be able to find it, and maybe he just doesn’t want to be seen. My attempt at deterring her had the opposite effect, though, and instead became a challenge. Her summer nights were now campouts in the living room with the windows open, searching the woods for her Mr. Owl.

​

I was afraid the neighbors would complain, but as usual, everyone kept to themselves except for the occasional grunt and side-eye. August rolled into September, and her goal was to see the thing before school would start again. With it starting a week away, my goal was to get her sleep schedule back on track so she’d be ready to wake at 6 in the morning instead of crawling out of bed at 10. Like her father.

​

She was right, though; this might be her last chance to see it, and it sounded close enough that-



“- It could be on the roof…”

​

Lynn’s mouth dropped open, and I cursed under my breath, realizing I just said the quiet part out loud. A few days ago, we filed a complaint about the foul smell coming from the air vents, to which the landlord threw me the keys to the roof and told me to “look for yourself.” I eyed the keys that hung by the door before I smiled back at her.

​

“You gonna tell mom?”

​

She shook her head no.

​

“Okay, but  you have to promise to go to bed on time for the rest of the week- Lynn!”

​

“I promise!” Her tiny legs were already rushing off to slip on her shoes and grab her camera, popping the lid off to check if she had batteries and her memory card inside. She gave me the thumbs up, and I returned it.

​

Outside in the hallway, the dark spot had only grown. The bulb around the corner had broken, and let the black seep further around the bend. Lynn hated the dark spot. I picked her up into my arms and carried her through, feeling the exhaustion in her heavy breaths. She smiled at me when she noticed me looking at her sleepy-lidded blues.

​

“I’ll go to bed after, I promise.”

​

I set her down beside me when we reached the door to the roof, putting a finger to my mouth to shush her. We wouldn’t want to scare off Mr. Owl. As carefully as we could, we unlocked the first door and climbed the small set of stairs, then unlocked the second door, propping it open with a piece of wood, and peered out onto the roof. Lynn forced her eyes open and gripped her camera tight in anticipation. The two of us tiptoed a few steps out of the door, heads swiveling in opposite directions to search for the bird.

​

Lynn caught the smell before I did. Her nose scrunched up, and she rushed to cover her mouth with her hand, tugging on my shirt with the other. I responded with a quick “hmm?” before I did the same, almost gagging at the disgusting air that was fighting to invade our senses.

​

“Lynn, go inside-” I broke the silence, patting the side of my work pants for a flashlight I knew I had hooked on somewhere.

​

“Daddy, but Mr. Owl-”

​

“Lynn, no- hold on, something isn’t right-” I clicked on the light and began to scan across the roof, trying to keep Lynn between me and the open door.

​

Trails of brown and red tore across the ground; some shone bright red in the weak light of my flashlight, some brown, long dried, and some green. Slowly, my light lit up more. White and pink, torn and broken bone and muscle, snapped and splinted. Some small, some connected to fingers, hands, paws, and tails. Patchy matte spots of orange, black, and gray. Oscar, Randy, and so many more. Then my light reflected a gray-yellow, a curved talon as it tapped loudly on the concrete, and immediately I clicked off my light.

​

Hoo-hoo-hoooooh.

​

Clouds cleared just enough for faint moonlight to break through, lighting up the edge of the roof. A hunched man, large red-stained gray feathers draped on him like a coat. His naked, thin, branch-like legs pulled to his chest as he sat in the fetal position at the edge. Each foot ended in 5 long, bony talons that painfully broke through his skin like toes through a torn sock. His eyes reflected red and white moonlight as he did a quick sweep of the roof, not noticing us at first glance. The feathers that lined his body left his human face untouched. Each quill looked as if it was painfully forced underneath the skin.

​

His head swiveled back into position, and then he opened his mouth as wide as he could, skin stretching and cracking as the skin on his face split. Then came the sound.

​

Hoo-hoo-hoooh. Hoo-hoo-hoooo-

​

Flash- snap-

​

His head cracked towards us. Lynn held her camera in her shaking hands and quickly looked up at me, panic quivering through her small face. As quickly as I could move, I picked her up and stepped back into the stairwell, kicking out the prop and pulling the door shut behind me. Sharp boned talons dug into my hand and pinned me to the door before it could close, but the weight of the owl slamming against it sent me flying. The talon tore through my palm and out fingers as I fell backwards down the steps and through the first door. I held onto Lynn as tight as I could, taking the impact for both of us. Her small, tear-filled blue eyes bulged wide as I moved her hair out of her face, now staining her with the red from my torn-open hand.

​

At the top of the stairs, the second door began to splinter, cheap wood and rusted metal hinges cracking as the owls' call twisted into a furious gasp for air.

​

“Go back to the apartment- go, go!” I shoved her inside and slammed the first door shut.

​

“Dad, your hand-”

​

“I’m okay- I’m okay, let's go, come on-” I lied, but the adrenaline was still enough to bear the pain. I scooped her back up in my arms and pushed down the hallway, breaking into the darkness. Someone opened their door behind me, poking their head out to curse and ask what the hell was all the noise, but then the second door began to shake and rattle.

​

“What the hell-” The man grumbled. Turning for a second before we stepped into the dark spot, I  shouted for the man to get back into his apartment, but he brushed me off, going instead to the roof access door. He never made it. The hinges cracked against the weak drywall and forced the entire door off, slamming against the man and pinning him against the wall. I only stayed long enough to see bone and talon splinter through the door and into the man’s shoulder before I kept going; the high-pitched screams and tearing told me enough.

​

I slid Lynn out of my arms as I patted myself down in front of the apartment door.



“Dad-”

​

Keys, where are the keys? I slid my hands into my pants pockets and recoiled in pain as my hand began to split apart more, torn down the middle and ring finger.

​

“Dad-”

​

The adrenaline was nowhere near enough to keep the pain away this time, and I struggled to realize that I had dropped the keys somewhere outside in the rush. I cursed to myself as I bent over to pick Lynn back up. I’d take her and run down the stairs; outside we could-

​

“Dad.”

​

She had stopped crying, stopped moving, and the carnage had grown silent. There was something past the faint ring of pain. The loud breathing between Lynn and me, and the sound of the other residents beginning to wake, stir, curse, and wonder what the hell was going on. Then, beyond that, more faint than it should be, was the owl.

​

Hoo-hoo-hooooh.

​

Two dots watched us from the dark spot of the hallway. White turned red and white again, reflecting off whatever faint light it could catch. It hovered low to the ground before slowly lifting to her height.  

​

Hoo-hoo-hooooh.

​

The sound of the stirring residents stirred something in the owl too. Like statues, we froze, flight or fight gnawing at both of us. It didn’t want to be seen, and I didn’t want to see it. My injured hand reached down for my daughter’s, but she wasn’t there.

​

“I’m sorry, Mr. Owl." Lynn’s voice quivered, taking slow steps towards him. I reached out and grabbed her shoulder, spilling red onto her from my open wound, but she slid from my grip. The owl growled, keeping me still as I fell forward to my knees. Lynn kept going, one foot in front of the other.

​

“I’m sorry, I shouldn’t have tried to-” She slowly lifted the camera towards it, and it screeched. The pale human face extended from the shadow, its lack of a neck now noticeable as its head seemed to extend straight from its shoulders that stayed hidden in the shadows. My daughter flinched, but kept her hands extended. “I shouldn’t have tried to look for you.”

​

She was only a foot away from it, camera raised up to its mouth. The owl's lips peeled backwards, extending out black and red human gums that broke and oozed a disgusting yellow. Then underneath, the black and white shine of a bony beak cut its way loose from the skin, taking the camera between it, and crushing it in a deafening snap.

​

Then it opened its wings. Pale arms, skin stretched and punctured with endless quills. Each pinned point, another eye glaring at the two of us. The light above Lynn gave out, and covered her in the growing black.  

​

I lunged forward as the plastic camera hit the ground, grabbing my daughter in my arms and quickly covering her with my body. The owl took off with a thunderous beat of its wings, its taloned feet knocking me across the head as it escaped back down the hall. My vision went red, then burst with bright and disgusting static, then, like the dark around us, black.

​

Lynn’s cries were the first sound I heard when I woke up, followed by the loud sound of sirens outside, and the panicked voice of my wife being questioned in the room. I brushed the tears from her red-stained face and held her close as I tried to sit up. Immediately, I was set down again by paramedics that I didn’t see.

​

The police found about half a dozen dead cats on the roof, their bodies mixed in with the remains of some local man who we didn’t know. Whatever did the deed skinned the man alive.

​

Or- whoever. The local investigation decided a local junkie was the likely main suspect. They probably hid up on the roof, high out of their mind, and fed on cats. When cats weren’t enough, they took a human victim. The neighbor who got attacked refused to give much testimony; I don’t think he ever actually saw the thing anyway. Lynn and I decided that it was probably better not to go off about some owl monster. She picked up on the look on my face immediately, the smart girl, and just said she didn’t see the man either.

​

I suffered a concussion, so that was that on witness testimony.

​

We moved quickly after that, further into the city to avoid any woods or wildlife in general. No grouped trees, no old sheds, no woodline. It wasn’t a bad idea; we were closer to our jobs, and Lynn was closer to school. The doctors managed to sew my hand back together, with only minor nerve damage and occasional joint pain, but it could have been much worse.



Lynn’s a smart girl. She tried to push past the incident, but I could see how it swam in her mind behind her bright blues. She was less adventurous, wondered less, wandered less. Some days she’d just sit in her room for hours and stare at the wall, breathing heavily as she replayed the night in her mind. When I find her like that, I sit with her, and we both try to forget together.

​

Even though our apartment is now constantly flooded with the faint lights from the street outside and our ears peppered with passing cars, every so often I still hear the faint sounds of an owl somewhere outside. I shut the windows, close the blinds. Lynn knows not to look, but one night, as I sat awake in the living room after work, ears perked up to the sound of a distant hoo, Lynn came to me, tired, dark rings around her eyes-

​

And an SD card in her hands.
