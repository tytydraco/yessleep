( I first heard this story during an interview for my thesis on convenience store workers. I wanted to understand their working conditions, especially on the night shift. I got more than I bargained for) 

The first few weeks at the convenience store were pretty ordinary.

I worked the midnight shift at a 24-hour store, mostly alone. I'd clean the counters, restock the shelves, check the refrigerators, and serve the occasional customer who came in for cigarettes or something to drink.

It was my first proper job after months of searching. I'd handed out applications, visited one establishment after another, and heard enough promises of callbacks to last a lifetime. When the store finally hired me, I didn't care about the inconvenient hours. I was just happy to have work.

Eventually, I got used to the quiet. I also got used to the regular customers.

One of them was an older man who came in almost every morning to buy cigarettes before driving to work in the city. He was never particularly friendly. He rarely smiled, barely spoke, and always seemed to be in a hurry.

For months, I made it my little personal challenge to get a smile out of him.

One morning, I finally managed it.

I saw his car pulling in and prepared his usual cigarettes before he even reached the counter. I scanned them and placed them beside the register.

He stopped when he saw them.

Then he smiled.

"Ah, you remember," he said.

I laughed. "You're here almost every morning. How could I forget?"

He paid, but instead of leaving, he looked around the empty store.

"Do you ever get scared working here at night?" he asked. "You're young. Not safe to be alone."

I told him that sometimes I did, but we had security cameras and could lock the doors if anything happened.

He nodded.

Then he told me about his younger years. Like many people from the provinces, he had gone to Manila in search of work and a better life. Sometime in the late eighties or early nineties, he found a job as a gasoline pump attendant. He was assigned to the midnight shift.

One night, he fell asleep while on duty.

When he woke up, his manager was shaking him violently. The telephone had apparently been ringing for hours.

He apologized for falling asleep, but his manager wasn't interested in that.

"Look outside!" he shouted.

Almost all their motor oil had been stolen. The place was a mess. Thieves had come during the night, taken whatever they could carry, and left without waking him.

They had walked around the station while he slept. They had even passed right by him.

But they weren't finished.

After robbing his station, the thieves drove down the road to another gas station.

The attendant there was awake when they arrived. He tried to stop them and fought back.

They stabbed him.

He died from his injuries.

The old man fell silent. I remember the refrigerator humming behind me and the headlights of a passing vehicle briefly sweeping across the windows.

Then he looked directly at me.

"If you ever feel sleepy, lock the doors and sleep. Don't try to be a hero. The things inside that store can be replaced. You cannot."

I thanked him. He finished his cigarettes and left for work.

After that conversation, the store never quite felt the same.

I still cleaned the counters and restocked the shelves. I still served the occasional customer. But whenever a vehicle pulled in and stayed outside for a little too long, I'd find myself watching it.

I don't work there anymore. I've had other jobs since, and I've never seen that man again.

Sometimes I wonder if he's retired by now, or how his grandchildren are doing.

I never learned much about him. I only knew his usual brand, the time he usually arrived, and the warning he left me with.

For a long time, I thought he was simply giving me advice about working the night shift.

It took me years to understand what he was really saying.

\*\*There are things in a convenience store that aren't worth protecting. And there are people who won't hesitate to kill you for them.\*\*
