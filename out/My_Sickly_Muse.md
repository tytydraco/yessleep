Tongue like a jackhammer. All teeth, no love — Seraphina kissed with the tenacity of a failed terror plot. The embrace dragged on, neither of us enjoying it, but I didn’t pull back. Enduring a kiss seemed a small price to pay for what she’d given me. I figured the discomfort would be brief and easily forgotten.

Little did I know the true nature of our exchange.

 

The pictures on her profile were a testament to the art of photography. The woman waiting for me at the tea house was a poorly reanimated version of the digital article. Seraphina’s skin was pallid, she looked like she just rolled off her deathbed and, even past the incense burning by the bar, there was the gentlest hint of musk. It wasn’t a natural smell; it was the sort of stench reserved for long-abandoned warehouses.

The mismatch wasn’t just skin deep. By the time the tea arrived it became crystal clear that me and my date had nothing in common. As we floundered through ten minutes of stilted conversation filled with dead silence and awkward shrugs, I wished I ordered a cold drink that I could chug. My afternoon with Seraphina would have served as just another reminder about the ills of online dating, but then, just as I was trying to invent an emergency to free me of my obligations, she asked a question which would dislodge the foundations of my life.

‘So, what sort of stuff do you write?’

‘Anything that I can sell. Pulp horror, mainly. Lots of weird fiction.’

‘Oh, I have a weird story. About a dead dog. Wanna hear?’

Seraphina did, indeed, have a weird story to share.

 

*Last year, Seraphina’s cousin’s friend bought a house with her husband. Hour outside of Prague. One of those places by the woods that straddles the fine line between city and town. Quiet corner of the country. Peaceful. Relaxed neighbors.*

*Once the couple properly moves in, they throw a housewarming. Not a big affair, just a couple bottles, a couple friends — a calm, civilized, tipsy affair by all accounts. The city folk drink and chat and sing, but then, as the night starts coming to an end something discomforting happens.*

*The couple, you see, has a dog.*

*German Sheperd. Big gal. Obedient enough, but a menace if she gets hyper. As the party drags on, the pooch disappears. No one really bats an eye. She’s probably just out exploring and will be back by the time the porch door is meant to be shut.*

*The neighbors, also, have a dog.*

*Tiny thing. One of those fluffy white terriers that yaps like a car alarm. The couple would have invited the neighbors to the housewarming, but the lights in their cottage are off and there’s no car in the driveway. Wherever they went, presumably, the terrier went with them.*

*Except, it didn’t.*

*Just as everyone is about to turn in for the night, the German Sheperd shows up on the porch. Wagging tail, big bright eyes, something in her mouth — something fluffy and familiar and clearly dead.*

*Were they sober, the couple might have come up with a more clearheaded solution, but sober they were not. They chase the German Shepard around their new backyard until it gives up its prey.*

*The dead terrier is in no shape to look like it passed the rainbow ridge under natural circumstances. The dog’s fur is matted in mud, its limbs are mangled and its left eye has been dislodged from its socket.*

*Again, under normal circumstances, the couple would have just accepted reality and profusely apologized to their new neighbors. Alcohol and fear, however, often produce drunken offspring. The couple takes the dog inside of their new home and into their new bathroom.*

*The dead terrier is bathed, dried and, with a bit of nervous thumb-work, the dog’s eye is pushed back where it belongs. The animal is still, clearly, deceased, but it no longer looks as if it were throttled to death by a German Shepard.*

*Under the cover of night, the couple sneaks over onto their neighbor’s property and deposits the dead terrier into his little doghouse. Then, with guilt and fear brewing in their hearts, they lay down to an uneasy sleep.*

*In the morning, the dead dog still sits in the neighbor’s backyard. Late afternoon, when their car pulls into the driveway, the pooch is still there. By evening, the corpse is gone.*

*No yelling. No knocking. No drama.*

*The dead dog is gone.*

*The couple makes no effort to speak with the neighbors. The mortgage papers have long been signed and the gravity of their social faux pas would make cohabitation with the neighbors difficult. Occasionally, they’ll wave across the fence and say their hellos, but they keep contact to a minimum.*

*But then, about a month later, the husbands get to chatting over yardwork. The neighbors invite the couple over for dinner and wine. Not wanting to seem unfriendly, or, God forbid, suspicious, the couple accepts the invitation.*

*They eat and drink and catch up on neighborly things and, eventually, the conversation drifts towards pets. As the food is finished and more glasses of wine are poured, the neighbors share a story.*

*A weird story.*

*Just before the couple moved in, or somewhere around that time, the neighbors had a dog. Little white terrier. Chipper little thing. Loyal to a fault but a careless explorer. The terrier manages to get under a cyclist outside the house. The mistake is fatal. He passes at the vets’ office an hour or two later.*

*It’s a crying shame, but that’s life. They square up with the vet, take their dead pet and bury him in the backyard. Right beneath his favorite tree. Then, to put some distance between them and the heartbreak, the neighbors set off to spend a weekend in Prague.*

*And you won’t believe what they found when they got home.*

*Couldn’t explain it if you tried.*

 

As Seraphina told her story, I found a familiar shimmer in my chest. I asked if she wouldn’t mind if I jotted down a couple of notes. Inspiration calling and all. She didn’t mind. In fact, she seemed flattered.

The conversation shifted after the dog story, but I was scarcely present for it. I was in my own world, rolling metaphors and setting up plot points and doing all but writing the actual words. Seraphina had dislodged a nugget of inspiration with the story and I lost all capacity for social contact. I had to go home and write.

All while trying to hold on to the narrative blossoming in the back of my knuckles, I tried to brainstorm excuses to leave the tea house. I didn’t have to. After finishing her cup of tea Seraphina said that she had to get going, but that she’d love to meet again.

I said that sounded like a plan, even though it didn’t.

We didn’t talk much as we walked to the tram station. The date had, once again, descended into null chemistry and I was far too preoccupied with my short story idea to try to salvage it. She didn’t seem particularly bothered herself.

Aside from the pages she dislodged from the back of my brain, there was very little sympatico between us. Yet, just as she was about to get on her tram and disappear from my life, Seraphina lunged in for a kiss.

The embrace was completely unwarranted and made me fear for the sanctity of my teeth, but I rolled with the punches. We kissed, said goodbye and I watched as Seraphina leave my life by rail.

I didn’t think about whether I would see her again. The moment she was out of sight I stopped thinking about her all together. I was far too focused on the story I wanted to write.

 

The talons of inspiration dug into me in the tea house, but it wasn’t until I reached home that they sprouted blood. Sitting down behind the keyboard felt like divine dictation. Every paragraph, every sentence, every word of the story came to me complete and sharp and perfect.

For two days I paced through my apartment, holding my laptop to my chest as if I were breastfeeding a child. I communed with the word processor and melted my being into a state of flow. Wild jazz became the soundtrack of my existence, cannabis and coffee dominated my diet and any thought of personal hygiene dissipated into obscurity.

Usually, when I write a piece, I do not finish it in good spirits. Usually, through the act of creation, I become far too aware of the flaws and imperfections and cannot regard the story as passable until months later when I had forgotten the sights of its scaffoldings. With the piece that Seraphina inspired, however, I found no such feelings.

It felt like it was the best thing I’ve ever put to paper.

And, at the time, it was.

I went to sleep that night content, bathed in the godly light of a long flow state session. When I woke, I was still in a good mood, but something behind my forehead felt off. A headache. A *peculiar* headache. By late afternoon, the migraine had spidered its way through the rest of my skull. By the next morning, I went to see my GP.

I brought a book to the waiting room so I wouldn’t die of boredom, but my eyes couldn’t focus on the letters. Even the slightest suggestion of light was making me squirm. The doc prescribed me some pain-killers. They took the edge off, but I was no wiser on the source of the migraine.

I only planned on taking a single sick day, but I had to take off two whole weeks. The hurt from my skull shattered across the rest of my body. I started to cough and was constantly nauseous and every morning my body felt as if it had spent the night bouncing stairs. On the fifth day of my torture, briefly, I considered calling an ambulance. Before I could, my body surrendered to the fever.

When I woke up the next morning the phone was still in my hand, but I was feeling ever so slightly better. My exhaustion and fear of doctors kept me in bed. When my aches eased just a little bit more over the course of the day, I presumed I was on the mend.

By the end of two weeks, I was still feeling like a corpse, but I was able to move around and go to work. My bout of sudden sickness seemed to be fading, but I was paranoid about it making a return. So, I went over to the doc again and told him about my two weeks of torture.

He lectured me about not showing up to the hospital earlier and then sent me to get an MRI and visit a lung specialist. The brain scan found nothing of concern. The lung doctor ran some tests and prescribed me some antibiotics. Apparently, there was an infection in my lungs, but it was already on the mend. The antibiotics would knock out whatever was left over.

I considered myself healthy.

By then, Seraphina had long drifted from my mind. The story she inspired, however, quickly became my calling card. I sent it around as a writing sample to rustle up some copywrite work and, eventually, ended up selling it to a local short story magazine.

I had gotten stuff published before, but this particular publication had been around for a while and had even inspired me to pick up the pen in my younger years. The feeling of seeing a story of mine, a story which I really liked, published in that magazine swelled me with pride.

I felt like a *real* writer.

And then Seraphina texted me. She congratulated me on getting my story published. She said we should grab a drink sometime.

I told her we should, but I wasn’t planning on it. The memory of that less-than-intimate embrace was still rubbing up against my gums. I appreciated the inspiration Seraphina had provided, but I had no interest in seeing her again.

The weeks turned into months and Seraphina once more faded from my daily life. I focused on writing. Or, more accurately, I tried to focus on writing. I would set hours of my day aside to sit and think and hopefully produce something, but I found myself in the depths of a dreaded writer’s block.

That’s when thoughts of Seraphina started to bubble up in my skull again.

I thought less of her, and more of the story she inspired, yet she quickly became a background extra to my neuroses. There were other stories, mainly unfinished stories, that I had penned after that two-day terrier communion session. None of them compared to the dead dog tale. I started to worry that I would never be able to match my past work.

In an effort to prove to myself that I am, indeed, a writer, I decided to enter a short-story contest which my beloved magazine was holding. I had hoped that the deadline would kick-start the creative juices which have brought me so much joy and fulfillment before, yet they did not.

When inspiration refused to bite, I publicly announced to all my friends and colleagues that I would write something for the contest. I had hoped that the peer pressure would serve as fuel for my parched mind, but it did not. Instead, a burning hot malaise started to fester at the pit of my stomach.

With the late-entry deadline not a week away, I found myself stumbling around *Michalska Street* well after respectable hours. I was drunk enough to know it was time to go home, but my anxious throat was still parched. I elected for one last drink at the *Pavouk Lounge*. When I scrolled my contacts for company only one name caught my eye.

She responded to my message immediately. Within five minutes she was at the bar.

Were the lounge not accustomed to strange Bohemians with artistic souls, I doubt Seraphina would have been let past the bouncer. She showed up to the bar wearing sweatpants and a stained gray hoodie. When we hugged hello, that inescapable smell of rot hung about her like a terrible cloud. At the edges of her smile, the flesh of her gums was gray.

Seraphina looked to be in both poor grooming and health, yet I was not that much different. In the pits of my misery, I had managed to at least take a shower, but shaving and sleep had long become a comfort I did not find myself fit for.

Racoon eyed and disheveled and armed with gin and tonics, we both looked like we were on the same side of a losing war. At a distance we must’ve looked like a good match, yet the months apart had not helped our chemistry. Aside from our ragged appearance, Seraphina and I had nothing in common.

The labored small talk put even more strain on my already troubled psyche. In an effort to shorten my exposure to the awkwardness I took deep draughts from my drink. My cup had nearly run empty when liquor loosened my tongue.

I started to talk about my writing sorrows. Seraphina listened.

‘What sort of contest is it?’

‘February edition. Meant to be about love.’

‘What about a weird first date story?’

‘Got any?’

‘If you buy me a drink, I do.’

I should have gotten up from that table and walked to the subway. I should have turned my back on Seraphina and skipped out on the contest and accepted that sometimes the muse of inspiration leaves for a couple of months.

I should have gone home, but I did not.

 

*The year is 2014. Online dating has just transformed from a fringe industry to the premiere way of meeting people. Seraphina’s acquaintance from high-school, let’s call her Abby, just got out of a long-term relationship and is on the hunt for love. She downloads the apps and tests the digital waters.*

*Now, Abby is frosty and she knows men can be pigs, so she keeps all her first dates to a cup of coffee. Nice and simple and roots out those looking for something different than plain ol’ human connection.*

*She always keeps the first dates to a coffee, but then there’s this guy. Tall, dark, handsome stranger. After they finish off their cup of joe this fella suggests a glass of wine. Abby says okay. One glass.*

*But it’s not one glass.*

*The two drink at the café, then go grab a bit to eat and then continue drinking in a bar. Abby watches the rest of afternoon melt away and turn to evening. She wasn’t planning on spending the night out, but spice of life and all — she does.*

*When the handsome stranger invites her over for a nightcap, she doesn’t hesitate.*

*Abby has already resolved to sleep with this man and getting to his apartment does nothing to lessen her zeal. The place is nice and clean and her date seems chivalrous enough. They put on a record, pour another glass of wine and settle down on the couch.*

*After a couple minutes, they move on to the bedroom.*

*Now, there’s nothing wrong with the man’s bedroom per se. But something starts to bubble in the back of Abby’s head. Call it female intuition, call it fate — but Abby just doesn’t feel safe.*

*Her partner notices the sudden tenseness. The man pulls back and asks if she’s okay. She admits she isn’t. It’s been a while. Things are moving a bit too fast. He smiles and nods and says they don’t have to do anything she’s not comfortable with. Abby still feels like there’s something wrong, but the man’s chivalry tempers those instincts.*

*The man asks Abby if she’d like a back massage. Maybe that will help her relax.*

*There’s still a quiet voice in the back of her head telling her to leave, but Abby says yes. She takes off her bra and lays down on her stomach and the man straddles her back and starts to massage her. He asks her if she’s allergic to nuts. She says she doesn’t think so but would like to know why he’s asking.*

*Massage oil. There’s some almond milk in it.*

*Abby isn’t allergic to nuts. As the man’s hands travel down her back, however, she feels a tingling sensation. She’s enjoying his touch; she wants to relax — but that gentle warmth soon turns hot. The quiet voice in the back of her head that was telling her to leave the apartment starts to scream.*

*She can’t explain why and she feels awkward trying to do so, but Abby tells the man she has to go home. In a matter of seconds, she is dressed and out the door. The stranger makes an effort to calm her. At some point he even gently blocks the doorway, but there’s no dissuading Abby.*

*Urged on by instincts she can’t verbalize, she retreats back home.*

*In the morning, she wakes up hungover and lonely. The same forces that urged her out of the apartment just a couple hours prior seem silly and neurotic and stupid. She tries to reach out to the handsome man to apologize, but she can’t.*

*He’s blocked her on all the apps. She missed her shot at true love.*

*To make things worse, Abby wakes up with a bad case of eczema on her back. She ignores it for a day or two, but when the itching won’t stop, Abby goes to a doctor. Turns out it’s an allergic reaction. Not nuts, but papain. Enzyme used in a lot of meat tenderizers. Comes from papaya.*

*Abby figures there was some papaya in the massage oil and tries not to think about it too much. She flushes the whole night out of her head. The handsome stranger is gone and she’ll never see him again.*

*But she does see him again. A couple weeks later.*

*The handsome stranger appears as a news headline.*

*A cannibal apprehended, they say.*

 

The notes I took that night are horrid scratches that scarcely resemble letters, let alone ideas. They didn’t need to be legible though. As Seraphina spoke, the story cascaded through my mind with perfect clarity. Past the booze, past the exhaustion — fully formed paragraphs were sprouting up behind my knuckles.

When she asked if I wanted to have another drink, I declined. I had no interest in dulling my mind. All I wanted to do was to rush back home and get behind a word processor. In truth, I didn’t want to walk her home either, but I felt like I owed her at least that much.

Mercifully, Seraphina lived just a couple blocks off of *Michalska*. We stumbled our way over in just a couple minutes. As she unlocked the door to her complex, she suggested one last drink. A nightcap perhaps.

‘Don’t worry,’ she said, revealing her sickly gums. ‘No meat tenderizers or cannibals upstairs.’

I, once again, excused myself. I had writing to do. It was already late. All I could offer was a goodbye hug.

She turned the hug to an embrace.

Her lips were dry and her tongue was cold and the inside of her mouth tasted like expired medicine. The kiss turned my stomach, but I endured. I told myself I was standing still out of politeness, but maybe, by then, a part of me knew there was a tradeoff happening. Maybe, during that terrible kiss, I had realized those bursts of inspiration weren’t entirely natural.

She pulled me closer. Deeper into that discomforting smell and sickening embrace. She got her cold hands under my shirt and caressed my back. It’s only once she started to pull me into the apartment complex that I stepped back.

‘Can’t blame a gal for trying,’ she said. ‘If you ever need me, you know where I live.’

Maybe, by then, a part of me knew I was destined to see Seraphina again, yet if that realization was there, I was numb to it. All I wanted to do was to go home and write. All I wanted to do was turn the lightning in my head into thunder.

And that’s exactly what I did.

The next four days disappeared in a flash of white phosphorus. No music. No drugs. No sleep. I sat down on my couch, took out my laptop and typed. Every atom of my being was committed to bringing the story to life. It felt as if my entire life had led me to a point where I would be able to catch those particular words floating in the ether.

For four days and nights I sat at my computer, obeying my divine dictate, weaving tales from a sickly muse’s lips. I felt no hunger or thirst or pain. All that burned in my heart was the righteous fire of fate.

I was doing what I was destined for.

When the piece was finally finished, I did not celebrate. Flawless and masterful as the writing was, I had no capacity for pride. All my joy had been spent in the ecstasy of writing. All I could do was sleep.

My bout of sickness after my first meeting with Seraphina had long become a vague memory, but the morning after I finished the story it all came back with feverish force. The ideas and words that I jettisoned onto the page were replaced with tension in my skull. It felt like my head was going to pop open.

I went to my GP, settling down in the waiting room just as I did last time I felt ill. Quickly, my already ragged state deteriorated. My lungs heaved with some terrible poison. Hives broke out all over my back. My stomach refused to hold water.

Before it was my turn to see the doctor, I found myself laying on cold tile with spittle running down my chin. My consciousness held on as I was loaded onto a stretcher, but it did not hold on for much longer.

I spent two weeks lying on a hospital bed in Motol. The first week was a complete blur. All I can recall are faint flashes of being ferried from machine to machine and having people in facemasks shining lights in my eyes. As my condition stabilized, I found myself a ragged corpse under constant supervision.

On my first day of semi-lucidness two men who weren’t hospital staff entered my room. They started to speak to me, but before they had a chance to identify themselves, they were ushered out by a nurse.

I was very sick, she said.

I needed to rest, not be interrogated.

They came back the following day. This time, the men refused to leave. Apparently, what I was suffering from was a rare bacterial infection. A type of bacteria that is exclusively found on decomposing corpses.

The two men were detectives.

They had some questions for me. Strange questions.

I answered all of their inquiries truthfully and honestly. There was no reason to lie. I was not a serial killer and I wasn’t hanging out around any graveyards. Every question the detectives asked me came back as a negative, and I meant it, but there was one morsel of truth I had decided to obscure.

“Have you been in contact with anyone who you suspect might have had recent dealings with corpses?”

I did not mention Seraphina. Even in my diminished state, I knew that she was connected to my sickness. I knew that those bursts of angelic inspiration did not come from a healthy place. I knew Seraphina had been the source of the bacteria, that she was connected to something horrible, but I did not mention her. I did not mention her because I thought — I feared — that one day I might require her companionship once more.

That day came soon enough.

I was only in the hospital for two weeks, but the sickness stayed with me for months. Every day dragged on with coughs and aches and a steadily encroaching fear that my body would find a new way to betray me. Life quickly became utter misery.

My one ray of hope came about a month after I had been discharged from the hospital. A hand-mailed letter from the short story magazine. I had no memory of ever sending out the story, but apparently it was the last thing I did before falling ill.

I won. I won and the editor of the magazine suggested I send over more work. Something longer. A novella perhaps. Something in a similar vein like my contest story, or that dead dog story that I wrote a couple months prior.

Countless nights I spent chewing on my pen trying to come up with something, anything, that I could pitch to the editor. I had spent all of my career waiting for a letter like that. When it finally came, when it dawned upon me that I could not produce the work myself — it broke my heart.

 

I wanted to meet in public. She insisted we go straight to her apartment. I was not in a position to argue. After a couple of solitary drinks, I headed back to *Michalska* street.

There was nothing wrong with the building Seraphina lived in. Just one of those historical places on the edge of Old Town. The façade was whole, the door was modern, even had one of those fancy vnitrobloc terraces. The building itself was fine, yet, as I climbed up the stairs to her attic flat, a terrible smell assailed me.

Mold. Infection. Death.

She answered the door wearing a stained bath-robe that left little solace to imagination. Her skin was bloodless and her eyes were sunken and her breath smelled of rotten hospital food. She did not greet me. She simply wrapped her frigid bony arms around my shoulders, drew me near and started to whisper.

I recall little of the act other than it was repulsive. My mind had pried itself fully from my body and latched on to each putrid word that left Seraphina’s lips. At first, her sentences hung disconnected and unrelated as if it were not a story at all, but the more I listened, the more I thought — a majestic odyssey was born in my heart.

When the terrible carnal act concluded, she lay in bed still, as if she were set to be buried. The lack of breath in her chest further cemented the fear. For a moment I worried that she had died. That she had been a corpse all along.

Yet then, just as I dressed and prepared to leave the scene of the crime, she sat up. Seraphina smiled at me with her black gums.

‘Well, let’s do this again sometime,’ she said.
