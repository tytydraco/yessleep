**Part One-The Body**

The sea was flat the morning we found the body. Not calm, it was eerily vapid. No gulls to be seen, singing their familiar songs. There was no sea life in the tide pools off the Kuril‑Kamchatka Coastline. A stillness that would make even the strongest mind wander with its faint whispers in the air.

  
I was in the dive bay checking the hull seals on the *Calyptra* when I heard the voice of Dr. Kovac over the intercom. “Chavez, we got something on the hydrophones, might be the drone,” she said, voice tight with slight uncertainty. Her voice would seem tense when she was unsure or saw something unusual. Without committing to actually saying the word *weird.* This was her way of keeping composure, but we all picked up on it. I’d taken many dives into the trench, enough for me to catch the subtle nuances in my team's words, be it calm or frantic. I stepped out onto the catwalk overlooking the water. The Orpheus research facility sat on the cliff like a steel barnacle. Its rails rusting and accumulating random passengers, unable to let go. Below, the dive bay opened directly into the trench. Deep and dark, swallowing the morning light that shone above.

   
Kovac met me at the console, arms folded and eyes fixated on the screen. A pulsating waveform emitted from it, in perfect metronomic rhythm. “That’s not the drone,” I said. “No,” she agreed. “But the drone’s last transmission came from at the same depth. Six thousand meters. Near the shelf.” She didn't say the rest, but I put it together, and this sound was not there yesterday. Leaning in from behind us was Charles Brooks, our captain. “Could be a vent, or maybe a landslide.” “No, it’s much too rhythmic, steady; it’s too regular,” Kovac said. I put the headphones that were hung on the side of the console onto my ears and listened. KATHOOM. KATHOOM. This wasn’t something mechanical, not seismic; it was something different. Captain Brooks sighed deeply and said, “Chavez, Tanaka, suit up. You’re taking the *Calyptra* down to recover whatever’s left of the drone.” Miku Tanaka was one of my oldest friends; I’d known her since college. She was my partner in crime. I wouldn’t do any dives without her. I loved her, and despite having the fearlessness to venture into the maw of the deep, I never dared to tell her how I felt. I nodded, peering over to Miku for approval. She confirmed with a smile. We helped each other put our suits on and made our way to the docking bay. 

  
“Vessel comms check, Kovac to *Calyptra,* do you copy?” Kovac said in our ears. “*Calyptra* copies Kovac, loud and clear.” “Station comms check, all stations report in.” Crowley, our seismologist, came over the intercom, “Copy, all systems operational. Testing emergency signal ping. *Calyptra,* send ping when ready.” “Sending emergency signal in 3…2…1… Sent.” Miku replied. “Confirmed, signal received,” Crowley said. “Testing system hull pressure,” I said. “All systems check out ok,”  Brooks replied. Once all checks were completed, the *Calyptra* slowly and softly released, and we began the descent into the water. As the vessel began to move, the lights cut on and the steady cones of light surveyed our seascape. It didn't take long until the light of the sun faded behind us; only a faint crackle from the static of the radio remained. 

  
At around three thousand meters, the water turned pitch black. A vast void of nothingness, nothing to be seen on either side of us, or below. At four thousand, the temperature dropped dramatically. Luckily, our suits had internal warming modules for this purpose alone. At five thousand, the trench wall came into view. The jagged face of the cliff dropped below, into nothing, only what the imagination could muster. “Approaching the last known coordinates,” I said. “Copy that,” Kovac replied. “Chavez, we’re getting a bit of interference on your feed, recalibrating.” Static was singing from the radio; the lights on the sub flickered. Then I saw it. The drone lay on the edge of the basalt cliffside, twisted and mangled, like crumpled foil. But that’s not what made my breath hitch. There was a figure that lay beside it. “Miku, are you seeing that?” I said. “Yeah, try to get a bit closer if you can. What is that?” As the craft crept forward, we were able to get a full glimpse of it. Sat beside the remains of the drone was a human body, upright, hands folded in its lap. “Control,” I whispered. “We’ve got…We’ve got a body.” The sound of static extended our shock. “Uh..Come again, repeat that?” Brooks said over the line.It’s a person, no suit, no gear.” Miku said. I crept even closer; the lights swept over the body’s smooth and white skin, unblemished, eyes open, staring straight ahead. There were no signs of decay. No bloating, no marks from the occasional passerby searching for a snack. It was there, just waiting…For something. “Chavez,” Kovac said shakingly, “that’s impossible.” 

  
I took the manipulator arm and reached out towards the body, hesitating before touching the body’s shoulder. The moment the arm touched its body, an oscillating pulsation was sent from the tips of the robotic arm back into the hull. It made my own pulse stutter in the moment.  A faint and familiar feeling washed over both Miku and I. “Was that…?” she whispered. “Not sure, let’s get him up to the surface before we draw any conclusions.” “Bringing the body back up, standby,” my voice slightly choked as I announced our next move. I fastened the cupped robotic arm around the figure's waist and closed its metal fist. As we pulled it inward, no change to its upright position. As if gravity and pressure did not affect its anatomy. 

  
The ascent upward seemed to take five times as long as the descent. My imagination began to flip through various scenarios and situations that might have led to our friends placement and location. Miku seemed to be in the same headspace, her eyes moving frantically. Obviously thinking similar thoughts. “Hey, you ok?” I asked in concern. Any time she got into this mode, it was hard to get her back to reality; that much has never changed. “Yeah, just thinking,” she said quietly. “Of? Come on, what’s going on in that head?” “I’m just confused how, for one, a body got that deep in the first place, and two, how was the body in near perfect condition, unmolested by even the undercurrents?” “Yeah, same here, and I for one can't think of any reason. I’m sure Doc Kovac has an explanation of some kind,” I assured her. “You’re probably right. Just weird, right?” “Absolutely.” 

  
As we approached the surface, there was a throbbing sensation emanating from my chest in perfect sync with the pulsing heard on the hydrophones. Like it was some sort of heartbeat-like drum from miles below. When the *Calyptra* broke the surface, the sky above was dark and foreboding. Clouds rolled in from the east, blanketing what was left of the sun's warmth, swallowing the horizon. Brooks and Crowley dragged the body from the arms' grip onto the platform, awe-struck. Kovac stared at the lifeless body, as if afraid to blink. “No signs of decomp,” she murmured, “no signs of rigor mortis. No lividity. Skin temp is…Normal?” Brooks glanced at me and said, “You ok, Chavez?” “Yeah, fine,” I lied. Because I could still feel it, the constant beating. 

  
The wind was picking up on the bay platform; rain began to fall on the metal lining of the overhead like fingers tapping in nervous tempo. Kovac sprang up from her knees. “The storm’s approaching fast; we need to get inside before it cuts us off.” Miku and I took responsibility for our find, and each picked an arm and began to pull the body into the lab. We heaved the man onto a nearby gurney. Before we proceeded with his inspection, we scrubbed up and put on protective gear. He had been down there for who knows how long with no interaction. We needed to be somewhat careful with handling him. “Based on his clothing, I'd say he was from the early nineteen hundreds,” Kovac deduced. “How is that possible? There is absolutely no way a body could survive that kind of depth unaffected by the elements or pressure. The parasites and scavengers would have picked his body clean in a few months, much less a damn century.” Miku chimed in rebuttal. “I get where you’re coming from, Tanaka, but the evidence is clear. Just look at the clothing. They’re tweed and wool knitted suspenders for Christ’s sake,” the doctor exclaimed. “Who have you known that has worn something like this besides your great-great-great-grandfather?” Miku lowered her head, pondering what Kovac had mentioned, agreeing mentally. “The clothing is the least of our concerns anyway; take a look here at its skin. It’s milky white, with a slight sheen, and a layer of thin mucous-like substance attached to it. Possibly why it was preserved so well.” 

The doctor poked and examined further, checking other areas for discoveries unknown. She took her pen, pried open one eye, and dropped the lid as soon as she opened it. “My god!” She shouted. “What?! What was it?” I asked in concern. “Take a look for yourself.” I took the pen and slowly opened the eye once more; beneath the eyelid was another, covering the entirety of its ocular globe. “What..The…Fuck…” mouthing silently. I wasn’t someone who would be put easily in unease, but this was a rare occurrence it seemed. After regaining her composure. Kovac continued her examination, writing and noting what she observed on her notepad. “What are we looking at here, doc?” Brooks asked, with a certain stoicism. “This is a first for me, Brooks. I’ve never seen anything like it in all my 63 years.” I scanned the body slowly and closely, trying to catch something unnoticed and unobserved by our studious leader. Some things seemed out of the ordinary. Like how his skin felt. It was so smooth, unnaturally so. No hair on either arm or legs. His pupils were incredibly large, as if permanently dilated. Washerboard markings on either side of his neck. The goopy, slime-like coating stuck to my gloves; I shook it off my hands in disgust. In the middle of my visual probing, a stray arm came into view. It was Crowley; he had a metal probe and was prodding the arms and legs like a child. “Crowley, what the fuck are you doing?” I yelled, “Put the prod down, you idiot; we’re all professionals here, act like one.” Crowley was undeniably green; he was fresh out of school. The only reason he got on this team was due to his grandfather being a large investor in our work. We needed all the help we could get, since our grants were running scarce. 

  
It was closing in on an hour since we hauled our shiny friend to safety, and we could hear Mother Nature’s anger on the other side of the facility.  Waves began to roll over the shore, as if trying to reclaim what we took. Thunder cracked on the horizon in retaliation. Within the hour, the storm advanced in full swing. Once at its peak, we lost all radio communication to all nearby towers. Satellite uplinks went silent as the waves crashed like fists onto the lab’s carapace. We were stranded with no line of communication to the outside world, normal for weather this fierce.

  
We gathered in the dining hall while Brooks went to spring life into the emergency generator. “God damned storm,” he muttered from down the hall. “Hmmm, now where is that blasted kerosene?” I could hear him rummaging in the maintenance room, pushing aside old boxes and bottles of half-used cleaners. “Aha! Got ya, ya little bastard,” he said, triumphant. When he stood with the can gripped tightly in his hand, something dripped from his nose. Slow and thick. He wiped it away without a second thought, smearing the viscous fluid across the back of his hand. A powerful tremor shook beneath him, KATHOOM. KATHOOM. He braced himself against the wall. “Storm must be right on top of us,” he said, voice shaking ever so slightly. “Gonna catch a cold in this weather, just my damned luck.” By the time he made it back to us, he looked almost defeated, as if the walk back took a lot out of him. He dropped into his chair with a soft grunt, rubbing his nose again. “You good there, Brooks?” I asked. “Never did like storms on the ocean,” he said. “Feels wrong, like two forces of nature fightin’, competin’ for dominance.” I glanced at Miku clattering away on her laptop, most likely logging the day’s events. Kovac was staring out the darkened window, listening, waiting for something she couldn’t name. Crowley was sitting, legs crossed, fixated on his phone. Everyone was doing something different; none of us were really present. We were cold, tired, and locked inside with something we didn’t understand. Whatever we managed to bring from the deep…It hadn’t come alone.
