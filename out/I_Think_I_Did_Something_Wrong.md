Thanks to the online algorithm, I managed to secure a quick job at XXXX, a high-rise building in the not-so-nice part of town.

All I had to do was investigate the walls for roaches and any type of vermin.

I was used to unclogging toilets or wiring outlets, but they offered a premium rate for the inconvenience and I couldn’t turn that down. 

It can’t be that hard.

The ad said that it was urgent and had to be done before midnight.

I briefly talked on the phone with the owner after accepting the job and the only thing he was really keen on was that I pick one unit and finish before midnight.   
   
One unit out of all sixteen floors.

I arrived at 10:30 p.m. on the dot, parking as close as I could to what might be the curb, the massive snow bank confusing me as to even where the sidewalk starts and ends. 

The snow had been picking up all day, and there was a warning of a severe blizzard later that night. 

Orange streaks of light came from the lightpost above me, flickering every couple of seconds onto the fresh powder.

My work boot sank into an ankle-deep pile of snow running through the middle of the road, soaking my already moth-eaten socks. 

After dragging my feet through the snow, I unfolded the roll cover and climbed up on my hands and knees, searching for the tools that I thought I’d need.

Tools in hand, fingers numb and uncooperative, I was on my way to the front door.

The door didn’t budge.

I cupped my hands against the glass door, eyes darting left and right, waiting for someone to let me in.

Minutes later, the elevator chime echoed into the main lobby, a worn mop head emerging from the opening doors.

Out came an older Hispanic man, about my height with a caterpillar mustache and an eye that moved on its own. 

He finished mopping the last of the elevator and stepped into the main lobby, wringing out the excess water into a mop bucket before reusing it.

I repeatedly pounded my fist against the glass until he finally noticed me.

Judging by his face, he didn’t seem too fond of me. 

“What are you doing here?”

“Your boss hired me to do some investigating.”

I pressed the call button twice, vigorously rubbing my hands together, my eyes glued to the mechanical arrow ticking downwards.

“You don’t want to do this.” 

“Well, I have a job to do. Just like you.”

Just as I finished my sentence, the loud chime echoed and the double doors opened. 

The man took a step, now partially in front of me, his eyes glazed with moisture.

“Have a good night man.”

I walked around him and stepped inside, keeping my finger on the close door button, ready to be in and out before the snowfall picked up even more.

With closed eyes and a waving index finger, my lucky number was floor eleven.

I stepped out of the elevator, the waterlogged ceiling panels emitting a stale, sweet smell. 

“Hello?? Pest control!”

No one answered the first door.

I tried the next door down the hall and no one answered that one either.

Minutes later, I’d knocked on seven out of eight doors and was starting to get frustrated.

Unit 1108 was the last unit at the end of the hall, a metal door with far too many dents and chipped paint, decorated with a wreath hanging by a sheetrock screw. 

This time I decided to try to open the door rather than just knock on it. 

I gripped the door handle and twisted it open, a haphazard tangle of green and red dangling above the door, slowly fading by the bulb.

“Hello?”

No one answered.

The door closed behind me with a soft click, the stench of ammonia wrapping around me.

Now that I was inside, I could hear a faint noise off in the distance.

It was boiling water in a pot.

I entered the kitchen, turning off the stove, the bubbles simmering down.

Weird.

I flicked the stovetop light on and scanned the fridge, ready for a living Rorschach of roaches hiding behind it. 

It didn’t budge.

My hands kept slipping, my palms now coated with a yellow, greasy film.

I stood with my hands on my hips, my eyes roaming the fridge door.

It was full of magnetic souvenirs, overdue appointments, and a vintage Polaroid of a couple. They looked happy. 

That’s when another sound caught my attention.

It was muffled crying accompanied by a blood-curdling creak from one of the bedroom doors.

“Hello? Who’s there? Please tell me it’s you, George.”

The lady was slurring her words.

“No Ma’am. It’s pest control.”

She finally came into sight, an older woman in a robe with messy, matted hair.

Judging by her wrinkles even visible in the dim lighting, she looked like she’d been dealt a bad hand in life.

“No you’re not. You ruined my life!”

Her breath reeked of alcohol.

“Why George? Why us?” 

She started walking closer to me, something dangling loosely in her hand from behind her back.

“I’m not George, I–”

She threw it to the ground, the object shattering into a hundred pieces.

I did a complete 180 and didn't look back. 

Thankfully the elevator didn’t move from when I got to the eleventh floor.

I grabbed my tools and rushed inside, hiding in the corner and frantically pressing the close door button.

The elevator reached the first floor and I was on my feet.

I took my first step, almost tripping over the mop bucket. 

It was in the same place as before, a mixture of sediments sitting at the bottom. 

My phone read 11:00 and the snow had built up a substantial amount but I wasn’t staying here any longer.

I retraced my way back, my boots following the line of hollow footprints leading to my driver's side door. 

I got to my truck, throwing my tools inside and locking the door, checking my backseat for anything or anyone.

There was nothing.

I quickly sent the boss a text apologizing for how I wasn’t able to complete the job before midnight.

Right as it started sending, a massive yellow plow truck appeared in my rearview mirror.

It passed me, specks of salt lightly tapping against my truck.

It had cleared a decent amount for its first trip of the night. 

I looked back down at my phone, the text still sending. 

My eyes darted back to the building, waiting to see if the lady was going to run out of the elevator and look for me.

I put my truck in drive and sped out of there, adrenaline being the only thing keeping me awake for the long drive home.

The chirping of birds from the unlatched hopper window above my bed had woken me up.

I turned to my side and grabbed my phone and remote from my nightstand, turning on the TV for background noise as I unlocked my phone.

It turns out the text didn’t send last night because I was blocked.

I deserved at least some type of compensation for driving all the way out there.

What a waste of time.

Rent was way overdue and I started stressing, scrolling through forgotten apps on my phone, hoping I’d find a forgotten transfer.

After about half an hour of sending a couple texts and taking random surveys, I should’ve been at least $40 up.

I logged into my bank account and immediately sprang up, lifting my upper body off the bed.  
   
A transfer of $5,000 was sent to me last night. 

I don’t get it. I didn’t even complete the job.

At that moment, the TV caught my attention, my palm pressing into the remote hidden under my bedsheets.

“LAST NIGHT’S WEATHER WAS ON PAR WITH THE BLIZZARD OF ‘87!!”

I turned the volume down before shutting the TV off, now sitting up with my legs hanging off the bed, the remote resting on my thigh.

This couldn’t be real. 

Just then, the remote fell onto the floor.

It ricocheted off my foot, under the bed, a sharp sting–  
   
“Breaking news! Last night, both George and Maria Sanchez went missing.”

I went on all fours, searching under my bed for the remote.

“They were last seen at XXXX–”

I crawled from under my bed and stood up. 

“If you have any knowledge of their whereabouts, please call XXX-XXX-XXXX.”

There was a photo in the center of the TV screen.

A man with a thick mustache and a lazy eye.

And a lady with a drunken smile.

They looked happy. 

I think I did something wrong.
