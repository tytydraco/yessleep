Hey there, I’m Jon… and today I’m going to give you a really quick lesson on reading ancient Latin. Very exciting. So the first thing we’re going to learn are the words:

No, we’re going to do that together. It’ll be fun!  
So we’ll start by getting the easy one out of the way. Pro… you’ll see that word a lot in the legal system: Pro *rata*, pro *bono*, *quid* pro *quo*. Pro is easy; pro means “for”. Or, more specifically, “for,” as in “in favor of.” So you have pro.  
Next, you got the same word twice. Anima. Translated literally, it means animate; to have life. Specifically, it can also mean soul. So Anima. Not to be confused with \*animus\*. Very important to read it as “anima”, not “animus” when we get to reading it yourself.   
Anima pro anima... good.

Now let’s try

Usque ad finem temporis

So we have ad, which generally means “at” or “towards”. Usque means “until,” temporis is “time”; hence why time is associated with the words temporal or temporary. And then finem, which is the “end of” something. Some of you die-hards out there might try to pull a fast one on me; slip in \*finis\* instead of finem. Finis only applies if the word “end” is usually the last word of the sentence, or “ad finem” (or “ad extremum” if you want to get technical).   
So Usque ad finem temporis. Very good.

So now we want to say those two phrases together like so:

Anima pro anima  
Usque ad finem temporis
