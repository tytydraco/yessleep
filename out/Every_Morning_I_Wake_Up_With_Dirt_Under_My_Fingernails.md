It started a week or so ago. The first time it happened and maybe even the second could be chalked up to coincidence. By now there was no denying that this was a full fledged pattern.

Every morning I would wake from a dreamless sleep with black dirt caking my fingers and buried beneath my fingernails. The dirt was usually dry, sometimes wet if the previous night had been particularly rainy. The phenomenon was bizarre and I would be lying if I said it didn’t seriously unsettle me. Each morning I was without recollection of doing whatever it was that I did in my unconscious state to get my hands dirty.

Living by myself in a remote and rural town left me with few options. My first attempt to lock myself in my bedroom was unsuccessful. Apparently, my sleepwalking self had enough dexterity to work past the bedroom door lock. The problem began to escalate when I awoke to an acute pain in the tips of my fingers. Recently, I had been waking to find my nails cracked and red with dried blood, and I could feel an intense strain in my wrists and forearms. To top it off, I was beginning to feel the fatiguing effects that losing several hours of sleep each night had on a person.

The town doc said that there was no “cure” for sleepwalking, but considering the severity of my case he recommended behavioral therapy. I remember the way he stared at my cracked  fingernails as I explained my situation. They felt so naked and exposed as I held them in front of the doctor. Dr. Jensen was a stoic man, a typical white labcoat type, but in that moment, there was something so definitively human about his reaction.

“Take care now, Dan. Let me know if anything changes. I’m sorry I couldn’t be of further assistance.” He dismissed me from his office with a melancholy wave.

Despite my dead end with the doc, I remained resolved to conquer my bizarre behavior. From the doctor’s office I drove my truck directly to the nearest hardware store. I wasn’t the type of person to own one of those camera doorbells, and I didn’t fancy the idea of watching myself sleep, but it was the only solution I could think of to get the answers that I sought. I was greeted by a familiar-looking teenager with a gardening apron on. His hands were covered in thick gloves and he was holding a planting shovel. 

“Hey, Dan! Petey says he’s been missing you these past few weeks. Says you two haven’t been out fishing in a while.”

Thankfully, his employee nametag pinned to his apron saved me from the awkwardness of having to ask his name. It was a small town. Everybody was expected to know everybody.

Ravi.

Upon reading the name, I remembered that the kid had an unfortunate history. He was orphaned as a young baby and was struggling his way through life before Petey took him under his wing a few years back.

“Love Petey to death, but that old dog is starting to lose it. We were just out fishing last Saturday. Good thing he's got you to look after his place, huh Ravi?”

The worker grinned sheepishly. I know that I am no saint, but I try to spread kindness to strangers. Making someone else's day is kind of like filling a hole in your heart that you didn’t know was missing.

“Anyway, what can I help you with today?”

The wind in my sails died and I was surprised to find that I grew nervous to answer. It was like I was being asked to reveal a shameful secret about myself.

“I’m looking for one of those digital doorbell cameras. I think I've got critters in the backyard. I want to catch whatever is eating my trash.”

Ravi failed to hide his concern. The poor kid had deep bags under his eyes making him appear much older than he actually was.

“We don’t carry any Ring cameras, but we do have trail cams. That should be able to achieve what you are looking for. Back aisle, left shelf.”

I thanked the kid with a nod. Before I was on my way I watched him nervously play with his gloved fingers as his eyes drifted to my hands.

Goddamn technology. 

It took me the rest of the night, but I finally managed to set up the two trail cameras that I purchased. I aimed the first one directly at my bed. The second I placed in a spot that covered most of my backyard and my back door. I didn’t have any coverage on the front of the house, but if I had to, I would be able to swap the cameras come tomorrow.

I waved my hand in front of each camera and received a notification on my phone. I clicked the setting to record all movements, and I was able to watch my movements back, albeit in low quality.

I crawled into bed and tried not to think about the watchful eye of big brother, carefully observing my every move. Sleep didn’t come easy, but it did eventually arrive. I knew that it had rained last night the moment I felt my fingertips graze my bedsheets. I didn’t even consider washing my hands before I kicked my feet out of bed, my soles were appropriately muddy and my back ached more than it had the past few mornings. 

I snatched my phone off my nightstand and I opened my trail cam app. The bedroom camera picked up and recorded several instances of usual tossing and turning. Then, at 1:23 AM I rose from my bed and walked straight to my bedroom door.

There is something so indescribably chilling about watching yourself move whilst having no recollection of it happening. My past self paid no heed to the camera and a stone cold expression was plastered on my face as I unlocked my bedroom door. By the time the backyard camera picked up my movement, it was 1:25 AM. The camera recorded me standing with my bare feet in the grass for several seconds as steady rain fell. The night vision on the trail cam was poor, but my eyes caught a reflection indicating that they were open. Suddenly, my past self began to walk. Steady and straight, like a man possessed, I entered the thick woods and disappeared from the camera’s view.

My heart raced with concern even though I knew that this particular story ended with me safe and sound in my bed.

If last night was any indication of the past week, it meant that I wasn’t just messing around in the garden. I was unconsciously wandering deep into the wilderness with no protection from the elements or wildlife.

The next and last video from the backyard camera caught me returning from my outing at 3:27 AM. I was gone for almost two hours on the dot. Lifelessly, I walked past the camera and made my way inside. There were no further pit stops before my bedroom camera recorded me crashing into bed.

“What the hell am I doing out there?” I whispered to myself.

Since it was a Saturday, I had all morning and afternoon to figure it out. At first, I ventured into the woods, hoping to find any signs of my digging. Being the piss poor tracker that I am, coupled with the rainfall from last night, I quickly deemed tracing my steps an impossibility. With that endeavor foiled, I hatched a new plan.

It would take a bit of preparation and it was certainly risky, but I was desperate for answers. If I left the house every night at the same time, then I would likely be  mid sleepwalk at around 2:30 AM. By setting an alarm at that precise time I could attempt to wake myself up and catch myself in the middle of the act. The looming issue with this plan was that I would awake in the woods with no recollection of how I got there. This meant that I would have to find my way back consciously.

Night came ominously quickly as I made my final preparations. A compass and survival essentials were tucked safely into a small pack. I laid down on my bed that was stained with soil. Washing the sheets every morning was a Sisyphean task that I had given up on days ago. I planned to sleep with my phone in my pocket, the survival pack on my back, and my boots on my feet.

I passed by my last chance to abort the mission as I set my alarm and closed my eyes. My nerves warred against my exhaustion in a drawn out battle that had an inevitable conclusion. Eventually, exhaustion won and sleep came for me.

I awoke with a sharp, startled gasp at the sound of my alarm. Before I was even aware of my surroundings, I reached into my pocket and silenced it. It was a muscle memory of mine that was reinforced with thousands of repetitions over the course of my entire life.

The dirt on my hands was illuminated by weak rays of moonlight shining over the trees. Taking in my new environment, it became apparent that I was in a shallow hole within some clearing of trees. My survival instincts returned to me and I reached for my pack. My heart dropped to my gut when I realized that it was no longer on my back.

“Fuck.”

Panicked, I began to climb out of my hole when I heard something that stopped me dead in my tracks. There was a hushed voice that was repeating some sort of chant. I peeked my head over the plane of the earth before staggering back from the unexpected sight.

In the moonlight, the scene unfolded in front of me like it was some sort of bad dream. Dozens of people were scattered around the clearing. All of them were sleepwalking, just as I had been thirty seconds ago. Like me, most were digging holes with their bare hands. A few were taking loose soil and filling in holes that were recently dug.

I refocused on the chanting. The sound was coming from a hole next to mine. I peeked inside and saw Ravi, the boy from the hardware store, hunched over on his hands and knees. He was repeating something in a hushed whisper.

“Mother, father, I am your son no more. Mother, father, I am your son no more. Mother, father, I am your son no more.” 

Beside his hole was a framed portrait of Ravi. In it, he is holding a high-school diploma with an Indian couple standing behind him proudly. The man in the picture shared Ravi’s round face and small nose.

I was so stunned that I couldn’t speak. I didn’t know if I should attempt to wake him or if I should call the police.	

As I scanned the clearing I noticed at least a dozen familiar faces.

Petey was a few yards away, cupping dirt with both hands and tossing it into a shallow hole. His clothes were completely stained and I caught the glimmer of something blue in the hole as he buried it with another handful of black dirt. I narrowed my eyes, identifying the half-buried object as a fishing rod.

Next to him was the sheriff. He dug viciously with both hands as he repeated a mantra.

“I have not served. My only service is to you. I have not served. My only service is to you.”

Next to him was a scattered pile of medals and a folded American flag despite the fact that I had no recollection of the sheriff ever serving. I briefly considered waking him and having him call for backup, but the experience was so overwhelming that I hadn’t fully processed it yet.

Desperately, I searched for anyone that wasn’t in this trance-like state. As I wandered, I noticed Dr. Jensen, who had finished flattening a freshly filled hole. He lowered himself to his knees, put out his arms and called into the night sky.

“I am prepared to share this piece of me! Come and take it!”

There was a rustling in the tree line. I’m not a very bright man, but at that moment I had the wherewithal to flee. I ran and stumbled into the shallow hole that I had awoken from, tripping over a small pile of objects that I did not notice before.

I pulled them into my hole and investigated. The largest of the objects was a framed photograph that appeared like it had been taken almost two decades ago. In it, I was wearing a fine suit, holding hands with a stunning woman dressed in a wedding gown. Looking at her face, she was perhaps the most beautiful woman I had ever seen. And I had absolutely no recollection of her.

Also in the pile of objects was an engagement ring and a gold wedding band. I slid the gold band over my dirt-covered ring-finger. It fell into place comfortably, like it was built to be there forever. The realization was not slow and dramatic. It hit me fast and hard, nearly knocking me off my feet.

The last item beside my shallow hole was a small memorial card, the type that you would receive from a funeral or wake. Printed in large bold letters was: 

“In loving memory, Suzanne, \[1988-2026\]”

She was clearly older in this picture than the one of the wedding, but she was radiant all the same. It was too much to process, and at some point, tears began to flow. On the back of the card there was a short poem.

“Be kind. For when you speak to another, you are not forming one memory, but two.”

I had to put a hand over my mouth to stop myself from audibly sobbing. The woman in the picture was someone that I had never met, but she was also someone that I knew that I loved. And now, I had not lost her once, but twice.

My breakdown was interrupted when the rustling in the trees crescendoed. Dr. Jensen remained with his arms outstretched as he faced the monstrosity that emerged.

An abominable beast loomed over the doctor as it stepped out of the trees. It stood as tall as the shortest conifer with a long slate gray face that was cracked in patches. Its eyes were small, with layers of folded skin forming bags beneath them similar to a rhinoceros or an elephant. Its ears drooped lower than its chin and its trunk-like nose partially obstructed its mangy body. The chimera walked on two legs, with hands and feet that were similarly claw-like in shape. A long tail followed it out of the woods, balled into the end with a tuft of gray hair.

This amalgamation thumped in front of Dr. Jensen, earth shaking with every slow step. It stared at him with expressionless eyes before it cupped his chin in its claw-like hands. Standing atop the freshly buried mound, the creature’s droopy trunk extended forward and attached itself to Dr. Jensen’s temple. The doctor began to moan as the creature’s nose rippled.

I stared in horror as the rest of the sleepwalkers around me continued to dig mindlessly. After an agonizing minute, the creature finished its feast and Dr. Jensen’s body dropped against the soft forest dirt.

As soon as the doctor fell, those two elephant eyes turned directly towards me. Quickly, I bent over in my hole and I began to claw at the dirt. I tried to act incapacitated, matching the pace of the townsfolk around me. As I stared at my gold band taking clump after clump of dirt from the ground, I found the strength to speak.

“This memory is not mine, it was meant to be shared. This memory is not mine, it was meant to be shared.”

Heavy footsteps clomped as the memory-eating monstrosity approached my hole. I continued to chant, trying with all my courage to keep my voice from shaking. The footsteps stopped when the creature’s body was fully blocking the moonlight from getting into my hole. I continued to work, appearing as mindless as I possibly could. The creature bent down and placed a claw on my chin. 

I dropped my facade then. I was completely helpless as I stared into its small black eyes.

With a snort, the creature’s nose was upon me.

“No! No!” I attempted to resist, but it was impossible to struggle against its inhuman strength.

As the trunk latched to my temple, I became sedated. The last thing I remember before falling asleep was meeting Suzanne for the first time when I saw a beautiful woman in desperate need of an umbrella on a rainy day.

I woke up in my bed with my gold wedding band wrapped around my stained ring finger. For a moment, I was naively hoping that everything that I had experienced for the past few weeks was one horrible nightmare. The trail camera pointed at my bed crushed my hopes of such an outcome.

I found my phone in my pocket and opened it, ignoring the low-battery warning. I quickly navigated to the trail cam app and loaded the videos from the bedroom. The most recent one showed me returning home at 3:07 AM. I walked straight inside and I collapsed in my bed.

I switched over to the backyard camera. Timestamped several minutes prior, I watched a video of myself returning from the woods. In the background of the video I was able to make out the silhouette of the horrifying monster standing at the edge of the tree line. Masked by shadow, the creature watched me enter my home as it stood menacing and unmoving. After several seconds, the camera shut off due to inactivity.

My heart was nearly beating out of my chest. I cried all morning and all afternoon. Memories of Suzanne came back like a tsunami, each wave more crushing than the last. I remembered how we met, I remembered buying our first house together, I remembered the day that we got married. And I remembered the day that I eventually lost her.

Whatever that thing in the woods was, it let me have Suzanne back. As I lay in my bed, agonizing over memories of her, I needed to remind myself that this is for the better. Once again, exhaustion won the battle and I drifted to sleep. For the first time in a long time, I dreamt. It was a colorful collage of memories and new experiences that I shared with my wife. Time, people, and places were blended together into a beautiful mosaic that filled a hole in my heart that I didn’t know was empty.

When I woke up in the morning I quickly shut my alarm off. As I blinked away the grogginess of sleep, I saw that there was no dirt beneath my nails.
