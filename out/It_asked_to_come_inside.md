I’ve spent thirty-two years trying to forget that house and that night.

I’ve lived in six different towns, worked a dozen different jobs, and slept rough on less than desirable beds more nights than I’m willing to admit. I’ve changed my phone number four times and have not spoken to my brother in nearly ten years. None of it has put space between me and what happened in that house.

There are many things a man can leave behind. His childhood home, the people he grew up with, the memories he made with those people. I have learned that much.

But I will never be able to leave that night behind.

I was twelve when it happened, when it all started, living with my parents and younger brother Michael on a stretch of farmland a few miles up the road from the nearest village. Ours was an old, quite unremarkable house, built from cracked grey stone and surrounded by fields that seemed to stretch endlessly into the green hills beyond. The nearest neighbour lived far enough away that on a quiet summer’s eve, you could stand outside our back door and hear nothing but the gentle breeze floating sweetly between the branches of the hawthorn tree in the yard.

My parents liked it that way. They loved the tranquillity of it all.

My father was a quiet man, not particularly affectionate but dependable in the ways that mattered. My mother was a far more outgoing and affectionate person. When my father would go out to work the fields on cold winter mornings, my mother would take me and Michael under a thick blanket by the fire and read to us softly.

My mother used to say that the countryside got into a man’s bones. That after enough years of working the same fields, watching the same seasons pass, a man began to believe there was nothing in the world he didn’t understand.

Perhaps she was right.

I know that sometime during the autumn of that year, strange things began happening around the house.

The back door would be left ajar in the morning, though my father swore up and down that he had locked it. Muddy footprints appeared across the kitchen floor and stopped abruptly beneath the table. At night, something moved through the fields, seemingly circling the house without ever coming close enough to be seen.

My father blamed foxes, although I never could understand why a fox would concern him so much. He’d dealt with them all his life.

Then, one evening while sitting down for supper, Michael asked a question that made the whole house fall silent.

“Da, who’s that fella who keeps knocking at the door?”

My father stopped cutting his meat.

My mother looked up from her plate.

“What man, son?” she asked.

“The one outside. He comes at dark most nights.”

Michael was eight. He had a habit of inventing things. I think it was his way of keeping himself entertained in the isolation of the farm. Usually, my father would dismiss him with a grunt and tell him to stop talking nonsense.

This time, he set his knife down.

“Has he ever spoke to you?”

My brother nodded. He didn’t seem worried or afraid.

“What does he say to you, Michael?” my mother chimed in.

“He asks me to open the back door.”

My father stared at him for a long moment. Then he stood, crossed the kitchen, and checked the lock on the back door.

He checked it twice.

That very same night, I heard the knocking.

I woke to the sound of shallow knocks at the back door.

Three slow, deliberate knocks against the hard oak door.

I lay frozen under my blankets, listening to the wind press against my window, the shadows of the hawthorn’s branches in the yard casting eerie silhouettes in the moonlight. I could hear my parents stirring downstairs. A chair scraped across the kitchen floor. A cupboard opened and closed.

Then came a quiet voice.

It was a man’s voice, low and patient.

“Let me in.”

I listened with fear pumping all the way through my body.

I eventually mustered all the courage I had. I crept from my bed and opened the bedroom door. The hallway was dark, but a thin line of light showed beneath the kitchen door.

The knocking came again.

Three slow knocks.

The whole house was silent now.

I heard my mother whisper something, too softly to understand. My father’s footsteps crossed the kitchen. The lock turned.

I should have stayed where I was. I should have woken my brother and hidden beneath the blankets. Instead, I crept down the stairs and looked through the gap in the kitchen door.

My father stood with one hand on the latch.

My mother was behind him, pale and trembling.

“Don’t,” she whispered shakily.

He turned towards her.

For a moment, neither of them moved.

Then something knocked again.

Not on the door this time.

From inside the house.

Three slow, deliberate knocks came from the pantry.

My father looked towards it. My mother covered her mouth to keep from screaming. I did the same.

My brother’s door opened upstairs behind me.

I spun around, expecting to see my brother at the top of the stairs.

There was no one there.

From the pantry came a soft scraping sound, as though someone were dragging fingernails down the wood.

Then a voice whispered my name. It sounded like it was right in my ear.

“Shaun...”

I ran.

I scurried up the stairs without looking behind me. I dived into my bed, burying myself beneath the blankets. I listened as the house filled with footsteps. They moved from room to room, sometimes heavy and deliberate, sometimes light enough to sound like a child running.

My brother began to cry. I could hear him in his own bedroom across from mine. My mother shouted once. My father shouted back.

Then everything went quiet.

I do not remember falling asleep.

I remember waking as the first light of dawn was coming up over the hawthorn tree. There was a silence so complete it seemed like the whole world had vanished.

When I went to check on my brother, he was already gone from his bed.

I found him standing at the top of the stairs, staring down into the kitchen.

My father was there, kneeling beside my mother.

Then my father looked up.

There was blood on his hands.

My mother lay motionless on the floor, her eyes open and fixed on nothing. Beside her was the kitchen knife from the table.

My father had killed her.

The thing that still haunts me now, as I write this, is what my father said when he spotted us.

He smiled, lifted one bloodstained finger to his lips, and whispered.

“Don’t let it know you saw.”
