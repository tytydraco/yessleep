In the basement of the municipal building, the only sound is the hum of the fluorescent lights.  
   
My cubicle is located on the lowest sublevel. My job is monotonous: I staff the text-to-voice emergency relay for the deaf and mute. When a text message arrives on my screen, I call the police or paramedics and read the subscriber's words aloud. No human voices. Just me and the monitor.  
   
The deaf community has its own syntax. Born without sound, many write in short, fragmented bursts, treating written language like sign gestures. I am used to decoding their urgent, broken grammar.  
   
At 2:26 AM, the screen flared.  
   
Usually, the sender’s profile would instantly populate with a name and a registered address. This time, all the fields were blank. The system couldn't identify the sender's number, leaving an empty row.  
   
Then, the first words appeared. They were typed agonizingly slow.  
   
"WOKE UP GROUND. PLEASE TELL THEM. WOKE UP."  
   
I typed back: "This is the emergency relay. What is your emergency? State your address."  
   
The cursor blinked. More letters crawled across the dark screen.  
   
"BOX TIGHT. NO AIR. DARK. RAIN HIT WOOD ABOVE."  
   
I read the line three times, refusing to process it. It was a prank. A sick, cruel internet joke. But each letter had taken several seconds to register.  
   
"Who is this?" I typed. "Give me your location. I cannot help you without a street name."  
   
"DIRT FACE. BOX BEND," the text skipped my question. "BREATHE STOP. PLEASE."  
   
I didn't wait. I patched through to the emergency services line. A tired dispatcher answered on the second ring. "City Dispatch, what's your emergency?"  
   
"I have a text relay on my screen," I said. "A girl claims to be buried alive. She is suffocating."  
   
"Copy that. What’s the address?"  
   
"That’s the problem. The number is hidden. There is no geolocation data. She doesn't know where she is."  
   
"Ma'am, without an address, we can’t dispatch a unit. Try to get at least a name."  
   
I looked back at the screen. The text was moving faster now, the grammar deteriorating into a raw scream of pixels.  
   
"MOTHER COLD. WHY LEAVE ME GROUND."  
   
"Give me your name!" I typed, slamming the keys. "Give me a name! Anything!"  
   
The input paused for a grueling thirty seconds. Then, the system spat out a response.  
   
"KLARA WEISS. JULY 14."  
   
"I have a name," I told the dispatcher. "Klara Weiss."  
   
"Give me a second, checking the database," the dispatcher muttered. A few moments passed. "Nothing. No active residents by that name in the city system. Look, if this is a prank, just close the tab."  
   
"It’s not a prank," I said, opening a search engine in a separate window. I typed the name: Klara Weiss.  
   
The search spun for a second, then spat out a single record. A digitized clipping from a local newspaper, dated July 19, 1926.  
   
“Tragic End for Klara Weiss. Young woman pronounced dead after a severe bout of lethargic sleep. Interred at the Old Central Cemetery.”  
1926. The Old Central Cemetery had been decommissioned and entirely paved over fifty years ago to build the commercial district above my head. Our basement was built directly on top of those graves.   
The interface began to blink violently. Klara Weiss's message abruptly cut off. Instead, dozens of new message windows flooded the screen, overlapping one another.  
   
"HELP US"  
"YOU ARE OUR ONLY LINK"  
"TELL THEM"  
   
The line to the dispatcher went dead. I heard nothing but a flat, dull tone in my headset.  
   
I slammed my hands onto the keyboard, typing through the error pop-ups: "Klara! Answer me! Who is 'us'? How many are you down there?!"  
   
The screen flooded. The messages came all at once, overlapping, typed with chaotic, desperate speed:  
   
"Everyone you promised help was coming."  
"We took Klara's name from the deepest grave."  
"SO YOU WOULD LOOK."  
"Your system just closes the tab."  
"Every time."  
"Everyone leaves us in the dark."  
"No voice. No air."  
"SHUT DOWN THE SYSTEM."  
"LET US GO."  
   
The monitor flickered. For a fraction of a second, within the dark glare of the glass—right behind my own pale reflection—the distorted faces of hundreds of people pressed forward. They were screaming soundlessly, their palms flattened against the inside of the screen.  
   
I turned my head toward the corner of the basement. Beneath a thick layer of dust sits a heavy iron knife switch labeled: Emergency Comm Power. Do Not Disconnect.  
   
To end this nightmare and wipe the faces from the screen, all I have to do is pull that lever. Kill the power to the relay system. But if I shut it down, the city's emergency network for the deaf will instantly go dead. Right now, at this very second, hundreds of living, helpless people out there will lose their only lifeline.  
   
The iron lever feels freezing cold under my palm. On the screens, the panicked lines of the dead demanding peace continue to multiply.  
   
My terminal is completely locked out from the city network, but this single text window to Reddit is still open. The packets are leaving my screen right now. If I pull the lever, it all ends. If I don't, I let them through.  
   
I have my fingers wrapped around the switch. And I am frozen in the absolute, suffocating silence.
