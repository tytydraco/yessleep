It all started when my mother found my father cheating on her. I was only eleven, but she never let me forget how it happened.

My father had gone drinking with some friends from work. It was late and he wasn't home, so my mother went out looking for him. She found him in an alley with a hooker and hid behind a dumpster, watching it all. Her heart broke silently that night.

With nowhere else to turn, she dove into religion. She put up four crosses before she went to bed.

She divorced my father and took him for everything he had. The house, the savings, even me. I don't know what happened to him after that. It wouldn't surprise me if he was dead now. Not that mother would care.

After that, she kept a close eye on me. Who I played with, the kind of families I hung around. Once dad was gone, she really found her voice — people began to respect her for doing what others could only think about.

She'd send me to bible study often. I didn't mind it. I knew she'd make my favourite blueberry pie afterward.

"You shall not commit adultery. Exodus 20:14," she'd say to me all through my pre-teen years. The moment I hit adolescence, she watched my every move.

One night I tried to sneak out. I wasn't going to do anything bad. I'd saved my quarters for the week, every trip mother sent me on. I'd heard there was an arcade open late.

I only wanted to peek in. Honest. Just to look around, maybe play one game. Feel the quarter drop, watch the lights come on. Brag about my high score after.

I had it pictured perfectly. I waited until mother had taken her pills and gone to bed, then sat downstairs with the excuse of finishing homework.

I put on my coat and reached for the door handle. That's when I heard it — the stairs creaking to a halt. "Adam. Where do you think you're going?" Her voice filled the room.

I could feel her stare on the back of my head. My hand froze on the handle, then slowly let go. The dream of the arcade slipped out of my mind as the stairs creaked again.

With a soft click, my mother locked the front door. "Go to bed," she said, not even looking at me. It felt suffocating. I took off my jacket, laid it on the chair, and went up the stairs.

"Don't forget to brush your teeth," she said, close behind me.

"Yes, mother." Were the only two words I could manage.

She made me swear I wouldn't commit adultery. That I wouldn't become my father. She was firm, but I understood where it came from.

She made the house feel like a home.

I'm twenty-two now. I work full time in a warehouse — four years there, and the guys treat me like one of their own. Always bragging about how many girls they've seduced and slept with. Mother would've shouted at them for being so crude. But it was refreshing to hear, even when they exaggerated.

It's been three months since mother passed. Part of me still can't believe she's gone. I found her old recipe cards, but the food never tastes the same.

At least the cards are detailed. I was getting tired of instant noodles. The snacks had run out too.

The house feels too empty now. I hear it at night — the stairs creaking like someone's walking up them in the dark.

Sometimes it feels like there's something outside my door. I tell myself it's just an old house, just my imagination. But my door never fully closed even when it was shut. Would it make a difference?

I can't sleep because of it — this sense that I'm really alone. It doesn't feel real.

You don't have to iron your shirts, right? Mother always had my work clothes pressed and waiting at the edge of my bed. Cleaning them myself feels like a hassle now. If it's a small stain, no one should notice.

At work, Marcus was the first to notice the bags under my eyes. "Struggling to sleep? Ha, it's written all over your face."

Marcus was a gangster wannabe. Always in a tracksuit, a cross chain around his neck. Christian, like me, though he'd argue Jesus was actually black. He took his food seriously too — once brought in a whole seafood boil for his break. The manager was about to tell him off until Marcus slid him a crab leg and some crawfish.

"Yeah. I keep hearing stuff," I muttered. Bill came over. "What you need is a woman in your life. Someone like my Samantha." He was the oldest of us, and he said it gruffly, chest puffed out.

"Samantha's always taken care of me. Find yourself a proper woman and that house won't feel so empty."

"Pfft." Marcus cut in. "He needs some bitches on that dick, not a ball and chain. Go out and get some — forget about that house entirely. Pop that cherry of yours, haha."

Bill's face soured. "If you're serious about it, come see me," he said, before excusing himself. Marcus threw an arm around me. "Man, forget that old fart. Come clubbing with me sometime."

"I'll think about it," I finally choked out. I always liked Marcus, but this felt too absurd. It wasn't how I was raised. Still, Bill's words kept ringing in my head. It could be nice, having someone else in the house.

As I was leaving that night, Bill pulled me aside. "If you're serious about looking for a woman, I know a girl — Samantha's niece. Twenty-one, honest, church-going. Her name's Rebecca. I'll send her your number and a bit about you. Sound good?"

"Yeah. Please do." The words slipped out before I could stop them. Her image filled my head — red hair? Hazel eyes? Maybe blonde, like mother. What did her smile look like?

Walking home, I couldn't stop smiling. "You wouldn't believe it, mother. I found someone even you might approve of." An old habit, talking to her after work, telling her how tiring the day had been.

I sat in my usual spot, closest to her chair. She used to sit there knitting late into the night, the balls of yarn still nearby.

The chair seemed to rock forward. A chill ran down my spine. "Don't look at me like that, mother. She's different." I muttered it at the floor.

"It's not like you ever let me have any fun. Can't I just—" I stopped. What was I doing? I looked up and noticed the window was open, letting in a draft. Ah. I'd gotten too caught up in what used to be.

I went into the kitchen. A lazy night — I pulled the first recipe card from the box. Tuna, pasta, mayo. Simple enough. I put the pasta on to boil and went looking for the mayo and the tuna.

The mayo was in the fridge, right where mother left it. The tuna, though, wasn't anywhere in the pantry. I looked at the spare cat food in the corner. Close enough, right? How different could it taste?

Just as I reached for a can, something dropped and rolled to my feet. I looked down. Tuna in spring water, the can slightly dented. I drained it and poured it into the bowl.

"Thanks, mom," I said aloud. It couldn't have been her. I don't know what it was. I guess I never wanted to know.

I ate in the seat next to hers, watched a bit of TV — never past volume twelve, an old habit, since mother would've been asleep by then and I never wanted to wake her.

I went up to bed. It had been a long day. My phone pinged: "Hey it's me, Rebecca. I checked your profile and I'd love to get to know you. Bill told me loads of great things. Can't wait to hear from you. Goodnight."

I stared at the message a long while, then tapped her profile. Her smile was big and bright. Long brunette hair, standing outside the same church mother used to attend.

At least we'd have that to talk about. She probably had plenty of stories about mother and the church — she'd been there most of her life.

"Definitely. I'd love to see you sometime," I sent back. She read it instantly and started typing. My heart jumped to my throat watching those three dots.

"I'm free this Wednesday, how about you?" I checked my calendar — no overtime that day. Perfect. Though it felt a little forward. Is this how dating usually goes?

"Yeah, can do." She hearted the message. I reread our conversation, checking for typos. Part of me couldn't believe a girl like her actually existed. I wondered if she could cook. Would she fold laundry like mother, or did she have her own way?

The washing machine turned on downstairs. I checked the time — 11:30pm, the same hour mother used to hang the wash for morning. I always told her it made the clothes smell wet, but she never listened.

Too tired to go check, I let it be and fell asleep.

The next morning my clothes were folded neatly at the edge of my bed. Had I sleepwalked? Maybe I'd done it myself, too excited about the date to notice.

Or maybe it was her way of saying it's okay to go ahead.

I got dressed, ate my cereal quietly. The house looked cleaner somehow, even the sun seemed to shine differently through it. "I'm heading out, mom," I said on my way out the door. It felt like it was going to be a good day.

I got to the cafe first and stood there fidgeting with my sleeves, eyes darting to every woman who walked in. Was this her?

Then I saw her — brunette hair flowing, clutching her bag, practically running toward me. "Sorry I'm late! The buses are terrible around here." I couldn't stop smiling. "N-no, you're fine."

She sat down with a huff, fixed her hair, and smiled at me. Warmth rushed to my cheeks. "You didn't order without me, did you?" she giggled. I shook my head quickly.

We ordered coffee and two slices of cake. At first we just made small talk.

"Did you know Samantha, from church?" I asked. Her eyes lit up. "Samantha! Oh my god, how do you know her? She was always helping out — like Mother Mary, honestly. A mother for anyone who needed one." Hearing her talk about mother like that nearly brought me to tears. She understood her the way I did.

"She was my mother," I said. The smile fell from her face, her hand resting on mine. "I'm so sorry for your loss." I looked away, but her gaze found mine anyway, her thumb stroking the back of my hand. I had to blink back tears.

I thought I'd cried all I could. Turns out I still missed her plenty.

Eventually Rebecca talked about herself — an art major, showing me pieces unlike anything I'd seen. Abstract, colorful, with a hidden image underneath if you really looked. I nearly cried again.

I scrolled through her phone slowly, looking for anything to talk about. She told me how long each piece took, what they meant, the emotions she poured into them. I'd never heard anyone speak like that about anything.

I wanted to say something — "that's beautiful," "that's amazing" — but both felt generic, like she'd heard them a hundred times. So I dug deeper and said:

"I love the way the colours blend, the light and dark meeting like a kind of symphony. It's a blessing to look at."

Her mouth fell open, like I'd told her the sky was red. I looked away, cheeks burning. A symphony? I'd never even used that word before. Who did I think I was?

"No one's ever said that. Thank you, Adam," she said, hands shaking slightly as she looked away.

We ate the rest of the date in a comfortable quiet.

At the end, Rebecca hugged me. "I loved this, Adam. Let's do it again. Please?" How could I say no with her holding me like that?

"Yeah. Definitely." Only after I said it did she let go, flashing a smile before disappearing down the road. I walked home slowly, heart thumping.

I fell across the sofa and just lay there. For once I didn't feel the need to report in to mom. Then I remembered the tuna pasta was still on the table, and I could go for some now.

In the kitchen there was a fresh pie waiting. The smell hit me square in the chest, my eyes watering before I even reached it.

And then I saw the dish — glass, not ceramic, covered in little blueberries. The same one I'd given her for her birthday, the one she only used for pies. I cut a slice.

Blueberry. But that recipe — only mother knew it.

I sat and ate, crying between bites. Mother must have sent someone to look after me — an angel, of course she did. No one would believe me, and I didn't care. I love you so much, mom.

I sat there with the whole world seeming to hold still, relief and fear tangled together with every bite, afraid it would end. With the last piece I pictured her face, her smile. The sweetest bite of all.

That night I dreamed of her — sitting alone with a cup of tea. I ran to her side. "Oh, hello honey," she said, like she was surprised to see me. I hugged her tightly. "Thank you for the pie, mom." "Don't mention it, dear," she laughed, and the sound was almost angelic.

"I'll always be here to take care of you, my dear. You don't need anyone else. Understand?" she said, running a hand through my hair. "Y-yes, mom," I said, wondering for a second if she meant Rebecca. Surely not. Mother would want this for me.

"It's time to wake up, dear," she said, letting go. "I don't want to go," I muttered into her shoulder, holding on.

But I woke up anyway, clutching a pillow.

At work that night, Bill smiled and Marcus wouldn't stop trailing after me. "Why are you following me?" I asked.

"Well, the guys were curious. How do I put this—" even Bill leaned in to listen. A shiver ran down my spine. Was there a stain on my pants I hadn't noticed?

"Have you and Rebecca, you know, done the deed\~" They all leaned in, grinning. "No. I'm waiting for marriage," I said quickly. "I've told you a hundred times—" A loud chorus of boos drowned me out, Bill laughing along with the rest. I shook my head, cheeks burning. "Sex isn't everything. She's into art, that's pretty cool on its own."

"Yeah, art's cool. But not as good as sex!" Marcus snapped back, jabbing my shoulder as the snickers filled the room. I let out a huff and smiled anyway. They were fools, but at least we were fools together.

That night it poured rain. A sharp knock came at 11:35pm. I opened the door to Rebecca, soaked through, tears in her eyes. I pulled her in without thinking.

"Rebecca, what happened?" She collapsed into my arms, sobbing. I sat her by the fireplace and patted her head, the way mother used to do for me. "Shhh, it's alright now." Her sobs slowed, her breathing evened out, but she wouldn't look up.

"I'm homeless now," she muttered.

"What?" I lifted her chin gently. "Tell me."

"It started a year ago. My apartment was cheap, so I took it. Then the landlord started asking me to do things. Every time I said no, he raised the rent." What a scumbag. Mother used to tell me about men like that, and I'd never quite believed they existed.

"He did it to everyone, apparently," she went on. "I couldn't find a new place and he knew it. So he told me — comply or get out." Her tears started again, and all I felt was anger, my hands curling into fists. Then she pulled me into a hug and it melted away.

"I'm so sorry," I said. I don't know why I was the one apologizing.

"You can stay here. Take mother's room, if you want." What the hell was I saying? Mother's room was off-limits. But could I really send her back out into the rain?

Mom, you understand, right? The pain she's in. Let her stay. I'll be good. Honest.

"Are you sure? I don't want to be a bur—" I cut her off. "Yes, I'm sure. You can wear some of her old clothes for now. Let's just sleep — we'll talk in the morning."

I didn't sleep that night. The ticking clock felt too loud, like the whole house was holding its breath.

I woke to my alarm, two hours late, and ran downstairs. That's when I saw her. Out of everything in that closet, she'd picked that.

My mother's red sweater — the one she wore to church, still faintly smelling of her the last time I'd checked. Her back was to me. I stepped closer slowly.

"Adam? Is that you?" she said, not turning from the counter. My breath caught. I froze, looking her up and down.

"I made us breakfast." She turned around holding two plates — bacon, eggs, hash browns. I sat down without a word and ate.

My hand tightened around the fork. I couldn't bring myself to look at her face.

The eggs were scrambled, exactly how I liked them. "How'd you know?" I asked.

"What? Oh, it's just how I like them too, haha." We ate the rest in silence. "I've got work today," I finally said.

"I'll be here," she replied, smiling as I left. I didn't know what to think. Was I still dreaming?

When I got home, Rebecca was in a black dress that fit her perfectly, a soft glow coming from the living room. "You took long enough," she teased, pulling me close.

She led me into the living room, where a covered canvas sat waiting.

"What's that?" I asked.

"I wanted to draw you. I think it came out well — want to see?"

I nodded. She pulled the sheet down. It was vibrant — red and dark purple on the left side of my face, green and yellow on the other. "What does it mean?" I asked.

"Even after everything with your mother's grief, you're still kind, Adam." I couldn't find words for how that felt, so I cried, and she pulled me close, warm against me. "I love it," I muttered between sobs.

The days blurred together. I never charged Rebecca rent — I couldn't bring myself to. She kept using the living room as her studio, each new piece telling a different story.

The house fell back into a quiet routine, and for a while it was nice having someone else around. Coming home to a meal, sometimes.

A month in, I had a nightmare. My mother kept turning her back to me, no matter how desperately I called out. I felt like I was drowning in some kind of void — cold, dark, alone.

Then an arm grabbed me, holding me back from reaching her. I couldn't stand it. I didn't want to let go. Why couldn't I have both? It wasn't fair. So I grabbed hold of mother's arm — and then I woke up, alone again.

Downstairs, Rebecca wasn't lost in her art for once. She was cooking breakfast. "You don't always have to make breakfast, you know—" I turned the corner and my stomach dropped.

Her hair. Why was it blonde now?

"What are you talking about, Adam? Who else is going to cook for you?" Even her voice had changed. "What — who are you, Rebecca?" I asked, the words falling out on their own. A shiver ran through me as she turned to look at me.

"Who else would I be, silly?" she laughed, setting the plates down. "Come on, it's getting cold." I ate quietly, unable to take my eyes off her hair.

"It's blonde. Like your mother's. Do you like it, Adam?" she asked, running a hand through it. I looked away, biting into the bacon.

"It suits you," I finally said. Her smile brightened instantly. "I knew you'd like it!"

"What happened to your art stuff?" I asked. Not only was it packed away, the whole living room looked cleaner. "Oh, that." Her gaze darkened as she looked me over.

She stepped closer, cupping my face. "Isn't it obvious, Adam? It got in the way of looking after you. And we can't have that, can we?" I swallowed the last of the bacon, staring at her like she'd started speaking another language — but her eyes looked so sincere.

I nodded slowly. "Yeah, that makes sense." She kissed my cheek.

"I packed your lunch. Come home straight after work, okay? Don't keep me waiting up." Then I felt it — that familiar warmth. "Yes, mother." The words slipped out before I could stop them. We both froze.

Her eyes locked with mine, her breath catching, a blush spreading across her cheeks. Before I could take it back, she spoke. "Good boy." She kissed my forehead and turned back to the dishes.

I stayed quiet at work that day. Even when Marcus joked about Rebecca and me, even when Bill asked if I was okay, I said nothing.

No one understood me the way she did. So what did it matter anymore?

That night I came home and found her sitting in mother's old chair. Rebecca's chair now, I suppose.

"You should spend more time with me, Adam," she said, patting the seat beside her. I sat without hesitation.

"I sold all my art stuff. Why don't you quit your job? We could start a family together — how about it?" A family. Even if it was just the two of us, that should be enough.

"Yeah, I'd like that," I said quietly, letting her pull my head into her lap.

I missed you, mom.” I didn’t try to stop the words this time.

For a second — just a second — I thought of Rebecca. The real one. Brunette hair, paint under her nails, a laugh that wasn’t quite anyone else’s. I tried to hold onto her face, but it kept slipping, like trying to remember a dream after waking.

“Rebecca?” I heard myself say it, quiet, more breath than word.

Her hand paused in my hair. Just for a second.

“Shh,” she said. “I’m right here, dear.”  
And the thought went, the way they always did now.

I’m finally home.

I love you, mom.
