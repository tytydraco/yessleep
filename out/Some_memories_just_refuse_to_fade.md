Why do I leave everything to the last minute? Why do I make things so hard for myself? Do I hate myself? I'm an adult, and I've always asked myself these questions. A sensible, responsible person would think: “I'll do my best!”, “Why put off until tomorrow what I can do today?”. But not me. You're probably thinking: “You idiot! Just change your attitude and be more responsible with yourself and others who depend on you!”. And I know you're right.

I think about it, I reflect, and then I reflect some more. And I come to the conclusion: “if only it were that easy.” Sometimes I imagine what it's like to be a normal person, to interact, to exist in society. But when I observe those behaviors, I honestly change my mind and prefer to be the way I am.

Many might read this and assume they understand me; many perhaps won't. But I honestly believe not everyone would want to understand—maybe no one would. If they only knew how hard it is, how exhausting, how entirely draining. But, strictly between us, it has its advantages. One of them is being able to tell my brain, “forget this, it's not important.” It became so habitual that it already knows what to forget and what to remember. But there are some things that, no matter how hard I try, it refuses to erase. The kitten, for example.

In my memory, I don't see myself as me. I am just a small, divided body. The poor little thing was running out of breath, her pupils shrinking until she passed out and went limp. May panicked, trying to wake the kitten up by shaking her, petting her, and looking around to make sure no one was watching. When she realized the kitten was breathing again, she was relieved; she thanked God she hadn't killed such a beautiful, innocent creature. But then, Cloe took control of the situation. May cried, terrified, as she watched her own hands, now guided by Cloe, reach out to try and strangle the poor animal once more. She wanted to see it die; she wanted to feel its neck snap. May fought internally, battling her own muscles to stop Cloe. And finally, she succeeded. She let go of the kitten, who immediately ran away. They were both 7 years old, sharing the same space, neither having absolute control over anything, and praying their parents wouldn't find out what they had just done.

Things like that are hard to forget. Back then, Cloe was the one who ruled our mind. May had almost no power at all. Cloe only thought about how useless everyone else was, and how she could manipulate them into doing whatever she wanted. It stayed that way for years on end. The same shell, two existences. May, always shy, couldn't look people in the eye; while Cloe, deep inside that very same mind, thought of countless different ways to kill them all.

It all started in 1987.

Verônica was walking out with her newborn baby in her arms, accompanied by her best friend, Isabel. Her husband, Jon, was waiting in the car. The maternity ward was just one wing of a much larger hospital complex—a place that received everyone from walk-ins to complex, life-threatening emergencies.

At the exact moment Verônica stepped through the exit doors, an emergency transport sped up to the entrance, unloading multiple victims. They were horribly mutilated bodies—missing arms, legs, some even decapitated. Almost all of them were already dead, casualties of a catastrophic traffic collision.

Verônica froze, paralyzed by the sheer terror of the sight.

"Aaaaah! Oh my God, somebody help me!" she screamed, feeling the edges of her vision darken as she thought she was going to pass out right there.

Quickly, her friend Isabel grabbed her arm, pulling her away as fast as she could.

"Don't look! Don't look at it! Let's just go!" Isabel pleaded, terrified that such a gruesome, sorrowful scene would permanently scar her friend, who was already so fragile with a newborn baby in her arms.

Behind them, a swarm of doctors and nurses rushed out into the chaos to begin the rapid triage of the few survivors.
