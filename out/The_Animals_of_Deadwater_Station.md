Note: this story is set in outback Australia, enjoy.

If you're reading this, don't bother trying to find Deadwater Station on a map. Even if it is still there, I wouldn't recommend visiting. Nobody comes to Deadwater Station unless they have a reason. After what happened, nobody had one. Do not stop there. Do not ask the locals about what happened.And whatever you do… Do not look the animals in the eyes.

Deadwater Station wasn't much of a town. Deadwater Station wasn't much of a town. One school. One fuel station. One general store. One road in. One road out. And everyone knew that if you left, you didn't come back unless you had to.

Families inherited jobs the same way they inherited surnames. The Blythes ran the general store. The McDermonts owned the fuel station. Mrs Penbrooke had worked in the clothing shop for so long that most people couldn't remember anyone else behind the counter.

My family were cattle farmers. We'd been raising cattle on the same stretch of red dirt for generations. Granda used to joke that the soil got its colour from Callaghan blood. Later, Grammy added horses to the station and started breeding them too. Looking back, maybe we should have stuck to cattle.

Maybe we should have left Deadwater Station altogether.

I loved that part of my life. It was the only thing tethering me to Deadwater Station. My daily routine since I left school was to rise at 4, feed the dogs, cats and myself, before opening up the barn for work. It was the same every day and I loved it. Standing next to my horse Banjo, watching the sun rise in the red dust tinted sky, making it look like a striking, apocalyptic artpiece.

Banjo knickered as he nudged my pocket - I was too busy watching the sky that I forgot to give him his morning Carrot Tax. I should have noticed the birds had stopped singing as I moved. Instead, I smiled as I handed him his half. He wasn’t the prettiest horse, being the fifth generation from my Grammy stallion. He was chunky, broad shouldered and a dark bay with large hooves and a head that could knock you over in one swing. But he was my chunky boy and I loved him with my soul. 

‘We're going to move the cattle closer to the contamination bay today.’ I said as I scratched his neck. ‘The cattle are going to get their bath, and you will tomorrow.’ 

Banjo looked at me with soft eyes as he kept munching on the fat carrot. Looking back, I wish that was all he'd wanted from me.  

‘Not one thought behind those eyes.’ I chuckled to myself as I wandered back into the tack room to grab his gear. Grammy had turned the old shearers shed into a make-shift stable a few decades ago and it still stood tall, with mud-stained beams holding up old tin slabs, and a sawdust floor that attempted to muffle my steps on the old half timber half tin floor that had been patched and repaired for years on end. The little tack room was small but had years of history. On one wall was the old schoolhouse chalkboard with a calendar drawn on it. It was bordered with photos of the station over the years, from the first settlers to last year when my little brother graduated primary school. The other walls had hand-made wooden tack holders on them. In one corner was a stack of old buckets and some food bins for the horses' meals.

‘Your mum wants you to help clear out the attic tomorrow.’ Dad said as he brushed past me to grab his saddle.

‘We cleared it out last year.’ I whined, I’m too old for whining, I know. But when it comes to house cleaning, is anyone really too old for it? 

‘There are some things that Granda has left unopened, and since he passed away, Grammy would like to see what he has been hiding in some chests.’ Dad pulled down my saddle and gave it to me.

‘So it's like a mystery.’ I grinned.

‘Yes, but not like those silly haunting ghost story mysteries that you like so much, Kim.’ Dad rolled his eyes.

Unlike the rest of my family, I ventured outside the world of book classics and into modern horror. I would order a book online every month, and by the time the new book was in the post office, I would be wrapping up the old one. I followed Dad out into the yard to start on the day's work. 

I should have noticed odd things, the fact that the day before counted sixty-three cattle in the paddock. Today, there were sixty-four. Dad insisted there had always been sixty-four. Maybe he was right. I don't think he was.

The next day started much the same. Rise at 4, feed the dogs, cats, and myself, open the barn, watch the sunrise with Banjo while he scouted me for carrots, but instead of tacking him up and helping Dad herd the cattle, I made my way back into the house to help Mum and Grammy. 

The original homestead was built in 1890, but burnt down from a fire in 1925, killing everyone but my Granda’s Grandfather. While the house built in 1925 was good for its time, it slowly became too small. My Granda then extended it with his sons, my Dad’s older brothers, until it became the house it is today. 

I made my way through the home to the stairs up to the attic. It was originally built as a little home, with a double bed and kitchenette and bathroom for my parents when they got married. However when Mum got pregnant with me, she was finding it too hard to walk up and down the stairs. The room ended up turning into the dumping room. 

Mum and Grammy were already up there dragging out some chests from the back corner. A thin layer of dust covering everything. I sneezed as I walked past the old spindle in the corner.

‘This is his brother's stuff.’ Grammy said. ‘Its an 1880s elm trunk passed down for years. It was all of his stuff when he left for Melbourne.’ 

‘I remember Dad talking about his death, it was tragic.’ Mum shook her head as she opened another chest.

‘What happened?’ I asked as I walked up to them. Mum jumped and turned around. 

‘Nothing dear.’ 

‘He died in a horse riding accident.’ Grammy interrupted. ‘He was doing something and the horse suddenly went crazy and threw him off and trampled him to death.’

I blinked, that was not what I was expecting. Gun fight? Yes. Car crash? Yes. Freak horse accident? No. 

‘That was also the year that his Dad and brother died by a cattle stampede.’ Grammy continued, not bothering to turn around. ‘One of them died from a fractured skull and the other one died from internal bleeding, I can't remember which was which though.’

The way Grammy said this so casually left a chill down my spine.

‘Grammy, Kim doesn't need to hear this.’ Mum scolded.

‘Yes I do! It’s interesting.’ I corrected Mum. 

‘Kimberly no.’ Mum gave me the look. I backed down.

‘Anyway, Kimmy here.’ Grammy stepped back to reveal the open chest. ‘You go through this, sort things out in piles of paper, clothing, toys and other stuff like that.’ 

I moved up behind her to see the open chest and started to rifle through its dusty moth ball scented contents. I pulled out a few items, namely a battered felt Akubra with sweat stains, leather stockman's gloves like my Grandas, stiff with age, rusty spurs wrapped in an old handkerchief and a faded blue work shirt with hand-sewn repairs. A lot of these items I recognised, my Granda used to wear them too. More items were pulled out, stuff I’d seen before and stuff I hadn’t. A pocket knife sharpened nearly to nothing, matches in a tobacco tin, a compass with a cracked face, a pocket watch that stopped decades ago, and a box of .303 rifle cartridges that were swiftly confiscated by my fun-busting mother.

I then pulled out a tin box, the size of an A4 paper, and about 10 centimeters deep. It was dented and rusted, and took a bit of persuasion to open the lid. Once it popped open, dust flew into my face. The smell of musk and dirt filled my nostrils as I coughed and spluttered. The top few papers were photos of family and the station, letters from family folded and re-folded dozens of times, the old cattle station pay book from World War II, and maps of the stock routes that I had saved on my phone and ridden a dozen times now. 

A leatherbound book was at the very bottom. In gold foiling there was J. M. Callaghan scrawled across the front. I opened it and inside was black ink spread across the pages, smudged and bled. I flipped through the pages to see the last dozen or so were left blank. The last diary entry was put in for August 18th, 1971. The scribbles were barely legible, but I managed to make them out.

*18 Aug 71*

*I can’t take it here anymore, the animals, they’re all so weird. They just stand there and stare. It's so off putting. Michael doesn’t see it but Dad and Ma do. Michael swears up and down we are all going insane. I can’t stand it. The cattle, the cats, the dogs, they’ve all possessed. It's creepy as hell. I used the RAX to contact Mandy and she said she's got a room for me in Melbourne. I have booked the next train out of here in two days. The trip is said to last three days, but anything to escape this hellhole. Deadwater Station is not a town. It is a feeding ground. The animals are not sick. They are not possessed. They are waiting. I don't know what they are waiting for. But I know who they are waiting for.*

I thought Uncle Joseph was crazy. That's the worst mistake I've ever made.

‘Was Great Uncle Joseph ok Gram’ I asked, not taking my eyes away from the diary for fear the words would vanish.

‘What do you mean, Doll?’ Grammy asked, peering over my shoulder. ‘Oh that, well your Granda did always say he was a bit special. I think it was because of his time in Korea, but you never know with Vets what's going through their poor broken heads.’ She sighed before turning back. 

We spent the rest of the afternoon sorting and reading through old documents. My mind kept wandering back to what Great Uncle Joseph wrote, that all the animals were possessed. I wondered what that meant, I’d grown up here, I’d seen generations of cattle pass through and not one of them seemed possessed or crazy. I searched for every explanation I could find. Disease. Poison. Stress.Anything. Anything except the possibility that Joseph was right.

‘Kimmy, be a doll and go get us some tea?’ Grammy asked about three hours later. I politely agreed and headed down the creaky stairs to the kitchen. That's where I noticed Tux, our cattle dog. He was standing outside the flyscreen door staring at me with his pale blue eyes. 

‘Hi boy.’ I smiled at him. ‘Do you want to come in?’ I opened up the door to let him in. It wasn't unusual for Dad to let him wander at this time of day, Tux was an old man, encroaching on 15 he had seen his good years pass a while ago. 

Tux didn't move. He stood there, still as a log, staring at me. He was nearly blind but I could have sworn he was intently watching clear as day. 

‘Tux?’ I patted his head, he was warm and his fur moved with my touch, but he stayed frozen. My mind jumped back to the words of Great Uncle Joseph and his animal’s possession. At the time, I thought Tux was getting old. Looking back now, I think he was trying to warn me.

I shook my head, Tux had always been a weird dog, and Brett, my younger brother, was always teaching him new stupid tricks. This could be one of them. I delivered the tea to Mum and Grammy before being dismissed to go pick Brett up from school. Five minutes later Tux was still there. Same position. Same stare. I don't think he'd blinked.

Brett was definitely old enough to walk home himself. We didn’t live too far away, but I did what Mum told me to. I grabbed the keys to our dusty old unregistered Holden Rodeo and walked outside. It took me a moment before I realised that there was a large flock of birds sitting on the power line, they were all perched an even space apart staring towards me like I was a juicy warm.

It was two old brick buildings with five or six classes inside. Most of the years were mixed because the school didn’t have enough students for a full year group. Brett stood out the front of the gates chatting to his mate.

The Rodeo spluttered to a stop in front of him, the constantly broken radio shouting static over the car’s shoddy engine was enough to get anybody’s attention.

‘Hi.’ Brett said as he slung his backpack into the footwell of the car and jumped in.

‘How was your day?’ I asked as I turned around and headed home.

‘Boring.’ Brett slouched down into the seat. ‘Craig kept saying KFH was staring at him. He wouldn't shut up about it.’

‘KFH?’ I repeated.

‘Kentucky Fried Hamster, the class pet.’ Brett clarified as I turned around the only roundabout Deadwater Station. The name took me back a moment before I registered what Brett had said. 

‘Was KFH staring?’ I asked.

‘Uh yeah,’ Brett snarked, ‘I just said that. He was staring at Craig and wasn’t moving all day apparently.’

I would have paid more attention to what he said if I hadn’t have noticed the cattle in our fields. The cattle were standing along the fence. Every single one. Not grazing. Not moving. Just watching.

‘Brett.’ I adjusted my grip on the steering wheel, I was unsure if the sweat on my hands was nerves or the blistering summer heat.

‘What?’

‘Look at the cattle.’

He glanced up from his phone.

‘What about them?’ the words were thick with irritation

I looked back. They were grazing again. Every single one.

I kept my mouth shut for the rest of the ride, Brett was in his usual Tuesday grumpy mood. There was no point in the conversation. 

I tried to shake it off, but the feeling prevailed. For the next few days I noticed nothing, seeing a herd of cows staring without blinking, without grazing, and without reacting to my presence created an unsettling impression that they're imitating a cow's regular behavior —as if they're following instructions I couldn't hear. Their familiar faces suddenly seemed alien, and the longer I looked, the more I felt that they knew something I didn't. 

The feeling of unease that had slowly crept up my spine stayed for days, even though nothing of the sort happened again. I was trying hard to convince myself that it was all in my head, that I was making it up since reading Uncle Joseph’s letter, but I couldn’t. It was too eerie, too much of a coincidence to convince myself that it simply just happened. 

I kept looking over my shoulder at the animals, thinking that I could catch them in the act of being a statue, but I never could. I was afraid to talk to anyone about what had happened to me for fear of being thrown into the nuthouse. Animals staring at you? Not moving? Surely that was a case of the crazies. But I wasn’t crazy, it *had* happened. I’d never been so sure of anything.

Days passed and nothing happened, I didn’t see Tux or the cows acting weird, and Brett didn't comment on KFH staring at anyone in class. I wish I could say that I almost forgot about it, but I couldn't. Their beady eyes burrowing into mine like if they stared long enough my soul would suck into theirs, and we’d become one. 

‘Did you hear what happened to poor old Elmer Blythe?’ Grammy asked, jumping me out of my dinner-endused thoughts.

‘No?’ Mum responded as she had a mouthful of mash potato.

‘Poor old thing got attacked by his dog and died this morning. It was on the radio.’ Grammy stated as though it wasn’t big news. This town was so small we had a radio, I loved the town but sometimes it felt too small. ‘According to his wife, it kept stalking him and when she left the house it attacked, got his artory in his leg. You know what it's like at that old age, everything is so thin and fragile, even a paper cut can bleed like crazy.’

‘Did it get his leg?’ Brett asked, half in shock and half in awe. Usually Mum didn't allow this type of morbid discussion at the table.

‘Elmer bled to death, right there on the kitchen floor.’ Grammy pointed a crooked finger to the floor.

‘Grammy, that’s enough.’ Dad said sternly. ‘That dog was trouble, they should have put him down ages ago. Killing cattle and sheep, attacking other dogs. The Blythes sure learned their lesson.’

‘That dog was a rescue, Devin.’ Grammy lectured. ‘Stop blaming it.’

I sat there staring at my half empty dinnerplate. Mr Blythe was killed by his own dog, he bled out. Mr Blythe loved that dog with his whole heart, and it turned on him so quickly. And it was stalking him? There has to be a connection to the other things that have been happening.

I didn't sleep well that night.

The wind scratched at my bedroom window with the branches of the old gum tree outside, and every time I closed my eyes I saw those cattle again, standing shoulder to shoulder along the fence line, staring.

When I finally drifted off, I dreamed of eyes. Hundreds of them. Cows. Dogs. Cats. Horses. All staring. Waiting.

The next morning, I woke before my alarm. The house was silent. Too silent. Usually by four o'clock I could hear Dad moving around the kitchen or the distant barking of the dogs outside. Not today. I pulled on my boots and stepped outside. The dawn sky glowed red above the paddocks.

Banjo was the only living thing on that property I trusted completely.

That's why in the morning he stopped acting like my horse scared me more than anything else. Banjo stood by the stable gate. Watching me. He didn't like his carrot. Didn't paw the ground. Just stared. A chill crawled up my neck.

‘Banjo?’

The horse blinked. Then suddenly lowered his head and began grazing as though nothing had happened. I stood there for several seconds before forcing myself to move. I never told anyone.

I wish I had.

Three days later, old Mrs Penbrooke died. The entire town heard about it before lunchtime. According to the police, she had gone outside to feed the chickens before sunrise. Her grandson found her near the coop. The chickens had surrounded her body. Not pecking. Not feeding. Just standing there. Watching

The story spread through Deadwater Station like wildfire. Some people blamed a heart attack. Others blamed age. Nobody could explain why every chicken on the property had been gathered around her. Or why they reportedly refused to move when people approached.

For the first time in my life, I saw genuine fear in the town. It took us too long to understand what was happening. The animals never attacked first. They watched. They waited. And when they were finished watching… someone died.

People started keeping animals locked away. The radio station spent days discussing it. Dad called everyone idiots. Mum stopped listening altogether. Grammy just became quieter.

A week passed.

Then another.

Then Craig disappeared. The same Craig from Brett's class. The one who said KFH was staring at him. He vanished while riding his bike home. The search party found the bicycle beside Miller's Creek. One shoe. One broken helmet. KFH was standing next to them, blood covered his fuzzy coat like mud. No movement, no blinking, just standing. Waiting.

Nothing else.

Officially, Craig was listed as missing. Nobody in town uses that word anymore.. The tracker dogs refused to follow his scent. They would reach the creek bank and stop. Then stare into the water. Every single one.

The town held a vigil. Brett didn't speak for days afterwards. Neither did most of the kids. Then Doug the farmhand died. I found him. The old cattle hand was sitting on the veranda one morning. Peaceful. Like he'd simply gone to sleep. I cried harder than I care to admit.

Dad buried him beneath the pepper tree near the stable, his favorite place. For a while I thought things might finally return to normal. I was wrong. Two nights later, something killed all three of our barn cats. There wasn't much left to identify. Whatever attacked them had done so quickly. The strange thing wasn't the attack.

Foxes and feral dogs sometimes got into sheds. The strange thing was where we found them. All three cats had been arranged in a straight line facing the homestead. Facing my bedroom window. Nobody could explain that. Not even Dad.

That was the first time I saw fear in his eyes. The next death came during muster. We were moving cattle through the southern paddocks when one of the horses [panicked.It](http://panicked.It) belonged to Uncle Ross. A horse that had worked cattle for nearly fifteen years without incident. One moment it was walking quietly. The next it exploded.

Buck.

Rear.

Scream.

Uncle Ross hit the ground hard. Before anyone could reach him, three nearby cattle charged. Not like startled animals. Not like a stampede. Like they had chosen him.

Dad yelled. Men shouted. The horses scattered.

By the time they drove the cattle away it was too late. Nobody said much at the funeral. But everyone remembered the same thing. Witnesses swore the cattle stopped immediately afterwards. As though someone had flicked a switch. They simply stood there.

Staring.

Watching.

Waiting.

The old stories started spreading after that. Stories that hadn't been told in generations. People dug out journals. Letters. Newspaper clippings. And everywhere we looked we found the same pattern. Deadwater Station had suffered animal attacks before.

Not once. Not twice. Over and over. Every few decades. Every generation. The details changed. The animals changed. But the ending was always the same.

People died.

Then everything returned to normal. Until the next cycle. I remember sitting on the veranda one evening with Brett as the sun disappeared behind the hills. Neither of us spoke for a long time.

The cattle in the distant paddock stood perfectly still. Hundreds of them. Silent silhouettes against the red sky.

Then Brett quietly asked, ‘Do you think they know who's next?’

I wanted to laugh. I wanted to tell him he was being stupid. Instead I found myself staring back at the herd. And for the first time in my life, I wasn't sure they were animals anymore.

Far out in the fading light,  I counted the cattle.

There were sixty-four. There had always been sixty-four. Except now… There were sixty-five. And the new one was standing in the middle of the herd. Looking directly at me.
