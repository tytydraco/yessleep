Bill and I didn't stay on that porch a second longer than we had to.

The second he told me about the tracks down by the creek bed, we backed straight into the house, threw the deadbolts on both exterior doors, and started pulling the heavy curtains tight across every window. Bill’s place is an old brick ranch with a solid concrete basement built a hell of a lot sturdier than my clapboard farmhouse so we moved Sarah, Wyatt, and Sarah’s mother into an interior bedroom away from any outside glass.

By 6:30 PM, the sun dropped behind the Kentucky ridges, and the holler went pitch black.

Bill and I took up spots in the dark living room, sitting on opposite sides of the front bay window with the curtains cracked just an inch so we could watch the gravel driveway and the front pasture under the porch floodlight. For three hours, nothing moved. Just the steady tick of the grandfather clock in the hall and the low murmur of the women talking softly to Wyatt behind the closed bedroom door.

Then, just past ten o'clock, the floodlight flickered.  
Out near the edge of the yard, a massive shadow unfolded itself from the tall pasture grass, standing nearly seven feet tall with a thick smear of grey creek mud packed over its scarred left shoulder. As Bill raised his rifle toward the glass, the creature slowly lifted its good arm and pointed one clawed finger straight past the house toward the dark ridge timber behind the barn.

At that exact second, a heavy, metallic *thud* echoed off the shingles right above our heads.

Bill and I both froze, staring up at the ceiling. Heavy, rhythmic footsteps slow, deliberate, and massive walked across the roof and stopped directly above the hallway where Sarah and Wyatt were hiding. And from the dark pasture behind the house, two more sets of deep, rattling breaths answered from the woods.

They hadn't stayed in Ohio. All three of them were here, and they had the house surrounded.

The heavy footsteps on the shingles stopped directly above the hallway ceiling.

Bill didn't hesitate. He swung the barrel of his .30-30 upward, his finger resting just outside the trigger guard, while I backed slowly toward the hallway door, keeping my shotgun pointed at the front bay window where the wounded creature was still standing just past the floodlight.

"Get them into the basement," I whispered to Bill, keeping my eyes locked on the dark window glass. "Right now. Concrete walls, no windows."

Bill gave a sharp nod, keeping his rifle raised toward the ceiling as he backed down the hall. A second later, I heard the heavy deadbolt on the basement door click open, followed by Sarah’s muffled, terrified voice ushering Wyatt and her mother down the wooden stairs.

Just as the basement door swung shut, the floodlight outside the front window shattered with a sharp *pop*.

Darkness swallowed the front yard instantly. Before my eyes could even adjust, the wounded creature outside let out a low, guttural growl and the heavy weight on the roof shifted, leaping down onto the back deck with a thud that rattled the dishes in the kitchen cabinets.

I took the basement steps three at a time, landing on the cold concrete floor with my shotgun raised toward the top of the stairs.

Bill was already standing beside the furnace, his .30-30 aimed straight at the basement door. Behind him, huddled near the back corner behind a stack of canned goods and firewood, Sarah had her arms wrapped tight around Wyatt.

Upstairs, the sound of heavy steps thudded across the hardwood floors. Chairs scraped violently across the kitchen tile. Glass shattered. Something massive slammed against the hallway walls, testing every door frame until heavy claws scraped loudly down the outside of the basement door.

The wooden door bowed inward an inch under sheer weight, the steel deadbolt groaning against its weight.

Wyatt wasn't crying. While Sarah and her mother were trembling against the concrete wall, my eight year old boy was staring straight up at the top of the stairs with wide, steady eyes.

"Wyatt, get your head down," I hissed, racking a fresh slug into the chamber.

"They're not mad at you, Dad," Wyatt said quietly. His voice didn't shake at all.

I glanced back at him for half a second, my heart hammering in my throat. "What are you talking about? Keep down!"

"The big one with the hurt shoulder," Wyatt said, looking straight at me. "When I brought him the bowl of scraps by the hog pen back home... he didn't growl at me."

Upstairs, the scratching at the basement door suddenly stopped. The entire house went dead silent, as if whatever was standing on the other side of that oak door was listening to every word.

"Wyatt, not now" Sarah started, trying to pull him closer, but Wyatt held onto her sleeve and kept talking.

"He knelt down by the fence so he wasn't taller than me," Wyatt said, his voice trembling just a little now. "He smelled like wet leaves and cold dirt. He took the meat out of the bowl with his hands, and when he looked at me, he talked."

My blood went ice cold. "Talked? Wyatt, wolves don't talk."

"Not like people" Wyatt shook his head. "His mouth didn't move right. It sounded like wind blowing through dry cornstalks, real low in his chest. He pointed at the bowl, then he pointed at me, and he said *'Mine.'*"

Bill looked over at me from beside the furnace, his face pale in the dim yellow glow of the single bare bulb hanging from the rafters

"Then he dropped something into my empty bowl before he walked back into the trees," Wyatt whispered, reaching into the front pocket of his jeans. "I kept it because I thought it was cool."

He opened his small hand. Sitting in his palm was a smooth, flat river stone, wrapped tightly in a braided strand of coarse black hair and a thin strip of dried sinew.

In the old Inuit stories the ones about a woman and a dog, and the children born between two worlds you don't ever accept a gift from what walks out of the dark, and you never feed it from your own table. Because once you share meat and take their mark, you aren't a stranger anymore. You belong to the pack.

At the top of the stairs, the basement door didn't rattle again. Instead, a slow, deliberate knock echoed off the wood. Three heavy raps, spaced evenly apart, just like a man standing on the front porch.

And from right behind the oak door, a deep, raspy voice sounding like grinding stones and dry leaves spoke a single word loud enough to rattle the rafters.

*"Boy."*
