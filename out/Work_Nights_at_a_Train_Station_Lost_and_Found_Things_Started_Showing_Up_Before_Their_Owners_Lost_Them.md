I work nights in the lost and found office under a major train station.  
And when I say under, I mean literally under it. Two floors below the platforms, past the staff lockers and the old service corridors, in a windowless storeroom about the size of a supermarket.  
Most people picture a little counter with a sad row of umbrellas behind it. That is what passengers see upstairs. Everything else comes to me.  
Suitcases. Phones. Passports. Children's toys. Toolboxes. Crutches. Dentures. Once, a prosthetic leg with a sneaker still tied to it.  
Somebody handed in a jar of human teeth last winter. Nobody claimed it.  
A few months before that, we got a wooden box containing an urn of ashes. Nobody claimed that either.  
After five years in that place, I honestly thought I had used up my ability to be surprised.  
I was wrong.  
My shift runs from 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. The public counter closes at midnight, so after that it is mostly just me, the hum of the ventilation, and whatever station staff drop through the receiving hatch.  
The hatch is a metal box built into the wall. Security opens it from the corridor, puts the item inside, and shuts their side. Then I open it from the storeroom. Simple. It keeps random people from wandering in, and it means I do not have to buzz a guard through every time someone finds a wallet on a platform.  
Every item arrives in a clear plastic bag with a paper tag. Where it was found. What time. Who found it. Train number, platform, room, whatever applies.  
Routine work. Boring work, usually.  
The first item came through three weeks ago.  
It was around 2:30 in the morning when I heard the outer door of the hatch bang shut.  
I waited for the usual knock or a voice over the intercom.  
Nothing.  
I opened my side.  
There was a small evidence bag inside. In it was a blue disposable lighter, the cheap kind sold at the tobacco kiosk near Platform 6.  
The tag said:  
Found: Waiting Room 2  
Time: 3:10 a.m.  
Employee: —  
I looked at the clock on the wall.  
2:37.  
That bothered me, but not much. People fill those tags out half asleep. They put the wrong platform, the wrong day, sometimes the wrong month. I figured whoever had found it meant 2:10.  
Then I noticed the scratch beside the striker wheel.  
I had the same lighter in my pocket.  
Not the same brand. Not the same color. The same lighter.  
A few days earlier, I had used my keys to rip open a plastic package and gouged a long white line into the side. There was also a little melted spot near the bottom where I had held it too close to a heater.  
The lighter in the bag had both marks.  
I took mine out and set them next to each other on the desk.  
Same scratches. Same amount of fuel. Same black smudge near the base.  
I turned them over.  
The tiny printed serial numbers matched.  
I know how that sounds. I knew how it sounded while I was standing there.  
My first thought was not ghosts or time travel or anything like that. My first thought was that the guys in security had found a very elaborate way to screw with me.  
They all knew I smoked. They had seen the lighter. Somebody could have bought another one and copied the damage. The serial number made that explanation harder, but not impossible. Take a photo. Print a fake label. Bored night-shift people will do ridiculous things for a laugh.  
So I logged it, put it on the shelf, and tried not to give them the reaction they wanted.  
At 3:10, I decided to walk up to Waiting Room 2 and see who was on duty.  
I got as far as the stairwell before my supervisor called. A designer suitcase had been entered under the wrong storage number, and the owner was coming first thing in the morning. I spent the next forty minutes hunting for it between thirty identical black suitcases.  
I did not think about the lighter again until close to four.  
I reached into my pocket for a cigarette.  
My lighter was gone.  
I checked the desk. The floor. The toilet. Every pocket twice. I even emptied the rubbish bin because I thought I might have tossed it away with a receipt.  
Nothing.  
I could have dropped it on the way to the stairwell.  
Which meant that at 3:10, it really might have been lying in or near Waiting Room 2, exactly where the tag said it would be found.  
I did not go looking for it.  
There was already one waiting for me on the shelf.  
Two nights later, the hatch slammed again.  
This time the bag contained a small silver key with red electrical tape wrapped around the head.  
I recognized it before I even picked it up.  
It was the key to my staff locker.  
I had wrapped that tape around it myself because the key looked almost identical to my apartment key.  
I checked my pocket.  
Mine was still there.  
The tag on the bag had the next day's date.  
Found: Staff Stairwell, Level -2  
Time: 5:42 a.m.  
Employee: —  
I called security.  
"Who just used the lost and found hatch?"  
The guard on duty, Pavel, sounded half asleep.  
"Nobody."  
"Someone just put a bag through."  
"Then someone used it."  
"Can you please check the corridor camera?"  
He sighed like I had asked him to inspect the roof in a thunderstorm, but he checked.  
A few minutes later he called back.  
"Nobody came near your door."  
"Did the hatch open?"  
"No."  
I was standing in front of it while we spoke. My side was still hanging open.  
"Pavel, I am looking at a package that came through it."  
"Maybe it was already in there."  
It had not been. I check the hatch when I start my shift, and I had opened it less than five minutes earlier for a wallet from Platform 4.  
I made him send me the footage.  
The corridor stayed empty the whole time.  
At 2:53, the image jumped. Just one ugly digital twitch.  
After that, there was a dark wet patch on the floor beneath the hatch.  
That was all.  
No person. No opening door. No bag.  
Just the wet mark, as though water had seeped through the wall.  
In the morning I showed everything to Irina, the woman who relieves me at six. She has worked there longer than I have and is the least superstitious person I know.  
She looked at the key, the lighter, and the camera clip.  
"Security is messing with you," she said.  
"How did they get my locker key?"  
"Copied it."  
"Why would anybody do all this just to scare me?"  
She shrugged and poured herself coffee.  
"Because men are idiots at night."  
That sounded enough like the world I knew that I let myself believe it.  
Before leaving, I checked my pocket again. My original key was still there.  
I went home, slept, and woke around three in the afternoon.  
The key was gone.  
I remembered hanging my work trousers over the chair. I searched every pocket. Moved the chair. Pulled the bed away from the wall. Checked the washing machine, even though I had not done laundry.  
Nothing.  
The following evening I came in early and took the duplicate key from the evidence bag.  
It opened my locker.  
My spare shirt was there. My mug. A half-eaten packet of biscuits that should probably have been thrown out months ago.  
Only one thing was missing.  
For years, I had kept an old photograph taped inside the locker door. Me and three friends on a fishing trip. Nothing sentimental, really. I just liked the picture.  
Now there was a pale rectangle where it had been.  
I asked Irina if she had moved it.  
She did not even know I kept a photograph in there.  
About an hour later, a new item appeared in the database.  
I did not enter it. Nobody did. I was looking at the screen when the line popped into existence.  
Item: Photograph  
Description: Four men standing beside a lake  
Found: Passage B-4  
Time: 1:15 a.m.  
The date was that day's date.  
It was not even three in the afternoon yet. 1:15 a.m. was more than ten hours away.  
The record had been created under my employee login.  
I printed it and took it to my supervisor.  
He barely looked up from his phone.  
"Database glitch."  
"It described the photograph from my locker."  
"Then somebody entered it earlier."  
"Under my account?"  
That got his attention for maybe three seconds.  
"Change your password."  
"And the future time?"  
"The clock is wrong."  
"The clock is synced to the station server."  
He gave me the look managers give when they want you to understand that your problem has become inconvenient.  
"Change your password and get back to work."  
So I did.  
At 1:15 that night, I was alone in the storeroom.  
The lights went out.  
Not completely. The emergency lamps came on, washing the shelves in that ugly green light that makes everything look dead.  
Then, somewhere near the back, somebody snapped their fingers.  
Once.  
A few seconds later, again.  
The second snap came from a different aisle.  
I turned on my phone flashlight.  
"Pavel?"  
No answer.  
"This isn't funny."  
Another snap. Farther away this time.  
I should have stayed at the desk. I know that. But when you are scared and still trying not to admit you are scared, you do stupid things to prove nothing is wrong.  
I walked between the shelves.  
The snapping stopped.  
At the very back is the unclaimed section, where we keep things until their legal storage period expires. Old coats, cracked suitcases, bags of damp clothes, boxes nobody wants to open.  
That part of the storeroom usually smells like dust and wet cardboard.  
That night it smelled like a lake.  
Not clean water. Mud, weeds, and that cold mineral smell you get when you pull something up from deep water.  
A plastic bag lay on the floor at the end of the last aisle.  
My photograph was inside.  
The tag said Passage B-4, 1:15 a.m.  
For a while, I only stood there with the flashlight pointed at it.  
The picture still showed the four of us: me, Kostya, Lyosha, and Anton. We were sunburned and grinning, with a pike Anton had caught hanging between us.  
But there was someone on the far side of the lake now.  
A dark figure standing near the tree line.  
It was small because of the distance, but it was unmistakably a person.  
There had been nobody there when the photo was taken. I remembered the empty shore. I remembered joking that we had the whole lake to ourselves.  
I picked up the bag.  
The figure's face was too far away to make out.  
Still, I had the sick certainty that it was looking directly at the camera.  
A finger snapped behind my right ear.  
I spun around so fast I almost fell.  
Nothing.  
The main lights came back a minute later.  
I took the picture home.  
Yes, I know that was stupid too. I should have left it there. Burned it. Thrown it under a train.  
But by then it was the only physical proof that something was happening. Without it, I was just a tired man on a night shift telling people that his lighter had arrived from the future.  
The next afternoon I met Kostya in a café and slid the photograph across the table.  
He stared at the dark figure.  
"Who's that?"  
"You see him?"  
"Of course I see him. Some fisherman, maybe."  
"There wasn't anyone on that shore."  
Kostya gave me a tired smile.  
"That trip was eight years ago. You don't remember every tree."  
I turned the photograph over.  
The date of the trip was written there in my handwriting.  
Under it, in darker ink, were three words I had never written.  
**THREE CAME BACK.**  
Kostya stopped smiling.  
There were four of us in the picture.  
I called Anton while we were still sitting there.  
His number was disconnected.  
I messaged him. The app said the account did not exist.  
Then I called Lyosha.  
"When did you last speak to Anton?"  
There was a pause.  
"Anton who?"  
I laughed because I thought he was being an asshole.  
He was not laughing.  
I gave him Anton's full name. Reminded him of the fishing trip. Told him about Anton dropping his phone into the lake and keeping it in a bag of rice for two days.  
Lyosha said the three of us had gone fishing.  
Three.  
I sent him the photograph.  
He called back almost immediately.  
"Where did you get that?"  
"My locker. Who is the man holding the fish?"  
"I was about to ask you."  
My hands started shaking badly enough that I had to put the phone on the table.  
"That's Anton."  
"I've never seen him before."  
That evening I drove to Anton's apartment.  
We had not spoken in almost a year, but I knew the building. I knew the floor. I knew the stupid brass number on his door was always hanging crooked.  
The door number was different.  
An old man answered. He said he had owned the apartment since the 1990s. He had never heard of Anton.  
I checked my contacts in the stairwell.  
Anton was gone.  
Every message. Every photo. Every group chat. Gone.  
Except for the fishing photograph.  
He was still in that one, smiling beside me with the pike in his hand.  
On the back it still said:  
**THREE CAME BACK.**  
I called in sick that night.  
My supervisor complained, but I did not care. I spent the evening checking old hard drives and cloud folders for anything that proved Anton had existed.  
There was nothing.  
The first night off passed quietly.  
On the second night, at exactly 3:10 a.m., my work app sent a notification.  
New item registered.  
Item: Mobile phone  
Description: Black smartphone, cracked lower-right corner, dark green case  
Found: Baggage Tunnel 3  
Time: 4:26 a.m.  
Date: tomorrow  
It described the phone in my hand.  
Same model. Same case. Same crack.  
I had never heard of Baggage Tunnel 3.  
I called Irina.  
"Is there an old baggage tunnel under the station?"  
She did not answer right away.  
"Who told you about that?"  
"So there is one."  
"There was. It was sealed before I started."  
"Where?"  
"Behind the oversized property section, I think. Why?"  
I told her about the database entry. Not everything. Just enough that she asked for a screenshot.  
I sent it.  
A few seconds later she replied:  
*There's nothing in the picture.*  
I opened the screenshot.  
Blank white screen.  
But when I switched back to the app, the entry was still there.  
That was the point where I decided I was done.  
I wrote a resignation letter. The plan was to hand it in the next morning, empty my locker, and never go below that station again.  
Around midnight, my phone died.  
The battery had been at sixty percent.  
I plugged it in. Nothing. Held every button. Nothing.  
Eventually I gave up and went to bed.  
I woke at 4:26 because the room was lit by the phone screen.  
A video was playing.  
The image was almost black. The camera moved through a narrow concrete corridor with rusted pipes running along both walls.  
Someone was breathing hard behind the camera.  
I knew the sound before I understood why.  
It was me.  
The phone tilted toward a corroded metal sign.  
BAGGAGE TUNNEL 3.  
I tried to pause the video. The screen did not respond.  
On the recording, I kept walking.  
At the far end of the tunnel stood a steel door. Somebody knocked from the other side.  
Three soft knocks.  
A pause.  
Three more.  
The version of me holding the phone moved closer.  
One of our lost and found tags had been tied to the door handle.  
The video was grainy, but I could read it.  
Item: Station employee  
Description: Male, 34, dark hair, work uniform  
Found: Baggage Tunnel 3  
Status: Awaiting collection  
Owner: \[my full name\]  
The video ended.  
The phone went black again.  
At sunrise I threw it into a rubbish container three neighborhoods away. I bought the cheapest button phone I could find and a new SIM card.  
Then I went to the station.  
I still do not know why. Habit, maybe. Or the stupid belief that if I quit properly and collected my things, whatever was happening would have to stop.  
My staff pass did not open the door.  
The guard scanned it and frowned.  
"This card is inactive."  
"I'm here to resign. I just need to get my things."  
"Identification."  
I handed him my passport.  
He looked at the photograph. Then at me. Then back at the photograph.  
"This isn't yours."  
"What?"  
"The man in the photo isn't you."  
I took it from him.  
It was my face. Obviously my face. Same scar in the eyebrow, same crooked nose, same photograph I had seen for years.  
I asked if he was joking.  
He called over another guard.  
The second man looked at the passport and shook his head.  
"Different person."  
"Then tell me what he looks like."  
Neither of them would answer.  
They told me to leave before they called the police.  
Outside, I phoned Irina.  
"Hi. I'm at the staff entrance. Can you come down?"  
"Who is this?"  
I thought the new SIM had confused her, so I told her my name.  
Silence.  
Then: "How did you get my number?"  
I reminded her who I was. I named her son. Told her about the night we spent two hours looking for a lost cat carrier only to find the cat asleep inside a suitcase.  
She hung up.  
People moved around me in the morning rush. Hundreds of them.  
A man clipped my shoulder hard enough to spin me halfway around. He did not look back.  
A woman stepped directly into my path and then startled when I spoke, as if I had appeared out of nowhere.  
It was not that I was invisible.  
It was worse than that.  
I was becoming easy not to notice.  
I went home.  
My key turned in the lock but the door would not open.  
There were footsteps inside.  
I knocked until a woman answered.  
I had never seen her before.  
She said she had lived there for seven years.  
Behind her, my apartment was gone. Different wallpaper. Different furniture. A framed family photograph where my coat rack should have been.  
I gave her my landlord's name.  
She said she owned the place.  
My job was gone.  
My home was gone.  
The people who knew me were beginning to forget me.  
The fishing photograph was still in my coat pocket.  
When I pulled it out, there were only three men in it.  
Kostya. Lyosha. And the dark figure across the lake.  
Anton was gone.  
So was I.  
The words on the back now read:  
**TWO CAME BACK.**  
I spent the rest of the day walking because I had nowhere else to go.  
I called friends. Former coworkers. My mother.  
Nobody knew me.  
My mother thought I was running some kind of phone scam. When I used a childhood nickname only she had ever called me, she became frightened and threatened to contact the police.  
By evening it was raining hard.  
I was sitting under a bus shelter when a station security employee walked past carrying a clear plastic evidence bag.  
Inside was my smartphone.  
The one I had thrown away that morning.  
I followed him.  
He entered through a staff door on the side of the station. I slipped through before it closed.  
He never looked at me.  
He walked down to the lost and found corridor and placed the phone into the receiving hatch.  
Before he shut the outer door, I saw the tag.  
Found: Rubbish containers, East District  
Time: 3:10 a.m.  
Date: tomorrow  
Then he left.  
I stayed by the hatch.  
A few seconds later, someone opened it from the other side and removed the bag.  
Then I heard my own voice.  
"Who just used the lost and found hatch?"  
The exact words I had said three weeks earlier.  
There is a small reinforced window in the storeroom door. I looked through it.  
I saw myself behind the desk.  
Not something that looked like me. Me. Three weeks younger, wearing my uniform, holding the phone and reading its tag.  
I knew, somehow, that I was not looking at a copy.  
I was looking into the past.  
Behind me, somewhere deep in the corridor, fingers snapped.  
The younger me inside the office looked up.  
I raised my hand to knock on the glass.  
I wanted to warn him. Tell him not to open the hatch. Not to take the photograph home. Not to watch the video.  
A calm voice spoke behind me.  
"Recovered property is not permitted to contact its owner."  
I turned.  
A man stood at the far end of the corridor wearing an old railway porter's uniform. Dark wool jacket. Peaked cap. Brass number on the chest. The kind of uniform I had only seen in photographs from the seventies.  
Water ran from his sleeves and pooled around his shoes.  
There was no face under the cap.  
A white property tag hung there instead, swaying slightly as though he were breathing.  
**EMPLOYEE NOT IDENTIFIED.**  
He held out a clear plastic bag.  
It was long enough for a person.  
"Get inside," he said.  
I ran.  
The corridor stretched as I moved. The stairwell door should have been twenty steps away. It kept sliding farther into the distance until it vanished completely.  
Metal shelves appeared on both sides of me.  
At first, they held ordinary lost property.  
Then I saw the first person.  
He had been folded into a clear bag as neatly as a winter coat. Knees against his chest. Arms bent behind him. His face pressed flat to the plastic.  
There were hundreds more.  
Men, women, children. Some dressed in modern clothes. Some in uniforms that must have been a century old.  
None of them moved.  
Every bag had a tag.  
I found Anton on the third shelf of the next aisle.  
He lay on his side, eyes open, still wearing the jacket from the fishing trip.  
His tag read:  
Found beside a lake  
Owner not located  
I stopped without meaning to.  
His eyes moved toward me.  
His mouth stayed closed, but I heard him whisper as clearly as if he were standing beside me.  
"Don't let them process you."  
Fingers snapped behind me.  
Close.  
I ran again.  
I have no idea how long I was in there.  
The lights kept going out. Whenever they came back, the bags had shifted. People who had been facing the shelves were now facing the aisle. Heads turned inside sealed plastic to follow me.  
A little boy tapped one finger against his bag as I passed.  
"You're not my mother," he whispered.  
I did not stop.  
At the end of one aisle was a red door beneath an EXIT sign.  
A tag hung from the handle.  
Item: Door  
Condition: Locked  
I pulled it anyway.  
It opened into my apartment.  
My real apartment. My wallpaper. My old shoes beside the door. My coat hanging where I had left it.  
A telephone was ringing in the kitchen.  
It was an old beige landline, the heavy kind with a coiled cord. I had never owned one.  
I picked it up.  
"Lost and found," a woman said. Her voice was bright and professional. "Are you calling to report a missing person?"  
"No."  
"Please state the missing person's name."  
"I said no."  
"State the name and we will arrange return."  
The fishing photograph was lying on the table.  
Now only Kostya and Lyosha remained.  
The dark figure had left the far shore. It was standing waist-deep in the lake, much closer to the camera.  
"I didn't lose anyone," I said.  
The woman laughed softly.  
"Then why are you here?"  
Someone knocked on the front door.  
Three knocks.  
A pause.  
Three more.  
The woman's voice changed. Not much, but the cheerfulness disappeared.  
"Do not open the door. Your owner has arrived to collect you."  
"Who is my owner?"  
She said my name.  
From the hallway, my own voice answered.  
"I know you're in there. Open up. I came for my things."  
That was several hours ago. I think.  
It is dark outside the windows, though my watch says it should be afternoon. The lights in the buildings across the street never change. Nothing moves down there.  
The phone rings every time I put it down.  
The man outside leaves sometimes.  
When he comes back, he knows more about me.  
At first he only knew my name.  
Then he told me my mother's name.  
Then the street where I grew up. The school I attended. The name of the first girl I kissed.  
A little while ago, he described the dream I had last night.  
The problem is, each time he says one of those things, I forget it.  
I know I had a mother, but I cannot picture her face.  
I know I went to school somewhere, but I cannot remember the building.  
I had to look at my passport to remember my surname. Now the passport photograph shows a stranger even to me.  
I am writing this because I do not know how much longer I will remember how.  
The photograph is on the table in front of me.  
Only one figure remains.  
The thing from the lake has climbed out of the water. It is standing right beside the camera now, close enough that its head is cut off by the top of the frame.  
The message on the back says:  
**NOBODY CAME BACK.**  
My voice is speaking through the door again.  
It is not asking to be let in anymore.  
It says the apartment belongs to it.  
It says my job belongs to it.  
It says my memories were only ever things it had misplaced.  
I think I believe it.  
A few minutes ago I looked in the mirror above the sink.  
I know the face in it is supposed to be mine.  
I feel nothing when I look at it.  
The telephone just rang again.  
This time, the woman did not ask for a name.  
She said, "Claim approved. Owner identity confirmed."  
There is a key turning in the lock now.  
And there is a clear plastic tag in my pocket that was not there before.  
It has one word printed on it.  
**RETURNED.**
