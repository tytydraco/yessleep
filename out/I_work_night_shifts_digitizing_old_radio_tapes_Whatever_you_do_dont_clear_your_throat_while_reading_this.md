**I work night shifts digitizing old radio tapes. Whatever you do, don't clear your throat while reading this.**

The night shift at the Mount Victoria transmitter station was usually quiet, which was why I liked it. The station sits on an isolated ridge far above the fog lines of the valley, housing three empty rooms, four walls of vintage mixing consoles, and forty-eight miles of dead copper wire.

  


My job is simple: I digitize archival audio reels from the late 1970s. Most of them are mundane—local weather reports, long-forgotten talk shows, static-heavy classical broadcasts.

  


Tonight, I pulled a tape labeled **Session 41 – Patient Vocal Trajectory**.

  


I locked the quarter-inch tape onto the feed spools, pressed down the magnetic head, and hit play.

  


The studio speakers hummed with warm analog hiss. Then came the voice of a doctor:

  


>*“Log entry, October 14. Subject exhibits profound somatic dissociation. He insists his lungs are no longer his own.”*

I leaned closer to my monitor, adjusting the equalizer frequencies. On my screen, the green audio waveforms spiked sharp and jagged.

  


A second voice came through the tape—raspy, wheezing, agonizingly thin:

  


>*“They aren't mine, Doctor. I can feel the weight of them. They’re too dry. They taste like salt.”*  
  
  
*“Your chest scans are clear,”* the doctor replied calmly on the recording. *“What you are feeling is a phantom sensory illusion brought on by acute grief.”*  
  
  
*“No,”* the raspy voice gasped. *“You don't understand. When I inhale, I feel someone else breathing out somewhere in the dark. We are sharing the stroke.”*

I shivered. The mountain wind rattled the heavy glass pane of the soundproof booth. I checked the tape box, but there were no patient names listed—just handwritten notes detailing a gradual collapse of the subject's internal boundaries.

  


I fast-forwarded ten minutes. The tape whirred, then settled back into playback.

  


The doctor’s voice sounded noticeably tighter, frayed:

  


>*“October 28. Subject has developed secondary symptoms. He reports feeling muscle contractions in his own throat whenever I speak.”*  
  
  
*“I’m not imitating you,”* the raspy voice whispered. *“Your larynx is pulling mine. Stop talking, Doctor. Every word you say stretches my vocal cords.”*

I paused the tape. I rubbed my eyes, feeling a strange tightness settling in my own jaw. The studio was stiflingly warm, the air thick with the smell of heated vacuum tubes and ozone. I reached for my mug of cold black coffee, swallowed, and stopped.

  


The swallow felt coarse. It felt as though my throat were lined with dry velvet.

  


I shrugged off the tension, hit play, and kept typing up the digital catalog log on my laptop.

  


The tape clicked into its final segment:

  


>*“November 3. Terminal entry. Subject’s vocal patterns have completely synchronized with the recording equipment. Doctor is non-responsive.”*

On the audio reel, there were no longer two distinct voices. There was only a single, wet, rhythmic rasping sound—a wet bellows expanding and collapsing.

  


>*“If you are listening to this reel,”* the single voice croaked from the speakers, *“do not clear your throat.”*

My hand froze above my keyboard.

  


>*“The resonance requires an active auditory bridge,”* the voice on the tape continued, low and deliberate. *“The paper reel is inert until a live set of lungs matches the frequency of the playback.”*

A sharp, violent itch burst in the back of my throat.

  


My immediate instinct was to cough, to clear the obstruction, but I held my breath, my pulse spiking into my ears.

  


>*“You feel it now, don't you?”* the voice whispered through the studio monitors. *“The dry salt in the chest. The velvet on the palate. You thought you were listening to an archive.”*

I tried to stand up from my console chair, but my abdominal muscles seized in a violent, involuntary spasm—drawing in a massive, ragged lungful of air against my will.

  


Across the room, the magnetic tape reel spun off its hub, flapping endlessly against the plastic stop-gate with a wet, rhythmic slapping noise.

  


I opened my mouth to scream for the security guard downstairs, but no sound came out. Instead, my vocal cords twitched in perfect unison with the static on the speaker, vibrating at a frequency that wasn't my own.

  


I pressed my hand to my neck. Beneath my fingers, my larynx wasn't moving up and down anymore. It was flattening out, smoothing, and unwinding down into my chest like dark, flexible tape.

  


I wasn't digitizing the archive. The archive was recording me.
