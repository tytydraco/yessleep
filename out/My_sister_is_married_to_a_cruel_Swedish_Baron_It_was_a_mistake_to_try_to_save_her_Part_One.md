Riisto took a long drag of his pipe, casting his unfocused eyes heavenward. He clearly held disdain for my idea- my \*scheme\*, he would call it- but I couldn’t fully reckon why he didn’t just speak plainly and tell me so.

“It’s risky.” He exhaled these words in a cloud of snow-white smoke, tapping dirt-caked fingernails against his knee in thought. His silver eyes focused on me, then, and in them I glimpsed faint traces of fear. Was he actually entertaining this?

“I have to know.” My voice shuddered with ill-contained emotion; I always was the most sensitive of my siblings, the least stoic. “Riisto, I have to.”

He sighed wearily, his chair groaning as he shifted his weight. “…You won’t do this with just any chaperone, mind you.”

I flushed. I had no idea to what lengths this would go, what risks I would need to take in order to glean truth from that wicked family. I only knew precious little: that Varvara had married Baron Brahe less than a year ago, in a grand procession from our family home to his obscene little castle on the western bank of the Karelian White Sea. That I had not seen my sister since. And that I knew, even before gossip caught like wildfire across the forests of Karelia, that my sister was not well.

Her sielulintu- her soul bird- had visited me. I tried to tell Riisto of it, to convince him that something was \*very\* wrong, but he shushed me, told me that a Christian woman shouldn’t speak of these things, and that our sister was just fine. He must not have really believed that.

I am twenty-two, still a ward in my older brother’s care. He and his wife are good to me, particularly as they have no children of their own, but they are eager to find me a match. While women younger than twenty aren’t seen as ready to marry, people are suspicious of a woman that reaches twenty-five without marrying. My brother hopes to spare me this fate.

Truth be told, I have long been more like a daughter to my brother. Ever since Papa met some particularly reckless rogues while carrying a cart full of Polish amber on the road to Petroskoi. Ever since Mama drank her weight in sugar wine and walked into a lake like a daughter of Aino.

“I cannot- Riisto,” I bunched the dense linen of my apron in my fists, “I would rather you didn’t-“

“No,” he cut me off, his voice uncharacteristically loud and firm. “You will not ruin your chances of marriage by calling on the Brahe home alone. I will not have it, Tarja!” Perhaps Riisto felt some fleeting defensiveness over his own honor, of how he sheltered his own sister, but the fear in his eyes betrayed his concern rested with mine.

I bit the inside of my lip. “His brother will talk to me.”

Riisto snorted. “Yes, and you’re proposing I let that talk happen over a mattress. Tarja, you have a future to think of. You think he will talk because he’s indiscreet, but what will he say about you to anyone who will hear? To men who might wish to call on you?”

I glanced down in shame before finally lifting my gaze. He met my eyes resolutely. “You are a young lady, Tarja. I promised you- I promised myself- I would find you a good match, and I intend to keep that promise. You have a future to think of. Do you not want a family? A home of your own, a husband?”

I knew that he was right- that to visit with any other chaperone would be improper, and that I shouldn’t plan to sacrifice my reputation for answers. Still, I was acutely aware that my brother feared more than just gossip.

“You’ve heard the rumors.”

His eyes darted away, and he gripped the arm of his chair. “Yes. It would be foolish not to consider them a warning.”

“Riisto,” I couldn’t resist the catlike smile that crept to my lips, “you’re the one that told me putting stock in these fancies wasn’t sensible.”

“The Brahes are an old family. One of the oldest in Europe. How they amassed all that power so very long ago, I can’t say…but the Devil writes up such contracts every day.”

I struggled not to roll my eyes, even as I tried to ignore the fact that my brother’s voice trembled. Riisto saw himself as something of a layman pastor. He mocked the old spirits, but his beliefs were nearly just as superstitious.

“So we will call on them together.” I would not hedge disagreement- Riisto had to be convinced that this was \*his\* idea, all along.

He took another shaky drag of his pipe. “Yes. You cannot leave my sight.”

I nodded in agreement, even as I fully intended to break this vow.

“I’m serious, Tarja.” The black of his pupils seemed to swallow his gray eyes, and I almost didn’t notice the tremor that ran through him. “If…if what they say is true, these people aren’t to be trusted. We will make it clear to them that I have important business to attend to the next day, and that my colleagues know we are making a social visit to them.”

He grit his teeth before continuing. “They must know that if we don’t leave their home, merchants from every corner of Karelia will want answers. Swedes, Russians, Germans, Finns, Karelians- they will all investigate and talk amongst themselves.”

I tried to hide my relief. “Do you really have so many contacts?”

Riisto smiled at me slyly, in spite of himself, and I caught a glimpse of the shrewd businessman that hid behind such a grave face. “My word and my story are not one and the same. Them \*believing\* that I do is to our benefit.”

—-

Red, above all other colors, holds a mystical importance to us Karelians. It is the color of menstrual blood, of afterbirth, of the flush of a woman’s vulva. It is the color of an unlucky moon, the blush of a salmon, the fruits of summer. It is red that wards off the evil of hiisi and demon alike, a red girdle that entices a lover to wrap his arms around a maiden’s waist, red thread that embroiders a matron’s apron and welcomes abundance to her lap.

Red is the color of my hair. I plait it, very decently, and thread it with ribbons as green as the forest. They match the canvas of my skirt. I cannot be a temptress with ruby lips and a fiery core- I must be a chaste maiden who merely suggests the color on her cheek. At least, as far as my chaperone is concerned.

I’ve heard whispers at festivals and in taverns that redheads are a particularly lusty folk. That a red-haired bride is the delight of a young groom; that a man with red locks makes love like an incubus. Perhaps this is why my brother is so concerned with my virtue- we are working against an impression already formed.

Still, when I call upon a charm to keep Riisto and me safe, it is not in Jesus’ name. I beseech red-haired Mielikki, mistress of the forests, to help me snare my prey.
