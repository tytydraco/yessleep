It was October 24, 1983, just a week before Halloween. My friends and I had gathered at my mom’s family’s house for an early Sunday dinner. The smell of roast beef and mashed potatoes still hung in the kitchen, and a small countertop radio played Journey and Billy Joel through a haze of static. The adults lingered over coffee, laughing about work and hunting season, while the younger kids tore through the living room in a blur of noise and motion.

We’d already eaten, and sitting around listening to grown‑ups talk wasn’t how six teenagers wanted to spend a Saturday night. So we slipped outside.

The cold autumn air hit us the second we stepped onto the porch. Fallen maple leaves scraped across the boards as a steady breeze pushed through the neighborhood. Somewhere in the distance, a dog barked, and the faint smell of burning leaves drifted through the air.

We were fifteen and sixteen, reckless, and wanted an adrenaline rush. There were six of us: Jackson, David, Tony, Martha, Taresa, and me.

Jackson was the cautious one. If something looked dangerous, he was always the first to say so. David was the opposite, he chased anything that promised excitement, even when it probably wasn’t smart. Tony talked just to fill silence, Martha never backed down from a challenge, and Taresa was the steady voice of reason that kept us from doing anything too stupid. I mostly just went wherever the group went.

Earlier that week, David had stopped by the local hardware store to pick up spark plugs for his dad’s truck. While waiting at the counter, one of the older employees overheard him talking about abandoned places.

According to David, the man leaned forward and asked quietly, “You boys ever heard of Kingston Road?”

None of us had.

The man didn’t smile. “My grandfather used to tell stories about a place hidden deep in those woods. Big estate. Rich family built it around the turn of the century. Folks said forty‑seven people lived there.” He paused. “Every last one of them died within a month.”

David had laughed when he told us the story. “What happened?”

The old man had only shrugged. “Depends who you ask.”

Some blamed sickness. Others blamed murder. A few said the family went insane. But his grandfather never believed any of those explanations.

Before David left, the man pulled out a faded receipt, flipped it over, and sketched a rough map leading to an old logging road that hadn’t appeared on county maps in decades.

David unfolded that same wrinkled piece of paper now. “You guys still up for it?”

Tony grinned. “Absolutely.”

Martha crossed her arms. “We’ve spent all week talking about this. I’m not backing out now.”

Jackson didn’t answer right away. He stared down at the map for several seconds before speaking. “I still think it’s weird.”

David looked up. “What is?”

“The guy.”

“What about him?”

“He didn’t sound like someone telling a ghost story.”

Now thinking back, Jackson was right. When David repeated the story, he always mimicked the old man’s expression perfectly, with little to no emotion. If anything, he’d sounded worried.

Still, none of us were about to let an old campfire story ruin our night.

Twenty minutes later, we piled into David’s pride and joy, a two‑tone 1977 Ford F‑250 Crew Cab with a faded blue body and white roof. Rust crept around the wheel wells, and the bench seats were patched with duct tape, but the big V8 engine started every time.

David patted the dashboard. “See? Told you she’d make it.”

Tony laughed from the back seat. “You say that every time.”

“Because it’s true.”

The engine rumbled to life. We pulled away from the neighborhood, leaving behind porch lights and traffic until the town disappeared in the rearview mirror.

The farther we drove, the fewer houses we passed. Streetlights became telephone poles. Telephone poles became empty fields. Eventually, even the fields vanished beneath walls of towering pine trees.

The pavement narrowed, cracked, and finally gave way to packed dirt. The truck bounced violently over roots and potholes. Branches scraped along both sides of the cab, long screeching sounds that felt like fingernails dragging across metal.

The chatter slowed after we left town, only comments were shared occasionally but now almost nothing was being said. The woods swallowed every sound. Even the engine seemed quieter.

David slowed the truck. “There.”

Our headlights washed over an old wooden post leaning at an angle. A rusted metal sign hung from two bolts barely holding it together: **KINGSTON ROAD.** The letters were almost unreadable beneath decades of rust.

Jackson sighed. “I’ve got a bad feeling.”

Tony rolled his eyes. “You always have a bad feeling.”

“No.” Jackson kept staring through the windshield. “This one’s different.”

David chuckled. “It’s an abandoned mansion, Jack.”

Jackson shook his head. “I know.” He swallowed hard. “I just don’t think it wants us here.”

For a moment, everybody looked uneasy. The only sound was the truck idling beneath the trees. Then David shifted into first gear. “Only one way to find out.”

The truck rolled forward, and the forest seemed to close around us.

Less than five minutes later, the road ended. Towering iron gates rose from the darkness, stretching nearly ten feet high. Thick vines coiled around the bars, and years of rust had stained the metal a deep reddish brown.

The center latch caught my attention first. It hadn’t simply broken, the heavy iron locking mechanism had been bent outward, twisted as if something had forced it open from the inside.

David killed the engine. The sudden silence was absolute. So silent I could hear the ringing in my ears intensify.

We climbed out of the truck. The cold hit harder now.

Taresa zipped her jacket, shoving her hands deep into her pockets until her coat bunched up past her ears. “Why does it suddenly feel twenty degrees colder?”

Nobody answered, not that she could have heard us anyway with her head buried in her collar.

Our flashlights cut through the dark as we pushed open the warped gates. The hinges groaned, echoing through the trees.

Ahead of us, beyond an overgrown circular driveway, stood the mansion.

Even after all these years, I still struggle to put into words what we were looking at. But if one thing’s for sure, the place was massive, and I mean *massive*.  Three stories of weathered brick rose above the treeline, topped with pointed Victorian cone roofs and chimneys that jutted straight into the black. Sagging porches wrapped around the ground floor, and broken bay windows stared down at us like hollow eyes. It didn't sit in one place either, it stretched for fifty yards in either direction, and went back into the dark farther than our lights could even reach. 

The place looked abandoned. Yet somehow… it also looked aware of us.

I unfolded the wrinkled receipt one last time. On the back, beneath the map, the guy from the hardware store had copied down what his grandfather swore were the last words spoken by the estate’s owner. The handwriting was jagged, pressing deep into the paper:

*They hunt… disfigured… wrong… in the walls… They don’t move right… They don’t look like us…*

David forced a tight grin, breaking the silence. “Well. Guess we’re here.”

Together, we climbed the cracked stone steps toward the mansion’s enormous front doors, twelve feet of dark oak reinforced with black iron bands. Time had scarred the wood, but unlike the rest of the estate, the doors didn’t look ready to collapse. They looked… maintained.

David gripped one of the brass handles. “You ready?”

A couple of us nodded. The rest just stared at the iron bands on the door, nobody eager to speak up. 

He pulled. The left door resisted before letting out a long, low groan that echoed into the dark interior. A stale breath of air drifted out, damp and heavy. It smelled faintly of mildew, old wood, and something metallic I couldn’t place.

Jackson frowned. “Does anybody else smell that?”

Tony sniffed. “Like… pennies.”

I knew exactly what he meant. It was the unmistakable scent of old iron. Back then, I couldn’t identify it. Years later, I realized what it reminded me of.

Old blood.

David clicked on his flashlight and stepped inside. “Come on.”

One by one, we followed.

The hardwood floor creaked beneath our boots as our lights swept across the grand entryway. It looked exactly how you’d expect an abandoned mansion to look; peeling wallpaper, rainwater dripping through holes in the ceiling, a crooked chandelier missing half its arms, broken furniture buried under decades of dust.

“See?” Tony laughed nervously. “Just an old hou—”

He stopped. “So…” His voice dropped to a whisper. “…turn around.”

We all looked at him. Tony wasn’t looking at us. He was staring farther into the mansion.

Slowly, every flashlight turned in the same direction.

The grand hallway beyond the foyer looked completely different. The decay simply… stopped.

The warped floorboards gave way to polished hardwood that gleamed beneath warm, golden light. Elegant wallpaper stretched flawlessly down the corridor, untouched by time. Dark mahogany tables lined the walls beneath framed paintings that looked freshly cleaned. Brass sconces burned with steady electric light. Every bulb worked. Every fixture shined.

Not a single speck of dust floated through the air.

The dividing line between the ruined foyer and the immaculate hallway was almost perfectly straight, like someone had sliced reality in half with a knife.

David stared. “What…”

Martha stepped forward, crouching at the threshold. She ran one finger across the polished floor. “No dust.” She looked back at us. “It’s clean.”

Taresa lifted her flashlight toward the ceiling. “There aren’t any wires.”

“What?”

“The lights.” She pointed again. “I don’t see electrical wiring anywhere.”

David leaned closer. She was right. The sconces glowed warmly against the walls, but there were no visible wires. no conduit, and not even a faint hum to show where the power was coming from. 

“This place has been abandoned for decades,” Jackson said quietly. “There shouldn’t be electricity.”

Tony forced a laugh. “Maybe somebody’s fixing it up.”

“For who?” Martha asked. “There isn’t even a road that leads here anymore.”

The air in the hallway went dead. Sound didn't carry right—even our breathing sounded flat, like we were standing inside a closet full of heavy coats.

I took a step forward. The polished floor felt strangely warm through the soles of my boots.

The second my foot crossed the threshold, every brass sconce flickered. Just once. Then the light went steady again.

David looked over his shoulder. “Did you…”

His sentence never finished.

**BANG.**

The sound echoed through the hallway like a gun going off.

We all jumped. Tony spun around so fast his boot slid out from under him. “The door!”

We swung our lights back toward the entryway. The heavy oak doors were gone. Where the entrance had been seconds earlier was just smooth plaster, painted the same cream color as the hallway, without a single crack to show a door had ever been there. 

For several seconds, everybody froze. Our minds refused to accept what we were seeing.

David was the first to react. “No.” He rushed to the wall and slapped both hands against it. “No, no, no!” He shoved hard. Nothing happened. “It’s got to be hidden.” He began feeling every inch of the plaster, searching for a seam. “There has to be a door.”

Tony joined him, pounding his fists against the wall. “This isn’t funny!” His voice echoed down the corridor before fading into silence. “There was a door right here!”

Jackson stood frozen, his face pale. “I told you.”

Tony and David are still frantically looking for a fault in the wall.

Martha took a shaky breath. “We’ll find another exit. There has to be one.” Her voice trembled. “There has to.” She sounded like she was trying to convince herself more than anyone else.

Then… something moved.

Not in front of us. Behind us.

A shadow stretched slowly across the polished wallpaper.

At first, I thought one of us had stepped in front of a light. But the angle didn’t make sense. Every sconce lined the hallway in perfect symmetry—the shadow should have stretched downward.

Instead… it crawled sideways. Across the wall. Then upward. Like it was climbing.

My stomach tightened. “Guys…”

Neither of them looked back, David and Tony were still clawing at the plaster.

The shadow reached the ceiling joists and stopped. For a second, it held a human shape, just stretched wrong. Too tall, too thin, with long arms hanging far past where its knees should’ve been.

I swept my flashlight right over it. The shadow just vanished into the paint.

I blinked hard, my throat dry. “Did anybody else—”

“No,” Jackson interrupted quietly. “I saw it too.”

Neither of us looked away from the ceiling. Every instinct in my body screamed that something had been standing there only moments before.

Then, from somewhere deep inside the mansion

**CRASH.**

Glass exploded. The sound echoed through dozens of unseen rooms before fading into silence.

A few seconds later came another noise. A slow heavy scraping sound… like something massive being dragged across hardwood.

Then it stopped.

David swallowed. “I think—”

The floor beneath our feet vibrated. Once. Twice. Then again. Almost too faint to notice.

The vibrations grew stronger. A low rumble rolled through the hallway. Dust drifted from somewhere high above us despite the spotless ceiling.

“What is that?” Martha whispered.

Before anyone could speak 

a massive oak bookcase at the far end of the hall tipped forward. Eight feet of solid wood and hundreds of leather-bound books slammed toward the floor. 

I flinched, waiting for the crash. It never came.

Halfway to the floor, the bookcase just caught itself, hanging suspended at a forty-five-degree angle in thin air. No wires. Nothing holding it up.

For a beat, everything went totally dead.

Then the whole eight-foot block of oak shot down the hall straight at us.

It accelerated with terrifying speed, skimming inches above the polished floor as though hurled by something impossibly strong.

“DOWN!” David screamed.

We dove in every direction just as the massive bookcase roared over our heads with enough force to stir the air around us.

It slammed into the wall right behind us with an explosive crack. Wood splintered and plaster erupted in every direction as hundreds of books burst into the air like a flock of startled birds. The impact punched a jagged hole straight through the plaster, revealing a dark corridor beyond. 

For several seconds, nobody moved. The only sound was hundreds of loose pages drifting softly to the floor.

We slowly pushed ourselves back to our feet, each of us staring at the gaping hole where the bookcase had struck. Dust floated lazily through our flashlight beams, mixing with loose pages that drifted to the polished floor like dead leaves.

Tony let out a nervous laugh. “Okay… okay, somebody explain that.”

No one could. There wasn’t an explanation. Bookcases didn’t stop in midair. They didn’t launch themselves down hallways. And they certainly didn’t punch through solid plaster walls.

He flipped it open.

“What the…”

He turned a page. Then another.

“There’s nothing in here,” David muttered.

I leaned over his shoulder. The paper was crisp and white, but every single page was completely blank.

 He flipped through faster. Every page… blank. He tossed it aside.

Another book lay open nearby. Blank. Another. Blank.

It was as if someone had gone through an entire library and erased every word ever written.

Jackson quietly looked back toward the smooth wall where the front doors had vanished. “I don’t think this place wants us learning anything.”

That sentence sat with me longer than I cared to admit. I don’t know why. Maybe because, deep down, I was beginning to believe him.

David shined his flashlight through the hole. “There.”

Beyond the shattered wall stretched another hallway, darker than the one we stood in. Unlike the polished corridor behind us, this one wasn’t lit by elegant sconces. Only our flashlights reached into it.

The flashlight beam just died twenty feet out. The hallway didn't end, the light simply stopped, swallowed whole by the pitch dark, like hitting a solid wall of black water. 

We should go back,” Taresa said.

David looked at her, then back at the wreck. “Back where? The front door is gone, Taresa. The bookcase just blew a hole into a completely different hallway.”

She opened her mouth, but couldn't find the words. The entrance was gone for good.

Tony pointed his flashlight straight down the main hall, then swung the beam toward the jagged, splintered hole in the plaster.

“Okay, so our choices are…” He motioned down the main corridor. “Option A: keep walking down this hall.” Then he aimed his light into the pitch-black gap. “Or Option B: climb through the hole.

Martha crossed her arms, shivering slightly. “I vote for the hole. At least we know something is back there.”

Jackson shook his head. “I vote we stay on the main path and look for a real exit.”

“Same difference,” Tony muttered.

We climbed through the broken opening one at a time, chunks of plaster and splintered oak crunching beneath our boots.

The air changed immediately. It was a deep, freezing cold that hit like a physical wall. Every breath we let out bloomed into pale clouds.

David frowned. “Is anybody else’s flashlight getting dimmer?”

I looked down. Mine was. The beam wasn't as strong as it was minutes ago. Fresh batteries. Brand‑new flashlight. Yet the light only stretched a few yards before dissolving into blackness.

The hallway itself was narrower than the first one. The wallpaper had changed too. Instead of elegant floral patterns, faded gray paper covered the walls. Except… it wasn’t faded evenly. Dark stains spread beneath the paper in long, irregular shapes.

At first I thought they were water stains. Then one of them slowly moved.

I stopped walking. “…Guys.”

Everyone froze. “What?”

I pointed. The stain remained perfectly still.

I frowned. “No… I could’ve sworn… Never mind.”

We kept walking.

The floor creaked beneath every step now. Unlike the immaculate hallway behind us, this place actually felt abandoned. Rotting wood, peeling wallpaper, broken furniture. Everything looked normal again.

Almost.

After several minutes, Martha spoke. “Has anybody noticed…” She hesitated. “…there aren’t any windows?”

David turned his flashlight toward the wall. She was right. From outside, we’d seen dozens of windows covering the mansion. Inside… there hadn’t been a single one. Not in the foyer. Not in the grand hallway. Not here.

Just walls. Endless walls.

Tony looked upward. “That’s impossible.”

Jackson quietly answered. “I think we’ve already crossed ‘impossible.’’

We rounded another corner. The hallway ended at a small sitting room. An old fireplace stood against one wall, its bricks blackened with soot. A faded rug stretched across the floor, and several antique chairs surrounded a small wooden table. Everything was coated in a thick layer of dust.

Finally, something looked abandoned.

David exhaled a quiet sigh of relief. “This is more like what I expected.”

Tony walked over to the fireplace. “Looks like nobody’s been here in years.” He brushed dust from the mantel. Beneath it sat a framed family photograph; black and white, probably taken sometime in the early 1900s.

The picture showed dozens of people gathered on the mansion’s front steps. Men in suits. Women in long dresses. Children smiling at the camera.

Forty‑seven people.

“Must be them,” Martha whispered. “The family.”

I stepped closer, shining my beam over the faded photo. Everyone looked normal. Happy, even. Just an ordinary family.

Until I noticed one detail.

Near the back of the group stood a little girl, maybe eight years old. Unlike everyone else, she wasn’t looking at the camera. She was staring toward one of the upstairs windows. Even in the old photograph, her expression looked terrified.

A chill ran down my spine. “Guys.” I pointed.

David leaned in. “That’s creepy.”

“No.” I swallowed. “Look where she’s looking.”

Every flashlight turned toward the second‑floor windows in the photograph. There… barely visible behind the glass, stood a figure. So faint it almost blended into the darkness. It was impossibly tall. Its head nearly touched the top of the window frame. One arm hung far longer than the other.

After some silence, Tony finally laughed uneasily. “Well… that’s definitely fake.”

“I don’t think it is,” Jackson said.

“Old cameras picked up weird shadows all the time,” Tony replied, though his voice wavered.

Martha carefully lifted the photograph. “What year is this?” She turned the frame over. Written in faded ink across the back were just six words:

*Our last family photograph. October 1909.*

She slowly set the picture frame back on the fire place mantel

“I think we should keep moving,” David said softly. 

As we turned to leave the room, something made me glance back one last time.

The photograph was still sitting on the mantel. Except now… every single person in the picture was looking directly at us.

I felt every hair on my arms stand up. “…Don’t move.”

They stopped moving

For several long seconds, the six of us simply stared at the photograph. The smiling family hadn’t changed, they were standing in the same places, wearing the same clothes, frozen in the same moment from more than seventy years ago.

But now every pair of eyes was fixed directly on us. Not toward the old camera. Not toward the photographer. At us.

David stepped closer. “Did… did they always look like that?”

“I don’t think so,” Martha whispered.

Tony shook his head immediately. “No. I swear that little girl was looking at the window.”

“So did I,” Jackson said.

Nobody wanted to admit what we were all thinking. Photographs don’t change.

David slowly reached toward the frame.

“Don’t touch it,” Jackson said.

David hesitated. “…Why?”

“I don’t know.” Jackson never took his eyes off the picture. “I just don’t think we should.”

For once, David actually listened. He lowered his hand. “We’re leaving.”

We turned toward the doorway. The room suddenly felt smaller than before. The fireplace behind us no longer offered any comfort, it felt like something standing at our backs. Watching. Waiting.

We filed out into the dark hallway. Nobody spoke for nearly a minute, and for once, even Tony didn't try to crack a joke to break the silence. 

The silence was broken by a faint sound somewhere ahead.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

It wasn’t loud. It almost sounded polite, like someone gently tapping their fingernails against wood.

*Tap… Tap… Tap.*

The sound echoed through the corridor.

David stopped walking. “Everybody hear that?”

Five heads nodded.

“It sounds close.”

“No,” Jackson whispered. “It sounds far away.”

We all looked at him. “What?”

He pointed farther down the hallway. “Listen.”

We did.

The tapping echoed strangely. One moment it sounded twenty feet ahead. The next it seemed to come from somewhere behind us. Then above us. Then below. The mansion twisted sound in ways that made no sense.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

Taresa hugged herself. “I don’t like this.”

Neither did I.

The hallway bent sharply to the right. Beyond the corner lay another stretch of corridor lined with closed wooden doors. Every door looked identical; dark oak and brass handle, no other distinct features, Just a long row disappearing into darkness.

David counted. “One… two… three… four…” At least fifteen doors.

Tony sighed. “Guess we start checking.”

Jackson grabbed his arm. “No.”

Tony frowned. “What?”

Jackson pointed at the floor.

We all looked down. A thick layer of dust covered the hallway. Except for one thing.

Footprints. Bare footprints. They weren’t ours.

The prints were larger than any normal person’s: long, thin, almost human. Except each foot had six unnaturally long toes ending in sharp points that dug deep into the dust.

The tracks emerged from beneath one of the closed doors, crossed the hallway, then disappeared into the opposite wall. Not another doorway. The wall itself.

My mouth went dry.

David slowly crouched. “They’re fresh.”

“How can you tell?” Martha asked.

He pointed. The edges of the prints were still sharp. No dust had settled into them. As though whatever made them had walked there only minutes ago.

Tony looked at the door. “So…” He forced a weak grin. “Maybe we don’t open that one.”

David stood up and motioned us to continue forward.
