I haven't spoken publicly about what happened to Harrison until now. The police have heard my account, although I don't think they believed much of it. There are also things that I've never been able to bring myself to tell his family.

I don't know whether the account is still active. But if someone contacts your child online and invites them somewhere near Ketchum Woods, please don't dismiss it as a joke.

There were three missing children’s posters pinned to a noticeboard outside the Ketchum Woods State Reserve. The oldest depicted a little boy in a yellow raincoat who'd apparently disappeared a year earlier. The other two were girls, aged six and nine; both went missing months apart. The younger girl had a bright red butterfly clip in her hair.

Harrison stood beside me, chewing gum. "Holy shit. Three kids from the same area?" He leaned closer to read the dates, then pointed at the photograph of the youngest girl. "Grace, get your camera out. We should get a shot of these before we head in. Actually, hang on, let me stand beside them. We can work it into the intro."

I took out the camera and adjusted the focus as he stepped beside the noticeboard. He straightened his black hoodie before giving me a thumbs-up. 

"What's going on, everybody? Harrison here. Today we're following up on some seriously disturbing messages from an individual targeting children in the Ketchum area. Now, as you can see behind me, this community is unfortunately no stranger to missing persons cases involving minors. We're not saying these disappearances are connected, but we believe parents deserve to know what dangers might be lurking in their own backyard."

He held a solemn expression for another second before looking at me. "Pretty good, right? Get a close-up of the posters. That'll make a killer thumbnail."

I reluctantly complied, lingering on the photographs of the three missing children. It felt wrong, using their faces to promote a video when we didn't even know whether their disappearances had anything to do with our investigation. 

Harrison must have noticed my expression, because he came over while I was packing the microphone away.

"Hey, are you doing okay? You haven't said much since we got here."

"I'm fine. I'm just not sure about this."

He reached into the car and handed me a bottle of water.

"Look, I know this one's different. We've done our homework, and we're not going in there looking for a fight. I promise you, if anything looks dangerous, we turn around and get out. Deal?"

I studied him for a moment. He'd promised me the same thing on other shoots, and we’d still ended up staying longer than I wanted. 

"Deal," I said.

He gave me an affectionate pat on the shoulder, then looked back toward the reserve.

"Right. Let's go catch this sick fuck."

The investigation began when someone calling themselves sweetmother contacted one of Harrison's decoy profiles. Posing as "Charlie," a fourteen-year-old boy with a cartoon fox for a profile picture, he'd struck up a conversation with the account. Within a few days, they'd invited him to meet in Ketchum Woods. I wasn't entirely convinced we were dealing with a genuine predator. The messages were so blatantly suspicious that I'd initially assumed someone was playing a particularly unpleasant joke. Did they really think a fourteen-year-old would venture into a nature reserve to meet a stranger just because they'd offered him something to eat? Harrison disagreed. He thought the invitation alone was enough to justify investigating and pointed out that children didn't always recognize danger as readily as adults did.

There was something else strange about the account, too. Every message arrived at precisely 3:07 a.m., even when Harrison replied during the afternoon. He assumed it was some kind of automated service. I wasn't so sure, but I kept that thought to myself. I'd learned that arguing with Harrison rarely got me anywhere, particularly when he thought he'd found something worth investigating. That hadn't always been the case. 

I first met Harrison through the university film club, which he'd helped establish several years earlier. He owned professional equipment and took an interest in my camera work when I barely knew anyone. He’d offered me a paid position on his documentary channel, claiming I had a natural eye for framing. Back then, I thought he was doing me a favor. I just wish I'd learned to say no to him sooner. 

Harrison hadn't started out catching predators. His channel originally featured small documentaries about local issues: neglected housing, struggling charities, and families who'd been cheated by unscrupulous businesses. Some were moving, and I was proud to help make them. People would thank him with tears in their eyes, and he'd talk about those moments for weeks afterward. 

Everything changed when a confrontation with an alleged scammer attracted more attention than his previous documentaries combined. Harrison began seeking out similar cases, and before long he'd abandoned the community stories altogether. He said exposing online predators was the most important work we'd ever done, but the confrontations became increasingly aggressive. He'd block people from leaving and demand explanations even when they seemed genuinely confused. I considered quitting more than once.

But the money made it difficult, and I still felt indebted to the person who'd given me my first real opportunity. Whenever I questioned what we were doing, Harrison brought up the people we'd caught, and eventually I stopped talking back. 

Then came Patrick. He was a thirty-four-year-old accountant Harrison had accused of arranging to meet an underage girl. During the confrontation outside a shopping center, Patrick insisted he'd believed the account belonged to an adult woman and accused Harrison of misrepresenting their messages. I thought he was making excuses.

Afterwards, Harrison asked me to cut the forty-minute confrontation down to fifteen, removing most of Patrick's explanations.

I remember hesitating.

"Harrison, wouldn't it be better to leave some of that in? People might think we're hiding something."

He was sitting beside me at the editing desk, scrolling through his phone. He glanced at the screen and shook his head.

"Grace, he thought he was meeting a teenager. Of course he's got an explanation. If we let every creep spend twenty minutes telling us why he's innocent, we'd never upload anything. People watch because they want to see someone held accountable." 

I protested that removing too much would make the confrontation misleading, but Harrison reminded me that the channel's revenue depended on engagement and that my editing work was part of what made the operation financially viable.

I didn't want him to think I was incompetent, so I made the edits.

The video reached almost two hundred thousand views before Patrick released the complete conversation.

Several of his claims were supported by messages Harrison hadn't included. As the controversy spread, the platform eventually removed our channel. Someone identified me as the editor, and before long strangers found my social media accounts and sent me abusive messages. A production company I'd been speaking to about a possible internship stopped replying to my emails. I was terrified that anyone who searched my name would see the controversy before they saw any of my work.

Of course I was angry at Harrison for dragging me into it, but I couldn't pretend the criticism was entirely unfair. I knew which parts of Patrick's explanation I'd removed, against my better judgment. That was what made the whole thing so difficult to defend, even to myself.

Harrison appealed the channel's removal, then moved to a smaller video platform where a fraction of his old audience followed him. He insisted we'd been punished for exposing predators and became convinced that one undeniable case would restore his reputation. He still owed me fourteen hundred dollars and promised he'd settle it if I helped him with three final shoots.

I refused at first. The last thing I wanted was another controversy. But Harrison offered conditions I thought I could live with: no editing, and nothing would be uploaded without my approval. I eventually agreed, mostly because I needed the money and wanted to put the whole thing behind me. 

The first two gigs were uneventful. One suspect never showed, and Harrison agreed not to publish the other confrontation when the messages proved ambiguous. He paid me five hundred dollars between them, leaving nine hundred outstanding. 

Ketchum was supposed to be my last gig with him. Our plan was straightforward. We'd meet whoever was behind the account near the public walking tracks, record the confrontation, and leave. Harrison had assured me we wouldn't be going anywhere isolated, which was one of the reasons I'd agreed to come.

After filming his introduction beside the noticeboard, we collected our equipment and headed toward the northern entrance. It was only then that we discovered the track was closed. A chain secured the metal gate, and a weathered notice warned of storm damage. Farther along, a fallen tree had torn a gap in the fence.

I'd wanted to report the messages and leave it there, but Harrison insisted nobody would take them seriously unless we caught the person ourselves. I reluctantly agreed, on the condition that we stay on the track and leave if anything looked unsafe. Harrison rolled his eyes but promised, then squeezed through the damaged fence.

The track beyond the gate was narrow and badly overgrown. The forest itself was beautiful, with enormous oaks and beeches growing so close together that their upper branches formed a continuous canopy. Under different circumstances, I might have enjoyed being there.

Harrison spent most of the walk complaining about the lack of reception. He'd intended to stream the confrontation live, without considering that a closed nature reserve might not have reliable mobile coverage. 

We'd been walking for around twenty minutes when Harrison spotted the first candy.

It was a strawberry chew in a bright red wrapper, sitting on a moss-covered stump beside the trail. He picked it up and held it toward the camera, turning it over between his fingers. 

"Somebody dropped it," I suggested.

"Sure. Somebody just happened to drop a perfectly good piece of candy in the middle of a closed walking track." He pointed toward the ground. "And look, there's another one."

A yellow wrapper lay near the roots of a tree several meters ahead. Beyond it, something blue glinted among the fallen leaves.

Harrison started following the wrappers, stopping every few meters to look for the next one. I stayed close behind him, occasionally glancing toward the track we'd left. 

We found the elderly woman shortly before midday.

She was standing just beyond a bend in the track, sorting something into two plastic shopping bags. She wore a blue cardigan, a long brown skirt, and a pair of muddy rubber boots. As we approached, she bent down to retrieve something from the ground.

Harrison stopped so abruptly that I nearly walked into him.

He turned, put a finger to his lips, and mouthed for me to keep recording.

Then he called out to her.

"Excuse me, ma'am? Sorry to bother you. Could we ask you a couple of questions?"

The woman straightened slowly. She remained with her back to us for several seconds before turning around.

Her face was deeply wrinkled, with heavy bags beneath her eyes and a large, crooked nose. She looked at Harrison, then at me, before offering a small, uncertain smile.

"Are you lost, dearies?"

"We've been speaking to someone online who's arranging meetings with children in this reserve. We're trying to find out who's behind it. Have you seen anyone suspicious?" 

The elderly woman seemed to consider the question, then asked, "Have you brought him?"

Harrison paused.

"Sorry?"

"The boy. Has he come with you?"

My stomach tightened. 

Harrison looked toward me, his eyebrows raised, before turning back to her.

“He said he'd come when the sun was high." She glanced beyond us toward the empty track, looking mildly disappointed. "I thought he might be along by now."

Harrison's expression changed, and he took a step forward.

"And you've arranged to meet him out here?"

"Well, he said he would come."

"Right. And how old is this boy, exactly?"

The woman hesitated, then held one hand at approximately shoulder height.

"About so big, I should think."

Harrison let out a short, humorless laugh.

"Well, that's interesting, because I happen to know exactly who you've been talking to."

The woman stared at him.

He smiled, evidently enjoying himself.

"My name's Harrison, and I investigate people who prey on children. We've documented every message you've sent, including the directions to this reserve. So I'd advise you to think carefully about what you say next." 

She looked Harrison up and down, then reached out and squeezed his forearm through his sleeve. 

"Goodness. You're a solid young man, aren't you?"

Harrison pulled his arm away.

"Ma'am, don't touch me."

"Oh, don't be like that."

The woman smiled, revealing a set of small, yellow teeth.

She turned toward me.

"And the girl. Is she your sister?"

"No, she's my camerawoman. Now, I'd appreciate it if you could stop changing the subject and explain why you've been arranging a meeting with a minor."

The woman gave a disappointed little sigh.

"Oh, dear. I was expecting someone younger."

Harrison laughed again in disbelief.

"Yeah, well, there is no fourteen-year-old. You've been talking to me. So why don't we start with your name?" 

The woman's expression became strangely blank.

She looked at Harrison for a long moment, then bent down to retrieve her shopping bags.

"I'd best be going."

"Whoa, hang on. We're not finished here."

The woman didn't respond. She gathered her shopping bags and shuffled away, one hand curled loosely around several brightly wrapped sweets. As she stepped off the track, a few slipped between her fingers and scattered across the dirt. She moved through the undergrowth surprisingly quickly, slipping between the trees with barely a sound. A branch caught Harrison's sweatshirt as he made to follow her.

"Harrison, wait."

He stopped and looked back at me.

"What?"

I lowered the camera. "I don't know about this. That was really weird."

"No kidding."

"She didn't even seem to understand what you were accusing her of. And when she touched your arm? What was that all about?"

Harrison glanced back toward the trees.

"I don't know. She's probably some confused old bat."

"That's what I'm saying. She doesn't seem like the sort of person we've been looking for. She might not even understand what she's doing."

"Grace, she arranged to meet a kid out here. Being old doesn't give her a free pass."

Before I could answer, a yellow sweet rolled against my shoe. I looked up to find the woman standing between two trees some distance away, watching us. 

"Come along, then," she called.

She smiled before disappearing into the woods.

Harrison stared after her.

"What the hell is she playing at?"

"I don't know," I said. "Harrison, seriously. She's creeping me out. Can we please just leave?"

"Grace, we've come all this way. I'm not letting her disappear now."

He started after her before I could argue.

“Harrison, wait!”

Within seconds, I could barely make out his black hoodie between the trees.

I looked back toward the walking track. I wasn't sure I could find my way back alone, and Harrison was already disappearing between the trees.

I swore under my breath and hurried after him.

The candies continued for another few minutes, scattered irregularly among the roots and moss-covered stones. Soon we were far from the walking track, surrounded by woodland so thick I could no longer distinguish the route we'd taken. My phone had lost reception, and the offline map placed us beyond the reserve boundary. Harrison insisted the GPS was inaccurate, though he sounded less certain than before.

A few minutes later, the trees opened into a clearing.

In the middle of it stood a house.

It was surprisingly pretty, a two-story cottage painted cream with green window frames and a red-tiled roof. A narrow gravel path led through a tidy vegetable garden toward a little front porch, where several pots of flowering plants stood beside a wicker chair. Beyond the house, I could see a brick outbuilding with smoke rising from its chimney.

Harrison stopped beside me.

"Well, I'll be damned. Granny's got herself a nice little setup."

Harrison started up the gravel path. I stayed beside the trees, watching him approach the porch. The cottage looked perfectly ordinary, but I couldn't stop thinking about the way the woman had examined his arm or how she'd smiled when she asked us to follow her.

"Grace, you coming?"

I glanced back into the woods. There was no sign of the walking track, and I wasn't confident I could find my way back alone.

By the time I reached the porch, Harrison was knocking on the door.

"Hello? Ma'am? It's us again. We'd really appreciate a few minutes of your time."

A woman's voice answered from somewhere deeper in the house.

"Come in, dearies. I've just put the kettle on."

Harrison turned and grinned at me before stepping inside.

I followed reluctantly, keeping the camera raised.

The living room looked surprisingly ordinary; there was a large upholstered sofa, and a television blared a daytime gardening program. The room smelled of lavender polish and something sweet baking in the oven.

Then I noticed the skull above the fireplace.

It was yellowed with age, with a candle burning inside its open mouth. Two smaller skulls sat beside it, each fitted with a little metal holder where the eyes should have been. Bundles of dried herbs hung above the windows, tied with red thread. A collection of crude dolls occupied a shelf beside the fireplace, their bodies fashioned from cloth, straw, and what appeared to be human hair. One of the smaller dolls had a red butterfly clip fastened to its straw-colored hair. They reminded me of something from a horror film, the sort of prop we'd make for a student production. 

Harrison approached the mantelpiece.

"Are those real skulls?"

"I don't know."

"Well, get a shot of them. That's creepy as fuck."

I raised the camera.

On a side table beneath the window sat a modern smartphone, plugged into a charger beside an enormous leather-bound book. Its screen was still on, displaying a conversation I recognized immediately.

*Charlie.*

"Harrison."

He followed my gaze, then grinned.

"Well, there we go. That's our account. Gotcha."

I glanced back toward the skulls on the mantelpiece.

"I really don't think we should be here."

Before he could answer, the woman appeared in the doorway. She'd removed her cardigan and scarf, revealing a faded floral dress. Her white hair hung loosely around her face, and she carried a large plate of little cakes dusted with powdered sugar.

"Oh," she said pleasantly. "You've made yourselves at home."

Harrison straightened immediately.

"Ma'am, we need to have a serious conversation."

"Would you like something to eat? I've made plenty."

"No, thank you. What I'd like is an explanation for why you invited a fourteen-year-old boy into the woods."

The woman placed the cakes on the coffee table and looked at him with mild irritation. She glanced toward me.

"Does your sister want a cake?"

"She's not my sister," Harrison replied impatiently. "And I'd appreciate it if you'd answer the question."

The woman seemed to consider this.

"Well, I thought he might like something nice."

"Something nice?"

"Of course, dearie."

Harrison glanced at the cakes.

"And what exactly were you planning to do when he arrived?"

"Feed him."

"Feed him?"

"Well, children get hungry, don't they?"

"Ma'am, I don't know what kind of game you think you're playing, but we're not buying it. You're going to stay right here and explain everything you've been doing."

The woman sighed, looking down at the cakes.

"Such a fuss. People never used to make this much of a fuss."

Harrison moved closer.

"Well, times have changed."

"Yes," she agreed sadly. "They certainly have."

Her eyes travelled across his shoulders and arms, then settled on his stomach. Harrison shifted uncomfortably under her gaze.

"Okay, that's enough. You're clearly not taking this seriously, so I'm calling the police."

He reached for his phone.

"Don't do that."

She said it so quietly that Harrison almost seemed not to hear. It was the first time she'd spoken without that cheerful, grandmotherly lilt.

Harrison raised one hand to stop her.

"Stay right there, ma'am. I'm not asking you again."

She continued forward, and Harrison caught her wrist. 

"Look, I'm trying to be reasonable here, but if you won't cooperate—" 

The woman moved.

I barely saw what happened. One moment Harrison had hold of her wrist; the next, she'd twisted his arm around, and there was a sharp crack.

Harrison stopped mid-sentence. He stared at his hand, which was bent backward at an impossible angle, before the pain seemed to register.

"What the fuck?" he screamed. "What did you do to me?"

The woman looked almost pleased.

"Oh, you are a strong one."

She tightened her grip, studying the way his injured hand trembled. For a moment, I thought she might be examining the damage she'd done. Then her eyes travelled up his arm, lingering on the muscle in his upper arm. 

I couldn't understand how she was holding him there. Harrison was nearly twice her size, and she'd barely seemed to exert herself. He pulled against her with his whole body, but she didn't even move.

"Grace, get away from her!"

Before Harrison could break free, she seized his hair with her other hand and slammed his face into the edge of the coffee table.

The cakes scattered across the carpet.

He collapsed to his knees, blood pouring from his nose, and I stood frozen behind the camera until the woman turned toward me. 

I swung the camera reflexively.

The metal body struck the side of her head with enough force to wrench my shoulder. She staggered sideways, knocking over a small table.

She straightened. There was a deep indentation above her ear.

She touched the injury, examined the blood on her fingers, and looked at me with an expression of disapproval.

"Now," she said quietly. "That wasn't very polite."

Before I could react, something struck me between the shoulders.

My legs stopped working, and I hit the floor hard enough to knock the breath out of my lungs. The camera slipped from my hands and struck the boards beside me. When I tried to reach for it, my arm went rigid.

The woman stood over me, holding one of the little cloth dolls from the mantelpiece. It wore a strip of black fabric that looked exactly like the lining of my jacket.

She bent its leg backward with a sharp jerk. 

I screamed. Something deep inside my leg twisted, as though someone had hooked their fingers into the muscle of my thigh. 

The woman crouched beside me and brushed a strand of hair away from my face.

"Poor thing. You've frightened yourself half to death."

I tried to crawl away, dragging myself across the floor with my arms, but she caught the back of my jacket and pulled me toward her with effortless strength.

"Sleep now, dearie."

The last thing I remember was the smell of lavender on her fingers.

I woke in a bedroom that looked as though it belonged in somebody's grandmother's house.

Through the pale green curtains, I could see morning sunlight moving across the branches of an apple tree. The camera was gone. So were my bag, my phone, and everything else I'd brought into the woods. For one ridiculous moment, I found myself thinking about the cost of replacing the equipment and what Harrison would say when he discovered I'd broken his lens.

I tried to sit up, but ropes secured my wrists and ankles to the bedframe. I struggled instinctively, twisting until the frame rattled beneath me. Something seized inside my wrists with such force that I cried out. The pain vanished when I stopped pulling.

Above the bedroom door hung several dolls, including a little black one with red thread wound tightly around its wrists. I remembered the doll the woman had been holding in the sitting room.

The door opened, and the elderly woman came in carrying a tray.

She'd changed into a clean blue dress, and her white hair had been brushed back into a loose bun. There was no visible injury to her head, although I distinctly remembered striking her hard enough to leave an indentation above her ear.

"Good morning, dearie. I was beginning to wonder when you'd wake up."

She placed the tray on the bedside table and adjusted the folded napkin. There was a bowl of soup, a glass of water, and two little cakes dusted with powdered sugar.

I pulled against the ropes again. The pain returned so suddenly that I cried out.

"Please," I said. "Please, just let me go. I won't tell anyone about you, I swear."

She looked mildly surprised.

"Oh, there's no need for all that. You've had a nasty fright, that's all. A little breakfast will do you good."

"Where's Harrison?"

"The big one? He's downstairs, dearie. And a great deal of trouble he's been, I can tell you. Such language I've never heard in my life, and I've been around long enough to hear quite a bit of it."

"Is he alive?"

"Of course he is. What a peculiar question."

"Then let me see him. Please. He was hurt, and I don't know what you've done to him."

"Now, dear, there's no need to upset yourself."

"What are you doing to us?"

My voice had risen almost to a shout. I tried to pull myself upright, but the ropes held fast, and I felt that awful pressure tightening around my wrists again.

The woman sighed and sat on the edge of the bed.

"You're a skinny little thing, aren't you? I noticed that when you arrived."

She reached for my hand. I tried to pull it beneath the blanket, but she caught my wrist before I could move it away.

"Please don't touch me."

"Oh, hush. I'm only looking."

Her fingers were cold and dry. She turned my hand over, pressing her thumb into the flesh beneath my knuckles, then examined each finger in turn. I tried pulling away again, but her grip was surprisingly strong.

"Honestly. Look at these little fingers. Nothing on them at all."

She gave my index finger a gentle squeeze and clicked her tongue.

"You'll need plenty of good meals before you're ready."

"Ready for what?"

"Oh, don't you worry your little head about that."

She smiled and reached toward the tray.

I don't know what came over me. Perhaps it was the way she'd been handling my hand or the thought of what she might already have done to Harrison. But when she leaned close enough to reach for my face, I jerked my head forward and tried to bite her hand. She drew back before my teeth could close around her fingers.

"Now, really."

There was no anger in her voice, only the weary disapproval of someone dealing with a badly behaved child. She reached into her apron and withdrew the little black doll.

"I was hoping we wouldn't have to do this again."

Her smile remained, but she watched me without a trace of warmth. Then she tightened the red thread around the doll's wrist. 

My arm jerked against the bedframe, and the same awful pressure seized my wrist. I cried out, twisting away from her, but the pain only grew worse.

"There," she said, loosening the thread slightly. "That's what happens when you make a fuss."

When she spoke again, her voice was as sweet as before. I was beginning to understand that she wasn't confused or unaware of what she was doing. She knew exactly how much she was hurting me. 

I stopped struggling.

She tucked the doll away, straightened the napkin on the tray, and picked up the spoon.

"Now, open up. No sense letting it get cold."

I stared at the spoon. My wrist still ached, and I could feel her watching me, waiting for another objection.
