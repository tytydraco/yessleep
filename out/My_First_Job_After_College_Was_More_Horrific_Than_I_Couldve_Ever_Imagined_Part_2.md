Most of my life, much of my existence really, is blunted. I move through life half there, detached; what little emotions I have are translucent, and struggle to break into the surface of my consciousness. Sometimes, however, I awake as if from a long dream, into what I imagine reality should be. Things are clear, lucid, I feel an ache in my chest. I close my eyes and I’m flying among the vibrant stars, reaching out and touching the dust of the milky-way. If only I was always like this, I say to myself, I would have everything figured out, I would see clearly. But, alas, it seems I am resigned to a mute hollowness. Why can’t I fix it?

I was brought back to my senses, a loud car horn startling me out of my thoughts. It was Frank, parked in front of the courthouse, glaring at me with impatience. I gave him a wave and stepped out of the stark shadows beneath the overhang into the bright afternoon sun. It was like stepping through a veil, one moment submerged in blackness, the next in the harsh light. I made my way down the steps, my eyes taking a moment to adjust. I breathed in all sorts of smells, pollen and a hint of cooking pizza, and enjoyed the lazy clouds making their way across the sky. At least it’s good to be alive on a day like today!

“This is awful, on a miserable day like this you’re taking your sweet time?” Frank scowled at me as I opened the door and sat next to him.

“How so?”

“Today’s rookie day. Every couple months, we do a joint training mission with all the newbies.” He pulled out, barely checking his mirrors. A car honked and he flipped them off. “Like a kindergarten field trip or something. We’re picking up Emma then heading to the site”

I rested my elbow on the window and watched the passing buildings blur together. “Well sorry I took so long. They really had it out for me.”

“For a damn parking ticket for your bike?”

“Yeah, two-fifty fine and everything. Whole thing. The judge got pretty mad too.”

“I can’t say you don’t deserve it,” he said, chuckling.

“I swear, they hate me, specifically. I feel like they even follow me sometimes.”

“Who follows … what? The judge?” Frank glanced at me with a raised eyebrow.

“No, not the judge. Well maybe,” I started becoming more animated, “Who knows who they delegate for that kind of stuff. But I feel eyes on me! And cars too, black vans!”

“Why would anyone take interest in you?”

“You know these nefarious organizations. Who knows what kind of twisted goals they have? When I couldn’t get a job, I thought they were on my ass for being unemployed.”

“So a conspiracy against bums?” He said, half in disbelief, half in mild amusement.

“Yes, that’s it, a conspiracy! It’s all connected!” I exclaimed.

Just then we stopped and Emma opened the door, only catching the last part, “Oh I think the Buddha spoke of that.”

“What?”

“Interconnecteness? I saw it on instagram reels. Or was it Jesus?” Oh damn, she doesn’t even use Tik-Tok. 

“Well, you know, I downloaded Instagram reels one time,” I replied. "First video, saw a Chinese construction worker turned into paste. Like fucking red mist. Brutal. After that I deleted it. But I was traumatized for a few hours, seriously.”

“The algorithms suggest what you like.” Frank interjected with a judgmental glance. He turned the car key, the engine rumbled to life, and we were off down the street again.

“Yeah, see! Spyware! My point exactly!”

In the days following my first at work, I must say I’m proud of how much I stepped up my game. After my shifts, I dedicated one hour to studying the employee handbook. Sometimes this would be cut into by tv watching or staring into space, I’m not perfect after all. Nonetheless, I managed to at least open the heavy, yellowed thing and spend time reading its contents and memorizing important details. 

In between policy amendments and pages upon pages of stuffy bureaucratic nonsense, there was useful practical information. Blue prints and instructions for the various odd gadgets that I now had access to, like the trusty suction-burst and others. It wasn’t necessary, far from it, as no permit was required for their use. So my studying was essentially non-essential. But, if I was going to improve at this job, and not find myself in a dangerous situation pants down, I had to learn it.

I always considered myself as having two modes. One, entirely inert, unable to find the energy to take a piss in the morning without an hour of social media scrolling. Two, typically as a result of external pressure, awake, focused, fluid, with a sense of mastery and competence. 

The best example, before a difficult exam, my brain could hold surprising amounts of information, even if I forgot it right after. And during those two hours of writing, I felt unstoppable, everything else falling away, and me becoming a conduit of the syllabus, hitting every point the professor wanted to hear. Academic sucking up as I called it.

Our first stop was at a gas station, around an hour from our destination, and two hours after we began. Emma had to go to the bathroom, and so Frank reluctantly pulled into a run-down, rusted establishment off the highway. It had a convenience store that served hot food, but after one look at the green skinned hot dogs, I settled on a drink. Outside, me and Frank waited, me sipping a lukewarm beer and him a Dr. Pepper. The high sun beat down and the sharp scent of gasoline and old piss floated up with the dust. 

“Don’t drink on the job?” I said. 

“Me? Not a drop these days, one year sober. Doc said it was bad for my health,” he said, his leg bent against the wall. 

“No offense but you don’t strike me as the health conscious guy.” I gestured to the cigarette limb that seemed perpetually attached to his mouth.

“Everyone needs their vices, you learn that around here quickly. That’s why I’m not writing you up for intoxication and negligence.”

I nodded in understanding of the first bit and in thanks for the second. A silence hung over us for a beat. At a pump, an overweight man with greasy black hair had his card declined, and he continued to mash it in and out of the reader in frustration. 

“I have a temper too. So it’s a good thing I’m not drinking, or I would’ve kicked your ass a while ago.” He laughed. 

I smiled slightly, unsure if he was completely joking, and changed the subject. “Hey, I’ve been meaning to ask. How the hell did you throw your hat so well back at the house?”

“This?” He removed his hat and inspected it. “It’s weighted, part of old tech produced by the bureau. Great for neck muscles.”

Emma walked out of the store, the bell over the door dinging. She stretched and fixed her hair. Not to be crude, but it always amazed me how women had the courage to use convenience store bathrooms. They *must* be the stronger sex, because every one I’ve been to is run down, the worst having unidentifiable residue on the toilet seats. Who knows how many buts have sat on it and the last time it was cleaned? There are options, sure, hovering, placing toilet paper over it. I wonder what Emma did?

“OKAY! Time to keep this road trip going, I’m so excited, I love long car rides, should we play eye spy next, or maybe—” she rambled as she marched to the car. 

With dawning horror, I tried to gulp but my mouth was too dry. She was a bare asser, no doubt about it. 

Another hour of incessant car games and the car rumbled to a halt at a modest farm house. In front of it, a beat down and loose dirt lot where rusted tools were scattered.  Beyond it, overgrown grass where crops may have once been grown, which stretched into the horizon, and a forest on one side that cut it off. I opened the car door,  stepped out onto the dirt, and a plume of dust scattered. Four other cars were parked outside, and a mass of seven people stood near the entrance.

“Finally. Five more minutes and we would’ve started without you,” said a large man and waved us over. He had an unkempt beard and a craggy forehead, his brows folding over his eyes. 

“Ha. The kids had to use the bathroom. I told them to go before we left.” Frank walked up to him and punched him in the arm. I joined the periphery of the group and my face scrunched at the comment. 

“Hey you must be the new guy. When did you start?” A guy said, around my age, with black hair. 

“Like two weeks ago. Names Stevie.”

“I’m Martin, I’ve been here for a month.”

“And I’m Casey.” Another guy, brown hair and glasses, smiling. 

“Nice to meet you guys.” 

I surveyed the group, three more men and one woman. That made ten of us with Frank, Emma, and I. 

“Okay, listen up!” Frank's friend boomed. “For those not aware, my name is Willy. This is a training mission, first and foremost. But it’s also an opportunity for you to meet your colleagues, learn to work as a team, and recognize that our culture plays as hard as we work!”

I shuddered. Here it comes. 

“As such, before we do anything, I’ll give everyone an opportunity to introduce themselves. State your name and four fun facts about yourself.”

Four! I’ve endured my fair share of draconian icebreakers before, but four? The highest I can ever think of is two, maybe three. Beyond that I always draw a blank, and end up standing there with a mock thinking pose until they move on. Here, I can’t afford that. My very career depends on getting this right!

“Why don’t you go first Patty?” Willy looked at the woman next to him. She was dressed in an oversized suit with an ill-fitting tie.

“Oh, well…” Her voice cracked a bit, and she wiped her palms on the pant legs. “I-I guess I like baking, and nature, and movies, and…” she spat out and stopped. “Uh… what else… oh my…”

Thank the Gods above, a sacrifice, and more time for me to think. I tilted my head up, then to the ground. A ladybug inched up a grass blade. I scratched my face. Still nothing. 

“I’m Lucas…”

“I’m Fredrick…”

Their voices blended together and then I couldn’t hear anything else over the pounding of my heart. The entirety of my being, all of my brainpower, was tunnel visioned on thinking of two more things that could be considered interesting, yet also non-offensive enough for a work environment.

“Well?” Willy said, his voice a harpoon that pierced my daze. Everyone was staring at me expectantly. 

“Me, uhh… You know…” I said.

He nodded, waited a second. “What?”

I shifted, and put my hand up to my chin, face burning. Even my other go to’s had escaped me. “I like dogs?”

“What kind of dog breed is your favorite?” Martin said and smirked at me.

“Uhhh…” Shit shit shit. “Poodles? They don’t shed?”

Silence again. Willy’s eyes widened and his seamed forehead lifted. Next to him Frank’s face was blank and expressionless. “Okay, next.”

I exhaled, a one hundred pound burden lifted off me. That could’ve gone a lot worse. Once the circuit had been run through, we moved to the house. I lagged behind the group with Casey and Martin, my hands in my pockets. A thunderhead rolled in across the horizon, the grey whirlpool of vapor devouring the pleasant blue. The air stilled, as if it was holding its breath, awaiting the impending storm. 

“So Poodles are really your favorite?” Martin said. 

“Oh that?”  I leaned against the stair railing. “I’m actually more of a cat person. I think my grandparents had a poodle though.”

“Wait, you don’t like dogs? Then why was that a fun fact?” Casey said. He looked at me like a science experiment, or one of those frogs he undoubtedly bisected alive as a kid. 

“I like them, just prefer cats.”

“Everyone look at me, look at me!” Willy stood on the porch and did jazz hands and a wiggly little dance. “We’re here because we got reports of an enmeshment incident. A few casualties around this area. There’s already a partially set up base of operations inside. After we get it all ready, we’ll scout a bit, take out any we see. Easy.”

In the field, a few figures appeared, and an inconsistent gait gave them a wobble. No one besides me noticed, their attention still on Willy. He yammered on and on: set up this there, do this and this or it’s a rule violation, don’t get written up. As he went on, the figures got closer and closer. I thought they were drunk farmers, angry that we were on their property. But when I made out their features, the scattered eyes and the red growths, my mild curiosity was replaced by familiar dread. 

“H-hey, guys?” I pointed. Willy shot me a glare, his lecture brought to an end. Then a grin stretched his wrinkles taut. 

“Looks like they came to us, that makes our job easier. Lucas, Damien, you’re with me.” 

They confidently strutted down the steps and Willy rolled up his sleeve to reveal a hidden blade that was strapped to his arm. He shook his hand and it extended, slicing some tall grass nearby. I made my way to where Frank and Emma were standing, up the stairs and near the door. Emma’s eyes tracked the men with a sparkle.

“More of those spider guys?” I asked.

“Hmmm-hmm.” Emma hummed.

“And if we’re lucky that’ll be all of ‘em and we can go home early,” Frank said. 

“Before the storm?” I glanced at it. It loomed above and methodically gnarled itself forward.

Emma held her finger out. “Should be about an hour. If there are more we’ll get caught in it.”

“Hour? How do you know that” 

“I’m interested in meteorology.” She shrugged. “That was one of my fun facts. Mereology too. Weren’t you listening?”

“Uh-huh.”

A cloud passed by overheard, momentarily darkening the area. Thunder rippled in the distance, and a breeze rustled the tall grass. The three men continued forward, efficiently, like this was as simple as flipping the hundredth burger of the day. I frowned. Some of the grass was moving differently, out of tune with the rest. 

The men had made it further into the field, within spitting distance of the figures, and Willy raised his hand in preparation to strike. I flicked my eyes up and tracked a single raindrop as it made its journey, landed on Willy's shirt, and rolled off, its final destination a hand reaching around his leg. I opened my mouth, nothing came out, I closed it and my eyes. The screaming started soon after. 

“What the fuck?!” Frank exclaimed.

The sweet darkness of ignorance felt like the warmth of a womb. But all babies must be brought screaming into the light of reality. I squinted my eyes open. All around, more figures were shooting out of the grass, too many to count. They descended upon the intrepid professionals, getting their fingers into any hole they could. 

They pulled, spreading open Willy and the others eye sockets, their mouths and tongue. The clothes, fiercely ripped off, allowed their fingers to dig into the skin, the intestines and guts. Willy writhed and yelled on the ground, his cool demeanor reduced to a frightened animal running on shock and adrenaline. He attempted to crawl away, the contents of his stomach exposed, yet more grabbed him and he was soon torn apart.

“Everyone inside now!” Frank ordered. 

He didn’t have to tell me twice. I swung open the door and dove inside, followed by Emma, Martin, Casey, Patty, another guy (Lucas?), and finally Frank. He slammed the door and braced against it. Everyone else stood, breathing hard, their perspiration running down their faces. Being the only one on the ground, I was primarily embarrassed. I got up, dusted myself off, and gave my voice an edge.

“What now, we need to figure something out,” I said.

Everyone's eyes shot towards me, like they were seeing me for the first time. I stood up straighter. My hands shook a bit so I hid them behind my back and gave my best stern expression. The eyes lingered for a beat then moved to Frank, who wore a grimace.

“We need to figure something out, get moving,” Frank said, and he left the door to pace the room. 

I nodded along with the others. Apparently they forgot this was my idea or hadn’t heard me. Best course was to pretend I hadn’t said anything either and kick myself later. 

“It had to be some kind of ambush,” Frank said.

“Is this behavior out of the ordinary?” Martin questioned.

“Not particularly. But the scale is highly unusual,” Emma responded.

“Oh my God, they’re dead…” Patty covered her mouth and rushed to a corner, where she promptly began sobbing.

I scooted to the window and opened the curtain. Hundreds, perhaps thousands of them, enough to saturate the field with bodies, made their way towards us. I yanked it shut and the metal rings scraped the bar.

“Not good,” I said.

“How many?” Frank asked.

“More than we can handle with our stuff.” There was machinery around the room, but little of any kind of heavy duty weaponry. Mostly spy gadgets and smaller trinkets.

“Okay. First, we need to see what we’re working with. Emma and Casey, that’s on you. See if there’s any useful weapons or items. Me, Martin, Fredrick, and Stevie will fortify the room. Got it?”

Two things to note. First, Lucas, of course, was the one who was retired with Willy. So it was actually Fredrick who joined us. Second, as I was clumsy searching the room for a hammer and board to adhere to the window, I noticed a buzzing in my pocket. I thought it was my phone, perhaps my mom checking in on me. But, when I reached into my pocket, I pulled out a disc shaped thing. It was pulsing and a red light was flashing angrily. 

“What is that?” Frank was staring, jaw clenched.

“I don’t know, I found it in my pocket. Weird thing, right?”

“You –  you fucking idiot.” His hands cradled his face. “Are you wearing the same pants as our first assignment? Did you even wash them?”

“Well, I-I only wore them once you see, and –”

“THAT’S A FUCKING EMITTER! IT DRAWS THEM TO US!” he bellowed. 

The room went silent and everyone was frozen. Before I could respond, clomping footsteps made their way up the outside steps and the door burst open.

“Moshi moshi!” A crazed man stood in the doorway, his hair a mess and eyes wild. Who is that? 

Suddenly, he exploded. Blood, gore, and bone fragments splattered the room and the gush of air from it tossed my hair back. I spat out what might’ve been some brain and large intestine mixed with skull, coppery and meaty in my mouth. I imagine this was close to what people experienced going from black and white film to technicolor. The once monochromatic wall of the room was now covered with a neon red. 

Too much stimulation, an assault of colors and shapes gave me a screaming headache. I pressed my hand against my head, half consciously backed into the corner, besides Patty, and slid down the wall into a fetal position, holding my legs close and shivering. There was an occasional drop of liquid, and I couldn’t tell if it was a leak in the ceiling, dripping blood from my face, or tears. 

The scene resumed around me, though it took on a rather dreamlike quality. Frank barked orders and there was a mad rush as people fortified. There was banging on the walls, the figures trying to break in. Emma was next to a pile of machines, tinkering, reverie and intense focus on her face, completely suited for this environment, a fish in water. She was wiring multiple suction bursts to a central matrix, and attaching a smattering of other gizmos and gadgets. I focused on Frank's words, unsure of how much time had passed.

“The first thing that is non-negotiable is drawing them away. Obviously, given enough time, they’ll break in. The good news is, what got us into this mess will also get us out of it,” Frank said.

People rested and stood in a semi-circle around him. He picked up one of the circular emitters from a large pile. He was in a pragmatic mindset, but I couldn’t help noticing an offness to his mannerisms, like he was close to losing it and barely holding together. The more he spoke, the more that dissipated, and the tenor and confidence of his voice strengthened.  

He continued, “The bad news is, we’ll need volunteers. Given the present situation, there’s a possibility they won’t make it, and the plan will fail. Meaning, both personal risk and risk to the entire group.”

“Whats the status on back up? Or a clean-up crew,” Casey asked.

“Shit, at this rate, we’ll need an entire army,” Martin said. 

“At least an hour out.” Frank sighed. “We’re on our own. And I hate to say, this amount likely means a queen is nearby.”

Fredrick pointed to Emma. He was a jock, hat turned back, likely well versed in the art of getting drunk off light beer and putting roofies in women’s drinks. “That seems like a bad idea.” At least prudence hadn’t escaped him. 

“Like Frank said, we’re on our own. Plus it’ll be cool,” Emma said.

“I ain’t got nothing wrong with explosions in movies, but I don’t wanna die either,” Fredrick responded. 

“Ideally we’ll be long gone before it goes off,” Frank said. “And if not, better than being torn apart. I understand it’s desperate, however the tactical advantage is invaluable. If successful, it’ll seriously interrupt their echolocation, agitate them, give us an opening to get out of here. What’s the progress Emma?”

“Ten minutes until it’s ready, and I can install a fifteen minute timer. Thirty minutes total, if we wanted it to be fast,” Emma said.

“Fast would be best,” Frank said. “As I said, we need volunteers. Who’s willing to get out there, save our asses? There might be a bonus.”

“I do *noooot* trust that bomb,” Fredrick whined. Probably because he didn’t like the idea of a woman engineer. “Better out there than here. I’ll go man.”

“I suppose I’m feeling rather claustrophobic as well. Count me in. I was endowed, as a child, with utter self confidence and the belief that I can will–" Casey said. 

My head pounded, cut him off, and I put it between my legs. The image kept replaying in my head. The man exploding, the people torn apart outside, my obvious fault. I was terrified, sure, but an overwhelming guilt tore at me, the indisputable fact that I was responsible for the deaths of others, that my life was over. I staggered up, things snapped back into focus.  
   
“I’ll go,” I proclaimed.

Everyone stopped and stared at me.

“Absolutely not,” Martin said and walked up to me. “In case you forgot, you’re a dumbass. You’ll get us all killed.”

“I have to do this.”

“No you don’t.”

“And you want to?”

“Better me than you!” He shoved me.

Frank stepped in and broke us apart. He whispered something to Martin, who frowned then smiled and relented . 

Frank addressed everyone, “I’m not the biggest fan of it either. The damage is already done, though. We have three people, if only one succeeds, those are good odds. It’s a warm body game. Not like we have any other choice.” 

Frank turned to me and put a hand on my shoulder. It was imperceptibly shaking. “I can’t sugar coat it kid, you fucked up big time. But pull this off? We’re sitting pretty, capiche? So don’t fuck up again or weasel out or I’ll kill you.”

I nodded, a peculiar resolve bubbling within me. 

“Listen up everyone!” He faced the room. “Here’s the plan!”
