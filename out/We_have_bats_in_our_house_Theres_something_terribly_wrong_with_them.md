On Wednesday morning, I woke up to an alert no parent wants to see.

*RABIES ALERT*

*Animals in the vicinity of Brookhaven in Franklin Township have been testing positive for rabies at a rate higher than expected. We recommend the following tips for staying safe:*

*Do not touch wild animals.*

*Stay away from wild animals, especially raccoons, skunks, bats, and foxes.*

*If you see any animal exhibiting the following symptoms, contact Franklin Animal Control immediately: \[REDACTED\].*

We only moved to Brookhaven two months ago. It seemed like the perfect neighborhood. *Safe.* A little off the beaten path, out in the woods, but that was the way we liked it.

“But, like, a rabies *outbreak?”* I whispered to my husband David as he put frozen waffles in the toaster. “Has that ever even happened before?!”

“Bill says there’s an alert every summer. It’s just that time of year,” he muttered to me. “Like, it’s like Alpha Gal and Zika and all the things. You know, people are playing outside, animals out of hibernation…”

“Okay, but we never got anything like this is Sawyerville.”

“Yeah, 'cause they sprayed a hundred pounds of insecticide every year.”

“Maybe that’s better than rabies.”

“Rabies is *really* rare.”

He was right. As I brought up Google, it told me only 3 Americans die of rabies per year. A hundred thousand, though, got the rabies vaccine after an exposure.

You couldn’t wait for symptoms.

If you already had symptoms, you were dead.

*So we just get the vaccine if a wild animal bites us. That’s fine. We’re fine. And we’ll fence in the backyard like we’d been planning to do anyway.*

Unfortunately, our problem was not in the raccoons or skunks or whatever was in the woods.

We had bats.

I’d never looked for them before. It never even occurred to me. But that night I looked out the window, and saw no less than *ten fucking bats* swooping out of the eaves of our family room.

*You will likely not feel it if a bat bites you,* the website read, next to an up-close photo of the horrible, smushed-pug face of a little brown bat. *Simply picking up a bat, or finding one in your home, is an exposure.*

*If you find a bat in your home, and cannot capture it to test it for rabies, immediately head to the nearest ER to receive the rabies vaccine.*

I set my phone down and froze, staring out at the darkening sky, and the little silhouettes swooping and swerving mere feet from the window.

*No, no, no.*

\*\*\*

“Well bats, you know, can’t be removed between July 1 and August 15. That’s the roosting season, and the pups can’t fly yet, so they will die if we install the grates and their mothers can’t return to feed them.”

“You… you can’t remove them?”

“Not until August 15, no.”

“Listen. I have a five year old girl and a seven year old boy with autism. *They will not know if a bat bites them.”*

“It’s federal law, ma’am. They’re a protected species.”

*Like fuck they are!*

“We’re in the middle of a rabies outbreak, and you’re telling me you can’t remove the bats in my house?!”

“It’s not a rabies outbreak, it’s just that time of year.”

“What happens if they get in the house?”

“If they’re in your living space, you need to call animal control.”

“But they could get in at any time! I can’t wait until it’s too late!”

“It’s only…twenty-two days until August 15. Just sit tight. Call animal control if you find one in your house. Try to catch it with a box or something, like a Tupperware, so they can test it and you don’t need to blow ten thousand dollars on the shots for no reason. It’ll go by fast. Promise.”

*You expect me to… catch a bat… in the house… with Tupperware?*

*What the actual* fuck?!

“So that’s it then. There’s nothing I can do.”

“Nothing you can do.”

*Nothing I can do, my ass.*

I’m all for the environment. But holy fuck, I’m not going to protect an entire colony of bats sitting in the eaves of my house. Risk my kids’ lives to save some baby bats. That’s right—the colony was probably a lot bigger than just ten, with all the babies sitting somewhere inside the walls, growing, waiting.

“You can go to jail if you kill them, you know,” David said behind me.

I turned away from the Amazon listing for wasp spray. “Well, I can’t just sit here and do nothing!”

“Okay. But also, if you spray them, it says they can burrow deeper into the house and actually maybe get inside,” he said as he looked at his phone. “I looked it up. They can’t chew through drywall like mice and squirrels can. If they could get in the house, they’d be in already. They’re just in the eaves or soffits or whatever. For the time being, we’re safe.”

“This is a nightmare,” I said, slamming the laptop and hiding my face in my hands.

“It could be worse. My parents, when they bought a new house, found all this black mold that the inspection missed. Had to fumigate the whole place.”

“Wait—shouldn’t the inspector have looked for bats?”

He shrugged. “They’re not in the house. Not really.”

*Why am I the only one taking this seriously?!*

*\*\*\**

We found the first bat on July 25th.

It was clinging to the outside of the chimney. I found it while the kids were playing outside. My head pounded and I screamed at them to go inside and tell David to bring out some Tupperware.

It wasn’t in the house.

But it had been where the kids were playing.

Such a small little thing I almost missed it. Wings all twisted and bony, clinging to the brick with its little claws. Fur almost blending in with the red brick. David managed to put the Tupperware over it, and drag a chair over to lean against it so it wouldn’t fall. We had captured it. Sort of.

We called animal control (who I’d put in my contacts as soon as I was done talking to the bat removal guy.) The woman reached out and grabbed it with her gloves…

And *peeled* it off the brick.

It was dead. Had been, for at least a few days. Fried in the heat wave.

“It probably died in the heat,” the animal control officer assured me. But I spent four sleepless nights googling, reading that rabid bats *were* more susceptible to heat death. *Why did only one die? Not all ten?*

When I got the voicemail, I felt like I was going to faint.

“The bat we removed from your home tested… negative,” the woman said, her voice sounding strange, uncertain almost. But I was barely paying attention.

The backyard was now off-limits to the kids. My husband thought I was being extreme. But the kids go out barefoot. If any of the bats were rabid, they would fall out of their roost (*our FUCKING house)* and be in the grass (to be trampled barefoot) or cling to the side of the house where my kids would touch it.

I cannot check every square inch of the yard for fallen bats every day. I just can’t.

\*\*\*

I bought three 1-acre-rated bug zappers and set them up around the yard.

Bats eat bugs. Take away their food source, I reasoned, and they will leave.

When doing my research, Google’s AI helpfully told me “Bug zappers mostly kill beneficial insects. A better alternative to removing mosquitos from your yard is to attract bats.”

Fucking hell.

*You’re killing beneficial insects! Pollinators! t*he results screamed at me. I’m sorry but at this point I don’t fucking care. Cut off the food source of the bats and they will go somewhere else. The end.

Do I sound unhinged?

Well, yeah. I *am* unhinged after finding a fucking bat on my fucking house and interrogating my children about whether they saw or touched a bat every single day.

I’m tired of telling my kids they can’t go outside and taking them on all kinds of impromptu field trips just to get them out of the house.

I’m tired of not feeling safe in my own home.

I’m tired of my husband refusing to move because it would cost too much. It would. We don’t have the money. He’s right. But we do have the money to buy bug zappers.

After having the zappers set up for a few days, I watched the house at dusk as I sat in the car in the driveway.

I watched them swoop out of a hole between the chimney and the house, plummeting down to the ground before swooping up at the last second. Their movements were wild and twitchy, every direction absolutely random as they scoured the yard for insects.

But the bug zappers hadn’t worked.

It was about a dozen, maybe more. Fucking hell.

So the next night I tried a new slant. I kept the outside lights on all night and set up a plastic owl on the deck. Owls eat them apparently. They’re afraid of owls.

Nope, did not a thing.

*How to attract owls into your yard?* I typed frantically into Google.

*Green spaces, shelter, treeline...* all things I already have.

“At least they’re not in the house yet,” David kept repeating.

He kept adding that word on the end now. *Yet.* Like it wasn’t a matter of if, but *when.*

*\*\*\**

After the kids went to sleep, I was in the kitchen, trying to do a quick load of dishes. As I stared out the window over the sink, into the darkness of the backyard—

Something swooped upwards, inches from the window.

I froze. *Was that a…*

*No. It had to be a moth.*

*A big moth.*

I turned off the faucet and walked over to the sliding glass door. My hand hovered over the light switch. Taking a deep breath, I flicked it on.

Something swooped wildly away from the light, and into the darkness.

*That was a bat.*

I stared out, waiting. At the scones outside, washing the deck in yellow light. The pitch blackness beyond. Nothing moved. All was still.

*Maybe the light scared it away.*

I turned off the light. Counted to ten. Held my breath and flicked it on.

*No.*

It was back. It swooped at eye level towards the glass, missing it by only an inch as its little body tilted upwards. In the lighting it looked too pale, almost gray, as its sharp wings fluttered wildly.

I watched in horror as it looped around the deck, over and over. Only three or four feet above the wood, at the level the kids would be standing. Then it wildly careened into the darkness.

*Fuck fuck fuck.*

Seconds later, it came back. Another few wild loops right above the deck, wings beating wildly. *Why won’t it leave?!*

*Fuck fuck why is here?!*

*So close to the house?!*

*Right over the deck?*

“David!” I yelled.

“It’s got to be rabid. Why is it doing that?!” I asked as we both stared at it, wildly swooping right over the deck.

“I don’t know. But it’s not in the living space.”

I looked at him.

“Yet,” he added.

“It might as well be! They play out there all the time!”

So now the deck was off limits. I found an old broom handle and shoved it into the groove of the glass door, so it couldn’t be easily opened.

Fuck this. Seriously.

\*\*\*

One fell into the chimney.

The flue was closed, so it couldn’t get into our living space, but I could hear it frantically screeching, the thumps of its little feet as it looked for an opening.

I called animal control again. Nothing they can do if it’s in the chimney, they told me. Call back when it’s in the living space.

*When.*

“What if it gets in?” I sobbed to David.

“It won’t,” he replied. “Yet.”

“What do you mean, *yet?!”*

“It won’t get in.”

Fucking hell.

I grabbed postal tape and taped the space under the door. And all around the hinges. I made the door absolutely impenetrable to even the tiniest insect. There was no way a bat could get through.

No way.

*How long does it take for a bat to die with no food or water?* I typed into Google.

Three days. Three days I left the family room sealed, the crack under the door taped tight. Fuck I hated this. But at least they weren’t in the living space.

*Yet.*

*I untaped the door and went into the family room and that fucking bat in the chimney was still alive, screeching for help, four days later.*

I called the pest control companies. *Can’t you come earlier than August 15?* No, no, fucking no because there are laws about this and we wouldn’t want the baby bats to die in your chimney.

*But there is a bat dying in my chimney right now!*

*It’s a baby!*

*Come and rescue it!*

*Sorry, can’t help.*

*What the fuck.*

Only 1-3 people die from rabies in the US per year. Many more die from the diseases that mosquitos carry. The bats eat mosquitos. Statistically speaking, you’re more likely to die from having mosquitos in your yard than bats. That’s what they say, right? That’s what the internet says. The fucking pro-bat internet.

Do I sound unhinged?

I mean, they’re not in the living spaces yet.

*Okay, so I’m a fucking eco-terrorist,* I thought as I waited in line at Home Depot with a spool of copper mesh and a long dowel. My uncle told me that he got his bats to leave by poking copper mesh in all the holes they went into. He did it within the legal window of course. It was August eighth and I was not going to wait another week.

“It’s still alive,” I told David as I re-taped the door to the family room. I could hear it chirping frantically. “*It’s been a week!”*

“Maybe it’s a second one that fell in there.”

“Oh, no, no.”

That night I watched them fly out. Easily twenty-five. When they satiated their hunger and took to higher skies around ten o’clock, I went out there and shoved copper mesh into the holes.

For a split second I thought about the poor little bat babies starving to death in the cavities of the wall.

*Sorry not sorry.*

I’m not a monster.

*They’re not in the living spaces yet.*

*This is an arms race.*

I slept well that night. I thought I’d done it. I really did. I sealed up that hole like fucking Fort Knox. That night I snickered at the chirping coming from the family room. *Soon. Soon this will all be over.*

In the morning I went out to check my work.

*Fuck.*

The copper mesh had been busted in. Like twenty bats fucking pushed on it all at once. The metal was *torn.*

What… the fuck.

As I stared at the hole, that horrible black void, I heard a single *chirp.* I ran inside and locked the door, putting the broom handle in the rut of the sliding glass door so the kids couldn’t get out easily.

“How did they get through it?!” I asked David, my voice shaking. “*How?!”*

“You’re overreacting,” he said. “They’re not in the living spaces yet.”

That night I ordered wasp spray. Overnight, 4AM-11AM delivery from the Zon.

This is where I crossed the line, you know. You could apply the philosophical principle of double effect to all the other cases. I just wanted the bugs out of my yard, so I set up the zappers. I just wanted to patch up holes in my house. Killing the bats or causing them to leave was only a consequence.

Try explaining away shooting wasp spray in the hole they come out of. There’s really no explanation. *I thought wasps lived there. I was trying to get wasps.* But there weren’t any wasps, and any police officer or animal control person or whoever the fuck was my jailer in this case would know that.

I didn’t care.

In broad daylight, I set up a ladder and aimed that wasp spray right in the hole in the copper mesh. *Got you, fuckers.*

I took a deep breath—and hit the trigger.

A horrible cacophony of screeching immediately sounded from the hole. *Fuck, fuck, fuck.* I hurried down the ladder, the metal wobbling underneath me.

Something whizzed by my ear, missing me by a hair.

*No!*

I booked it and ran. The chirping behind me got louder. I didn’t look back once and made it to the door, lunging inside, closing it behind me. Only then did I look.

A single bat flew through the air, just a flash of darkness before swooping up over the roof.

*That was close.*

I buried the wasp spray can at the bottom of the garbage like I was hiding a body. “You okay?” David asked, but seeing me breathless.

“I’m fine.”

That night, the bats came out that night like nothing happened.

*And the bat in the chimney was still fucking screeching, nine days later.*

The strobe lights.

Cheap Halloween ones. Three fifty-foot extension cords. Apparently they deter and disorient bats, if I’m to believe Redditor pm-me-your-toes. Timmy and Ava spent the last two hours turning the dining room into a rave with them—at least now I know Timmy doesn’t suffer from epilepsy, I guess.

After they went to bed I methodically set them up all around the backyard, pointing them at the sky. With a deep breath, I plugged them in.

The lights flashed wildly, slightly out-of-sync with each other, against the blue siding and the dark sky. A moth flit by, seeming to move in slow motion as it flashed across the sky. I checked the time. 8:38. Almost bat time.

I stationed myself by the family room window and stared out at the darkening dusk. The sky deepened blue and then I saw it--the first bat swooping down from the eaves, like a bullet through the night sky.

It caught in the strobe lights.

Like stop-motion animation, halting and jumping in its flight. It swooped straight down and I saw its small body, its leathery bony wings stretched wide, perfectly illuminated against the darkness.

There was something horribly wrong with it.

Its wings seemed to be twisted behind itself, grotesquely. Its little body seemed bent and broken. And its eyes... its eyes were pure red, like caught in a camera flash.

Then it was gone.

*That didn’t look like a normal bat.*

*It has to be rabid.*

But it looked worse than rabid. Not just weak or aggressive or acting strangely. It looked like its bones had been broken, many times over, and yet it was still flying.

The light must’ve just caught it in a weird, split-second pose.

*But it looked so wrong.*

Maybe the wasp spray had hurt them. Disfigured them. Maybe they were in the slow process of dying. Or maybe it was a baby, just learning how to fly and twisting all strangely.

I watched the others swoop out, but they avoided the strobe lights, flying further into the sky where they did loops high above our house.

So maybe it deterred them, *slightly.*

*\*\*\**

I went downstairs at 2 AM to get a snack. Was up tossing and turning for hours, and now I was hungry and that was keeping me up. I grabbed a yogurt out of the fridge—but before I went back up, I looked out at the backyard and the strobe lights flashing up into the sky.

The air was still.

No bats—at least none that I could see.

*Was that really all it took?*

*Some $5 strobe lights?!*

I peered out into the darkness. Nothing. I leaned over and flicked the light switch. The lights above the deck clicked on.

At first I didn’t see anything.

Then—

*“Oh God oh fuck no no no…”*

They were on the side of the house.

On the deck railing.

Little brown bodies clinging on with their sinewy hands. Small as mice. Still as death. Then one twitched, and several black wings stretched out into the night. One of them flew wildly into the air, and the rest followed, bony wings breaking and twisting.

*No no no.*

*\*\*\**

“We have to get out of here!” I sobbed.

It was 3 AM. David sat on the other side of the bed, not facing me. “We can’t afford to move,” he said tiredly. “You know that.”

“They were all on the house!”

“They’re not in the living spaces yet!”

“Stop fucking saying that! *They will get in the living spaces! Don’t you get it?! And then it will be too late! And the kids!”*

I got up to tear him a new one.

My foot caught on something, poking out from under the bed.

*What the…*

I pulled it out. It was a pair of bolt cutters… and in its jaws, a little piece of tangled copper wire.

“David—”

I froze.

*Oh, God.*

It was coming from the wall.

An awful, high-pitched chirp. Chaotic and frantic.

I’d recognize it anywhere.

My heart dropped. “David!”

The chirping got louder. I got up and slowly stepped forward, scanning the wall for cracks and holes. Bats couldn’t chew through wall—I knew that. But if there was an opening...

*Scrtch scrtch scrtch*

The chirps and scratches got louder, then softer, as the bat got closer, then further away. It was going the entire width of the wall. Searching for a way out.

“Do you hear it?!”

“I don’t hear a thing.”

I stared at the wall. “Get the fuck out of my house,” I whispered under my breath, as the frantic chirps continued. I lifted a hand, shaking, and pressed it against the wall. I could feel the slightest of vibrations, imagining its little body colliding with the other side as it haphazardly flew along the inside.

*We’re safe. It can’t get through walls.*

The screeching sound rang out from behind me. I spun around, staring at the opposite wall.

*Scree-sc-scree*

In front of me now.

*Oh no. Are there two?!*

I spun around, listening. *Scrtch-scrtch-scrtch.* Coming from every fucking direction.

*They can’t get in—*

*They’re not in the living spaces—*

But then I heard a metallic *thump.*

*Oh God Oh God—*

*It’s in the vents—*

*The kids—*

I tore out of the room and ran down the hallway. Burst into Ava’s room. Asleep. I grabbed her by the shoulders. “Come on, we have to get out of here,” I said, not even sure of my plan. The bats were outside. Was it safer to stay in?!

As she groggily pulled herself up, I ran into Timmy’s room.

He was already awake.

He stood there, near the window. And there, next to him…

A small shape on the wall.

*Nononono...*

*“Timmy,”* I called. *“Timmy, come here, sweetheart.”*

He didn’t respond. He just stood there, standing in the dark room, facing the window.

“Timmy!”

His hand reached up towards the little shape, clinging on the wall.

*“DON’T TOUCH IT!"*

I lunged forward. It was too late. His little fingers wrapped around the bat, and he brought it to his chest, looking down.

*We have to catch it--*

*Get it tested--*

*Oh God--*

I frantically glanced around the room for something, anything, to catch it with. I grabbed a cup off the desk and started for him. “Okay, just put it in he—”

My breath caught in my throat.

Timmy was looking at me.

His eyes sparkled in the near darkness. His face was tilted down, but he was staring straight at me, from underneath his eyebrows. His lips were curled into a smile, and a thin trail of drool dripped down his chin.

*No, no, no.*

“They’re in the living spaces.”

I whipped around.

David was standing in the doorway, silhouetted in the darkness. His voice was low and gravelly. My heart began to pound. The bat chirped frantically in Timmy’s hands. It rang clear through my soul.

David took a step forward.

Then another.

And another.

There was something wrong with his face. Something *horribly* wrong. His eyes were black pits, staring hollowly at me.

“Stop—”

Those black pits began to move.

I watched in horror as two bats squirmed their way out of his eye sockets. Their black wings stretched out, flapping in the air, glistening wet with viscera like a newborn child. Their movements jerky, uncoordinated, new like a foal learning to walk.

“I couldn’t let the babies die,” David repeated, as one of the bats crawled down the side of his face, its claws burrowing into his cheek. “We had to save them.”

I turned around. Pried the screeching bat out of Timmy’s hand. David took a wobbling step forward. “The bats are in the living spaces now,” he repeated. “Isn’t that wonderful?”

He took another step—and his body wavered forward.

He fell to the ground.

And then I heard it.

Chirping. So much chirping, a horrible chorus grating on our ears. *It was coming from David.* The shirt on his back began to writhe and ripple, as something roiled under his skin—

“Run!” I screamed.

I got Ava and the three of us ran for the stairs. I guided the kids to the car, not looking back as the chirping became a roar. I threw the door open and we ran to the car. I saw the bats clinging to the side of the porch, dark little shapes in the moonlight, but I pretended like I didn’t see them.

We had to run.

We *had* to.

\*\*\*

We made it to the hospital. We got our rabies shots.

Timmy seems to be okay. So does Ava. It’s been a month now and the doctor thinks they are halfway out of the woods for contracting rabies. Although it’s not rabies I’m worried about.

David’s body was never found.

There were more rabies alerts sent out to the town, that I read about online from the safety of my parents’ house hundreds of miles away.

But there is something I haven’t told anyone.

Not my parents. Not my kids.

For the past few days, I’ve been having the worst headaches. It feels like there are twin knives slicing through my brain.

Right behind my eyes.

Tonight, I left. I told everyone I’m visiting a friend for a night. I’m driving to a remote hiking trail in the state park and hiking to the cliff that overlooks a gorge.

I can’t let them, or anyone else, catch this.

As I stare at myself in the rearview mirror, I can see the flash of a little black claw curling out.

And hear the muffled chirps of something deep inside my brain.
