If you work in healthcare, you know a catatonic patient cannot react. When that woman stared straight at me, I thought I was hallucinating. I had no idea she had been searching for me for twenty-two years.

It all started when I was twenty-two, a year after getting my nursing license. I was working at Mission Hospital in Asheville, North Carolina, a massive complex where the night shifts felt like they would never end.

If I chose that exact career and that exact place, it was to follow in the footsteps of my adoptive mother. Twenty-two years earlier, when I was only two months old, someone abandoned me in the parking lot of that very hospital, wrapped up in a bundle of blankets. It was my mom, who was the nurse on duty at the time, who found me out there in the cold.

I was healthy, but I had a deep, small cut on my right forearm, a scrape the doctors attributed to friction against the asphalt, and I still carry the scar today. After a year in foster care, she and her husband did everything they could to adopt me.

The police never found a car, a security camera, or a single trace of whoever left me there. I grew up with that mark on my arm and a constant, obsessive need to figure out who my biological parents were and why they had gotten rid of me.

It was a Wednesday night, and I was working the night shift. Everything was going smoothly until the radio at the triage desk alerted us to an incoming ambulance.

*"Mission Triage, this is Medic 4. We’re five minutes out with a female patient, approximately forty years old. Found lying by the side of the road by a passing driver. Initial acute psychomotor agitation, followed by a sudden collapse with an altered level of consciousness. Suspected overdose or severe psychiatric crisis. She’s dirty, non-responsive, and her vital signs are borderline. Prepare a trauma box."*

"Ah, there’s always drugs involved," a coworker commented dismissively, shaking his head.

A few minutes later, the paramedics pushed the gurney through the doors at full speed. But the second I laid eyes on the woman’s face, a wave of chills washed over me. Her blonde hair, matted with mud and sweat, was plastered to her forehead. Her eyes were wide open, staring blankly into space like a taxidermied animal, and her lips were dry, cracked, and caked with dirt. There was no panic left on that face; there was nothing at all.

But the most disturbing part was that, amid the chaos of the ER, those glassy eyes locked right onto mine.

"Ma'am, can you hear me?" I tried asking, lightly touching her shoulder.

No response. Not even a blink. Just a stranger's gaze pinned squarely on me.

I moved into action. I placed the ECG leads across her chest, clipped the pulse oximeter onto her finger, and wrapped the blood pressure cuff around her arm.

Her blood pressure was dangerously low; her body was severely dehydrated, and her vital organs were struggling for perfusion. Her oxygen saturation had also plummeted far below the safe threshold. Her heart, however, was fighting: on the monitor, the green line traced a frantic, irregular tachycardia.

We had to move fast. We started her on a rapid bolus of normal saline to push her pressure back up and placed an oxygen mask over her face. Through the flurry of tubes, alarms, and shouted orders, the woman didn't move an inch. Not a groan, not a reflex. She just kept staring at me.

I wanted to scream at her to take her eyes off me. I had never felt so uncomfortable, so irrationally terrified of a look that seemed to dig right through me, without having any idea why.

Following emergency protocols to rule out hidden bleeding or injuries, I grabbed my trauma shears and sliced her hoodie and pants open from side to side.

Her body was a mosaic of layered bruises, purple and yellowish ecchymoses telling the story of both old and recent abuse. Decades of small, circular scars dotted the skin of her arms and chest: cigarette burns repeatedly snuffed out on her flesh. She was horrifyingly emaciated, and the soles of her shoeless feet were cut up and covered in dirt.

Together with a coworker, I gently rolled her onto her side to inspect her spine. That was when we saw it.

Carved into the flesh of her back was a deep scar, a geometric symbol that looked as though it had been sculpted with a cold blade: two interlocking circles crossed by a single, deep vertical line right down the center.

We all stood there horrified. Who in their right mind would get their back disfigured like that? It looked like the work of some sick ritual, a live-flesh "tattoo" that had healed terribly.

Instinctively, I reached out with my gloved hand and lightly touched the center of that symbol, right along the vertical line.

A sudden jolt of heat shot through my fingertips, almost burning.

"She’s hot... the skin back here is scorching! Let's get a temperature!" I blurted out in surprise.

But the infrared thermometer registered a normal reading, leaning even toward hypothermia.

Still, the most chilling part remained her face. Even though she was turned on her side, she twisted her neck toward me. Those glassy eyes never detached from my figure. Not even for a millisecond.

The blood results came back thirty minutes later, leaving the entire team open-mouthed. The toxicology screen was completely negative. No alcohol, no meth, no opioids or psychiatric meds. Her system was clean, aside from the extreme malnutrition.

We told ourselves it had to be a catatonic mental breakdown, triggered by some devastating event she had suffered shortly before: unmitigated violence, a brutal attack that had driven her to flee barefoot until she collapsed on the asphalt.

None of us could have imagined the truth yet.

They transferred her to a secure room in the Intensive Care Unit, logged simply as *Jane Doe*. An unknown woman without a name.

A police officer intercepted me in the hallway, and I reported everything we had found to him.

"Did she have any ID? A phone?"

I shook my head. "No, the paramedics found her with nothing on her."

The police took her fingerprints, but the system returned no matches. A forensics tech took a DNA swab right away, but we knew it would take days, if not weeks, to sequence it and find a potential hit.

Just as the officer thanked me and headed out of the unit, a throbbing shock hit my temples. A sharp, violent, sudden migraine, like a blade driven straight into my head.

I went into the staff med room to take a painkiller with a sip of water; I still had several hours left before I could go home.

Shortly after, I went back into her room to check her vitals. Her numbers had stabilized. She was still staring at the ceiling, motionless.

I stepped closer to the bed and whispered the first thing that came from my heart:  
"You're safe now. Whatever happened to you... we've got you now."

I thought she hadn't even heard me. But as I turned to leave, I saw two slow tears roll down from her wide-open eyes.

My heart sank. I gently wiped her cheeks and sat in the chair beside the bed. That was when I felt the strangest sensation: despite the horror of that night, sitting next to her brought me a deep, almost irrational sense of peace.

It was as if warmth were emanating from her body. It was the same feeling of pure, untainted comfort I used to feel as a child, cuddling my favorite stuffed animal before falling asleep.

I stayed in that room for a few minutes until an emergency in the unit forced me to step away.

When a moment of calm finally came around four in the morning, I decided to step outside for a smoke and get some fresh air.

The employee parking lot was wrapped in an unnatural silence. I leaned against the brick wall, lit a cigarette, and took a deep drag.

That was when I noticed it.

In a shaded area far off, well beyond the reach of the lampposts, stood the tall silhouette of a man. He was completely motionless, arms hanging flat at his sides, his torso tilted slightly forward as if he were staring directly at me.

My heart jumped straight into my throat. 

"Is someone there?" I called out, thinking it might be a confused patient.

No answer. I blinked and leaned forward to get a better look into the dark. When I opened my eyes again, the figure was gone. In its place stood nothing but a burnt-out light pole.

I let out a shaky sigh of relief, calling myself an idiot.

*"Jesus... this night is off to a terrible start,"* I muttered to myself, convinced fatigue was playing vicious tricks on my mind.

I went back inside and spent the last few hours of my shift monitoring the woman. The last time I stepped into her room for checks, I noticed the IV flow had stopped.

I walked over to the bed, moving on pure autopilot: I crimped the plastic tubing slightly to make sure it wasn't kinked and gently tapped the drip chamber to get the drops flowing again.

Right then, the heart monitor let out an anomalous sound. Not the usual sharp alarm tone, but a dull, low, prolonged beep. It sounded eerily like the distorted whine of an electronic toy with dying batteries.

A cold shiver ran down my spine. I turned to look at the screen immediately, but the tracing picked back up its regular rhythm, and the pitch of the beep returned to normal.

I bent down to make sure the power cord was plugged firmly into the wall outlet. When I stood back up, my eyes caught the reflection in the large glass window.

Behind my silhouette, hovering in the shadows of the room, were two red dots. They looked like two glassy eyes glowing in the dark, pinned squarely onto the back of my neck.

My heart fluttered violently in my chest, taking my breath away. I spun around, terror paralyzing my legs, ready to scream or fight back.

There was no one there. The room was empty, wrapped in complete silence.

My hands were shaking uncontrollably. I closed my eyes for a few seconds, forcing myself to breathe, trying to find a logical explanation before I lost my mind. The only red light in the room was the heart monitor's LED indicator. I convinced myself it was just a strange optical trick, a glare refracting off the glass surface from sheer exhaustion.

But a part of me knew damn well that the angle of that glass could never project the LED light right behind my back.

I lowered my gaze back to the woman. Her open eyes were staring at me in silence.

I took a step back.

I decided to get the hell out of that room immediately. *I had to.*

At seven in the morning, I finally handed over the shift to my coworker. As I walked to my car in the parking lot, that strange feeling of being watched returned. I spun around and saw a man in his fifties a few yards away, leaning against a car. He had salt-and-pepper hair and a cold stare pinned right on me.

In a hospital that size, there was nothing unusual about it, he could have been a janitor or a patient's relative, yet his presence there sent an unprovoked chill through me.

"Rough night?" he asked with a tight smile.

"Very. Have a good one," I replied curtly, getting straight into my car.

He raised a hand to wave in a way that was far too slow. I was exhausted, but as I started the engine, I was already thinking about when I could get back on shift to see that stranger again.

The hospital was twenty minutes from my house, and I always took the same route.

Halfway there, while idling at a red light, I noticed a black van in my rearview mirror. When the light turned green, I turned right; the van turned with me. I took another right onto a backstreet I used as a shortcut: the van was still there, riding right on my bumper.

A sudden chill crept up my spine. Trying to tell myself it was just a coincidence, I attempted an emergency maneuver: at the next intersection, I made a sharp left instead of going straight.

The van turned left.

Panic set in. My hands were sweating on the steering wheel, and my heart started pounding frantically. I instinctively pulled into the very next side street, and with a lump in my throat, I saw in the mirror that the van kept going straight, disappearing from view.

I let out a long sigh of relief, pulling over for a moment before driving off again.

*"I seriously need to sleep. I'm officially losing my mind."*

Just to be safe, I didn't head home right away: I extended my route by another ten minutes, constantly checking to make sure no one was tailing me. The roads were deserted.

At ten that night, I crawled under the covers without setting an alarm. The next day was going to be my day off.

But I couldn't sleep a wink. I had spent the entire day trying to erase that woman and the hallucinations from my shift out of my mind, but my head kept drifting back to that symbol carved into her back.

I grabbed my laptop and opened the browser. I typed into the search bar:

*brand carved into skin ritual meaning interlocking circles*.

The first few results only showed dermatology articles, body scarification, or old Celtic symbols that had nothing to do with it. I had to scroll down to the second page of Google to stumble upon an old, archived Reddit thread buried in an occult forum.

An anonymous user had replied to a photo of a scar, though it was different from the woman's. They had written a few chilling lines:

*"It's not just a tattoo. Carving connected geometric shapes into live flesh is an ancient binding ritual. The one who cuts the mark ties their beloved to themselves on a deep level, fusing flesh and soul. Tearing away a piece of that bond or attempting to break it leads to madness and the total destruction of the body."*

I slammed the laptop shut, a cold shiver running straight down my spine. It was just nonsense written by some nutjob on the internet. It had to be. Yet, in the darkness of my bedroom, the thought of that dark scar haunted me until I finally drifted off into a restless sleep.

In the middle of the night, I was jolted awake.

My dog was barking with a furious intensity, a deep, vicious growl I had never heard him make before. I got out of bed with my heart pounding a mile a minute. I flipped on the hallway lights and found him planted right in front of the living room sliding glass door.

I realized I hadn't pulled down the shades. I approached cautiously and peered through the glass: the small backyard was wrapped in pitch darkness, completely still. No shadows, no movement.

Then it hit me.

Out in the dark yard, right in the middle of the lawn, stood my old plastic patio chair. Except it wasn't where it always was. It had been moved ten feet and positioned so it faced the glass door directly. Facing me.

An icy chill paralyzed my feet. The dog stopped barking abruptly, letting out only a soft, frightened whimper before backing away toward the hallway.

I forced myself to breathe, desperately reaching for a logical explanation: the evening breeze must have pushed it across the grass, there couldn't be any other reason. But the chair was heavy, and the night was dead still.

With trembling hands, I yanked the shade down as fast as I could, shutting out the dark. I patted my dog's head, more to reassure myself than him, and went back to bed, leaving every single light in the house on.

On Friday afternoon, my phone rang while I was taking refuge at my parents' house. A coworker had called in sick, and the charge nurse asked if I could cover the night shift. I had zero desire to head back into that nightmare, but I couldn't say no.

I pulled up to Mission Hospital around 6:45 p.m.; my shift was set to start at seven. I parked in the employee lot and sat there for a few moments with the engine off, gathering my strength.

I looked up.

Directly to my left, on the building's second floor, was the window of one of the Intensive Care rooms. The neon light inside began to flicker violently, strobing on and off. I figured it was a power surge, but if there had been an electrical failure, the emergency generators should have kicked in, and the equipment alarms should have started going off.

Instead, only that single window kept flashing in the dark.

I threw my car door open and stepped out to get a better look. When the light in the room went completely dark and clicked back on a second later, it revealed a figure standing bolt upright behind the glass, staring straight down at the parking lot. My heart leaped into my throat. The light went out again. A second later it came back on: the room was completely empty.

My mind was racing. I couldn't figure out which room that window belonged to from the outside.

"Good evening."

I screamed, jumping forward.

Standing right in front of me was the man from the morning before.

He took a step back, throwing his hands up with a look of fake apology.

"I'm sorry, I'm so sorry! I didn't mean to scare you at all, please forgive me."

I held my breath, clutching my hand to my chest.

"I'm running really late... I was just wondering if I could use that glass door over there to go visit my mother?"

I shook my head, trying to get my heart rate back to normal. "No. That door is an entrance reserved strictly for authorized personnel."

The man didn't move an inch. His gaze slid slowly down from my face to my short-sleeved scrub top, lingering in a creepy way on my bare arms. Then his eyes locked back onto mine, flashing a tight smile completely devoid of warmth.

"Thanks a lot. You know... you look incredibly familiar."

An icy chill shot down my spine. I was terrified and wanted nothing more than to jump back into my car and speed off, but I had to clock in.

"Have a good night," I cut him off coldly, shutting down the conversation.

I tried taking deep breaths as I walked down the hallways, desperately trying to calm myself. I had a shift to run; I couldn't afford to lose my mind. During change-of-shift report, I immediately asked the off-going nurse if there had been any electrical issues or power surges.

"No, not at all. Everything’s been smooth sailing all afternoon," he replied, surprised. Then he handed over Jane Doe’s chart. "The doctor stopped by earlier. She’s still in a deep catatonia, zero improvement. They even ran a STAT head CT to rule out any lesions or aneurysms, but it came back completely clear. Clinically she’s healthy, but it's like she's trapped inside herself."

I started my rounds to check everyone's vitals. When I stepped into Jane Doe’s room, everything looked normal: the monitor was tracing her slow heartbeat, and the IV was running smoothly. She lay there, rigid and still as a statue.

Yet, a strange impulse drove me to walk over to the far wall.

I looked out the window.

My car was parked directly below, slightly to the left.

The room with the flickering light and the figure standing at the window was hers.

I bolted out of that room right then, my breath short and my hands trembling violently. It wasn't possible. It couldn't have been her: if she had gotten out of bed, she would have ripped off the ECG leads and the IV line, setting off every alarm on the floor. It was clinically and physically impossible.

Later, I had no choice but to go back into her room to hang a fresh bag of saline.

The woman was still lying there, stiff as a corpse, her eyes wide open and staring into nothingness. I tiptoed toward the bed and reached my right arm up toward the IV pole. My short-sleeved scrub top left my arm completely exposed.

The second my arm passed a few inches from her face, her pupils snapped shut.

All at once, her chest jolted upward with an unnatural spasm, like she’d just been hit with a defibrillator. The cardiac monitor went haywire: the green line spiked out of control, and the room erupted into a deafening cascade of high-pitched beeps bordering on cardiac arrest.

The woman’s mouth gaped open. She didn't scream, but let out a dry, raspy wheeze, a airless gasp that made her jaw rattle. Her fingers lunged toward me with a speed and precision that were inhuman for someone in a deep catatonia.

I felt her grip crush my right arm. Her fingernails dug deep into my flesh. I let out a piercing scream of agony, using my other hand to pry her fingers off my skin, but her strength was monstrous, unnatural. I couldn't budge her a single millimeter.

Her grip only loosened when the door swung open behind me and the physician burst into the room with the crash cart.

At that exact second, the woman’s arm dropped heavy onto the mattress, as if she had dropped dead on the spot. Yet on the screen, her heart rate was already miraculously stabilizing.

The doctor stared at me in shock, his eyes wide: "What on earth just happened?!"

"I don't know... I... I was just changing her IV bag and she grabbed my arm!" I stammered, breathless, trying to pull myself together. "I couldn't..."

I stopped midsentence. A warm trickle was sliding down my forearm. My skin was bleeding.

"Go clean that up and get it bandaged right now!" the doctor urged. Then I heard him mutter under his breath, shaking his head, "I've never seen anything like this in my entire life..."

I ran to the sink to grab sterile gauze and disinfectant. She had punctured my skin with the tips of her nails, leaving four deep red marks carved right beside the scar on my outer forearm. But the strangest part was that I didn't feel any pain. Maybe it was just the adrenaline from the attack numbing me up.

Only a few minutes later, I felt a bizarre sensation: a cold tingling, followed by complete numbness. My right arm had gone entirely dead.

A right arm completely devoid of feeling is never a good sign. I found the doctor in his office to have him check it out. He grabbed a pulse oximeter and clipped it onto my right index finger. The screen stayed dark, spitting out an error signal: it couldn't detect a pulse.

So he tried my left hand. There, the display lit up right away, showing a mild tachycardia, but nothing alarming. We didn't think much of it, convinced the oximeter probe was just throwing its usual tantrum.

Around six in the morning, I was still sitting in the doctor's office; after all, there were only two of us working that night. My arm had stopped hurting, and the bleeding had ceased. Everything seemed to have settled back down.

Suddenly, a bloodcurdling sound echoed from the central monitoring station speaker. A deep, hoarse groan, like the muffled cry of a wounded animal. It was coming through the intercom in Jane Doe's room.

The doctor and I traded a terrified look and sprinted down the hallway.

We burst into the trauma box: the woman lay stiff and motionless on the bed, her eyes glued to the ceiling. But as we stepped closer to the gurney, I noticed fresh blood soaking through the sheets beneath her pelvis.

I gently rolled her onto her side while the doctor pulled up her gown to find the source of the bleeding.

"How the hell is this possible?!" he blurted out, taking a disoriented step back.

I leaned over the woman's body, and the blood ran cold in my veins. Right in the center of the mark carved into her back, along that vertical line separating the two circles, the skin had split open. There was a deep, necrotic, bleeding wound that had appeared out of nowhere.

"This is impossible..." the doctor muttered, running his hands through his hair. "She's been here for barely over twenty-four hours; there's no way she developed a pressure ulcer like this! The tissue is literally rotted!"

We moved purely on emergency instinct: I packed the wound with sterile gauze soaked in disinfectant to stop the bleeding, securing an advanced pressure dressing before positioning her on her side with support pillows.

When seven in the morning rolled around, I handed off my shift to my coworker. Her jaw dropped too as I filled her in on everything that had happened overnight.

When I finally walked through my front door, Jack, my dog, jumped all over me to welcome me back. I crouched down to pet him, but he immediately tried to lick the bandage on my right arm. "No, buddy... Mommy got hurt right there," I whispered, gently nudging his snout away.

I collapsed onto my bed and slept for a few hours. That afternoon, I got up still groggy and went looking for Jack. He was standing dead still in front of the living room sliding door again, staring out into the backyard.

"Jack, wanna go outside?"

No response. He didn't even turn around.

"JACK!"

He whipped his head around and trotted toward me, tail wagging. I clipped on his leash and we headed out. I decided to take the long way around the neighborhood: I desperately needed to clear my head of all that blood and those endless questions.

As soon as I reached the park near my house, I caught a glimpse of a gray-haired man's silhouette in the distance, just like the guy from the parking lot. My heart leaped into my throat. But as I walked closer, I realized it was a completely different person. I tried telling myself I was just being paranoid and played fetch with my dog on the grass for half an hour.

Then, the phone in my pocket buzzed with an emergency notification.

"Security Alarm: Motion detected in the backyard."

Terror froze the blood in my veins. I hated myself for not having installed external cameras yet.

A thousand thoughts raced through my head: Should I call the cops? But it wasn't the first time that damn sensor had gone off because the neighbor's cat jumped on the fence. I forced myself to believe that was all it was, especially since the app showed all doors and windows were still sealed tight.

I rushed back home, completely out of breath. The front door was securely locked, just as I'd left it. I stepped inside cautiously: everything was quiet, making it look like nothing had happened.

But when I got close to the sliding glass door in the living room, I froze in my tracks.

Scrawled across the glass from the outside, written in a thick red marker, was a lopsided message:

***"THERE YOU ARE MY BELOVED CREATURE.***  
***There was a small incident, but thanks to you, we are finally all safe."***

Terror hit me like a shockwave. Without a second thought, I instinctively turned around, dragging the dog out the front door. I ran into the street, shaking from head to toe, and dialed 911.

Ten minutes later, two officers arrived at the scene. We walked back inside together. But when I led them to the living room sliding glass door to show them the threat, the pane was completely spotless. Crystal clear. Not a single trace of red marker.

"I swear... I swear there was writing here in red! I saw it, I swear!"

One of the cops gave me a tired, condescending look.

"Ma'am, the door was locked and the sensors show no entry. Is it possible some neighborhood kids played a prank on you from outside and stress got the better of you?"

To keep from sounding completely out of my mind, I swallowed hard and gave a weak nod. "Yeah... maybe... I'm sorry."

Once the squad car pulled away, I collapsed onto the floor. The feeling of a noose tightening around my neck had become suffocating.

I don't know how long I sat there frozen on the living room floor in the dark. It was the loud ringing of my phone that snapped me out of it. My coworker's name flashed on the screen. I answered, breathless.

"Where the hell are you?!" he shouted from the other end of the line.

"What?... I'm at home."

"Everything is going to hell on the floor, you need to get down here right now!"

I jumped in my car and drove as if in a trance. When I pulled up to Mission Hospital, three police cars were parked out front, their emergency lights flashing. Officers were everywhere. Three of my coworkers rushed toward me, their faces twisted in sheer panic, and ushered me straight into the ICU.

"That woman... Jane Doe is gone!" one of them whispered hurriedly. "Another patient had a sudden cardiac arrest, so we were all crammed into Room 5, and when we stepped out... her bed was empty!"

I stood there frozen. "How the hell is that possible?! How could she walk out of the unit in her condition?!"

Just then, a police officer approached me with a suspicious glare. "Ma'am, where is your ID badge?"

I patted my scrub pockets with a knot forming in my throat. "I... I left it at home. I rushed over here as soon as they called me."

The officer pulled a clear plastic evidence bag from his coat pocket; inside was my magnetic keycard. "Is this it? It was sitting right on the patient's pillow."

My blood ran cold. "I was at home!"

"We know," the officer interrupted. "A man gained access to the floor using your stolen badge. But the second he passed through the security door, the entire surveillance system shorted out."

I was consumed by pure terror. The officer flipped the evidence bag over. Scrawled across the back of my badge in thick red marker was one final message:

*"Mom and Dad are free again. See you soon."*

I took a shaky step back, stumbling.

"Holy shit, your arm!" my coworker suddenly yelled.

I looked down: a pool of dark blood had completely saturated the bandage on my right forearm and was dripping heavily onto the floor. Yet I felt absolutely nothing.

They grabbed me and forced me onto a gurney. The doctor ripped away the gauze and recoiled in horror: "Your scar has split wide open... the tissue is necrotizing right before our eyes! We need to suture this right now!"

I was completely numb. I felt no pain, only listening as the noise around me faded into a muffled, distant echo. I turned my head and saw the officer speaking on his phone. He hung up and walked back over to my gurney.

"Ma'am... the genetic database match for the missing patient just came back. Do you know a woman named Sarah?"

I shook my head weakness.

"Sarah was abducted twenty-three years ago. She was declared dead a decade back, but it turns out she was alive the whole time." The officer locked his eyes onto mine with a chilling intensity. "You're twenty-two and adopted, correct? Have you ever been contacted by your biological parents?"

I shook my head again as my vision began to blur.

"We need to take a quick swab for your DNA..."

I woke up in an unfamiliar hospital room, suffocated by the stench of disinfectant. My adoptive parents were sitting beside the bed, their faces destroyed by tears. A detective was speaking to them in a low whisper:

"The DNA test confirmed a 99.99% match. The woman was her biological mother."

I tried to move, but my body felt heavy, like lead. I wanted to scream, but only a dry gasp escaped my throat.

I squeezed my eyes shut against the agonizing pressure, opening them again a moment later. The detective was continuing his story to my mother, who held both hands tightly over her mouth.

"We found both the captor and Sarah in an abandoned farmhouse a few miles from here," the detective said. "Unfortunately, there was nothing we could do. They were both struck by a sudden, simultaneous cardiac arrest."

No... it couldn't be. How the hell could that have happened?!

With a monumental effort, I managed to shift my right arm under the blanket. But what I saw when I looked down shattered my entire world. A desperate, inhuman scream ripped from my chest.

Below my right shoulder, there was nothing left. The sheet lay completely flat.

I thrashed against the mattress, howling like a wounded animal. A doctor and two nurses rushed into the room, pinning me down by force.

"What the fuck did you do to me?!" I shrieked through my tears.

My colleague looked at me with glistening eyes, his voice breaking: "We had no choice... The wound contracted a fulminant gangrene in less than an hour. The necrosis was climbing toward your chest at a speed I've never seen in my life. If we hadn't amputated your arm, you would have died too."

Four years have passed since that night. I never went back to nursing. I moved to a different city, sold the house, and tried to rebuild my life with a prosthetic that I can never truly feel is mine.

The doctors call what I experience "phantom limb syndrome." They say it's normal to feel itching, tingling, or pain where flesh used to be. But I know medicine is wrong, just as it was wrong that night.

When the room turns pitch black and the silence grows too heavy, I can clearly feel five fingernails digging into the thin air, right where my arm no longer exists. And sometimes, in the dark, the ghost of my scar burns all over again.

We were never free. They didn't die: they were just locked inside me.
