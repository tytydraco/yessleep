We were standing at the buffet table when my best friend, Anna, dropped dead. We were mid-conversation, gushing about the cake, wrapped with fondant alphabet blocks spelling out ‘baby’ and topped with a frosting bear in a blue tuxedo. Then her eyes rolled back, and she faceplanted into the hummus and vegetable tray.

I screamed as she bounced off the tabletop and the fell back into my arms. Foam dribbled from her lips, the bubbles taking on the color of her satin matte lipstick. We’d spent a dozen sleepovers all through elementary and middle school doing each other’s makeup, pretending to be Ariana Grande and Selena Gomez.

Her eyes were stuck open, the pupils swimming. Then a flash of blue flickered over her irises, like a flame burning through tissue, and was suddenly gone. She lay mostly limp, slightly spasming in my lap, her body rattling for the last time.

I screamed again, and this time it seemed to echo. No. Not that. Someone was shouting from the porch now. I glanced out to see another friend, Delphine, on her back on the deck, her legs kicking feebly as a circle of women knelt beside her calling her name.

My body was on fire. Was I about to meet the same fate? Had someone spiked the champagne with arsenic? I braced myself, tried to think of the prayers I’d known as a kid and subsequently forgotten. And then—nothing. No. I was fine. Panicked but fine.

It wouldn’t be until later, after the paramedics had arrived and called both women dead on the scene that we found Mona. She’d been in the bathroom when her heart burst, and they found her still sitting on the toilet, her neck bent awkwardly over the lip of the nearby sink, eyes forever looking sideways into the drain.

One party. Three deaths.

Someone must have driven me home. Not to my apartment. To my parents’ house. Maybe my dad picked me up. The whole time, my phone was buzzing. I assumed it was friends sharing the bad news, maybe checking in on me to see if I was doing okay.

I didn’t realize until later that the party was just the tip of the iceberg.

By the time I got home and checked my phone, I had maybe thirty messages:

Hector: Holy shit? Did you hear about Mia? Keeled over at her office, dead by the time her head hit the keyboard.

Jun: Brad Golden too. Just an hour ago. Must have been about the same time as Mia. He was picking his kid up from softball practice, and then bam. Dead in the car, in the middle school parking lot, pressed down on the horn.

Amanda: Tam Nishikira is dead too. And Melody Quinn. And Derek Winn.

Mia: I’m adding it up. At least a dozen people from our graduating class. Maybe more. Time of death was right around 3:30.

Hector: What the hell is happening? This doesn’t just happen. It just doesn’t.

In all, sixteen people died that day, all at exactly 3:32. At first we thought it was all just classmates from our graduating year.

But it was more specific than just that.

Every time I heard a name, I got little flashes of memory from elementary school: trading Starbursts for Tam’s Pocky, or Brad tearfully apologizing for accidentally destroying my drawing of Peppa Pig when he knocked over his water bottle.

I got out a few old yearbooks and started flipping through the pages, hoping to jog another memory. Maybe it was to remember the good times, or maybe I was still trying to find some deeper connecting thread, some reason why those sixteen people had their lights turned off in the same instant.

And then the moment of realization. I flipped to my class’s page of my fifth-grade yearbook, and there they were. All of them. All sixteen people who died had been in that class together.

But we hadn’t all died. At least, I hadn’t. I started going through the names, one by one, trying to search for other survivors.

*Mia Adams: Dead*

*Brad Crabbe: Dead*

*Anna Davies: Dead*

The list went on. I struck through each name in pen, which felt wrong, but my brain was too fuzzy to keep track of things otherwise. In the end, only two names remained. Mine and Anton Kosmov’s.

I hadn’t spoken to Anton in years, though of course I’d followed all of the news about him. Everyone in my town did. Just last year he’d made the tabloid headlines for marrying a former Victoria’s secret model fifteen years his senior. It was just the latest of a half-dozen Anton Kosmov updates that made you lift your eyebrows and try to imagine what his life was like.

Back in elementary school, Anton had been the shy one. He walked with a limp that was never explained to us and spent his afternoons drawing elaborate medieval battles in the margins of his notebooks. His teeth were a jumbled mess, made worse by the fact that he was constantly chewing gum. He was never invited to class parties.

By fifth grade, he’d gained a reputation for bad hygiene. He also had a mean streak. Muttering threats under his breath. Once, he was sent home for bringing a steak knife in his lunch box. Another time, he snuck in matches and started a small fire in the vacant lot that abutted the playground.

Despite all that, I had felt bad for him in fifth grade and tried to reach out. For my birthday that year, which I had at an indoor pool that winter, I invited the whole class, Anton included.

Part of me didn’t expect him to come, but on the day of the party, there he was. He wore an oversized black t-shirt that went all the way past his swim shorts, and he didn’t take it off when he went in the pool.

While the rest of us played Marco Polo and raced on the side-by-side looping waterslides, Anton lingered in the shallow end, knees on the pool floor, nostrils just above the surface of the water, watching us like an alligator.

When it was time for cake, he ate with his hands, wolfing his slice down in two or three greedy handfuls.

I flipped to the back of my yearbook and looked at the signatures. There was Anna, promising we’d stay best friends forever and kick middle school’s ass. There was Roopa, asking if I wanted to start a summer book club.

And there, in the corner, was Anton’s scrawl: *You were the only one who was ever nice to me,* it read.

I dropped the yearbook.

Something was gnawing at my stomach, some half-realization, some fear. I walked to the bathroom, trying to remember everything I could about the years that followed.

We all went to middle school after that. Anton got put into some kind of alternative program, some kind of nature-based thing where kids who were struggling did a bunch of gardening and hiking. Even there, he’d been on the fringe, quiet and unpopular, an outcast amongst outcasts.

Freshman and sophomore year in high school he was a shadow, almost always dressed in black, skinny and limping, staring a little too long at girls in a way that made us all uncomfortable. There wasn’t a more hated kid at school.

Until junior year. It still didn’t seem possible, but there it was. I was standing in the parking lot, talking with Anna and Roopa, getting ready to go in on that first day of eleventh grade when Anton’s car—a decrepit old Honda—pulled up near to us. Except, the guy who got out of the car wasn’t Anton. At least, it didn’t look like Anton at first.

This guy was six-one and totally ripped—like almost cartoonishly muscular—like a pro wrestler on six different steroids. He got out of the car and strode toward us, his gait strong, his limp absent. He smelled like vanilla. He smiled, and his teeth were straight.

“Ladies,” he said, touching the brim of an imaginary hat and bowing slightly. So, yeah, it was still Anton.

What the hell had happened to him? Of course, rumors swirled. Maybe he’d had some kind of knee surgery and they’d given him steroids for the healing process, which led to unexpected side effects. Maybe he’d been jacked all along but hid his muscles under his oversized black clothes.

 

My phone beeped and I checked my latest messages, finding a dozen new theories about what had happened to everyone. I ignored them all. No one had the answer.

No one but Anton, at least.

I scrolled through my list of contacts, trying to remember if I’d ever gotten his contact info. Maybe for that fifth grade birthday? Yes. There it was, assuming he hadn’t changed his number. I texted:

Me: Anton? It’s been a while. Is this still your number?

Anton: Olivia! I was wondering when I’d hear from you…

Me: I guess you heard about what happened?

Anton: Word’s gotten around.

Anton: We should talk. Face to face.

Anton: Can I send a jet?

Anton: No. Forget it. I’ll come to you.

Anton: Downtown Hilton, tomorrow at noon?

 

I went the next day. It was hot, a brutal mid-August. I’d spent an hour getting ready—my full hair and makeup routine, a pretty A-line dress with a floral print.

How long had it been since I’d seen Anton? Senior year of high school maybe? By then, everything had flipped on its head for him. That year, he’d arrived at school in a cherry red Ferarri, vaguely explaining that he’d had a very good summer, financially speaking. His all-black wardrobe had been replaced with tailored dress shirts and designer jeans. His physique remained as impressive as ever.

He had his choice of girls. They stared when he walked by, couldn’t help it. No one seemed to remember mocking him for leering at us just a few years earlier. For his part, Anton seemed not to notice or not to care.

Unless it was me. Maybe once a week, I’d walk across the main quad of the school and feel an icy prickle in my back, and then I’d turn to see him a hundred feet away, a half dozen sophomore groupies huddled around him while he started over tops of their heads in my direction. He’d smile, and I’d always expect to see those old crooked teeth peek out, but not. They were all perfect now, perfect like the rest of him. But those eyes. Those too-blue eyes with the too-small pupils. They were the same.

Sometime in the spring I woke one morning to find my entire lawn covered in flowers, red roses spelling out Prom? I ran down the stairs and outside, assuming I’d jump into the arms of my boyfriend, Clint, but instead I stopped dead in my tracks. There was Anton, kneeling by the flowers, a single rose clenched in his perfect teeth, staring up at me expectantly. Finally, he opened his jaw, letting the flower fall to the ground, and spoke.

“So what do you say?” he asked. “We go back, Olivia. All the way to the start. You and me, like it’s always been meant to be.”

“I can’t,” I said quietly, my heart sinking. “I have a boyfriend.”

“Clint? I can make him go away. I promise.”

“No,” I said. It was quiet at first, but as I continued to say the word again and again it built into a crescendo. “No. No. No!”

He stood, dusting some dust from his knee and shaking his head with a coy smile on his lips.

“I think you mean ‘not yet,’” he said. “That’s okay. I’m a patient guy. It’s one of my best qualities.”

I wish I had screamed at him then. Something like, “not ever!” or “not in a million years” or at least “fuck off!” but I just stood there dumb, unable to muster another word as he walked a little too happily off to his Ferarri, gunned the engine, and sped down the street.  

Later, when I told Anna about the promposal, she looked shocked.

“What?” I asked.

“I can’t believe you said no,” she said. “I mean. Good for you. Being loyal to Clint and all, but—”

“But what?”

“I mean. You have to admit. He’s hot. And rich. Like, really, really, rich. I doubt anyone else would have rejected him.”

“He’s still Anton,” I said.

 

When I mentioned Anton’s name at the front desk of the Hilton, a man in a dark gray suit came running over, greeting me by name. He led me to an elevator, which we took to the top floor, then down a long hallway until we reached a set of double-doors, which he pushed open with a bit of theatrical flourish.

The doors opened to a great ballroom that must have been used for weddings and big corporate events. Floor to ceiling windows surrounded us on three sides, the midday sun pouring in. At the center of the room, sitting at a small, ornate table sat Anton.

“Olivia!” shouted Anton, getting to hit feet. He jogged over, his gait strong. He wore a form fitting white t-shirt that clung to his ridiculous muscles like a paint job. He flashed his perfect teeth as he reached me and gave a short, awkward bow.

“It’s so good to see you,” he said. “It’s been—”

“Since high school,” I said.

A short awkward silence filled the room. After a few seconds I added, “Congratulations, by the way. On getting married, I mean.”

“Oh yeah,” he said. “Mariska is—I mean, she’s great.”

He gestured over to the corner of the room where a woman sat in a chair looking out over the city. She’d been so still I hadn’t noticed her earlier.

“Mariska!” he shouted. “Get over here and say hi to Olivia.”

Slowly, mechanically, the woman got to her feet. She was overdressed in a peacock evening gown that swished at her feet as she approached us. She was one of the most luminously beautiful women I’d even seen, but her face was blank, her eyes oddly cloudy.

“Hi,” she said as she approached. Then she stood motionless, as if waiting for something.

“Good girl,” he said. “Return to your chair.”

Mariska turned on her heel and slowly began to walk toward her chair.

“She’s very compliant,” said Anton. “Very.”

A shiver ran down my spine as we watched her walk.

“Do a cartwheel, Mariska!” Anton shouted.

Mariska complied, immediately leaping forward and vaulting off one hand and then the next, landing gracefully before continuing toward her chair.

I must have gasped or at least given the reaction Anton was hoping to evoke, because he smiled at me.

“We have fun,” he said, muttering a bit to himself. “We have fun.”

“I should go,” I said quietly.

“Nonsense! You just got here! And I know you want to talk about the big—you know—*event* that happened.”

I thought of Anna, the lipstick-tinged bubbles frothing from her lips. Yeah. Quite an event. I turned to see that the double doors were closed behind us.

“They’re not locked, I swear,” he said. “But I flew all this way to come meet you. And I have answers! Plus a few things I really, really need to talk about. Stuff that only an old friend like you would understand. Indulge me. Please?”

I nodded weakly.

“And hey!” he shouted. “I got your favorite food. Pizza with pepperoni, right? I still remember from your birthday party. Fifth grade! After we swam, we went to that little room with the tables next to pool. Plenty of pizza. Cheese or pepperoni. But you went for the pepperoni, right? See, I notice stuff like that.”

He gestured to the table at the center of the room. Sure enough, a small pepperoni pizza sat right in the middle. Anton gestured for me to follow him and I complied, taking a seat across from him at the table. I hadn’t had pepperoni pizza since middle school. This one looked especially cold and unappetizing. Anton took a slice and ate a bite, his face lighting up in pleasure.

“So good,” he said. “I had it flown in from L.A. Have some!”

I took a slice and ate a small bite, trying to force a smile.

“Doesn’t Mariska want any?” I asked, but Anton only laughed.

“I’ll have her eat something later,” he said. “She’s not a big pizza person. Models, you know? Fitness is everything.”

I looked over at Mariska, who was back in her chair, starting out the window.

“Speaking of which,” he said. “She needs her steps. If I don’t remind her to exercise—whew. It’s a lot to manage. Hey! Mariska! Get 10,000 steps. Laps around the room, okay? Not too loud.”

Mariska got to her feet and began to walk in circles around us.

“What’s happening?” I asked quietly, trying not to let me voice shake.

“Right,” he said. “Right, right, right. I mean, that’s the whole point of this. For you to understand. And for me to get to, oh you know, unburden myself, I guess? There are just so few people who really get me, you know? Maybe no one. Maybe just you.”

He reached into his coat, digging around in an interior pocket. I imagined a weapon. A small gun or a hunting knife. It wasn’t hard to picture him leaping across the table, stabbing me over and over in the chest.

Instead, he pulled out a candle. It was small and white, no more than four inches long with a red spiral running up the sides like a barbershop pole. A little birthday candle.

“The answer you’re looking for!” he announced, holding it up to the light.

I didn’t know how to respond. He held it out for me to take.

“Careful,” he said. “Be very, very careful.”

I picked it up from his sweaty palm and looked more closely. The candle appeared to be brand new, never used, its tiny wick still white and unburnt. As I examined it, Mariska walked closer, her methodical footsteps as steady as a heartbeat.

“I don’t understand,” I finally said. “I thought we were going to talk about the, you know, *event.”*

“We’re getting to that,” said Anton. “I swear. I’m trying to explain. Here. I brought a lighter. Try to fire it up. Wait. It’s not your birthday, right?”

I shook my head.

“Of course!” he said. “You’re a January birthday! Capricorn, right?”

He gave me the lighter and I stood there stupidly for a moment. Mariska’s footsteps echoed around me.

“Don’t worry,” he said. “Nothing bad will happen. I just want to, you know, take you on the journey here.”

My hand shaking, I flicked on the light and held it to the wick, the flame licking at the wax. I gave it a solid five second. Then ten. Then twenty. The candle wouldn’t light.

Anton laughed and gestured for me to return candle. I did so gladly.

“Okay. So now you see. It doesn’t light, right? But what if it were your birthday? That would be different. On your birthday, it would be a show. A pretty blue flame like nothing you’ve ever seen. It would hypnotize you.”

I looked at the candle again. It was clear it had never been used. I imagined the blue flame though. I’d seen a blue flash flicker across Anna’s eyelids on the day she died.

“The wick is white,” I said, brushing those thoughts aside. “I mean it’s never—”

“It fixes itself,” he said, brushing the air with his hand as if to shoo away a silly question. “Anyway, that’s not relevant to today’s conversation anyway. Just a detail. Logistics. Boring.”

Carefully, he put the candle back in his pocket.

“My father got the candle from a shop in New York before I was even born,” he said. “Said it was special. Gave it to my mom before he ghosted us.” He looked away for a moment, perhaps remembering something painful. “Anyway, he didn’t explain exactly how it works. Maybe he knew, maybe he didn’t. He just told her it only works on birthdays.”

He was breathing a little harder now, getting worked up. Mariska walked by us again, her steps louder than ever.

“Mariska, you’re being too loud. Go sit in your fucking chair!” he suddenly shouted.

Mariska complied.

Anton rubbed at the bridge of his nose.

“I’m sorry,” he said. “She’s just. You know. Frustrating. I never wanted to have kids, and she’s basically a child. Worse.”

“I want to go home,” I said quietly. “Is that okay?”

“After,” said Anton. “I really, really need someone to understand. And you’re—I feel like you’re my best shot. Please don’t go. Please.”

I didn’t move. As scared as I was about what might happen next, I still wanted to understand what had happened to Anna, to all of our former classmates.

“What did you wish for when you were six years old?” asked Anton. “At your birthday, I mean. When you blew out your candles.”

“I don’t know,” I said weakly. “A pony maybe? A Barbie dreamhouse? I don’t remember.”

“I do,” he said, looking down at himself. “I remember exactly. I was six. Huge WWE fan. I closed my eyes, an in my mind I said I wanted to be as buff as John Cena, super strong. Super hot with shiny teeth and, you know, a working leg.”

“I made the wish, and then the candle just blew out. Like, I didn’t blow it. It blew *itself* out. And I remember looking down at my little six-year-old body and I was like, just as scrawny as ever. My leg just as fucked up as before. But, even so. I felt like something had happened. I looked at the candle, and the wick was white again, like I’d never lit it. And I took it carefully out of my cake and hid it in a special place in my room.”

“Every year after that, I’d take that candle from its place and put it on my cake. I’d make my wish and the candle would blow itself out. Nothing ever came true. If anything, my life sucked more than ever, but I still felt like something was happening. Call it faith, instinct, whatever you want. I kept making wishes.”

“And then came my sixteenth birthday. Sweet sixteen. I woke up and—” he made a sweeping gesture at his own body. “I couldn’t believe it at first. When I feel asleep, I weighed maybe a hundred pounds, tops. I woke up looking like this. Never spent a day in the gym. Still haven’t except a couple of times to test it out. But basically, my body just stays like this. The teeth, the leg. Those were fixed too.”

“Your wish came true,” I said quietly.

“I knew you’d get it. Yeah. It came true. Ten years later.”

It was all starting to click into place.

“So when you were seven,” I said. “You wished for a fancy car?”

“I knew you’d understand!” he said, jubilant, rising to his feet and smiling wide. “Yes. I wished for a cherry red Ferrari with a million dollars in the trunk. And when I was seventeen, there it was, parked on the curb right outside my house.”

He nodded over at Mariska.

“Skipping forward a couple of years… I’m sure you can guess what I wished for when I was ten,” he said. “Had her poster on my wall. Wished we were married and that she’d do everything I told her to do. Sure enough, woke up last year and she was in bed next to me. I mean, she’s not exactly who I would have picked today. She’s thirty-five, you know? And I wish she was a little more… engaging. I mean, I can order her to speak, but it doesn’t feel great, you know?”

He was pacing back and forth now, and in his gait, I realized there was still a hint of a limp. Not for any physical reason, I suspected, but from a long-ago learned adjustment, a piece of his brain that couldn’t let go of the old injury.

“Fifth grade was so hard,” he said after a minute. “Everyone was so fucking cruel. Just, like, evil really. Did you know Brad and Armand actually pissed on my backpack. It really got in there, too. All over my homework, my sack lunch. Like, who does that, right? I just hated everyone. Everyone but you. So that year—”

“I get it,” I said. “It was your wish that year. Everyone in our class... Everyone but you and me.”

He sat back now, and I realized there were tears streaming down his cheeks.

“I didn’t think they’d actually die,” he said. “I was eleven years old. I mean, ever since I was sixteen I thought it might happen, but I kept hoping… Like sometimes in movies they say wishing people dead doesn’t work. I thought it might be a dud wish, you know? But then the day of my twenty-first birthday came. I woke up and texted Brad, and he texted back! I was so happy. So happy. And then… then the afternoon arrived. This time, it must have been clocked to the second from when the candle blew out when I was eleven.”

“I was just a kid,” he said. “I never wanted this. Me and Brad, we ended up making friends senior year of high school. You think I want Mariska? Or this body? This body is fucking ridiculous. I can barely put my shirt on. It feels like I’m wearing a half dozen jackets.”

“You could reverse it,” I said after a minute. “Make a wish this year, and a decade from now—”

“It won’t matter,” he said. He had his head in his hands now. “I won’t matter after next year.” He was speaking toward the ground now, the words hard to hear. “When I was twelve, I got into reading some dark shit. Fucked up underground comics, Clive Barker, some real edgelord stuff. And my wish that year. I mean. I just. I’m just so sorry.”

“What are you saying?”

“I mean. Imagine the darkest thing an angry twelve-year-old boy might wish for. And then like, double it.”

A hole had opened in my chest, a dark pit that seemed to stretch straight down for miles. Something terrible was coming, something that would make sixteen friends dying look like a day in the park.

“Why tell me?” I asked. “You could have just let me live my life, not knowing. I mean—”

“Because I need you to forgive me,” he said. “Because you’re the only one who understands. You were there growing up. You were nice to me. You know what I went through, that this isn’t my fault.”

I said nothing. I just stared at him, right through the perfect veneer of his straight teeth and his chiseled jaw and I saw the weak, simpering child I’d always know.

“No,” I said.

He looked up at me, his face a mask of shock, tears still streaking down his cheeks.

“What?”

“I don’t forgive you,” I said, my voice no longer quiet. “Not at all.”

“I was a kid,” he said. “I was just a kid who made a couple of stupid wishes. You must see that. Look at me, Olivia. You can see me.”

“I see you,” I said. “I see everything. And the answer is no. I don’t forgive you.”

Without another word, I turned and walked from the room. After a few seconds, I heard the sound of a crash as the table toppled over.

“Mariska!” Anton was shouting. “Mariska clean this shit up! Now!”

 

Anton has texted and called a few times since our meeting, but I never respond. He doesn’t deserve a response. It’s been a strange time for me, I guess. I stopped going to my classes. Most days, I go for long hikes in the mountains, savoring the way the trees rumble in the wind. Sometimes I see deer, and I watch them with envy. They don’t know what’s coming. Hopefully, for them it’ll be a short if horrible surprise.

And then at night, I wrestle with the sheets in the darkness, trying not to imagine what’s coming, my heart ready to explode, visions of dancing shadows and fire and razors decorating my walls and ceiling, still knowing that my darkest nightmares will never compare to what’s coming next year.

I guess I felt the urge to share this here. It’s not just a warning for me, for all of us. Time isn’t forever. In fact, it may be far shorter than you think. So if you can, step away from the screen for a few minutes. Take a walk somewhere that feeds you.
