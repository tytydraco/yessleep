At the end of the dirt patch that served as a parking lot, there was a wooden gate almost as tall as the trees themselves, leading into the thick forest beyond. Looking through the gate, there seemed to be no clear path through the woods, which meant that visitors would need someone to guide them through. 

Me and my girlfriend Evelyn were waiting for that someone to show up. The guide was already almost thirty minutes late. I was leaning on the rental car’s hood, the sun scorching above. The heat didn’t feel good, but it also made me stubborn in mind and lazy in body, so I didn’t move. Not yet, at least.

“Should you call the lady again, and ask if the guide is still coming?” I asked Evelyn. She was being smart and crouching in the shade of a lone tree near the car.

“Not yet,” she replied. “I already called her twice today, so I don’t want to pester her.”

“Fine,” I said, but I could tell that my tone did not agree, and I frankly found no energy to hide my contempt or explain it away. Evelyn turned away from me.

We’d been traveling for two days already. First a plane ride, then a bad night’s sleep at a motel, and now with a rental car to the edge of the forest. And still we had to walk through the fucking woods.

“Do you think they’re lost?” I asked Evelyn passively. Making conversation.

“I’m assuming they move through the woods pretty frequently, *Matt*,” she said with a nail struck through my name. What she meant was: quit being such a bitch.

“Then why’s there no path? Usually there’s a path,” I replied, intentionally not taking the hint. But I was tired, and I knew Evelyn was as well. We just had different ways of dealing with it.

Two other cars were parked next to us, and judging by how far off we were from real civilization, I made the assumption that we were at least waiting in the right place, and that there were people somewhere around here. 

“Maybe we just don’t see the path. We’ve never been here before, they’re here all the time. They know these woods.”

“I guess.”

There wasn’t even a faint wind to cool me down. I turned the ring in my right pointer with my thumb idly. Even the ring was hot to the touch, and right as I was about to get up to take a drink of water and move to the shade, a rustling came from the trees. Now *there* was a reason to get up, and my stubbornness thanked me. It was a sour feeling. 

The shape of a person moved lazily just beyond the gate, touching each tree with their hands like it was an elementary school theater production of Tarzan. No wonder it had taken them so long to get here, swaying about, moving as much sideways as they did forwards.

Evelyn got up as well, and started to move towards the gate. I followed her. 

“Hello,” she said just as our guide met the treeline, but then Evelyn stopped abruptly, and I almost crashed into her. I was about to snap at her clumsiness, but when I saw what she was seeing, my tongue lost all its bitter words and receded to inert silence.

The old man before us was short and anemic, his skin splotched gray where there wasn’t tan. His clothes looked like he’d worn them for years, moulding to his body in stretches and holes that somehow made sense all the while being absolutely repugnant. 

But him looking homeless wasn’t really what had made me and Evelyn stop in our tracks; the man’s neck was craned all the way back so harshly that I wondered if he’d held the awful posture for years. The closer I looked, the more this seemed likely. 

It was as if the back of his head was glued to his upper back, with the greasy remnants of a receded hairline clinging on to the skin in thick, slimy strings. There was no gap between the two body parts, and around the area of contact grew bumps that slowly seeped puss. Most of the skin around it held a rash that made everything either an inflamed red or a dry, crusty white.

He looked like a fucking monster. A monster that forever stares up into heaven.

And when the smell hit me, with the dreadful heat around, it was like being in a porta-potty at a festival. I had to try and disguise the gag that flicked my torso forward. The smell was that of infection and decay.

As the man’s neck was so far craned back, his Adam's apple was pushed to the front so severely that the skin holding it in place looked paper thin as the sun beamed down on it. The muscles and joints were withered, as they’d been without use for so long. In his current condition, it was difficult to imagine what the man would look like with his head upright. Perhaps he’d never been normal; this was perhaps a condition he’d had since birth.

Finally, I had to look away, even for just a moment, because I couldn’t take noticing something awful and new about him. Small cuts where his skin chafed. A singular glob of snot trickling down his cheek like a snail. If I didn’t look away, I would’ve puked.

The man stopped moving, and through the arcing pipes of his abhorrent neck came a wheezing, strained reply, “Are you Evelyn Jones?” 

“Yes,” Evelyn said. “And you’re Willard?”

The man coughed, and it sounded like he was choking on his own spit. He was facing away from us slightly, and it seemed that he made no effort to try and get a look at us. Not that he could, without bowing. “I am. Follow me,” he said, and started off back to the forest. 

Evelyn gave me an anxious look, and I could sense that my face had that concerned pout, as she so often called it. In an effort to shake off the uncomfortable feeling I had about the man, I shrugged. I turned to him and asked “How long is it until the grounds?”

The man froze, and his body became tense like a stick ready to snap. Something about the way the wooden gate framed him shot a shiver down my spine like the first drag of a cigarette.

“Who’s that?” he said, concerned and perhaps even a little bit intimidated.

“That’s my partner, Matthew,” Evelyn said before I had a chance to reply. Hadn’t she told them I was coming along? “He’ll be my escort. I hope that’s okay.”

Apparently not.

After a thoughtful moment, the man’s body seemed to relax, and he started back towards the woods, touching the trees with his hands like they were waymarkers. 

“The old lady’s not gonna be happy about that,” he said. “But that’s not my problem.”

There was a moment where neither me nor Evelyn moved as the man with the craned neck walked along, quickly becoming flashes of color between the trees. 

You know, it’s easy to say that you couldn’t know–how could you? But I remember feeling like I shouldn’t go there, that whatever lurked beyond that gate was not good. I was about to say something. Or maybe I just like to think I was.

Then Evelyn had taken enough steps to already be on the cusp of the gate. She turned to me and mouthed *sorry*, like it meant anything. Perhaps a grumble escaped my lips as I followed her, but I did not wish to fight more. Not in front of this Willard guy anyway.

As I went through the gate I suddenly became aware that, after looking at that weird gate for all that time we waited in the parking lot, I’d been subconsciously expecting something to happen; perhaps I’d feel different, somehow, or there’d be a rustling in the trees, or maybe an ominous wind passing through. And when nothing happened–at least as far as I could tell–I felt a little bit disappointed. 

Or maybe it was just my mind trying to find something to mull over that wasn’t the mouthful of things that were and had been annoying me during this trip that were just about to snap my tongue into action. I was suddenly acutely aware of how often I complained about stuff, and how little I could control my feelings. The only thing holding me back as I walked into the forest was Willard’s presence; I hated fighting in front of other people.

The forest was thick enough to give shelter from the sweltering heat of the sun, which was at least one annoying thing taken off the list. Willard moved slowly from tree to tree, sometimes stopping to take something in–a smell, or maybe a bird–or perhaps ponder the route. Evelyn was keeping her distance to the man, and I followed her. It was slow going. There was still no distinct path that I could discern, and after about ten minutes had passed I doubted Willard saw one either.

Actually, I wondered if Willard saw anything at all.

Soon the man stopped again, and next to him was a convenient tree stump. With half a dozen large strides I overtook Evelyn and made my way up onto the stump like a kid pretending to be a ninja. From my vantage point, I could see the man’s face in full. The sight sent a shot of vertigo to my chest, trickling down my legs.

His eyes were gone. Well, not gone exactly. There was still some kind of biological matter inside the sockets, but it was bulbous, black and wet. It was like the eyeballs had detached from the rest of his body, yet stayed in their sockets until they had rotted and fermented into something nearly unrecognizable. 

He was blind, alright.

Although I felt like a voyeur now, Willard didn’t seem to care. He didn’t even turn towards the sounds I’d made coming up on the tree stump, and he definitely knew someone was there. I wasn’t a very good ninja.

The whole thing felt incredibly awkward with a hint of terror mixed up in it all. I couldn’t move yet–that would have somehow felt disrespectful to his condition, like I was showing off how well I could traverse with my two seeing eyes–or maybe I was just scared. And I felt ashamed; was I really one of those people who gets scared when they see a disabled, homeless-looking guy? Well, if they look like that at least, then I guess so.

Willard took off again, pretending to be none the wiser. I stayed put, and Evelyn soon passed me. She didn’t even look at me. Instead, she simply sighed. I wanted to tell her what I’d seen, to tell her that besides Willard being really fucking weird he was *also* blind (not the best thing for a guide), but perhaps she’d already assumed as much. Or she just didn’t or wouldn’t care. 

I stepped back into my place at the end of the line and didn’t say a word until Willard spoke again some minutes later. “We’re here.”

Had the blind man gotten lost again? Because all I saw was more forest all around me. But as we took a few more steps forward, there was a wall of sunlight peeking through the trees. The forest reminded me of a heavy fog–the kind where you can’t see ten feet in front of you. If we’d really gotten lost, I doubt we could have found our way back. Reception was shit here, too.

Willard and Evelyn waited right at the treeline as I stumbled my way over. The light was harsh on my eyes after spending time in the shade of the trees, and I squinted until I could get a better look at where we were.

It was like someone had simply deleted part of the forest. Just a hole in the middle of it all, surrounded by a great wall of leaves and trunks. Inside this odd, circular plot of land that stretched along far enough where its edges were distant horizons, there were buildings and cottages of all kinds, and among them were all sorts of other things: rusted cars, statues of wood as well as iron, holes that looked much too deep to not have been dug by people. Small figures made of sticks and rocks were mounted here and there, and some swung on strings against a lazy wind. Although I knew from what Evelyn had told me that people lived here, I didn’t see anyone. They were probably hiding from the heat.

Willard took off again, and we followed. I found myself surprised by the place as we walked further. I had expected it to be more modern, well-kept, with staff and… I don’t know, a pretty little fountain? Whatever you’d expect from an all-inclusive artist retreat, as Evelyn had first described this place to me. I wanted to ask if this was along the lines of what Evelyn had pictured, but that would’ve felt rude in front of Willard.

Of course I hadn’t known Evelyn’s mum. I hadn’t even met her, since Evelyn had gone no-contact many years prior to us meeting. I never had a problem with that, but I was obviously quite surprised that when she died, Evelyn wanted to attend her funeral. She asked me if I’d like to come with, and I said sure. 

Now I was regretting the whole thing. Maybe I had from the start. And it was clear that Evelyn had a better grasp of the situation. Judging from the way she moved–like this was the type of crazy she was used to–she didn’t seem the least bit surprised by the weirdness of the place we’d found ourselves in.

As we meandered around the… village, I guess you’d call it–and Willard must have walked around here a lot, since he was staying on the footpath with no guidance, and even stepping over rocks when he came up on them–a small chapel came into view. Well, it had all the features of a chapel, except it didn’t have a cross, or any other religious symbol in sight. I guess they wanted to keep things neutral around here.

Willard led us to the wide, wooden double doors that were in desperate need of a paint job, and simply remarked “Alright,” before immediately heading off, probably back to whatever putrid hole he lived in. 

Evelyn looked at me, but her face didn’t seem to permit a visible emotion of any kind. She was closing off, but also asking for help. To be fair, she had turned into herself since we started on the trip, but only now did I notice how serious it was.

I didn’t really know what to do. On the one hand, I wanted to help her. I felt like it was my responsibility. On the other hand, I didn’t know how; I didn’t have a single idea what she was going through. Not really. Behind that nonchalance rolled a storm.

“You okay?” is all that I came up with.

It took her slightly longer than normal to find my eyes, like for a moment she was somewhere else completely.

“I’m fine.”

“You sure?”

“Yeah.”

“Shall we go in, then?”

“Yeah.”

But she didn’t move, still stuck, maybe not ready yet, so I took the lead, walked up and pushed the doors open.

In the air there'd been a foul smell, which I just assumed was the remnants of Willard’s presence. But once I opened those doors, the stench was clearly of its own variety, and coming from somewhere inside. 

One time when I was a kid we’d gone to visit family out-of-state, and although my mother was very diligent when it came to housework, for some reason she’d forgotten to clear out the compost before we left. When we came back almost two weeks later, the house smelled like nothing I’d ever smelled before; wet death, maggots writhing in rot, dog diarrhea. I remember everyone gagging, and even after my dad took out the compost, opened up all the windows and doors, and sprayed Febreze everywhere, it still took until the following week for the smell to fully lift.

This smell reminded me of that. 

Hopefully it wasn’t another weird puss-guy.

The chapel was bare from decoration; gutted from all that would give it personality. On both sides were wooden pews coated with chipped white paint. At the front, a simple podium with barely enough room for a holy book. The floorboards creaked loudly underneath my boots. Sunlight shot swords of light that crossed each other down from the small windows that curved near where walls met the ceiling. 

Evelyn followed behind me, and in a way I was proud I’d led her inside, that I could take the lead in at least something. And soon this shit would be over with, and we could get back to our regular lives and all its regular bullshit.

Above us, a little bit higher than the windows, was the only thing of note: a large, dirtied canvas covered the ceiling, draped in a way that reminded me of those boho shops that Evelyn frequented when we first started dating. Ropes and pulleys surrounded it, and I wondered if there was something wrong with the ceiling for it to need covering; it did not make sense for it to be just decoration in the otherwise bare building. Or maybe the ceiling leaked–the place had definitely seen better days. 

It was even hotter inside than it was outside, and the collar of my shirt felt like it would soon become fused to my skin. The thought reminded of Willard, and I tried to shake off that thought. As if it were velcro, I ripped the collar off my skin once more and tried to get some air to circulate underneath with a shaking hand, but the air was much too hot to hold any comfort, and there was something nasty about the foul smelling air touching my skin in places I otherwise kept covered. 

“Who are you?” snapped a woman’s voice from behind, making me jump a little. The shirt clung back onto my skin like a leech.

When I turned around, I immediately took a step back out of reflex. Although the woman standing in the doorway was old–probably in her sixties–and frail, there was something about her that just told me to keep away. 

Evelyn had been looking at something or other, and immediately went to greet the woman. 

“Hi, I’m Evelyn. And you must be Ms. Duval?” she positioned herself to face both me and the old lady and said “This is my partner Matthew. I hope it’s okay that he joins the processions.”

The old lady looked at me with fire in her eyes, and maybe there was something wrong with mine or the heat made things look funny, but it looked like her shadow stretched behind her for much longer than it should relative to the blazing sun pretty much still above us. Then, like a stretched out rubber band being let go, the old lady relaxed and looked pretty much like someone’s mother; non-threatening, but still with a silent authority that came with the burden of having lived a full life already.

“Of course. I’m just a bit surprised as you didn’t notify us beforehand, but we’re of course welcoming of all that wish to celebrate Lauren’s life with us.”

“Sorry to butt in like this,” I said while giving Evelyn a look that she pretended not to notice. “I didn’t realize there might be a problem with me coming.”

“No problem, no problem,” the old lady said. Off in the distance behind her, something moved between the buildings. It moved with a limp, and it was tall and thin. It didn’t look like an animal, but if it was a person there was something definitely wrong with them. And it wasn’t Willard, and that made me worried for some reason. It looked almost like a person holding their hands up in prayer.

“Sorry for Willard’s tardiness, by the way. He’s got a knack for finding the right things at the wrong time, and vice versa,” the old lady said.

I didn’t really know what that meant, but I gave her a courteous smile and said “It happens.”

“Well,” she continued, “if you need to switch up your outfits, I’ll just get out of your way and you can use the chapel. Just close the doors and you won’t be bothered.”

“Great, thanks,” Evelyn said. 

“Should we begin when you’re ready, in, shall we say, around ten minutes?”

Evelyn looked at me and shrugged before I could convey an opinion. “Sure,” she said, “We’ll be heading back out right after, anyway.”

“Oh, you’re not staying?”

“Sorry, no. Gotta get back home. It’s a long trip.”

“Ah, alright,” the old lady said, and smiled like a puppet. Then the smile withered, overtaken by something more brooding. “Listen I know you and your mother never got along well, but I know she would have been very happy to know you are here.” 

I could see how Evelyn got one of those invisible punches to her gut with that. By all accounts, she hated her mother. She’d been a fucked up person who gave Evelyn a fucked up childhood. But that’s the simple truth. The real truth was much more complicated, and it was here with us, because she was at her fucked up mother’s funeral, and I was there with her.

The old lady sighed like she’d been keeping that one in for a while, then resumed her puppet’s smile. “Anyway, I’ve marked out a seat for you Evelyn, just over there” she pointed at a pew, and I could see there was a paper with Evelyn’s name scribbled on, “and your escort can sit wherever he’d like.” She then walked out, closing the doors behind her.

When the doors clicked, Evelyn immediately rummaged through her bag and took out the black, long-sleeved dress she’d worn to my aunt’s funeral a few years back. She’d worn it before that in some situations, but apparently after that it had become explicitly a funeral dress. It was a shame–it was a nice dress, and not at all too funeral-y.

I did the same and took out the suit that’d been creased by travel, and dreaded how hot it would be. 

“So, you really didn’t tell them I was coming?” I asked Evelyn as we changed.

“Didn’t know I had to.”

“Well, it seems like she would’ve expected it. Puts me into a bit of an awkward position here.”

She turned to me, ravaging the sleeves of her dress on, and snapped “What the fuck do you care? You didn’t even want to come here. And you’ve been a fucking baby all the way over here. If you feel awkward, go wait in the fucking car.”

Her words felt like I’d just grabbed a cactus with my bare hands, and an anger rose up inside me and then died down just as quick as I let go of the hurt. She was right, of course. But I was right in something, too. Something much smaller. Our fights were often like that, back then.

“Sorry,” I said. “Didn’t mean it like that.”

“How did you mean it, then?”

“I don’t know.”

She sighed, zipped up the dress and said “Let’s just get this over with.”

“Alright,” I said and wrangled on my suit, sweating like an asshole in a chapel.

There was no mirror, but looking down at myself I averaged that my suit looked like shit, and I probably did as well. I felt like shit, too, not just from the heat, but the weird, cold war me and Evelyn had been in since we went on this trip. Well, before that as well, but for some reason my brain made a difference between the two. Back home, our problems were baked into our routines. We’d fight about how the dishwasher should be loaded, but that was normal. She’d also forget to put her shoes back into place, and I’d constantly stumble over them. Then I’d tell her and she’d say sorry but also you leave out your water glass on the counter for the night and the cat knocks it over. 

And so on. 

But now we were out of our routines, and that made the differences–and the way we regarded each other as people instead of partners–so much more clear.

I didn’t want to think about that. It scared me and hurt me and I just wanted to move on.

Besides, it’s fine to feel like shit during a funeral, isn’t it? It was getting to that point–or, I guess, had already gotten to–where I just sort of felt numb. Feelings didn’t really make sense anymore, so far buried and twisted as they were.

At least the horrible stench inside the chapel gave my mind something to grab on to, and to grovel over. I could tell it was bothering Evelyn as well, and in some fucked up way at least we had that in common; a shared enemy. It didn’t account for much–not much more than a shared similarity between two strangers–but that thought was too sad so I pushed it aside.

Once I was fully suited up, we sat in silence, breathing through our mouths, until Ms. Duval came back. She closed the doors behind her, walked up to the podium up front, and leaned on the cracked wood with a preacher’s grip. It seemed no one else was attending, which was weird, especially given that the old lady had marked out a spot for Evelyn, but fine by me. Ms. Duval cleared her throat and began.

“We’re gathered here today to remember our beloved Lauren Jocelyn Mitchell.” 

Evelyn had changed her last name to Jones the moment she could. I think it was the name of her grandfather or something. It felt odd to think of her as having been a Mitchell.

“We’re joined by her daughter, Evelyn,” the old lady continued, then gave me a look, “and her escort.”

I wondered where Lauren’s body was–it had been a while since she’d died, so they must’ve buried her already. No empty casket for this funeral either. Somewhere in the grounds was where she’d been laid to rest, if I had to guess. I’d never even seen a picture of her, so there was a part of me that was curious if she and Evelyn looked alike. Yet I was also relieved that I didn’t need to think about that; the choice wasn’t there anymore. We didn’t even have any pictures of her at home; Evelyn had never wanted to keep any.

The old lady looked down at the podium for a moment, then rejoined her speech with that odd, stretched-out puppet’s smile, like she had suddenly found something really funny. 

“When Lauren first joined us, she was a broken woman. But the truth is, she–like every and each one of us–was broken long before she ever came here. When things got rough, life had simply put a mirror up to her soul, and for the first time she’d had to look into herself. It is only after you find yourself that you can discover the wonders this world of ours has to offer. And beyond.”

“Lauren soon found her strength and grew out her spine to accommodate all that she’d discovered. Like so many others who’ve come here, searching, she became able to transform her burdens into strength; her failures for lessons; her ego for sacrifice. We all strive for greatness in one way or another. And I hope–”

A knock came from the wall of the chapel from the outside. Evelyn looked confused, as must have I, but the old lady only smiled, with a hint of annoyance in her eyes.

“And I hope that Lauren will be rewarded in the after for all that she accomplished.”

Evelyn wasn't crying, or even pretending to. Her face was blank like there wasn’t even a person behind it, and she stared forward with her back straight. I’d resorted to holding a napkin in front of my nose and mouth because of the smell, but she didn’t seem bothered even by that. Perhaps this was how it had been between her and her mom, and this was just the last hurrah. 

A knock came from the other wall, and a creaking began as the ropes that gave shape to the canvas above began to move in their rusted machinery. It wasn’t Ms. Duval that pulled them, but I couldn’t make out where the ropes connected to exactly, either. Before I could think about it further, the canvas suddenly receded to the sides of the room like the start of a Broadway show, only upside down, revealing the most horrifying thing I’d seen in my life.

High above us was a person hanging from the ceiling. No, not a person. A body. Rotting where it was black and blue, fermented in that southern heat to a bloat that was like a water balloon ready to pop. It was clearly a woman, and although her face and much of the rest of her body sagged, the idea quickly snapped into place: this was Evelyn’s mother. She must be. A wrecking ball’s force moved the active, regular part of my mind to the side, replacing it with terror that exceeded anything I’d ever felt before. So strong was this feeling that it made every decision leading up to this point feel like a mistake.

Yet my legs wouldn’t move. They didn’t know where to go.

The world moved through me instead of me moving through the world, as the trauma I was experiencing shaped itself around me.

Gigantic fishhooks held the rotting woman in place, piercing her putrid, slimy flesh in her arms and legs, making her torso an adequate example of gravity’s pull. The stomach was large and round and bloated tight.

Something between a solid and a fluid dripped onto my shoulder, and a gag punched my throat.

Ms. Duval was moving away from the podium to the back wall of the chapel with determination. Evelyn stood up and screamed, but her eyes were locked onto her dead mother. The light coming through from the windows suddenly vanished, like a storm had rolled over us, although there was no rain and the skies had been cloudless before. A voice floated through the air, but I couldn’t make out what it was saying.

At the back wall, the old lady pulled on something that looked like a haphazardly rigged lever. A rope connected to it shivered, and then there was a click that sent something large and flickering from the edge of the ceiling flying through the room. It was a large blade of some kind, something that you’d see in industrial farm equipment. It had been hidden by the canvas before.

For that short while as the blade traveled through the air, everything seemed quiet in that way where you know that after that quiet ends, hell will be let loose and things will never be the same again. You’ll carry that hell with you forever.

The crude blade’s trajectory was stunted by the length and angle of the rope with astonishing precision in such a way that it snapped midair to change directions. At its highest point, it kissed the dead woman’s stomach with an eerie silence, the cut mesmerizingly precise and sharp. As the blade withdrew, it pulled with it bleak blood. The cut was so clean, for a moment the stomach was held in place, until cruel reality kicked back in and it burst with the ferocity of a disturbed dam. 

All around us, and most of all on us, rained Evelyn’s mother’s putrid guts. The blood and plasma seeped into my clothes. A piece of tissue or perhaps intestine slapped my left eye with a thump, and something acrid spread a burning sensation all over it. 

Evelyn was moving. Screaming. Trapped in this pew of disgust and terror, panicking like a caged animal.

I tried to brush away the gunk from my eye with the sleeve of my suit, but it was much too wet to do a good job. With my bare hands I swiped my face over the way I did after using a cleansing foamer in the evenings, all the while getting up from my seat. Finally I could see decently, although it still burned, but my shoes slid on the wooden floor, slipping on whatever part of my girlfriend’s dead mother seemed to stick to the bottoms.

Gripping the pew in front of me with one hand, I took Evelyn’s with the other and pushed off. Thankfully the area of impact that was the wretched body was decently small, so once I got a solid footing outside the ring of guts it was much easier to maneuver myself out. I didn’t let go of Evelyn’s hand, and in that moment I made a promise to myself to get out of here no matter what. I wouldn’t let go of her.

Once we could both comfortably stand, I checked my surroundings. Ms. Duval was gone. The skies had cleared, and the chapel was once again filled with beams of light. Evelyn was breathing through her mouth, her eyes wide and her skin gray, shaking. 

“Okay, let’s go,” was all I said, and started leading us to the door. 

But she wouldn’t budge. 

I turned around and said “We need to leave now,” but she was stuck on something. Not physically, but mentally. She’d sometimes get like that when a thought wouldn't quite pass before it was resolved, the way she had when we were at the chapel doors. She looked up at me and tried to say something, but words wouldn’t come out. I asked her what’s wrong and she just stared at me the way an animal does when trying to communicate with a human. Frustration filled me; this wasn’t conducive to survival. 

Then her gaze moved to her feet, back to mine, and back to her feet.

When I looked down, I understood. 

Some two feet apart from each other, covered in all the bodily decorations that make a mother’s belly a wayward home, were two babies. Still not quite the way they should look were they to come out naturally, much too small they were, but they already had eyes and noses and little legs and arms. 

Little people. Now dead and gray and slimy, discarded and forgotten. These were the sisters, brothers, or both of Evelyn’s, yet somehow they couldn’t be. I refused to believe it. That’s not really who they were. They were just dead tissue.

Everything suddenly felt dreamlike; some type of chemical reaction to take the edge off the bleak and brutal splayed all around us. 

“Let’s go, let’s go, let’s go,” I heard myself say, as if I were somehow not inside my own head anymore, as I dragged Evelyn towards the door. She screamed and fought but I didn’t care–we had to get out. There was no other option.

After wrangling Evelyn to the front door, I turned the handle, but it wouldn’t budge. I wriggled it with violence, then the one on the other door, then kicked them both with pure panic and rage as a wimpy roar escaped my lips. The doors shook in their sockets but wouldn’t bend. Someone yelled something outside, and high above one of the windows broke with a crash, sending a scatter of shards all over. Among the shards, I noticed, was something that looked like a rock, about the size of my fist.

For a moment everything was quiet, and then the rock began to trickle smoke, which roiled up into a boil of smoke in a matter of seconds. 

*Fuck*.

I didn’t know what the smoke was, and I didn’t care to find out. The important thing was that we were trapped, and we had to get out.

“Back door,” I said to Evelyn. Her eyes had regained some of their strength and focused on mine. “There’s gotta be a back door.”

It took her a while for the cogs to start turning; for the words to start making sense. Once they did, she nodded and said “Let’s go.” 

I no longer needed to hold her hand as we both ran from towards the back. The smoke was getting into my eyes and lungs already, and soon it’d fill up the whole room. It was doing something to my psyche I didn’t like. I tried to ignore it and breathe less, neither of which really worked.

Evelyn coughed, but she moved with determination, scanning the back wall for a way out. I couldn’t see anything that looked like an exit; if there was one, it had been expertly hidden. 

I was losing hope. I could feel it. But filling that void was a trust in Evelyn, who was now moving with fierce determination. 

She gave up with the wall, but didn’t give up fully yet. She gazed from behind the podium at the chapel for anything that could help. It was like her dead mother and the remains of her unborn siblings weren’t even there.

As the smoke caught up to her, fear found its way into her teary eyes as she coughed violently, bending her towards the floor, hands on her knees. She was done, and if that was the case, so was I. I tried to look around, to find anything of use, but it was useless.

She was coughing and halfway blind from the tears that the smoke was pulling out of her, so instead of words she found my attention by flailing her arm in my direction until I took her hand. She pulled it violently, making me fall on my knees beside her, and I wondered if this was what happens when you try to help a person who is drowning: you both go down, and never come back up.

But she wasn’t drowning, not yet. She guided my hand along the floor until my fingers hit cold metal.

There was a latch.

Smoke filled my lungs with every inhale now, so I held my breath and took the latch with both hands and pulled. A trapdoor opened up, revealing pitch black and cold earth. I pushed Evelyn in and jumped in behind her before I could think. My mind was buzzed and numb from the smoke, and if I’d waited any longer, I would have collapsed. Anything was better than losing consciousness, because wherever I would’ve woken up, I doubt it would’ve been good. If I were to wake up at all.

My foot made contact with some part of Evelyn and she yelped, but otherwise I took the brunt of the fall on my side. Pain shot out in spiderwebs, but there was too much adrenaline in my system for it to be an acute problem. 

With my hands I tried to find Evelyn’s face, just to make sure it was still there, that she was breathing and alive.

“Are you okay?” I heard myself repeating. She was still coughing, but a little bit less now.

“Yeah,” came the reply, like she’d smoked two packs of cigarettes in between now and the last time she’d opened her mouth. My heart felt a little bit less dead.

The smoke was creeping in down to our hiding spot, but only slowly. Above, everything was gray-white, like a fog had rolled over us. 

For a moment we simply laid there and breathed, finding our strength. There wasn’t quite enough room to stand up, and even though we were leaning against opposite walls, our legs touched. Thankfully I wasn’t claustrophobic, but I couldn't remember if Evelyn was, or if she was afraid of heights instead. Given the circumstances, it seemed trivial.

Evelyn started to look around, touching the walls. It was dark on all sides, but once my eyes started to adjust, I could make out more details and join the cause. There were wooden beams that seemed to support the area around the trapdoor, but otherwise everything was hard dirt. Probably dug out by hand, but what for?

“There’s a light,” Evelyn said and started off crawling in one direction. “Follow.”

I looked to where she was going, then the opposite end behind us. There was a light there too, which meant that we were in a tunnel with at least two directions. Judging by the trapdoor’s location relative to Evelyn’s light, the direction she was going would lead behind the chapel. That seemed better than going towards the village. Then again, anything was better than staying there, so I followed her. 

The tunnel was just big enough to crawl on your knees. The light was far away, much farther than it would be if the tunnel would only lead to the immediate plot of land behind the chapel, which felt odd. If it was meant as an escape route, it was awfully long. 

We cleared our lungs and spit out the worst of the phlegm as we inched our way forwards. My mind found bits of clarity with each step, but my muscles felt like I’d been at an intense Pilates class. The shock was wearing off slightly, and questions started to tumble in, but there was no energy to go through them yet. Not with the pain coming out in bursts that began to finally make itself known.

The light was getting closer by the minute, but it had definitely been many minutes since we’d started moving. Even though we were slow, we’d still traversed quite a long way. I couldn’t see much in front of me besides Evelyn’s silhouette and the hue of the light, but as far as I could tell nothing of note had come our way yet.

Finally, Evelyn reached the light. She stopped and breathed, and I came up to her and did the same.

“What is it?” I asked after I’d caught my breath.

“Another trapdoor.”

Good. That was good. “Alright, tell me when you’re ready to go and we’ll get the fuck out of here.”

She sighed. “It’s on the ground.”

“What?”

“The trapdoor is on the ground. It goes further down.”

The world was a frown and a circus. I wanted to cry, but my tear ducts weren’t working the way they should, not after the smoke. Instead, a laugh escaped my lips. 

“Sorry,” I said.

A moment’s quiet, then Evelyn chuckled, and that made me laugh even harder, until we were both laughing like coyotes. Everything hurt, and that was what we were laughing at. We’d just been through the most horrible, nightmarish thing I could ever think of, and that was what we were laughing at. 

Simply put, there was nothing more to do.

Our little circus died down at some point. We sat in silence for a moment, and then Evelyn took my hand and said, “Fuck it, I guess we’re going [in](https://wintersnoah.wordpress.com).”
