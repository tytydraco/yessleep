Always throw the deadbolt and slide the chain before you set your bags down.

If you skip that five-second routine, anyone with a duplicate brass key can walk straight into your room before you even reach the nightstand.

Three years ago, my roommate Jenna and I threw two duffel bags into the trunk of my Civic and left our Scranton apartment right around midnight. We broke our lease early to get away from a bad family situation back home, and the plan was simple: run Interstate 80 west through Pennsylvania and hit Cleveland by morning.

But around two, the temperature gauge on my cluster shot past the red line. White steam started pouring through the radiator grill.

I had to dump the car off the nearest exit ramp onto an unlit two-lane state route, coasting into the gravel lot of an old two-story motel set back off the road.

The place looked half-abandoned. The asphalt was broken up with weeds, and an open metal staircase led up to an exterior concrete walkway on the second floor. The front office had one yellow bulb buzzing over the door.

A guy in a faded gray work jacket and a ball cap stood behind the counter. I handed him fifty bucks in cash.

He pushed across a heavy brass key stamped 214 and told us the first floor had shutoff pipe leaks, so the second deck was the only dry run. He said no one else was staying up there, so if we heard anyone messing around on the walkway, we should keep the door bolted and wait for daybreak.

We carried our bags up the metal steps. The outdoor walkway lights were completely dead, except for one flickering tube over an unplugged ice machine at the far end.

Room 214 was freezing and smelled like dry wallboard. It had two low twin beds with thin green blankets, water-stained acoustic drop tiles overhead, and a cheap laminate desk under an unlit mirror.

Jenna dropped her bag on the mattress nearest the door and sat down with her coat still zipped.  
I pulled the entry door shut hard until the latch clicked into the jamb, turned the deadbolt until it seated, and slid the chain into the track.  
Ten minutes later, Jenna went into the bathroom to run the faucet.

While the water ran, two hard knocks hit the center of our door. Just two clean, knuckle-on-wood hits.  
I put my phone on the desk, walked over, and checked the peephole. It gave that bent fisheye view of the concrete landing, the pipe railing, and the dark gravel lot below.

Nothing. The walkway was dead empty.  
I stood there for about fifteen seconds with my fingers touching the latch. Two more knocks hit the wood, right at eye level.

I pulled my flashlight from my pocket, unhooked the chain, and cracked the door open six inches.  
The night air was dead silent. Nobody was running down the balcony, and there were no footsteps on the metal stairs.

I pulled the door shut, turned the deadbolt all the way back into the frame, and hooked the chain.  
Jenna came out with a damp paper towel and parted the window blinds to look down at my car. She froze, looking sideways toward the dark room next to ours, room 212.

She let the blind drop, locked the window track, and told me the curtain in 212 was pulled back an inch. She said she saw two pale reflections staring out through the glass at shoulder height.

I grabbed the heavy light, pulled our curtain aside, and aimed the beam through the dirty glass next door.

The beam lit up a bare box spring, an unmade frame, and an empty dresser. Nobody was standing there.

I told her the yellow parking light was just catching dust on the window. She didn't answer. She just sat back down on her mattress, pulled her hood up, and kept her sneakers on.

The baseboard heater had no control dial, just a stripped copper peg. The room stayed cold.  
By three, Jenna passed out facing the wall. My phone dropped from two bars to No Service, and the GPS screen locked up completely.

I lay down on the second twin bed, listening to the muffled engine brakes off the distant interstate. The mattress felt firm, but after twenty minutes, the springs in the empty center of the bed dipped down.

The fabric creaked, the exact sound foam makes when someone sits down on it.

I reached over and hit the bedside lamp. The mattress was empty, but along the dusty trim beside the frame, three clean drag marks ran straight across the linoleum toward the built-in closet.

I swung my legs off the bed, walked to the entry door to verify the exit, and put my hand on the brass knob. The lock turned loose in my grip.

The deadbolt had been fully retracted into the casing.  
The chain was still in its slot, but the cylinder had been turned to the unlocked position from the inside.

I didn't touch the brass again.  
Looking down at the floor, I saw a straight line of coarse, dark hair strands along the carpet seam. Neither Jenna nor I have dark hair.

The trail started at a low utility vent by the bathroom baseboard, crossed the entryway, and vanished under the wardrobe door.

The wardrobe door sat open half an inch.  
I stepped over, brought the flashlight up, and pushed the thin wood back. The hanging rack held three bent metal hangers.

On the floorboards sat a gray work jacket with an unzipped plastic liner, a pair of dirty canvas gloves, and an open county blueprint sheet showing the open utility crawls connecting every bathroom on the second floor.

I turned around, grabbed Jenna by her jacket collar, and shook her awake.

I told her to grab her bag, keep quiet, and head straight for the walkway.

We reached the threshold. I slid the chain free, pulled the door open, and stepped out onto the concrete.

Forty feet away, standing dead center at the top of the main metal stairs under the yellow bulb, was a man.

He had a dark hoodie pulled low and his hands rested flat against his thighs. He didn't move, he didn't raise his hands, and he didn't say a word. He just stood there flat-footed on the landing, blocking the path down, staring directly at our open door.  
I didn't yell.

I grabbed Jenna's wrist, turned the opposite direction down the walkway toward the rear fire escape, and broke into a dead sprint.

We hit the rusted metal steps, cleared the landing, and ran across the gravel to my Civic.

The key turned, and the starter caught on the first crank. The temperature needle was back down in the normal range.

I put the car in reverse with the headlights off, cut hard onto the dark blacktop, and dropped the shifter into drive.

We drove twenty miles down that state route until we hit an all-night convenience store with three state trooper cruisers sitting out front.  
We walked straight inside and told the officers everything.

Two hours later, one of the troopers came out to our car after checking the property.  
He told us the motel office was locked with a padlock on the outside, and county tax records showed the property had been shut down and foreclosed three months earlier.

When the troopers cleared room 214, the space was empty, the front window was latched, and the deadbolt was locked from the inside.

Sitting dead center on the small nightstand between the two beds was a brass split ring holding two freshly cut keys to my Civic's ignition.
