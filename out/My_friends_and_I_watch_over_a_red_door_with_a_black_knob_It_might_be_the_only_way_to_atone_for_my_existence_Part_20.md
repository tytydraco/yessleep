When I was a kid, we stole our cable from the neighbor next door.

Even though we had it, I rarely had the luxury of enjoying it. The living room was usually where Mom dwelled, and whenever he wasn’t at the bar, Dad. Asking to watch something while Mom was out there was usually met with a rude bark to go play outside, and trying to use the device when my father flopped into his recliner for the night was basically asking to get shoved into my room for the evening.

It was always better to either stay there to begin with, or avoid the house altogether by staying out as late as possible. After a while, my desire to use the thing just faded altogether. In a house too poor to afford cable, you found other ways to entertain yourself anyway. Reading, drawing, playing pretend.

That all changed one night when I’d woken up with a throat as dry as a desert. I slipped out of bed and creaked open my door slowly, trying to make as little noise as possible since I could hear Dad’s shows running in the living room. I didn’t need any confrontation.

I crept down the hallway as silently as I could, the rickety floor betraying me with every step. From the corridor, it was easy to see the side of Dad’s chair, his arm lazily draped over the rests and backrest fully leaned out. My gaze was locked firmly on him the whole time I moved, but it wasn’t until I heard his soft breathing over the steady chatter of the TV that I relaxed in my stance. He was sleeping, which meant I could move without worry.

I treaded much faster towards the bathroom, but stopped in the doorway when a new sound caught my attention: the whimsical and musical noises coming from the TV. Usually, all that was playing was the monotonous drone of a news anchor or the static applause of a sports game rerun. Curiosity itched at my ears, tugging them so hard that it spun me in place.

On the glowing box, there was a show running that I’d never seen before. To this day I’m not even sure what it was. It seemed like the standard ‘teens thrust into a fantasy land to have adventures and fight monsters only to go back home in time for dinner’ cartoon that I would later learn were a dime a dozen. Based on the TV guide still being pulled up at the bottom of the screen, I figured my father must have fallen asleep while browsing the channels.

To a child like me that didn’t have a lot of TV experience, the magnificent colors and beautifully drawn landscapes were mesmerizing. Comics and drawing had always been a favorite pastime of mine while locked in my room, so seeing something that wasn’t a Disney movie and that actually looked a like a fantasy comic come to life had me hooked.

I don’t even know how long I stood there for, but I was too afraid to move or draw closer, lest I wake my father and shatter the fragile beauty of the moment. The volume on the TV was so soft that half of what the characters were saying was completely inaudible, and the magic and monsters on the screen were so grandiose and intricate that it made it nearly impossible to figure out what was going on.

That didn’t halt my enjoyment of it, however. Hell, I forgot to even get the drink of water that I’d come for I was so engrossed, and by the time I heard dad stir in his chair and jerk upright, I didn’t even want it anymore. Instead, as I slinked into the dark of my bathroom to hide from Dad, a new thirst began to grip me. I needed to see more of that show. I had to know more.

It took a while before I got the chance to. Whenever Dad would come home and pass out drunk in his chair, I tried sneaking out into the halls with the foolish hope that by some miracle the TV landed on the cartoon channel again. Of course, this wasn’t the case.

I was going to need to have the house to myself, and that chance finally came one day when one of my mother’s signature migraines decided to flare up. Dad was at the bar after work as usual, and given how late he usually stayed out, that meant I had at the very least an hour of time to myself that I could sit in the living room without parental scorn. Even better, it would be around the same time that I caught the show the first time around.

This didn’t mean I was completely safe, though. Dad was always a wild card on when he decided to return home, and I knew I would have to remain vigilant, but still, the moment my mother sauntered her way into her bedroom and I heard her fan click on, I was right there out in the living room, hauling my tiny self onto the recliner and sinking back into it while reaching for the remote.

When the last one ended, and in faded a slow zoom on a grim, ominous castle drawn in a familiar art style, the excitement in my stomach lifted me in my seat, and I sat onto my knees.

Like last time, I didn’t have any idea what was going on. I could hear the dialogue this time, and my brain processed the events that were unfolding, but the show was meant to be episodic, and I was very late to the party. Just like last time, though, none of that mattered to me. Everything else about it was so grand that it was easy for me to get wrapped up in its world.

I watched the TV practically with one eye, the other turning to the window every few seconds in fear that I might spot the headlights of Dad’s car flashing into the driveway. Even sitting on eggshells, the anxiety didn’t stop me from enjoying myself, however. The vibrant colors on the screen and the wondrous world it showed—no matter how absurd it might have been—made me feel a warmth that I’d never felt before.

I wanted to be there. I wanted to live in the fantasy land with wondrous hillscapes and powerful magic. To escape from the daily mundane through magic and adventure. I didn’t want to live in suffocating, drab Stillwater—the town that was painted with nothing but a muddy white snow for half the year, and consisted of roads so chewed up they were practically gravel.

I knew that she couldn’t. Not the real truth, at least.

At that moment, the clock rang out marking a new hour, and since it had only been an hour since our last kill, I almost didn’t bother to turn around to look at the door. That comfort was sorely unfounded however. I watched my friends faces go pale and shocked as they looked over my shoulder, and Lacey shot up, raising the gun.

“Shit!”

“N-No!” Carly cried, “It’s only been an hour!”

After we’d gotten hit with an attack after two hours near the beginning, I think we were all waiting for the other shoe to drop like this; we’d just hoped it would never actually come. Seeing the barrier swing open after so much excitement the last sixty minutes made a dread sweep through the room that rivaled anything we’d felt thus far. We had spent so much time discussing my vision and dealing with the spider from the loom that we hadn’t had time to set a new trap or even clean the space.

“Is this because that woman showed up?!” Bryce cried.

I yanked up my axe and shoved my chair to the side of the room, assuming a battle stance, “It seems like the door might not be too happy that we fended her off.”

“Everyone stay calm,” Lacey ordered, shining her flashlight down the dark floral cavern beneath the barrel of her gun, “The faster they come, the weaker they are. Just focus and we’ll be fine.”

She was right, and we all assumed our usual positions. At the end of the day, the time at which the monsters came was irrelevant; they all fell to the concrete the same, and we were borderline masters at putting them there at this point. So long as we did what we always did, we would be okay.

The problem was, the door didn’t do what it always did. What came calling down the halls wasn’t the usual shriek of a monster or scuttle of claws raking at the floor. It was a voice. As if to spite us for our confidence and defiance of it after all these weeks, it sent the one thing I wasn’t sure we could fight.

“L-Lacey?” A horse, soft voice crept down the hall. Distant and quiet, but blaring loud in our ears. Loud because we all recognized who it belonged to. “Lacey i-is that you?”

My friend faltered in her stance, her gun lowering ever so slightly as a breath iced over in her throat. The rest of us did the same. Of the dozens of beasts we’d fought, the door had never once sent us a creature that could talk, let alone one that was related to anything from this realm.

What came creeping into view at that moment was not a monster. It was a human; one that was an old friend. The pale, shambling from of Casey stepped into the fingertips of Lacey’s flashlight, throat still a slit, bloodied mess, and eyes glazed and glinting.
