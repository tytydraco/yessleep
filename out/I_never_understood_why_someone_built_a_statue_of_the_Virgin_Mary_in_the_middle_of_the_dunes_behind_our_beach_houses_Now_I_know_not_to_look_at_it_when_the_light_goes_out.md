I’ve spent almost every summer of my life in Barrancadero, a private beach community a couple of hours south of Lima, Peru. It’s not really a tourist destination. 

There are only a few dozen houses built on top of a coastal bluff overlooking the Pacific, with a path that takes you down to the beach. Behind the houses there’s absolutely nothing except sand dunes stretching for miles until they meet the fence along the Pan-American Highway.

The only thing out there is a statue of the Virgin Mary.  
It’s about 150 meters behind the last row of houses, sitting on top of the tallest dune. White concrete, maybe seven feet tall. There’s no chapel, no benches, no cross, not even a proper trail leading up to it. Someone just decided that the highest dune was the perfect place to leave a statue overlooking the ocean.

Nobody really knows why it’s there.

The older residents say it appeared when the community was first built over thirty years ago. Nobody remembers who donated it, who hauled it up there, or even who pays for the floodlight that illuminates it every night. Because that’s another strange thing. Every night after sunset, a single spotlight shines on the statue.

Except sometimes it doesn’t.

Every once in a while the light just goes out. We always assumed it was an electrical issue and never thought much about it until three nights ago.

I couldn’t sleep, so around one in the morning I walked onto the terrace with a glass of scotch. From there you can always see the statue. It’s become part of the landscape after all these years.

I noticed the light was off.  
*“Guess the spotlight failed again,”* I thought.

I stood there looking at the ocean for a few minutes before glancing back toward the dunes.

The light was still off.

That’s when I realized something felt wrong.

The statue wasn’t where I expected it to be.

At first I blamed the darkness. Maybe it was the moonlight, maybe I was seeing it from a different angle than usual. I walked closer to the edge of the terrace and stared for several seconds.

The statue was still standing on the dune. But it wasn’t facing the ocean anymore. It was facing the houses.

I know how ridiculous that sounds. It probably is. But I’ve been coming here for over twenty years. I know exactly which direction that statue faces because it’s always been one of those landmarks you stop noticing after seeing it thousands of times.

The next morning I rode my ATV up the dunes to check. We usually use them to get around the desert behind the houses, so it wasn’t unusual. When I reached the top, the statue was facing the ocean exactly as it always had. Its base is a massive block of concrete. There is absolutely no way someone could rotate it overnight without heavy machinery.

I convinced myself I’d imagined the whole thing.  
Until the following night.

At exactly 12:47 a.m. the light went out again.  
This time my cousin was with me.

“The light’s out again,” I said.

He barely had time to answer before we both saw it.  
The statue didn’t rotate.  
That’s the part that still bothers me.  
It wasn’t like watching something slowly turn.  
One second it was facing the ocean.  
The next second…  
it simply wasn’t.

It was already facing us, as if it had always been that way.  
We both laughed. 

Not because it was funny, but because sometimes your brain doesn’t know what else to do when something makes absolutely no sense.

Neither of us said another word after that.

The next afternoon we took the ATVs back up to the statue, not because of what we’d seen, but because we wanted to inspect the spotlight. We figured maybe someone had been messing with it.

The light hadn’t burned out.  
It had been unplugged.

The power cable was simply disconnected.  
While my cousin checked the electrical box, I walked around the statue’s base and noticed footprints.

Bare footprints.

They weren’t leading toward the statue.  
They started at the pedestal and went downhill toward the houses.

For a moment I assumed they were old tracks left by someone messing around. Then I placed my own foot beside one.

They were much larger than mine.

That night nobody mentioned the statue. We had dinner, watched a movie, and pretended nothing had happened.  
Around midnight the light went out again.  
None of us went outside.

A few seconds later we heard footsteps.  
If you’ve ever walked across sand dunes, you know how difficult it is. Dry sand absorbs sound incredibly well, so hearing footsteps from that far away shouldn’t have been possible.

But we heard them.  
Slow.  
Heavy.  
Bare feet walking through sand.

The sound came from behind the houses and moved toward the beach.

We never saw anyone.  
Only the footsteps.

They continued for almost five minutes.

The next morning every house facing the dunes had sand scattered across its second-floor balcony.  
Not the ground floor.  
The second floor.

There were no footprints leading inside, no signs of forced entry, nothing stolen.  
Just sand.  
An absurd amount of sand.

The day before we drove back to Lima, I mentioned everything to one of the oldest security guards. He’s in his seventies and has worked there since the community was built.

I expected him to laugh.

Instead he asked me one question.  
*“Was the light off?”*

I nodded.

He looked toward the dunes for several seconds before quietly saying, *“Don’t look at her when the light goes out.”*

I asked him why.

He didn’t answer immediately.

Then he said, *“Because when the light goes out… she isn’t looking at the ocean anymore.”*

Since then I’ve gone through decades of old family photos taken at Barrancadero. Birthdays, Christmases, New Year’s parties. The statue appears in the background of dozens of them.

Sometimes the spotlight is on.  
Sometimes it’s off.

What I never noticed until now is that every photo where the light is off shows the statue facing a slightly different direction.

Maybe it always did.

Or maybe I only started noticing after that night.  
I honestly don’t know which possibility scares me more.
