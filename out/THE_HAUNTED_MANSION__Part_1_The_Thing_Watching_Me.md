# Content warning: Graphic violence, murder, disturbing imagery.

Let me tell you something about memory loss. It is like waking up halfway through a movie without knowing how it began, yet being expected to follow the story as if nothing had happened.

My name is Wissam. I am twenty-one years old.

Today, my discharge papers were finalized. My father came to pick me up after I had spent quite some time in the hospital, fighting an illness the doctors described as malignant and stubborn. The real problem, however, was not the illness itself. It was that I could not remember a single thing that had happened inside those cold, miserable white walls. It was as if my consciousness had gone on vacation, or as if I had been in a deep coma.

My father asked me while we were driving home, “How are you feeling now, Wissam?”

I answered honestly. “Fine, thank God. But I really can’t remember anything that happened in there.”

He gave me a strange look.

“That’s odd… Anyway, the important thing is that you’re healthy now. We’ll stop to pick up your mother and brothers so we can all visit your uncle.”

I remembered my family perfectly, and I loved them without a doubt: my father, Dagher; my mother, Hiyam; my older brother, Omar; and my younger brother, Haitham. Five people who made up my entire world. I had no friends. I had only my family.

They welcomed me home with overwhelming warmth, as if they had not seen me in an eternity. I did not feel like a stranger. Instead, I realized just how much they had missed having me around.

I went into my room to see it again—that beautiful room, filled with memories.

“How are you?” Omar asked.

“I’m fine,” I replied.

“How was it in the hospital? It was a really serious, nasty illness. It’s good that you recovered.”

“Thank God. But I don’t remember anything that happened in the hospital.”

“Don’t worry. What matters is that you’re okay. Welcome home, brother.”

Omar was my older brother, but he was more than that. He was one of the people closest to my heart. I remembered our days together at school. Nothing could ever separate us.

He protected me from anyone who tried to bother me. I was never afraid, because Omar was always there.

We packed our belongings, and I brought along my gaming console so my brothers and I could play together at our uncle’s house.

Our destination was a remote village, neither large nor small, where my uncle Samir lived. He was a simple man, forty-five years old, with a face full of worries. Despite everything weighing on him, he still smiled. He had taught me to stay optimistic and smile even when life became difficult.

His wife, Faten, was my mother’s childhood friend. She was a little overweight—not beautiful, not ugly, just ordinary-looking. But I wasn’t the one who was going to marry her.

They had never been blessed with children.

The journey was long and miserable: five hours of continuous driving, interrupted by only one lonely stop.

As I hurried ahead of my mother and brothers toward the car, I noticed my father slipping a pistol into the glove compartment.

“What’s that, Dad?” I asked in surprise.

“The road is long, and night will fall before we arrive. Better to be prepared,” he replied firmly.

I advised him to hide it properly so my mother wouldn’t see it and panic. He agreed.

We set off after the Maghrib prayer.

We stopped at a bleak, dilapidated rest stop. It was a miserable place, run by an old man in his sixties. His wrinkled face looked like a map of cursed roads. But when you’re in the middle of the desert, you don’t have the luxury of choosing where to stop.

We prayed Isha, then stood around for a while. My mother, Haitham, and I were together. My father was in the bathroom, while Omar had gone inside the rest stop to buy a few things.

“I don’t like visiting your uncle,” my mother said. “Something about him makes me uncomfortable.”

“Why?” Haitham asked. “He’s nice. I’ve never seen him get angry!”

“That’s exactly what worries me. I think he’s hiding a lot behind that smile.”

I spoke up. “He’s just trying to cope with his difficult circumstances. You know that.”

“I don’t think so. I only agreed to come because I wanted to see Faten. She’s been my friend since childhood.”

“Of course, we know that,” Haitham and I said together.

She smiled, and Haitham and I laughed.

Then we set off again.

The road was boring, and darkness swallowed everything. Everyone fell into a deep sleep except my father and me. He concentrated silently on driving, while I suffered from motion sickness, which made it impossible for me to look at my phone.

I tried to wake Omar.

“Omar. Omar, wake up.”

“What do you want?”

“Let’s do something. This drive is boring.”

“Go to sleep!”

“I don’t want to. Let’s do something.”

“Go to sleep!”

“We could play—”

“GO TO SLEEP, YOU IDIOT!”

He shouted angrily.

I had nothing to say after that, so I stared out the window at the empty desert.

And that was when I saw it.

A completely black creature stood in front of a dilapidated hut that had no business being there in the middle of nowhere.

I could make out nothing about it except for two empty white eyes, completely devoid of life. There was no mercy in them.

It smiled.

It smiled directly at me.

The scene lasted only five seconds before darkness swallowed it.

Was it a visual hallucination? I had no answer. I had been experiencing more and more hallucinations since leaving the hospital.

Then the car stopped.

“What happened, Dad?” I asked.

“Don’t worry. Just a minor breakdown. I’ll fix it, and then we’ll continue.”

“That’s bad. We’re on a road where nobody passes.”

“Don’t worry. Stay in the car and wake everyone up.”

“Okay.”

I woke everyone to tell them the latest news.

We sat in the car without talking, without doing anything.

“Why don’t we play something?” Haitham suggested.

“Are you stupid or crazy?” my mother snapped.

“It’s the only thing we can do,” he replied.

“I agree with Haitham,” I said.

Omar announced that he was going to help our father.

Haitham and I passed the time talking. My mother was worried, so we tried to reassure her by telling her she had two strong men with her.

Of course, we meant ourselves.

In reality, we were cowards. It was only a joke, and my mother knew us well. Omar was much stronger than Haitham and me.

I looked out the window again.

There it was.

The black creature.

It was approaching us, moving slowly.

I could see its lifeless eyes staring at me. There was no mercy in them, no soul.

I did not want to tell anyone what I had seen. I didn’t want to frighten them even more.

It couldn’t be a hallucination if it kept happening, could it?

My father and Omar returned.

“Let’s go. I fixed it, thank God,” my father said.

We thanked God and continued our journey.

By nightfall, we reached my uncle’s village. It was drowned in grave-like silence.

On our way in, we passed a long bridge. Beyond it stood an enormous mansion, separated from the bridge by a calm lake.

Some people might have found the sight beautiful, with the moonlight reflected on the water. But to me, it was a nightmare. Shadows fell across the mansion’s facade like giant spiderwebs.

That mansion was hiding something.

My uncle and his wife welcomed us warmly.

I noticed the questioning look on my uncle’s face when he asked me, “Where have you been all this time? Why didn’t you come with your father on his last visit?”

My father gave me a sharp, meaningful look.

“I was a little tired,” I answered briefly. “Just a minor illness.”

We spent a long time talking and playing games—my uncle, Omar, Haitham, and me. My father didn’t like playing, so he simply watched and talked with us.

Eventually, we went to bed. The journey had been exhausting, and we needed rest.

The next morning, the smell of breakfast was wonderful. Faten was an excellent cook.

“Breakfast is ready! Come on!” she called.

Everyone was already seated when I arrived. I sat between Omar and Haitham and whispered to Omar.

“Faten’s cooking is much better than Mom’s, isn’t it?”

“Shut up, or Mom will hear you,” he whispered back.

“I’m just telling the truth.”

“What truth, Wissam?” my mother asked.

“I was saying that your cooking is much better than Faten’s, Mom. Sorry, Faten, but that’s the truth.”

Faten laughed because she knew I wasn’t telling the truth.

Omar let out a muffled laugh, and we finished breakfast without any trouble.

After dinner, the villagers began heading inside to sleep, and the streets became quiet. Omar, Haitham, and I decided to take a little walk. We loved exploring.

As we walked through the village, I noticed how empty it was. There were no people around. It felt like an abandoned village.

I liked the quiet.

Then I saw it again.

That damned creature was moving between the houses, coming closer and closer to me.

This definitely wasn’t a hallucination.

It was following me.

“Let’s get out of here,” I said quickly.

“What’s wrong with you?” Omar asked.

“Nothing. This place is boring.”

“I like this village,” Omar said.

“What do you like about it?” Haitham asked. “The smell of livestock?”

“You wouldn’t understand. I like the simplicity and the beautiful nature here.”

“I do too,” I said, “but I couldn’t live here for long.”

We returned to the house, where we talked and played for a while.

Then, suddenly, the conversation fell silent.

My uncle broke the silence with a mysterious smile.

“I have something interesting that you, in particular, will enjoy, Wissam.”

“What is it, Uncle?” Haitham asked curiously.

Samir spoke slowly, savoring his words.

“The mansion you passed on your way here. The one overlooking the bridge. People say it’s haunted.”

He continued.

“A wealthy family used to live there: a father, a mother, and their little daughter, Rania. Suddenly, the girl began talking to someone no one else could see. She started painting terrifying pictures in the middle of the night.

“Her parents noticed her behavior and tried to find a solution. They took her to doctors and even brought religious healers to see her, but nothing worked.

“In the end, the parents were found brutally murdered. The only things left were the girl’s clothes and two paintings, one beside each body.

“The first painting was titled *Happy Mother*. It showed her mother with her mouth split open into a smile, the wound stitched together. The painting depicted exactly how the woman had died.

“The second painting was titled *The Man of Fire*. It showed a burned man whose eyes were filled with panic and terror.

“That happened sixty years ago. No one has dared to go near that place since.

“So… would you like to explore it?”

My father looked annoyed.

“That’s ridiculous, Samir. These stories are nonsense. People around here believe them because they’re ignorant.”

But human curiosity—and that foolish desire to prove one’s courage—made me agree immediately.

“No, Wissam,” Omar warned. “It’s dangerous.”

“I don’t see a problem,” Haitham said. “It sounds like a great adventure.”

“I agree with Omar,” my father said.

“I know, Dad. I know you want to go, but you’re too afraid for us to let yourself enjoy it,” I said. “Anyone who wants to come can come. Anyone who doesn’t can stay here.”

Eventually, everyone agreed to go except Omar. But he finally gave in and followed us.

My brothers came, my mother came, and even my uncle’s wife, Faten, agreed reluctantly, despite the fact that I knew she was frightened of her own shadow.

We walked toward the bridge.

It was incredibly long. The only sound was the echo of our footsteps. The cold air rising from the lake beneath us made the place even more terrifying. The bridge was made of old, crumbling stone. It was a wonder it was still standing.

My father was worried about us, but when he saw that everyone had agreed to go, he reluctantly accepted.

Halfway across the bridge stood an iron gate leading to a path that connected the bridge to the mansion.

The air felt heavy. Every step echoed through the dead silence.

We reached the mansion’s main entrance.

The door was slightly open, and a powerful stench of decay drifted from within, like rotten blood left in the sun.

“Let’s go back,” my father said firmly. “This isn’t worth it.”

But my mother was stubborn and brave. She insisted on going inside.

Strange, isn’t it? My mother wasn’t weak or timid. She was the kind of person who wasn’t afraid of much.

The mansion’s garden was enormous. The flowers were dead. The garden had once been beautiful, but now it stirred fear in anyone who looked at it.

We all went inside except Omar, who seemed frightened for our safety. Eventually, he followed us. Being alone outside was even more terrifying than entering the mansion, especially when you were standing by yourself somewhere you had no business being.

Inside was a huge, lifeless hall. The place was gloomy, with countless rooms and a double staircase stretching to the upper floor, one side leading left and the other right.

We began exploring the ground floor.

There were no windows downstairs, only countless doors leading to rooms and wide, frightening halls. The sofas had been eaten away by time.

How strange life was. People died, and other people came to live in the places they left behind. They never met one another. You worked to make your life more beautiful, only for other people to come and take it over after you died.

Life was simple, yet we were the ones who made it complicated.

Omar interrupted my thoughts as I philosophized to myself.

“How did people even live here? The place is so huge. They must have struggled to tell the rooms apart.”

“I’m sure they got used to living here,” I replied.

“Obviously, they must have had a map,” Haitham said stupidly.

Everyone laughed at him, and we continued exploring.

They all went upstairs to continue their search, while I stayed downstairs, studying the place. There were seven of us. Surely, nobody would notice if I fell behind for a few seconds.

Suddenly—

**BANG!**

The mansion’s door slammed shut behind us, shaking the walls.

Everyone rushed back downstairs.

“What happened, Wissam?” my father demanded.

I was genuinely frightened.

“The door… it closed by itself.”

My father rushed to it and tried to open it, but it wouldn’t budge. It was as solid as rock.

“How can it not open?” Omar asked in disbelief.

We all tried. Nothing worked.

“What do we do now?” Omar asked.

“We have to find another exit,” my father replied. “We have no other choice.”

We searched the ground floor for an exit or a window.

“How can there be no way out?!” my father shouted impatiently. “How?! Can’t we get back into the garden from here? This is ridiculous!”

I tried to calm him down.

“Calm down. We have to find a way out.”

The place felt like a sealed tomb. As I had already said, there were no windows on the ground floor.

We had no choice but to search upstairs.

That was when Haitham suggested the stupid idea I had always mocked in horror movies.

“Let’s split up so we can cover more ground.”

“That’s the stupidest decision you could possibly make!” I snapped.

But my father settled the argument.

“Haitham is right. Your mother, Haitham, and I will be one group. You and Omar will be another. Your uncle and his wife will be the third. We’ll meet back here in an hour.”

“But, Dad, we shouldn’t split up! It’s dangerous!”

“Unfortunately, we have no other option. I don’t like it either, but it’s the only solution. Besides, you won’t be alone. Omar will be with you, and no group will have just one person.”

I reluctantly agreed and set off with Omar.

I walked ahead of him, moving slowly as I studied the high windows. They were far too high to jump through.

I turned around to speak to him.

He was gone.

“Omar… This isn’t the time for jokes. Come out!”

I searched the next room.

“BOO!”

“Now is not the time for jokes, you idiot!”

“How did you know it was me, Wissam?” he asked.

“It was obvious.”

We decided to mark the rooms using two small pieces of metal we had found on the floor. That way, we could tell which doors we had already opened.

We agreed to split up within the area we were covering, but we would not get lost. We would meet in Room 7.

I walked from room to room, searching and leaving marks to keep track of where I had been.

Then the real terror began.

I turned around and discovered that the marks I had just carved were completely gone.

“Omar! Where are you? The marks have disappeared! Where are you? Enough with these stupid jokes! I’m leaving you and going back to the meeting point!”

But my conscience wouldn’t let me abandon my older brother.

Suddenly, a powerful blow struck my head.

It was strong enough to knock me unconscious.

When I woke up, I was somewhere else—a place I had not been in before I closed my eyes.

My hands were covered in blood.

This was strange. My clothes were soaked in it, too.

I felt sick. I had a horrible feeling that the blood was mine.

Three hours had passed.

How had all that time gone by? Had they left me behind?

I remembered that I had to find Omar.

I started wandering through the rooms, opening doors and searching for him. I couldn’t find him, and my anxiety grew with every passing second.

I opened the next door with a trembling heart.

And there—

**My blood ran cold.**

Omar was lying on the floor.

He hadn’t been killed with just one or two stab wounds. His body was a map of injuries. His face, back, and legs were covered in stabs—wild, hateful, senseless wounds.

I broke down crying.

Omar. My brother.

All those memories we had made together…

I couldn’t catch my breath.

It had to be the malignant illness they had told me about. I tried to pull myself together, and somehow I managed.

I desperately tried to wake him.

“Wake up. Wake up! Come on!”

Nothing.

“I’m the reason this happened! I’m the one who insisted on this cursed adventure!”

Time passed as if an eternity had gone by.

I dragged my brother’s body and decided to check on my family.

I had to return to the meeting point.

I had to find out if anyone else was still alive.
