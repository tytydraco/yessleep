So I've been pumping shit for fifteen years. That's the job. You get a vacuum truck, a 3,000-gallon tank, and you spend your days sucking up other people's waste. You see things people don't want you to see. You smell things that stick to the inside of your nose for days. After a while you stop noticing. Mostly.

You learn to read the houses. The hoarder houses. The meth houses. The "my dog ate a sock and it's clogged" houses. But the house on Blackwood Lane was different.

The guy who called me out was named Gary. Late sixties, pristine polo shirt, holding two cold beers. He didn't offer to help me unload the heavy hoses. He just stood on the porch, sipping his drink, watching me work.

"Haven't had this tank pumped since we moved in," he said, pointing to the concrete lids in the backyard. "Thirty years. The drains have been backing up for months. Figured I couldn't put it off anymore."

Thirty years. A septic tank that old is basically a solid brick of waste. I told him it was gonna be a long, messy job and the lines might need jetting after. He just smiled. A tight, thin smile. "Oh, it's a mess alright. But it's time to clear it out."

I found the lids. Concrete, heavy as hell. I used the iron hook to pull them off.

When the first lid came off, the smell hit me.

It wasn't the usual methane and rot. It was sweet. Like spoiled meat and copper. Like a damn graveyard. I've smelled dead rats, I've smelled hoarder cats, but this was different.

I dropped the suction hose in. Fired up the pump.

For the first ten minutes, it was just thick, black sludge. But then the suction gauge dropped. Clog.

I cursed, killed the pump, and grabbed my muck rake—a ten-foot pole with a blade on the end to break up the crust. Gary was still watching me from the porch. Just standing there. Not helping. Just watching.

I stirred the tank. The crust was thick, almost rubbery. I hooked the rake and pulled up a chunk.

It wasn't waste.

It was a plastic toy. A little green army man. Melted and warped, but intact.

I frowned and dipped the rake again. A sneaker. Small size, velcro straps. A kid's shoe.

Then a lunchbox. Old metal, faded cartoons on the side, the kind they don't make anymore.

I stared at it. The cartoon character was barely visible under the gunk. Something about it made my stomach tighten.

I just stood there, trying to place it.

Gary hadn't moved. He was just watching.

"Find something interesting?" he called out. His voice was weird. Like he didn't care at all.

I didn't answer. I dropped the rake and grabbed my heavy-duty flashlight. I needed to see what was clogging the line.

I clicked it on and shined it down into the six-foot-deep concrete box.

The light hit the bottom. The sludge was thick, but I could see.

Sitting in the center of the tank, resting on a pedestal of compacted waste, was an old rusted oil drum. It was sealed. But the rust on the side… it was warped. Pushed outward.

I squinted, adjusting the focus on the light.

It was a handprint. A tiny, child-sized handprint, fingers splayed, pressed from the inside of the metal. The rust bulged in the shape of a tiny hand, like something had been pressing against it for thirty years.

But that wasn't the worst part. The sludge at the bottom… it wasn't settling. It was moving. Up and down. Up and down. Like breathing.

I've seen dead animals in tanks before. Rats, mostly. But this… this was moving.

Then it clicked. The lunchbox had a faded strip of masking tape on it. In shaky marker, it read: BILLY. That's when I remembered Billy Miller. Seven years old. Vanished in 1994. Gary was the prime suspect. Everyone knew it. The cops dredged the woods, brought in dogs, did polygraphs. Nothing. Gary walked free and married the mom a year later.

"You found his toys," Gary said.

I jumped. He was standing right at the edge of the pit. I hadn't heard him walk up.

"I threw them in there," Gary said, looking down into the tank. His voice was quiet. Not guilty. Just tired. "After. To keep it quiet."

"Keep what quiet?" I choked out, backing away from the edge.

Gary rubbed the back of his neck. He looked old suddenly. Smaller. "It wasn't supposed to… I was angry. He wouldn't stop crying. I just wanted him to be quiet for five minutes. So I put him in the tank. Just for a minute. But when I opened the lid…" He trailed off.

I looked at the oil drum. The handprint. "You kept him in there," I whispered. "For thirty years. You've been feeding it."

Gary nodded slowly. He didn't look at me. He just stared down at the pulsing sludge. "Stray dogs. Cats. Sometimes a deer from the road. It kept the house quiet. Kept the floors from cracking." He paused. "But it's been waking up. The walls are getting thin."

"It's hungry now," he whispered.

He looked at the tank, then down at his own hands. "My wife… she's been complaining about the drains backing up. She says it smells. She's been threatening to leave." He finally looked at me. His eyes were wet. "I can't let her leave."

He didn't say anything else. He just turned and looked down into the tank again. He swung his legs over the edge and dropped down into the dark. I heard a wet sound. A heavy, dragging noise. Then silence.

I didn't try to stop him. I didn't try to help. I just stood there, heart hammering, staring at the open lid.

I packed up my hoses in a daze. I didn't even put the lids back on. I just got in my truck and started the engine.

I reached for my phone to call 911. It was still lying beside the open tank.

I was going to drive away. I was going to quit.

But as I put the truck in drive, I felt something cold on the back of my neck. A small hand. Wet. Smelling of copper and old flowers.

I didn't look in the rearview mirror. I didn't look at the passenger seat. I just drove.

When I got home, I scrubbed my hands until they bled. The smell wouldn't come off.

This morning, when I checked my truck, the passenger seat was still wet. The army man had moved. It wasn't facing the house anymore. It was facing me.

I picked it up. The plastic was warm. And underneath it, scratched into the vinyl seat with something small and sharp, were two words:

BRING MOM.

I threw the toy in the dumpster behind my apartment. But when I got back from the gas station an hour later, it was on my dashboard. Facing the house again. Facing Blackwood Lane.

I haven't heard a phone ring. I haven't heard a voice. But the drains in my building have been gurgling all afternoon.
