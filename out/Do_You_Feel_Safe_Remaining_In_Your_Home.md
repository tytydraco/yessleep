I’ve been checking on old people for a living for seven months.

That’s obviously not what the company calls it.

My official job title is Residential Wellness Representative, which makes it sound like I have some kind of medical training. I don’t.

I knock on doors, make sure somebody’s grandmother hasn’t fallen in the bathroom, ask a handful of questions, and mark them alive in an app.

That’s most of the job.

The company I work for is called Everwell Senior Services.

Families pay us to check on elderly relatives who still live alone but probably shouldn’t. Some clients get one visit a week. Some get three. A few get checked every day. Everwell likes to advertise it as “independent living with peace of mind.”

The people we check on usually call it babysitting.

We’re not allowed to administer medication or provide medical care. If somebody is hurt, confused beyond what’s normal for them, or clearly sick, we’re supposed to contact dispatch and escalate from there.

Every visit follows basically the same routine:

Confirm identity.

Check general appearance.

Ask whether they’ve eaten.

Ask whether they’ve taken their medication.

Make sure they know what day it is.

Look for anything obviously unsafe.

Then there’s the last question.

“Do you feel safe remaining in your home?”

Most people just say yes. Some laugh. One guy always tells me, “Safer before you showed up.”

Then I submit the visit. Green checkmark. Next house.

It isn’t a terrible job. Honestly, most of the people are interesting, and a lot of them are lonely. You learn pretty quickly which clients actually need you there and which ones mostly want somebody to talk to for twenty minutes.

I’ve been shown war medals. Wedding albums. A dead husband’s entire model train collection. I’ve heard the same stories so many times that I could probably tell some of them better than the people who lived them.

I’ve also seen the other side of it.

Twice I’ve called an ambulance. I’ve walked into houses where somebody hadn’t eaten in two days because they forgot how to use the microwave. I’ve had to stay with a woman while she cried because she couldn’t remember her son’s phone number.

And once, about a month after I started, I found a man dead in his recliner.

His name was Leonard Shaw.

That was the first body I’d ever seen. Everwell gave me the rest of the day off and sent me a link to an employee counseling program.

I didn’t use it.

After a few weeks, the job went back to normal.

That’s important.

Because by the time any of this started happening, I knew what normal looked like.

Mrs. Bell was normal.

Her first name was Evelyn, but I don’t think I ever called her that. She was eighty two and lived in a small brick house by herself. I saw her Tuesdays and Fridays.

Mrs. Bell had a walker and bad knees, so answering the door took her a while. Every time I knocked, I’d hear her shout from somewhere inside.

“I’m coming. Don’t break the damn door down.”

Every time.

After a month I started answering through the door.

“No promises.”

The first time I did that, she laughed so hard she started coughing.

Her daughter lived several states away and paid Everwell to keep an eye on her. Mrs. Bell hated the entire concept.

“My daughter pays strangers to make sure I’m not dead.”

“That’s an oversimplification.”

“Is it?”

“Pretty much, yeah.”

She pointed at me. “See?”

She wasn’t confused. That’s another thing I need to make clear.

Mrs. Bell was old, but she was sharp.

She remembered my name. Remembered my schedule. Once, I mentioned that the air conditioner in my car had stopped working. Two weeks later, she asked whether I’d gotten it fixed.

There were clients I’d visited for months who still called me whatever name popped into their heads.

Mrs. Bell wasn’t one of them.

One Friday, near the end of my visit, she asked me a strange question.

“Do you ever get sent to the same person twice?”

I thought she meant duplicate appointments.

“Sometimes dispatch screws up.”

“No.” She looked annoyed. “The same person.”

“I don’t understand.”

She waved a hand.

“Never mind.”

I didn’t think much about it.

I’d learned not to chase every strange comment somebody made during these visits. Sometimes people were joking. Sometimes they were tired. Sometimes age did strange things to conversations.

Before I left, I asked the standard final question.

“Do you feel safe remaining in your home?”

Mrs. Bell rolled her eyes.

“Unless you know something I don’t.”

Three days later, she disappeared.

It was Tuesday morning.

I knocked.

Nothing.

I waited and knocked again. Usually by then I’d hear her yelling at me from inside.

Nothing.

I called her.

Her phone rang inside the house.

That’s when I started getting worried.

Everwell has a procedure for an unanswered door: call the client twice, call dispatch, wait ten minutes. If the client has authorized emergency access, dispatch can approve entry through a lockbox.

Mrs. Bell had one beside the garage.

Dispatch gave me the code.

I went inside.

Her walker was beside the couch.

That was enough.

Mrs. Bell didn’t go anywhere without it.

I checked the house.

Bedroom. Bathroom. Kitchen. Back porch.

She wasn’t there.

There wasn’t anywhere else she could be.

I called dispatch again.

After that, everything became somebody else’s problem.

Police came. Her daughter was contacted. I answered questions.

Had I seen anyone suspicious?

No.

Had Mrs. Bell seemed confused recently?

No.

Had she mentioned leaving?

No.

One officer asked whether there was any possibility she’d walked away.

I looked at the walker by the couch.

“I guess.”

But I didn’t believe it.

A missing person alert went around locally.

I remember seeing her picture shared online that night. For the next few days, I checked for updates more often than I probably should have.

Nothing.

No camera footage. No hospital admission. No body.

Nothing.

Then Everwell removed her from my schedule.

That wasn’t unusual. When somebody moved into assisted living, died, or otherwise stopped receiving the service, they disappeared from our route.

What was unusual was what happened to her profile.

About a week later, I searched her name because I wanted her daughter’s contact information.

Mrs. Bell wasn’t there.

Not inactive.

Not archived.

Gone.

I figured maybe missing person cases were handled differently.

I didn’t ask.

Three weeks later, I got assigned a new client named Margaret Harlow.

Eighty one.

Lived alone.

No cognitive impairment listed.

Routine visit.

I’d never been to the address before.

I knocked.

Mrs. Bell opened the door.

I think my brain recognized her before I did.

For the first second, there was just this vague feeling that I knew the woman standing in front of me.

Then everything clicked.

Same face.

Same voice when she said, “Can I help you?”

I just stared at her.

“Mrs. Bell?”

Her expression tightened.

“I’m sorry?”

“Mrs. Bell.”

She looked behind me like maybe I was talking to somebody else.

“My name is Margaret.”

“It’s me.”

Nothing.

That was the part that scared me.

There wasn’t even a hint of recognition.

Not confusion. Not dementia. Nothing.

She looked at me the way you’d look at a delivery driver who’d called you the wrong name.

“Are you from Everwell?” she asked.

I looked down at my phone.

Margaret Harlow.

The photo attached to the file showed the woman standing in front of me.

“Yeah.”

“Well, are you coming in?”

I almost called dispatch right there.

Instead, I went inside.

I needed to understand what I was looking at.

The house belonged to her.

Not in the sense that she’d moved in recently.

There were family photographs going back decades.

Margaret younger.

Margaret with a husband.

Margaret holding babies.

Margaret standing in front of the same house twenty years earlier.

I completed the visit mostly on autopilot.

She knew the date. She knew her address. She knew who her daughter was. She told me what she’d eaten that morning.

Her medications matched the list in the app.

Nothing was wrong with her.

At least nothing Everwell trained me to look for.

At the end I asked:

“Do you feel safe remaining in your home?”

“Yes.”

I should’ve left.

Instead I said, “Have you ever lived on Hawthorne Avenue?”

She looked at me.

“No.”

“You’ve never known anyone named Evelyn Bell?”

There it was.

Something.

Not recognition exactly.

More like I’d said something I wasn’t supposed to say.

It lasted maybe half a second.

Then her face went blank again.

“No.”

“Are you sure?”

“I’m eighty one, not senile.”

I apologized.

She walked me to the door.

Before I stepped outside, she said something quietly.

“You should stick to the questions they give you.”

I turned around.

“What?”

She was smiling.

“Have a nice day.”

The door closed.

I sat in my car for about five minutes before calling Kent.

Kent was my supervisor.

He’d been with Everwell for almost five years and had the ability to make anything sound unimportant.

I explained everything.

He listened without interrupting.

Then:

“You sure it was the same woman?”

“Yes.”

“How sure?”

“I saw Mrs. Bell twice a week for four months.”

“People resemble each other.”

“Not that much.”

He sighed.

“Give me the old client’s name.”

“Evelyn Bell.”

I heard him typing.

Then nothing.

“Kent?”

“Spell the last name.”

“B E L L.”

More typing.

“I don’t have anyone by that name.”

I thought I’d misheard him.

“What do you mean?”

“There’s no Evelyn Bell in the system.”

“She was one of my clients.”

“I understand what you’re saying.”

“I filed reports on her twice a week.”

“Maybe the account was archived incorrectly.”

“Then find the archive.”

Another pause.

When he answered, his voice had changed slightly.

Not nervous.

Just less casual.

“Dominic, don’t worry about it.”

That was the first time I got genuinely scared.

“What?”

“Complete the Harlow visit and move on.”

“I already completed it.”

Silence.

I hadn’t told him that.

Then Kent said:

“Good.”

And hung up.

I stayed in the car.

I opened Margaret’s profile again.

There was something I’d missed.

Account opened: three years ago.

I checked the address history.

Same house.

I checked the notes.

There were old Everwell reports from before I even worked there.

Nothing strange.

CLIENT ALERT AND ORIENTED.

NO FALLS REPORTED.

MEDICATIONS TAKEN AS DIRECTED.

CLIENT FEELS SAFE IN HOME.

Three years of normal reports.

According to Everwell, Margaret Harlow had been sitting in that house long before I ever met Evelyn Bell.

I took screenshots.

At the time, I didn’t really know why.

Maybe I just needed proof that the file existed.

Because Mrs. Bell’s didn’t.

That night, I searched Margaret Harlow online.

She was easy to find.

Her husband had died eleven years earlier. I found his obituary. Margaret was listed as his wife.

I found an old church newsletter with a picture of her volunteering at a food drive.

Property records showed that she and her husband had bought the house more than twenty years ago.

There wasn’t a gap.

There was no point where Evelyn Bell could have become Margaret Harlow.

According to everything I could find, Margaret had always existed.

Then I searched Evelyn Bell.

Almost nothing.

I knew I’d seen her missing person alert.

I remembered the picture they used.

Now I couldn’t find it.

The social media posts were gone.

The local pages I remembered sharing it had nothing.

Eventually I found a cached search result.

PLEASE SHARE: MISSING ELDERLY WOMAN.

The page itself wouldn’t load.

That was it.

I sat there staring at my laptop for a while before remembering the police.

Whatever Everwell had done to its own system, there had to be a police report.

I’d spoken to two officers myself.

The next morning, I called the non emergency number.

I told the woman who answered that I had information about a missing elderly woman.

She asked for the name.

“Evelyn Bell.”

I heard her typing.

“Do you know when she was reported missing?”

“About a month ago.”

More typing.

Then she asked me to spell the name.

I did.

She put me on hold.

When she came back, she said there wasn’t an active missing person case under that name.

“What about a closed one?”

“I’m not seeing one.”

“That’s not possible. I was there when the officers came.”

She asked for Mrs. Bell’s address.

I gave it to her.

Another pause.

Then:

“Sir, are you sure you have the correct address?”

“Yes.”

“There hasn’t been a missing person call at that residence this year.”

I didn’t know what to say.

I thanked her and hung up.

That was the point where I stopped believing this was some kind of database mistake.

Somehow, Evelyn Bell had been removed.

Not only from Everwell.

From everything.

And Margaret Harlow had apparently been there the entire time.

I still went to work.

I know.

That’s the part where everybody says they would’ve quit.

Maybe you would have.

I thought about it.

But what exactly was I supposed to tell someone?

A missing woman showed up under another name with decades of records proving she was that other person?

The police apparently didn’t even remember her being missing.

And Everwell was the only place that seemed to know something was wrong.

So I stayed.

But I stopped trusting the app.

I bought a notebook.

Every client went into it.

Name.

Address.

Date.

Anything unusual.

I started taking screenshots of my route every morning.

At first, nothing happened.

For almost two weeks, everything was completely normal.

Then I began noticing little things.

A client I’d visited every Wednesday vanished from my schedule without any closure notice.

Another client’s emergency contact changed overnight.

Once, the photo attached to a client profile showed a woman I’d never seen before.

I refreshed the app.

The right picture came back.

I started comparing old screenshots to current files.

Most matched.

Some didn’t.

Birth dates shifted.

Names changed slightly.

One address belonged to a man I knew I had visited, except the app now showed I’d never been assigned there.

None of it was dramatic enough to prove anything by itself.

Together, it started to feel deliberate.

Then I checked the names attached to Margaret Harlow’s older visit reports.

Six different Everwell employees had checked on her before me.

I didn’t recognize any of them.

That wasn’t impossible.

Everwell had a lot of employees.

But when I searched our company directory, none of the names existed.

I asked one of the dispatchers if she knew a guy named Aaron Pike.

She shook her head.

“What about Melissa Crane?”

“No.”

“Derek Vaughn?”

She gave me a strange look.

“Why?”

“No reason.”

That night I checked every one of Margaret’s old reports again.

Six employees.

All gone.

Not listed as former staff.

Not inactive.

Gone.

The next morning, Kent called me before my first visit.

He didn’t say hello.

“You’ve been looking through old accounts.”

It wasn’t a question.

“I was checking something.”

“Why?”

“Because things keep changing.”

“Things change.”

“Employee records?”

Silence.

Then he said:

“Do your job, Dominic.”

“I am.”

“No. You’re looking for problems that aren’t yours.”

“What happened to Evelyn Bell?”

Another pause.

Longer this time.

Then Kent said:

“You need to stop asking that question.”

And hung up.

I should’ve listened.

Instead, I became obsessed.

I started looking through everything I could access.

Old visits.

Closed accounts.

Archived routes.

I didn’t have administrator permissions, but Everwell’s system was old and badly designed.

If you had a client number, sometimes you could change one digit and land on another account.

Most were normal.

Some weren’t.

I found one account with visit reports dated years before the client was born.

One woman had three different dates of birth.

A man had been marked deceased twice.

Another had two active profiles at two different addresses.

Whenever I went back later, those records were gone.

Then I found something that scared me more than Mrs. Bell.

My own employee record.

I wasn’t looking for it.

I typed the wrong identification number into an internal search and my name came up.

Dominic.

Residential Wellness Representative.

But the hire date was wrong.

It said I’d started almost three years ago.

I stared at it.

I knew when I started.

Seven months ago.

I remembered the interview.

The orientation.

The first week.

I refreshed the page.

The record disappeared.

I tried my employee number again.

This time my normal profile loaded.

Hire date:

Seven months ago.

I didn’t sleep much that night.

The next morning, I almost called in.

Then Everwell sent me a priority check.

Daniel Mercer.

Seventy nine years old.

The address looked familiar.

I didn’t realize why until I pulled into the apartment complex.

Leonard Shaw.

The man I’d found dead six months earlier.

Same building.

Same apartment.

I sat in the parking lot for a long time.

There were reasonable explanations.

Somebody else had moved in.

Everwell had another client in the same unit.

Coincidence.

I knocked.

Leonard Shaw opened the door.

I couldn’t move.

He looked exactly like I remembered him.

Not the way he looked when I found him dead.

The way he looked before.

“Everwell?” he asked.

My mouth had gone dry.

“Yeah.”

He frowned.

“You planning on standing out there?”

I followed him inside.

My app said Daniel Mercer.

The man in front of me was Leonard Shaw.

I knew that as certainly as I knew my own name.

I started the assessment.

“Can you confirm your full name?”

“Daniel Mercer.”

“Date of birth?”

He answered.

It matched.

“Any falls since your last visit?”

“No.”

“Medication taken?”

“Yes.”

Everything normal.

I looked over at the recliner.

The same one.

That was where I’d found him.

He noticed.

“You okay?”

“Do you know me?”

“No.”

“I’ve been here before.”

“So?”

“You were called Leonard Shaw.”

He stopped looking at the television.

His eyes moved to me.

“You should finish your check.”

“I found you dead.”

His jaw tightened.

“Stop.”

“I watched paramedics come in here.”

“I said stop.”

“You were dead.”

He looked toward the front door.

Then back at me.

“How long have you worked for them?”

The question caught me off guard.

“Seven months.”

He stared at me.

“Seven?”

“Yeah.”

Something about that answer terrified him.

He stood.

“No.”

“What?”

“They should’ve caught that.”

“Who?”

He rubbed a hand across his face.

Then looked at me.

“You remember Evelyn?”

I felt my stomach drop.

“What did you just say?”

“Mrs. Bell.”

I stood too.

“You know her?”

“I knew you’d remember her.”

“What happened to her?”

“Nothing happened to her.”

“She disappeared.”

“No.”

“Then who the hell is Margaret Harlow?”

He hesitated.

“Also her.”

“That doesn’t make any sense.”

“I know.”

“How can they both be her?”

“They aren’t.”

“Then explain it.”

“I can’t.”

“Can’t or won’t?”

“They don’t leave all of it.”

I didn’t understand.

“Who doesn’t?”

He looked at my phone.

“Everwell.”

My throat felt tight.

“What is Everwell?”

“You really don’t know.”

“No.”

“Have you ever wondered why they make you ask the same questions?”

“They’re welfare checks.”

“No.”

“Yes, they are.”

“They aren’t checking us.”

I felt something cold move through me.

“What?”

“The questions aren’t for the clients.”

“You.”

“Name. Date. Memory. Routine. Recognition. Whether something feels wrong.”

“It’s what you measure.”

Every person.

Every green checkmark.

“What happens when someone notices?”

He gave me a tired look.

“You leave Everwell.”

“I’ve never seen anyone quit.”

“Exactly.”

Aaron Pike.

Melissa Crane.

Derek Vaughn.

People who had apparently worked there before me and now didn’t exist.

“Most of them stop remembering.”

“That’s not possible.”

“You found me dead.”

“You think you’ve worked there seven months because that’s what they left you with.”

“No.”

Not anger.

Exhaustion.

“People talk. They go to families. Police. Reporters. They start collecting records. They convince other people that something is wrong.”

“Something is wrong.”

“Yes.”

That answer stopped me.

Kent looked at Mrs. Bell again.

“We know.”

For the first time, I realized Everwell might actually be afraid of the same thing I was.

“What happens if people find out?”

Kent was quiet for a moment.

“We don’t know.”

“That’s bullshit.”

“No.”

He looked back at me.

“That is the part that scares us.”

I didn’t say anything.

Kent continued.

“We’ve seen what happens to records. We’ve seen entire families change overnight. We’ve seen people remember husbands they never married and children they never had.”

He paused.

“We don’t know whether the world is changing around these people or whether something is replacing what was already there.”

A cold feeling moved through me.

“And you keep sending people into their houses.”

“We need witnesses.”

That caught me off guard.

“What?”

“Digital records change.”

He tapped his phone.

“Paper records change. Photographs change. Police reports disappear.”

He pointed at me.

“But sometimes people don’t.”

I looked down at the notebook.

Kent nodded.

“That’s why Everwell exists.”

The welfare checks.

The same clients.

The same questions.

The constant visits.

“You’re using us.”

“Yes.”

“As what?”

“Something the changes can’t always rewrite.”

I thought about Mrs. Bell asking me if I’d ever been sent to the same person twice.

“You knew.”

She looked at me.

“Not all of it.”

“You remembered me.”

“Pieces.”

“When?”

“Before you remembered me.”

Kent looked irritated.

“Evelyn.”

She ignored him.

“You’ve checked on me before.”

My chest tightened.

“As Mrs. Bell?”

She shook her head.

“No.”

I stared at her.

“How many times?”

“I don’t know.”

“Margaret?”

“Sometimes.”

“What does that mean?”

Her eyes filled with something I couldn’t read.

“I don’t always remember being the other ones.”

The room went quiet.

Then something clicked.

“The old employees.”

Kent looked at me.

“Aaron Pike. Melissa Crane. Derek Vaughn.”

His expression told me enough.

“They remembered too.”

“Yes.”

“And you reset them.”

“Yes.”

“Where are they?”

Kent didn’t answer.

That was worse than anything he could have said.

“What happens during a reset?”

“You lose what you saw.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“You lose enough.”

“Enough for what?”

“To go back to work.”

I stared at him.

Then at the notebook.

Three years.

However many resets.

“How many times have you done it to me?”

Kent looked down at his phone.

I expected him to avoid the question.

He didn’t.

“Five.”

I felt sick.

“This would be six.”

Mrs. Bell closed her eyes.

I looked at her.

“That’s why you asked me.”

Her eyes opened.

“What?”

“The same person twice.”

She stared at me.

Then slowly nodded.

“I wanted to know how much you remembered.”

“And I didn’t.”

“No.”

“But you did.”

“A little.”

Kent stood.

“That’s enough.”

I backed away.

“No.”

“Dominic.”

“You’re not doing it again.”

“You said that last time.”

My stomach dropped.

Kent took one step toward me.

“And the time before that.”

I grabbed the notebook.

“I’ll go to the police.”

“You already did.”

I stopped.

“What?”

“The second time.”

He pointed toward the notebook.

“You wrote everything down. Names. Addresses. Dates. You went to the police.”

“What happened?”

“By the time they looked into it, none of the people you reported existed under the names you gave them.”

I thought about Evelyn Bell’s missing person report.

Gone.

“You went to a newspaper after that.”

I couldn’t speak.

“You contacted families the next time.”

“Stop.”

“You tried quitting once.”

“I said stop.”

Kent did.

The room was silent.

Then Mrs. Bell spoke.

“He deserves to know.”

Kent turned toward her.

“He’s known before.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

“It never changes anything.”

Mrs. Bell looked at me.

There was something almost apologetic in her face.

“You keep finding your way back.”

I looked at the notebook.

Maybe that was why I’d written everything down.

Not because I thought I could expose Everwell.

Because some part of me already knew I wouldn’t remember.

I opened to the final page.

DO NOT ALLOW SUBJECT TO COMPLETE EIGHTH MONTH.

“What happens at eight months?”

Kent didn’t answer.

I looked up.

“What happens?”

Mrs. Bell answered instead.

“You stop coming back right.”

Kent’s head snapped toward her.

“Evelyn.”

“What does that mean?”

She took a step into the apartment.

“Every reset takes something.”

Kent stood between us.

“Enough.”

“No.”

She looked directly at me.

“They think they’re removing the memories.”

My heart started pounding.

“They aren’t.”

Kent grabbed her arm.

Mrs. Bell pulled away.

“They’re removing you.”

Kent shouted her name.

She shouted over him.

“That’s why the others disappeared.”

I looked at Kent.

Aaron Pike.

Melissa Crane.

Derek Vaughn.

Six workers who no longer existed in the directory.

“What happened to them?”

Kent said nothing.

Mrs. Bell did.

“They ran out.”

The room went completely still.

I looked at the notebook again.

SEVEN MONTH RETENTION. ABNORMAL.

DO NOT ALLOW SUBJECT TO COMPLETE EIGHTH MONTH.

It wasn’t a warning for Everwell.

It was a warning for me.

Written by me.

I backed toward the kitchen.

Kent moved after me.

“Dominic, listen to me.”

“No.”

“You don’t understand what happens if we leave those memories in place.”

“And you don’t understand what happens if you keep taking them.”

His expression changed.

Just slightly.

Enough.

He knew she was right.

Maybe Everwell had never been resetting people.

Maybe it had been shaving pieces off them and calling whatever remained successful.

I looked at Mrs. Bell.

“Why are you helping me?”

She gave me a sad smile.

“Because you helped me once.”

I didn’t remember that.

Maybe I never would.

Kent looked toward the hallway.

Then back at me.

“You cannot keep what you know.”

“Watch me.”

I grabbed my keys.

Kent stepped in front of the door.

“You’ll come back.”

“Maybe.”

“You always do.”

I looked at Mrs. Bell.

She stepped aside.

Kent didn’t.

Then Mrs. Bell said quietly:

“Ask him.”

Kent froze.

I knew exactly what she meant.

I looked at him.

“Do you feel safe remaining in your home?”

Kent’s face went blank.

For the first time since I’d met him, he looked genuinely afraid.

Mrs. Bell smiled.

Kent turned toward her.

“What did you do?”

She didn’t answer.

Something buzzed in his hand.

His Everwell phone.

Kent looked down.

Whatever he saw on the screen drained the color from his face.

I didn’t wait to see what it was.

I shoved past him and ran.

Mrs. Bell didn’t follow me.

Neither did Kent.

I made it downstairs and into my car.

I drove until I was almost out of gas.

That was three hours ago.

The Everwell phone is still on my kitchen table.

I left the notebook behind too.

I know that was stupid.

But I remember the important parts.

For now.

Evelyn Bell.

Margaret Harlow.

Leonard Shaw.

Daniel Mercer.

Five resets.

Three years.

I keep repeating them because I’m scared that if I stop, one of the names will disappear.

There is one thing I brought with me.

A piece of paper Mrs. Bell slipped into my hand when I pushed past Kent.

I didn’t notice it until I stopped driving.

There’s an address written on it.

Underneath it are four words.

YOU CHECKED HERE FIRST.

I know the address.

I’ve known it my entire life.

It’s my mother’s house.
