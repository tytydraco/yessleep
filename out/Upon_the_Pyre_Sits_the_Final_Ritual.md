I’ve always hated my father. I think that’s because I knew he hated me. Despite that conversation being our last, and worst, I still can’t stop looking back on it. What his last words to me might have meant. I knew he wanted me to die by the end of this, that was evident enough, but I don’t know why he wanted me to fight against my fate. So much of what happened during this part of my life remains a mystery. Somehow, I’d like it to stay that way. 

The next three rituals would be done while March and Lillian were still away. I couldn’t have been more grateful for that. I didn’t want them to see me like this. Wounded deeply by my father and spurned by my lover. Hobbling around the house on clunky crutches. 

I remember the first time I had broken my leg in baseball. Lillian was the first to sign my cast. She had always been a good artist so she drew a cat beside her name and covered my crutches in tinsel. Chales and I had gotten acquainted during middle school summer camp, but Lillian and I had been friends since we were toddlers. We played with dolls together every afternoon, watched the Flintstones every Saturday, and stayed up late stargazing. She was like the sister I never had. When the boys from the baseball team made fun of me for drawing angels in the dugout sand, Lillian would be the first to defend me from the fence. She was the one who made the case to my father for me to join the theater instead of being abysmal at baseball.  She understood me. She never judged when I asked to use her makeup. Never got mad when I spoke too softly. I could never love her the way she wanted, the prince she had in her mind, but somehow we were what each other needed.

Even after we got married we always had our saying;

“Never steady without my Eddie…”

“Always silly with my Lilly.” 

The nightmares started getting worse then, when I was alone. I couldn’t look upon the face of Lillian, the woman who was my dearest friend, and March, the girl who gave me hope. Hope that maybe her generation could fight against the horridness of the world, better than a coward like me could. Both served as a warmth I no longer had. The house was cold and dark. The dreams were full of screams and blood. Despite the pain both caused me, I knew it was what I deserved. 

By the time of the next ritual, I was able to walk - albeit slowly- without the help of my crutches. This was due in no small part to pushing myself each day to try and use them less and less, knowing that no matter what the group would take them away. The pain medications certainly helped as well. When I entered the threshold of the infernal monument the revelers were everpresent cackling hyenas. Strutting about with confidence, green with money. I had learned to look at the floor, never look any of them in the eye. A glance was just enough to entice them. But for whatever reason, that night I decided to be aware of my surroundings as the butler led me to the changing room. I saw the lobby area full of older men, younger men, men of middle age, men I chose to try not to recognize, women clinging to arms, shoulders, backs. My chest tightened, my trachea felt choked. When I started making eye contact with the revelers, my eyes darted away in panic. Just in time to see a small hand grasping that of a tall old man. 

Walking only a few paces from me was an elderly man, thin as a rake and his skin clinging to his ribs, guiding a small girl in shackles. She couldn’t have been older than seven. Her hair was a stark auburn, just like March’s. She looked so afraid. He looked hungry. I saw red and my body acted before my mind could even keep up. I pushed past the butler and several other revelers before I got to him, not caring how much pain radiated from my feet. I tackled the bag of bones to the ground with ease, bringing my fist down on his face –Over and over, harder and harder– until I was pulled away. The militia men surrounded me but I was so enveloped in rage that I screamed and thrashed with the strength of a madmen, knocking enough of them down to continue beating the man until my knuckles were coated with warm blood. 

“Run!!” I shouted at the girl, “Get out of here please!!” 

She stared back at me as I was once again pulled back by the militia men. Her eyes welled with tears but there was enough recognition between us that she did. She ran through the shocked crowd, now silent, disappearing through the sea of people. I smiled, tears streaming down my face stained pink with drops of blood. Maybe she could make it out of here. Maybe it was just early enough in the ritual for her to escape. I looked down at the monster I had beaten. He was assuredly dead, his face almost beaten into a pulp and his skull nearly caved in. I began to laugh as the militia man dragged me away, loud and piercing through the somber silence of the crowd. I was vindicated to be the only one laughing now. 

When I was brought to the mirrored room the militia men beat me within an inch of my life, but I didn’t care. I may have saved that girl's life. From that man. From the revellers. From whatever else they had planned for her. I should have known that was a stupid hope to have, a selfish platitude to convince myself I had finally done something to help. Oh how wrong I was. I got dressed gingerly when they had their fill, making my way to the ritual chamber. I stood in my usual spot, the bruises and assuredly fractured bones starting to bloom with a dull ache. The leader paced to stand beside me not long after. 

“Welcome my brothers and sisters,” the Leader announced with a boom, “Today we had a bit of a change of plans with our sacrifice…”

There was a delighted murmur from the crowd and I tensed up. 

“The ritual doesn’t usually call for a devotion of this nature, but I have received a vision from our glorious gods, that our supplication should be actualized in the death of an innocent.”

It was hard to see through the crowd at first, but gradually the path between people was made clear enough to see the militia men. Then the small frame of the girl they were guiding through the crowd. It was her. The trembling, terrified, and teary-eyed girl I naively thought I had saved. Instead, I had sent her to her doom. 

I’m sorry, my hands keep shaking. More than what would delineate my age. I’m stopping, I’m not writing what happens next. I suppose my body can’t take this, seeing her face without skin again. She’s sitting across the table from me now. She’s crying, somehow, down her slick red muscles contorting with each sob. The therapist says she’s a symptom of my severe PTSD but I’m not fully convinced she isn’t a ghost. I’ve seen the devil, I have looked him in his one horrible eye before I gouged it out, so a ghost is not hard to believe in. 

Suffice it to say, I cried the most I had ever in my lifetime that night. My body didn’t stop shaking, not even when the marajuana smoke entered my brain to numb it. Not even when a needle entered my skin for the first time. I was angry, terrified, sickened at the man I had become. At the man these people had made me. This was my last straw, one that should have come a long time ago. 

The next morning I stumbled into the police station on my crutches, smelling of rancidity and vomit. I must have looked as bad as I ever had been at the time, but knowing me I probably looked worse after what was to come. My limbs felt light and my stomach clung to the back of my spine. 

I told the police everything. Ranting and raving in agony with desperate pleas for help. Their lights fractaled and spun like solar collisions in orbit. The world had begun to feel that way, surreal. Maybe it was the drugs, maybe not. I could see them looking down at the fresh needle pricks in my veins with skepticism and I remember being surprised that they had even taken a statement from me. I looked the most obvious a drug addict could look, but I suppose somehow they recognized me because of my involvement in the church. After all, we were a relatively small community, with only my fathers church as a place of worship for most. The police escorted me outside gently enough, with looks that screamed pity and bit with contempt. I was in shock for a very long time after that, the anger came much later. 

Specifically when I drove to my fathers house and smashed in his car windows. The nice car, the one he bought just to show off with a shining paint job that cast rainbows of choir singing in every direction under the sunlight. A monument to his southern charm, so tainted by blood and guts drive to the anxiously awaiting gullet of hell. The money he had used for it, practically dripping in viscera. As I brought the crowbar down on his windows the glass fluttered in butterfly wings and the flock of birds spiraled into tree leaves. I hated him. I hated the world. I wanted to hurt the way I had been hurt. I wanted to rip, and tear, and cut something the world found dear so deeply that it could never hold the hearth of laughter deep to its chest again. So that it could never feel the warmth, and see the hellfire that warmth was built upon. But I was just a man. And all I could do was wail with all of the water enveloping me, cold in the bathtub. Under the water that held millions and millions of floating, dead, children. Glowing like pixies.  

I heard the phone ring like it was oceans away, as I sat naked on the bathroom floor. I could see the waves of sound unraveling in front of my eyes like a distorted lightshow. The stage lights clouded with hairspray and sweat. Charles looked at me from the crowd where no one else mattered but him. My father? The Leader? The police? It all seemed so far below me. As if I was floating on the tightrope of heaven, just itching to step into thin air. It rang a second time, the dial tone like fireworks I used to see on the fourth of July. The sparklers Lillian and I used to chase around in the night while hot dog smoke drifted over from the grill. Tinkling laughter of my childhood, the one I wish I could dive back into. I finally answered it when it rang the fourth time. To my surprise it wasn’t anyone I had dreaded. It was the county hospital. 

Lillian and March had gotten into an accident. Lillian had died en route to the hospital and March was in unstable condition. The police thought it was a car bomb but lots of that didn’t add up. The car had caught fire from the cabin spontaneously, the air heating in a matter of moments, Lillian was one of the first things to burn, her flesh had melted almost to the bone. The police suspected me at first, due to my prior state at the station. However, they swiftly dropped hat line of reasoning as they found the car eerily intact save for a few scorch marks on the seats. Almost as if a contained ball of flames had oscillated for long enough to catch Lillian and March alight, and then vanished. Nothing like a misplaced cigarette or handheld explosive. March told me later that somehow Lillian knew to push March out of the car before it went up in flames. That was the only way March had survived with a scarred left torso and a broken leg. Lillian had saved her. And I could never thank her for that. I tried. I’ve tried so many times. But she’s six feet under the ground. In the dirt where the worms can tell her all the things I never got to.

All I ever did for her in life was be untrue and now my best friend was gone because of me. I had killed her. I had killed her for the sake of my survival. I sat at her grave for days, kneeling my ear down to the fresh soil, waiting to hear her say the thing we always would. Waiting for her to start the phrase. But it was always me finishing it in the humid air.

“Always silly with my Lilly.”

“Always silly with my Lilly.”

“Always silly with my Lilly.”

Shortly after her funeral, Charles and I’s relationship went public. I was disowned by my father in a sermon and Lillian’s mother took custody of March, telling me to never try contacting them again. I was alone. Truly and utterly alone. But loneliness was not what I deserved. Not truly. No. I knew what I needed. What I had been craving for a long time now. 

Do you know what free falling feels like? The air brushing past your face and slowly filtering out of your lungs? I fell about eighty feet and closed my eyes right before I hit the ground. But when I opened them I was back on the rooftop. I blinked several times in confusion, looking to the ground, where I was supposed to be mangled and broken, and then up to the sky. I jumped again, feeling my stomach rise to my cheeks before instinctively closing my eyes again as I reached the ground. Instead of death, I smelled the oncoming storm and cried out in frustration. I gritted my teeth before jumping head first this time, making sure to not close my eyes. In that split second of darkness as I met the ground, I felt myself floating in a vast void, before being yanked upward. I was on the roof again. 

I won’t go into the gritty details but I did try a lot of other methods that rainy February day. None of them worked. I thought I got close with the more penetrating approaches, I felt the pain of it all and the fading out of consciousness, but again, the void spun my gravity into life as I reached the brink of death. I couldn’t die. Not until they wanted me to. 

Which was sooner than I had anticipated. When killing myself didn’t work I decided to venture heavier into my vices. I was drunk most of the time and high when I wasn’t. More often than not it got to the point where I became sick. Vomiting the yellow bile of my empty stomach before the alcohol could even reach my system. I didn’t want to see anyone and yet I wanted to feel disgusted with myself. I wanted people to cringe at my filth. It felt like a little death, microdosing it without having to use a needle. Most of my extreme benders were contained to the house, and on one of them, drinking a cold Miller and watching *Singing in the Rain*, the phone rang. 

“Fuck you bastards,” I slurred. 

“Leviticus.”

The phone clicked. They hadn’t even given me the address. They knew I would be there. 

From the moment I stepped into the infernal house, I knew everything was different. The revelers were no longer hedonistically jaunty –prancing about– instead they were cold and still. In long robes that covered their bodies completely, like the sheet they put over my wife in the morgue. Almost as if to mock me. They filed me into the mirrored room, watched me unabashedly as I got changed into nothing more than my aging skin. The only one not amongst their ranks was the Leader. He walked backwards, smiling at me in all of his uncanny glory, as he guided me through the estate. All of the rooms that were once decorated with gaudy colors had white sheets covering everything like curtains before a grand performance. 

Even the ritual area had been completely gutted of everything. The goliath ballroom now stood empty of exclamation, the only sound a cacophony of padded footsteps. It reminded me of a millipede. The sound made me shiver without even looking over my shoulder. The only thing that wasn’t covered within the manor was the Minotaur. Now that I was able to wander somewhat freely, I could see the Minotaur in its entirety. The bull face was calm, placid as a river, while its muscular human body was holding the world in one hand and a knife in the other. Carved out of obsidian, it shone in the early morning light. 

The leader led me outside to a large pyre. And when I say large I don’t mean your standard witch burning pyre, I mean a pile of sticks and kindle almost two cars high. Even the grass lawn, that must have been miles wide, seemed to dwarf it. It was a clear day, blue sky starting to creep into the heather dawn. Suicidality was a veritable emotion in me, overrunning all fear. Not even when the militia men started trekking me up the pyre to its zenith. I was just tired. Burning would be painful but I felt such guilt for Lillian that I found the irony an even more intense agony. 

“My family, my world,” the Leader began, “you who have seen the light with me and not looked away. The burning soul of this divine veil. We can grasp it my friends. We have felt its fur but now we must brace its mane. With this final sacrifice, we shall ride the waves of infernal fire to immortality.” 

The Leader looked up to me as I finally reached the post of the pyre. I saw an emotion that took me many moments to decipher, glistening in his eyes. 

“And it was all thanks to you, Edward. Your lovely mother birthed you for this moment. She died for this moment. You will die for all of us. We will live on because of you.”

It was pity. Through his malicious grin he was pitying me. I felt a surge of anger flood through me. This man, this monster, this leader of evil, this putrid stench. The man who had violated me, my control, my family. He had no right to pity me. He had no right to thank me. He had no right to mock me. 

I practically lunged down the pyre, pushing the two militia men down the pyre, to the hard ground. I tackled the Leader down, bringing my fists in on his face again and again. I didn’t care about killing myself anymore, I only cared about killing him. He laughed, delighted at my fury. Moving quickly, I grabbed a sharp branch from the pyre, slamming it down into the Leader's chest. Followers began to try and pull me off him then since the militia men must have still been recovering. I snarled and bit at them, slashing them with the bloodied stake. They all backed away in fear, eyes darting down to their white robes. I focused my attention on the Leader once more, driving the stake deep into his heart. His laughs had become bloody coughs, spittling the red onto my face. I kept pushing so I could make sure it had broken through his back half. Then I tore at his face, my thumb popping into his eye socket and pushing the cornea out onto the grass. That's when he screamed. Even so, I couldn’t stand the delight in his grin, still plastered on his face. I lifted his head up off the ground, holding it tight to my chest and squeezed. Pulling until I heard his neck snap. 

The air went quiet then. Stale and cold on an otherwise pleasant day. The followers who once were crowded around me, trying to paw at my frame in an effort to save their leader, were now backed away as stone statues. A circular mass surrounding me. I felt my heart drop. Stumbling off the leader, I stood looking at all of their faces. Their eyes were glassy and all of their pupils were enlarged, staring at me. I thought they would attack me, claw me apart with the sheer mass of them. Inching past the side of the pyre to the treeline, I still was convinced of that. Until I heard a man scream in agony. I blinked in confusion, trying to see through the crowd toward the source, but all I could see was a warm light erupt through the robes of white. Then more of them began to scream, the ones nearest to me, going up in flames. Not just burning the flesh, completely eviscerating the body. I heard what must have been a hundred exclamations of fear, confusion, and anger. All centered around me. That's when I started to run. 

Through the forest, into the brush. Down a hill that I almost fell hard on. The followers had chased me through the heavy overgrowth, the light of their bodies flickering through the arbor structures. The faster that I ran, the more of the followers disappeared, burning out from the sheer wind generated from their movement. Still, I didn’t stop. My legs did not stop moving as fast as they could, until I reached the river. I didn’t know it was there, I was so focused on not burning that the water completely shocked my system as I fell into it. I broke the surface of the water, scrambling away from the shore and heaving deep breaths into the air. The followers in hot pursuit of me evaporated as soon as they tried to reach me. Out of habit, none of them exercised caution, instead, one after the other, the followers who hadn’t been snuffed, flickered out.  

I watched them all. In their ego and stupidity. Each believing they could survive the water for long enough to get to me. Perhaps what little brains they had left were cinders. I screamed. For the first time, it wasn’t out of fear. It was a confusing emotion. Almost like exaltation but I don’t think it was entirely that. Rage perhaps. Whatever it was, it felt good. Even better as I leaned back into the river and washed away the blood staining me. Letting my muscles relax. Letting myself go with the flow of the gentle waters. 

When I was found, the poor couple who spotted me thought I was dead. When the police carried me out of the water and I started thrashing instinctively, I must have given them quite a shock. It was obvious to them that I had been through some sort of traumatic event, despite my refusal to tell them. I didn’t act rationally while I was in their custody, so much so they took me to the nearest mental asylum to begin my treatment. Luckily, surprisingly, the institution was quite ethical. I was the one who put up most of the fight. I attempted suicide more times then I could count there. Without the drugs and the alcohol, I was left with my thoughts. And my nightmares. I couldn’t take it. I thought I would be left there forever but by some turn of fate, the institution became overcrowded only five months after I was institutionalized. Being low risk at that point, mostly because I was exhausted from all the medication they were pumping me with, they let me go to accommodate more patients. With enough medication and prescription to last me the rest of my lifetime, of course. 

My house was still where they drove me to. Rotting from the inside out. I tried living in it, but it was too much. It was like living inside the corpse of a whale. Warm, safe, but so full of death I could hardly eat with the stench. Every night brought me back. Every day I peered out of my window waiting for an army of flames to come and take me to hell. Though, judging by the fact my father had disappeared suddenly on that fateful day, leaving only scorchmarks on his bed and an open window, I gradually came to terms with the fact that the group was gone. However, the community continued to spit at me. I would get scowls at the grocery store and my car would constantly be hit by both hood and bat alike. I couldn’t get a job, not like I needed it with the small fortune I had amassed. Some part of me wanted to burn that money but then I remembered March’s college fund and thought better of it. I still couldn’t get visitation rights with her. Especially after my time in the psych ward. I think that’s what finally prompted me to move not a year later. I sent an envelope of money to my mother in law for March and set out for California. 

I bought a nice enough house near the desert, isolated enough to retreat if I ever needed to. That was when I started getting into heavy drugs. More people around meant more dealers and more dealers meant more supply. I was able to get a job to fund this, though not one that I enjoyed. Though, I suppose being a janitor at a gas station has its perks. Lovers came. Then went. Mostly due to my temperament and avid cocaine use. Meth was never my cup of tea, but cocaine could blot out the guilt, the fear, and the shame until it was all just a white hot star behind my eye. I almost overdosed on multiple occasions but somehow I always came back. The doctors could never explain it. But I sure as hell could. No matter how much I wrecked my body, I could never get it to shut down. Not with all the club lights and sleazy hookups I could muster. None of it ever brought light into the void.

It was March who helped book me into rehab. Once she had turned 18 she came to find me. It was simple enough as she got a full ride to UCLA and came to return the money to me. I felt terrible for having her take me in her car to the center but she insisted. I don’t know how she could bear to see me after all this time. After all I had done. She sent me letters while I was in recovery. She told me how she was doing. Her major in school, the flowers she took Lillian every weekend, and the horrible lies Lillian's mother had told her about me. Some of those lies were true, I had to establish, but I tried to clear my name as much as I could. I knew she didn’t have to forgive me for leaving her, for not fighting hard enough for her, but somehow she understood. And when I finally worked up the courage to tell her my story, she believed me.

My daughter is my light. I would not be alive right now if it weren’t for her. March is a successful reporter now, writing for the LA Times. She has a husband and a beautiful family. Lillian would be so proud of her. I see her face in my grandkids every day. I tell them stories about her and how brave she was.

As for Charles, when I was around 45 years old, he reached out. Somehow he had found my email address through my job working managerial at a printing press. Must have done some hard looking for it. I thought he would never want to speak with me again, but he wanted to meet back in Florida. Back where it all started. I agreed, hesitantly, asking March if she would like to take a trip down there with the family. Come spring, I was sitting on a park bench outside of the Everglades, telling Charles everything. Honestly, I expected him to leave when I got to the part about the drugs, but he stayed. He listened. And somehow I was lucky enough to earn another believer in him. And, slowly, something more.

We got married 2 years ago. We live a peaceful life with our cats Dolly and Pickles. A life where the horrors still haunt me, but it's easier to see through their darkness. It’s easier to know a light without fire.
