*Throwaway. I rented a room on Alder Court in October. I'm on night four, and I'm running out of reasons to call this a coincidence. If you've lived on a street that does this, please message me.*

I'm not superstitious. I've lived in eleven apartments in nine years, and the strangest thing in any of them was the previous tenant's mail. So understand that what made me take the room wasn't a feeling. It was arithmetic.

$400 a month, utilities included, in a city where I'd just been quoted $1,100 for a closet facing a dumpster. The listing was four lines:

*Upstairs room. Quiet dead-end street. Elderly landlady. No guests during Nine Nights.*

I read the last line the way you read terms and conditions. Some local festival. For $400, I'd have respected anything.

Alder Court was twelve narrow houses in two rows, all with the same sad siding and deep porches, and big maples dropping perfect yellow circles onto every lawn. Eleven had lights on. The twelfth sat dark at the end, curtains drawn, and on its porch a small shape had been turned to face the wall.

I didn't ask about that. It's the thing I keep coming back to. I didn't ask.

Mrs. Pell met me at the door in an oatmeal cardigan. Seventy-five, maybe, no taller than five feet, with the small sharp eyes of a bird that has noticed you. She took my hand in both of hers.

"You're early," she said. "Good. People who are early are *listeners*."

On her porch, beside the door, sat a figure about knee-high: a seated woman made of clay, wrapped in real cotton cloth, hands folded in her lap, a red thread tied around one wrist. Where the face should have been, there was nothing. A smooth blank oval, like a thumb pressed into wet clay and drawn away. I looked down the street. Every porch had one, each wrapped in a different colour.

"Who is she?"

"The Lady," Mrs. Pell said, as if that covered it. "Every house keeps one. During Nine Nights she speaks, and we listen. That's all. The only rule is you don't argue with her."

I laughed. She smiled and didn't.

The first night I lay in bed with the window open an inch, the street so quiet I could hear leaves landing. Around eleven, a voice came up from the porch. A whisper, dry and unhurried, barely louder than the leaves. A woman's voice, I thought, or something that had learned from one.

"He will eat a second helping at dinner tomorrow."  
I waited for the rest of it. There wasn't any. After a minute I decided it was a TV in someone's house and slept fine.

At dinner the next evening, with Mrs. Pell's neighbours around her long table, she lifted the serving spoon and said, "More?" And I thought of the whisper and felt a petty, contrary need to be a free man in a free kitchen.  
"I'm good, thanks."

Nobody spoke. Mrs. Brandt's fork stopped halfway to her mouth. Okafor, a huge gentle retired bus mechanic, set his down so quietly it was like putting something to bed. And Mrs. Pell's face didn't go angry. It went *afraid*. It was the look of someone watching a toddler walk toward a stove.

"Please," Okafor said softly. "Just take the second helping."

"It's rice, it's —"

"Please."

I took it. I ate every grain. The table breathed out together, and the talk started up again, too bright, like a room after a false fire alarm.

Afterwards I stood on the porch with Mrs. Pell and looked at the dark house at the end.

"Who lives there?"

"It's between tenants."

"For how long?"

She patted my arm. "Since the last time someone argued."

On night two the Lady said: "He will drop a glass at ten past four."

I'm not stupid. I spent the whole afternoon not holding glass. I drank from the tap with my cupped hands like a raccoon. At four o'clock I sat on my bed, hands in my lap, no glass within twenty feet, feeling extremely clever.  
At 4:09 Mrs. Pell knocked, holding a tray. "I made tea. In the good china." I stood to take the cup, and it left my fingers like it had been pulled. It hit the floor and broke across the boards. The clock on my phone read 4:10.  
She looked at the pieces and said, very gently, "Thank you for not fighting."

Fighting. As though there were anything to fight.  
On night three the Lady said: "He will tell Mrs. Brandt why he left home."

I haven't told anyone why I left home in six years. The next evening I was on the Brandts' porch with a glass of their wine, and Mrs. Brandt asked in the friendliest way how I'd ended up in the city, and the answer came out of me whole. No decision. Like a sneeze. All of it, the real version.

She nodded slowly, as though I'd confirmed something she'd already written down.

Last night was the fourth night, and I was ready. I'd stopped sleeping. I sat at the window with the lights off and heard the voice come up the porch steps and through the floor, close, like it was speaking inside the walls.

"He will open the third door tomorrow."

My room has two doors. The bedroom door and the closet. I measured. I counted. I photographed every wall. At six this morning, when the light came up grey, I checked again.

At the far end, under the slope of the ceiling, where I swear to God there was plaster, there's a narrow door. Painted the same white as the wall, flush, no hinges showing, a small brass handle gone dull from hands.  
I haven't touched it. I'm sitting on the bed with my back against the headboard and my phone in my hand, typing this, and I'd like it on the record that I'm not going near it.

Downstairs, the Lady has been repeating the same sentence for an hour. I can hear it through the floorboards, patient as a clock.

"He will open the third door."

Mrs. Pell just called up the stairs. She's made breakfast. She says there's no rush.

But the sentence just changed.

"He is already holding the handle."

I'm writing this with my left hand, because my right is cold.

I don't remember crossing the room.
