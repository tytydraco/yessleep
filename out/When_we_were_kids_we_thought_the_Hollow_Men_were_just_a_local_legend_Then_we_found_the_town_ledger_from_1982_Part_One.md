If you grew up anywhere near eastern Ohio, you know every small town has its monster.

In Blackwood Creek, population 1,800, ours were the Hollow Men.

When we were kids back in the late 2000s, the story was standard campfire lore stuff: tall, impossibly thin figures made of dry corn husks and river silt that walked the perimeter of the timber woods after dark. According to the kids at school, if a Hollow Man tapped three times on your bedroom window, it meant your family owed the town a debt, and you’d wake up with your lungs full of topsoil.

We laughed it off. We used to dare each other to sprint into the treeline past the old water tower at midnight. Standard midwestern rust belt childhood stuff.

Until last weekend.

My family runs the local scrap and cleaning business. The town council recently voted to demolish the old Blackwood Creek Municipal Building a squat, red brick relic from the 1920’s that had been abandoned since the bad flood in ’97. My buddy Nic and I took the contract to clear out the basement archives before the wrecking ball moved in.

Deep in the storage room, behind rusted filing cabinets full of unpaid water bills from the 70’s, Nic kicked over a heavy steel deposit box. The lock was long rotted away. Inside, wrapped in a oilskin jacket, was a thick, leather-bound book with gold foil lettering on the spine:

TOWN OF BLACKWOOD CREEK  OFFICIAL LEDGER (1980–1985)

We figured it was just tax records or old mayor meeting notes. But when I flipped to October 1982, the neat cursive handwriting changed.

The entries weren't about budget allocations or road maintenance. They were organized in a two column grid: Yield on the left, Designation on the right.

Here are a few exact transcriptions from the fall of 1982:

October 12, 1982

Yield: North Ridge Harvest  4,200 Bushels.

Designation: Standard Quota Met.

Note: The low whistle was heard near the silo at 03:00 hours. The perimeter holds.

October 19, 1982

Yield: North Ridge Harvest 1,100 Bushels (Early Frost Loss).

Designation: Deficit incurred (72% short).

Note: Town Council convened at midnight. The ledger demands balance. Subject chosen: Hayes, Michael (Age 9).

My stomach dropped when I read that name. Michael Hayes was my father's older brother.

All my life, I was told Uncle Mikey ran away in the winter of ’82. My grandmother died believing he’d hitched a ride on a freight train out toward Columbus. I kept reading.  
   
October 21, 1982

Designation: Transaction Complete.

Note: Michael Hayes delivered to the tree line at 02:45. The Hollow Men accepted the substitute for the lost grain. Earth was smooth by morning. Next year's soil guaranteed rich.

Every bad harvest year had a name next to it. 1980: Sanger, Clara (Age 82). 1982: Hayes, Michael (Age 9). 1984: Vance, Sarah (Age 17).

They weren't local legends. The town elders weren't just afraid of the Hollow Men they were trading with them.

Nic and I took the ledger back to my truck. We sat in silence for a long time, the engine idling while rain hammered against the windshield.

*"This is crazy," Nic said, his voice shaking. "This is just... some sick joke the old council wrote down to scare each other."*

*"My uncle disappeared that exact week, Nic," I said. "Look at the dates."*

Before Nic could reply, my phone vibrated in my pocket. It was a weather alert for our county: Severe drought conditions warning, crop yield projected 60% below average for the upcoming fall harvest.

I looked up from my phone toward the tree line bordering the scrap lot.

Standing right at the edge of the woods just beyond the beam of my headlights was a figure. It was nearly eight feet tall, unnaturally narrow, its limbs bending at angles no human body could manage. It didn't have a face, just a hollow cavity where the mouth should be, lined with dry, rustling corn husks.

It wasn't moving. It was just waiting.

And then, soft and distinct against the sound of the rain, came three slow taps against my passenger window.

The rain stopped, and there’s a tapping on the window, what the hell am I supposed to do?
