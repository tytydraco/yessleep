The ad didn’t look like it belonged on a normal job board. It was buried on some weird, invite-only domestic staffing forum under a plain text headline: 

*Residential Caretaker/Nanny Needed. Immediate Placement. High Discretion Required.*

The money was stupid. $4,000 a week, room and board included, plus a massive retention bonus after ninety days. I was twenty-four, drowning under my late mom's medical debt, and looking at an orange eviction notice taped to my kitchen counter. I didn't care if it was a scam or worse. I applied within five minutes.

Two hours later, a black town car pulled up to my crumbling Portland apartment. The driver didn’t say a word. He just handed me a cheap prepaid burner phone. It rang immediately. A woman’s voice—cold, clipped, totally accentless—spoke into my ear.

"Your background check is already cleared," she said. "If you accept, the car brings you to the residence tonight. If you decline, step out now."

I looked back at that orange tag on my door. Then I looked at the leather interior. I closed the door, and the driver hit the gas.

We drove south. Left the interstate, hit the state routes, then county lines, and finally drifted onto unmapped logging roads cutting through the absolute chokehold of the Siuslaw National Forest. For three straight hours, the only sound was the wet slap of the wipers and the dull hum of tires in the mud. Outside was just an aggressive wall of blackness swallowing the headlights whole.

The house didn't have a name. No mailbox, no property number, nothing. Just a giant, brutalist concrete bunker from the late sixties buried under ivy and wild ferns. It looked less like architecture and more like a concrete block that had erupted from the dirt. The walls were sweating under the freezing coast drizzle, stained with dark water tracks and green mold.

The parents were waiting in the foyer. David and Eleanor Sterling looked like high-fashion runway models preserved in liquid nitrogen. He was tall, sharp-jawed, with eyes so pale blue they were almost white. She wore this high-necked wool dress with her dark hair pulled back into a bun so tight it looked painful. Neither smiled. Neither offered a hand. They just stood exactly three feet apart, perfectly still, like mannequin displays left in an abandoned museum.

"You are pale," Eleanor said. 

Her voice had this weird, robotic cadence, like someone who learned English by reading a dictionary but never hearing it spoken. She didn't blink. Not once. 

"That is good. The last girl was too... vibrant. It agitated the foundation."

"Where is the kid?" I asked. My voice sounded tiny and pathetic against those soaring concrete walls.

"Leo is resting," David said. His voice was dead flat. 

He wasn’t looking at me; he was staring at the rain lashing the dark windows behind me. 

"He rests often. Your duties do not involve education or entertainment. Your duties are strictly systemic."

Eleanor stepped forward. Her movement was so silent it looked mechanical. She slid a heavy, leather binder into my hands. It was three inches thick, the pages yellowed and packed with dense typewriter text.

"This is the Protocol," she said, her long fingers dragging off the leather. "Memorize it. The house operates on an axis of compliance. If you deviate by even a minute, the consequences are yours."

They left five minutes later. No big goodbye, no checking on their son, nothing. They just walked straight out into the downpour and climbed into an idling SUV that vanished down the gravel driveway. The heavy steel front door clicked shut, and the deadbolts slid into place with a heavy, definitive chunk that sounded exactly like a prison door locking. I was completely alone.

I walked back to the kitchen—a cavern of stainless steel and cold gray stone. The only sound was the low, electric buzz of an industrial fridge. I poured a mug of lukewarm black coffee left in a pot on the counter, sat on a metal stool, and opened the binder.

The first ten pages were just agonizingly specific daily chores. Meal prep calculated down to the exact gram of protein. Leo was only allowed white meats and root vegetables—potatoes, carrots, turnips. 

There was a dark, underlined note: *Nothing grown above ground. No sunlight must touch the food before it is processed.* The cleaning schedules required this specific, foul-smelling antiseptic to be wiped across the counters in clockwise motions only. If you went counter-clockwise, the book warned, the steel would streak and "the scent would fade too quickly."

But on page twelve, the font changed. It went from standard typewriter print to a sharp, jagged, hand-inked script. The rules turned bad.

**Rule 7:** Between 2:00 AM and 3:15 AM, the grandfather clock in the eastern corridor may begin to chime continuously. Do not attempt to stop the pendulum. Do not shine a light into the glass casing. Cover your ears and remain in your quarters. If the chiming stops on an odd number, do not leave your room until dawn.

**Rule 11:** Leo does not have a reflection. If you pass a mirror in the hallway and see a young boy standing behind you in the glass, do not turn around. Do not acknowledge him. Walk briskly to the nearest room with a solid wooden door and lock it. It is not Leo.

**Rule 14:** *The Persian Rug.* The antique carpet in the center of the third-floor nursery is a structural anchor. It must never be folded, moved, or altered. Under no circumstances are you to look beneath the fabric. If you hear a scratching sound from beneath the rug, you must immediately sit in the oak rocking chair placed at the northern corner. You must remain in the chair, rock at a steady rhythm, and sing a lullaby until the scratching transitions into a rhythmic thudding. Only then may you leave.

I sat there in the dark kitchen for a long time, listening to the rain beat against the concrete outside. I tried to laugh. I figured it was an elaborate hazing ritual—just wealthy, isolated freaks taking an inheritance joke too far. Then the plumbing inside the walls started to groan.

It wasn't a normal air-bubble rattle. It sounded like a deep, guttural throat clearing, like someone hacking up wet sand and gravel through the pipes. The vibration traveled up through the floorboards, shook the metal legs of my stool, and crawled right up my spine. I stood up, the coffee cold and heavy in my hand, and decided to find Leo.  
The walk up to the third floor took forever. The house layout was dizzying—just narrow, raw concrete corridors with ceilings so low I felt like I was navigating a military bunker. No paintings, no family photos, nothing. 

Just a single bare bulb hanging from a black wire every twenty feet, throwing shivering shadows ahead of me.

The third floor didn't have any carpet, except for the nursery. The air up there felt different. It didn’t circulate. It was heavy, freezing, and stagnant, smelling like river mud and copper. When I pushed open the heavy oak door to Leo’s room, the temperature dropped so fast my breath plumed into a white cloud.

The nursery was huge but totally empty. No posters, no toys, no signs of life. Just a small iron toddler bed in the far corner, and in the dead center of the room, a massive, stunning Persian rug.

The rug was a deep, bruised crimson. Its geometric patterns were so intricate they seemed to pulse and warp if you stared too long. The wool was thick, but it reeked of iron, cedar, and something sickly sweet—that distinct, suffocating smell of rotting lilies left in old water.

Leo was sitting on the edge of the mattress. He was a terrifyingly pretty kid, with the same porcelain skin and dark hair as his parents. He was awake, sitting perfectly straight in a plain white nightshirt, hands folded neatly in his lap. His bare feet dangled just an inch above the bare concrete. He wasn't looking at the door or at me. His wide, unblinking eyes were locked on the middle of that rug.

"Hi, Leo," I said. My voice sounded incredibly loud. 

I stepped inside, careful to walk *around* the perimeter of the room, keeping my boots firmly on the bare concrete. 

"I'm taking care of you while your parents are gone."

Leo didn't blink. He didn't turn his head. But his lips moved slowly, like the words were heavy.

"The soil is hungry today," he whispered. His voice was incredibly thin. It didn't have that bright, bouncy cadence of a four-year-old. It sounded original, exhausted, and very old.

"What's that, sweetie?" I asked, taking one cautious step closer, watching his profile.

"They think they buried her," Leo said, his eyes widening slightly as if tracking something invisible moving across the red wool. "But you can't bury something that doesn't have a grave. You can only anchor it."

Before I could ask, the bare bulb overhead started buzzing violently. The glass hummed, the light flared into a hot, angry blood-orange color, and then it snapped out, plunging the room into pitch blackness. And then, from right under the center of that Persian rug, the noise started.

*Scratch. Scratch. Scratch.*

It was slow. Deliberate. The sound of long, thick fingernails dragging across raw wood from the underside of the floorboards. My blood turned to ice. Every bit of skepticism I had downstairs evaporated. This was primal, lizard-brain terror. The house didn't feel like a building anymore; it felt like a concrete ribcage holding something terrible and alive inside its bones.

The scratching got louder, more frantic. In the faint gray light from the rain-streaked window, I saw the silhouette of the rug. The center of it was rising. A lump the size of a human head was pushing up against the heavy wool, stretching the crimson fabric toward the ceiling.

Rule 14.

I scrambled through the dark, my boots slamming against the concrete, and threw myself into the heavy oak rocking chair on the northern corner. The wood groaned under my weight.

*Rock,* my brain screamed. *Rock and sing.*  
"Rock-a-bye baby, on the tree top," I started, my voice cracking and shaking so bad I could barely get the words out. I pushed off with my heels, forcing the chair into a heavy rhythm. *Creak-clack. Creak-clack.*

The second the chair moved, the lump under the rug froze. The scratching stopped. For three seconds, there was absolute silence, except for the wind outside.

Then, a voice came through the floorboards. It didn't sound human. It sounded like two pieces of rotten leather rubbing together under dirt and wool. It didn't use words—it just imitated my song, copying the exact pitch of my shaking voice.

"*Rock... a... bye...*" the floorboards wheezed. And from the darkness in the corner, Leo started to laugh.

The hands on my watch read 12:14 AM. I couldn’t hear the grandfather clock from up here, but the nursery had its own timer now. *Creak-clack. Creak-clack.* The chair groaned every time I dug my heels into the stone. According to the rules, I couldn't stop. I couldn't even slow down.

Every single time my momentum faltered, even a little bit, the lump under the crimson rug would twitch. It didn't slide; it just jerked upward—a sharp, violent spasm beneath the wool, like a reflex. And then the scratching would start right back up. That wet, jagged tearing sound. So, I kept rocking.

"When the wind blows, the cradle will rock," I sang. My voice was paper-thin in the freezing room. It didn't sound like mine anymore. It sounded like an instrument being stripped of its tuning. From the middle of the floor, the dry wheeze kept echoing my cadence.

*"...the... cra... dle... will... rock..."*

It wasn't a real voice. It was just air passing through a throat that hadn't breathed in a century. It copied the tremor in my pitch, the way my tongue tripped over the letter *R*, the short gasp I took between lines. It was learning me. With every stanza, the thing under the floorboards got better at matching human speech.

In the corner, Leo stayed perfectly upright on his bed. His nightshirt was just a pale blur. He didn't move his hands. He didn't blink. Every few minutes, when the voice underneath hit a note perfectly, a fast clicking sound came from his corner. It took me twenty minutes of staring through the dark to realize what it was: Leo was tapping his fingernails against his own kneecaps, keeping time with the thing's breathing.  
By 1:00 AM, my thighs were burning from pushing off the floor. The air had gotten so cold that frost was forming on the iron frame of Leo's bed. My fingers were turning a stiff, clumsy blue. I tried changing the song. I switched to an old campfire tune my mom used to sing when I was little. Anything to break the loop.

"Down in the valley, valley so low," I whispered, keeping the chair moving. *Creak-clack.* "Hang your head over, hear the wind blow."

The lump under the rug didn't move for almost a minute. I thought, for one beautiful, desperate second, that the change had confused it. That it was going back to sleep. Then the center of the rug buckled. It didn't just rise; it expanded, stretching the red wool so tight that the geometric patterns warped into long, screaming faces.

A sound ripped out from the floor—not a wheeze, but a sharp, deafening snap, like a thick branch breaking over someone's knee. The floorboards groaned like a massive weight had been dropped onto them from inside the earth. Leo’s head snapped toward me with a fast, ratcheting motion.

"She doesn't like that one," he said. His voice was completely flat. "She doesn't know the words to that one. Sing the first one, nanny. Sing the one about the tree."

My heart slammed against my ribs. My breath came in short, ragged plumes in the freezing air. I didn't want to sing it. Every time I did, the thing sounded more like me. But the rug was rising higher, the lump shifting toward the northern edge, dragging itself toward the very corner where my chair was pinned.

"Rock-a-bye baby," I screamed into the dark, crying out loud as I slammed my heels into the concrete, forcing the chair into a fast, violent rhythm. *Creak-clack! Creak-clack!* "On the tree top! When the wind blows, the cradle will rock!"

The lump stopped moving forward. It settled back into the center, sinking an inch, though it was still a distinct, heavy mound under the wool.

*"...cra... dle... will... rock..."* the floorboards groaned back. The voice was smoother now. The leather-dry rasp was gone, replaced by a soft, hollow tone that sounded almost exactly like my own voice if I were speaking from the bottom of a deep well.

By 2:00 AM, the physical exhaustion turned into delirium. My mind drifted from the horror of the room to the absurdity of that manual downstairs. Thirty pages of rules for a house with no name. I thought about the parents. David and Eleanor. Their perfectly still postures. The way Eleanor looked at me—not like an employer, but like a mechanic looking at a spare part. *The last girl was too... vibrant. It agitated the foundation.*

What happened to the vibrant girl?

My eyes drifted to the floorboards right under the chair. In the faint gray light from the storm, I noticed something I missed when I ran in. The concrete around the base of the chair's wooden runners wasn't gray like the rest of the floor. It was discolored. There were dark, circular stains stamped into the stone, arranged in a neat, repetitive track. They were burn marks. Friction burns.  
Someone else had sat in this exact chair, rocking with the same desperate, violent intensity, for hours. For days. The wood of the runners was worn down, the bottom edges sanded flat from a lifetime of being pressed into concrete by someone who couldn't afford to stop.

*"...when... the... bough... breaks..."* the voice beneath the floor murmured. It didn't wait for me to finish the line this time. It was leading the song. *"...the... cra... dle... will... fall..."*

"And down will come baby," I joined in, my voice totally dead, mechanically following the thing's lead. My ankles were numb. I couldn't feel my toes. The cold had seeped through my boots, turning my feet into blocks of heavy ice. Every time I breathed in, the smell of rotting lilies grew stronger, filling my throat until I felt like I was choking on wet dirt. The carpet was starting to glisten. In the dark, the crimson wool looked wet, as if some dark fluid was slowly welling up from the floorboards, soaking through the fibers.

"Leo," I croaked, keeping the chair moving. *Creak-clack.* "Leo, please. How long does it do this?"

Leo didn't answer. He was staring at the window.

I turned my head slightly, careful not to shift my weight off the corner of the rug. Outside, the black silhouettes of the pines were whipping violently in the wind. The rain had stopped, replaced by a heavy fog pressing flat against the glass like a cold hand. And then, through the pane, I saw them.

Two figures were standing at the edge of the tree line, right where the gravel driveway dissolved into the woods. They were completely motionless despite the wind. No flashlights, no umbrellas. Just standing side-by-side, exactly three feet apart, staring up at the third-floor window. David and Eleanor. They hadn't gone to a gala. They hadn't driven down the mountain. They were just waiting in the dark, watching the house, listening to the rhythm of the chair.

A sudden, loud *THUD* vibrated through the floorboards right under me. The rocking chair leaped an inch into the air, my teeth slamming together so hard I bit the inside of my cheek. The taste of copper filled my mouth. I looked down. The scratching was gone.

Beneath the rug, something massive was hitting the underside of the floorboards with a slow, rhythmic cadence. *Thud. Thud. Thud.* It sounded like a heavy, blunt object—a fist, or a forehead—being systematically driven into the wood from below.

*The scratching transitions into a rhythmic thudding. Only then may you leave.*  
The hands on my watch read 3:15 AM. The three hours were over. I slowly stopped pushing with my heels. The chair slowed to a halt, the wood giving one final creak before falling silent. The lump in the center didn't twitch. It remained perfectly still, the slow, heavy *thudding* continuing at a steady, heartbeat pace beneath the fabric.

I dragged my stiff legs out of the chair. My knees buckled the second my feet hit the concrete, and I had to catch myself against the wall. Leo didn't look at me as I limped toward the door. He was still staring out the window at the tree line.

"Goodnight, nanny," the boy whispered. But his lips didn't move. The voice came from the center of the room, from the wet crimson wool of the rug. And it sounded exactly, perfectly, like me.

My hands shook so bad I could barely turn the knob. I didn't look back. I just shuffled out into the narrow hallway, my frozen boots slapping against the raw stone. The bare bulbs overhead were dark, dead glass. I used the small, dying beam of my flashlight to guide myself down the concrete stairwell. The air got thicker as I went down, turning from the freezing chill of the nursery back into that heavy warmth that smelled of wet dirt and copper.

I didn't go to my room. I couldn't survive being trapped in a small space with nothing but my thoughts. Instead, I practically threw myself into the kitchen, lunging for the stainless-steel island where the binder still lay open.

I needed answers. If the thing under the rug could copy my voice perfectly without opening its mouth, I needed to know what page fifteen said. I turned the page to **Rule 15**.

The text here was different. The clean typewriter print was gone, replaced by that sharp, jagged, hand-inked script. It looked like someone wrote it in a massive hurry, pressing so hard that the pen nib had torn through the paper in several places.

**Rule 15:** *The Vocal Mimicry.* If you have completed the three-hour cycle in the oak chair, the Anchor will have acquired the phonetics of your throat. Do not be alarmed if you hear your own voice speaking from areas of the house where you are not present. This is a natural dissipation of energy. However, if you hear your own voice speak from *inside your own head*, you must immediately consume three ounces of the underground antiseptic compound located in the cellar pantry. Do not hesitate. If the voice inside your head speaks your own name three times before you drink, the transition is complete, and you will no longer require the contract.

My throat went entirely dry. *The transition is complete.* I turned the page to **Rule 16.**

**Rule 16:** *Dietary Processing.* Leo’s meals are not intended for nutrition. They are a dampener. Every morning at precisely 6:00 AM, you must descend into the cellar and retrieve one pound of the white root vegetables from the soil bins. You must grind them into a fine paste using the iron mortar on the counter. Do not use an electric blender; the frequency of the motor agitates the roots. If the roots bleed a dark, reddish fluid when crushed, do not feed them to Leo. Throw them into the incinerator immediately and begin again. If Leo does not receive his dampener by 6:30 AM, he will begin to remember his first mother.

I leaned heavily against the stainless steel, my mind reeling. *His first mother.* The thing under the rug. The pale woman with her eyes sewn shut with black thread that I almost let loose in my initial panic. I kept turning pages, eyes scanning the jagged text as the history of this nameless house unfolded. The manual wasn't just instructions; it was a diary of containment. It was a history book written by the nannies before me, each adding their own rules in different handwriting as the house evolved, or as they figured out how to survive another night.

On page twenty-two, the handwriting changed again. It was an elegant, looping cursive, but the ink was faded, almost gray.

*“To whoever is reading this after me,”* it read. *“David and Eleanor are not married. They are not even of the same bloodline. They are wardens. The Sterling name belongs to an estate that was burned to the ground in 1894, three miles north of this valley. They are bound to this concrete structure by an agreement made by their grandfathers. They cannot leave the perimeter of the trees. They hire us because the Anchor requires a continuous influx of unique vocal frequencies to remain subdued. New throats. New patterns. If a nanny stays longer than ninety days, her voice becomes part of the house, and the Anchor grows bored. Do not try to run through the forest. The trees do not lead back to the highway. They loop. I have run for six hours in a straight line only to find myself standing back in the grand foyer, looking at my own muddy footprints.”*

A cold sweat broke out across my forehead. I looked at the kitchen clock. It was 3:42 AM. The silence was broken by a sharp, metallic *CLICK* from the eastern corridor. It was the sound of a heavy pendulum swinging into motion. 

*Rule 7.* 

But it wasn't between 2:00 AM and 3:15 AM anymore. It was nearly 4:00 AM. The clock was late. Or the house was resetting itself.  
A deep, echoing *BONG* reverberated through the concrete walls. The sound was incredibly heavy, making the coffee in my mug ripple in circles. *BONG.* That was two.  
I stood up, terrified, intending to run down into the cellar corridor pantry like the rule said. But as I took a step away from the island, I heard a sound that made my entire body lock up in absolute horror.

   
Footsteps.

Slow, deliberate, bare feet dragging across the concrete floor of the hallway just outside the kitchen door. And then, a voice called out from the dark corridor. It was a woman's voice. Light, trembling, and terrified. I recognized it instantly, because I had listened to it for three agonizing hours upstairs.

"Is anyone there?" the voice in the hallway sobbed. "Please... my legs are so cold. I can't feel my toes anymore."

It was my voice. It wasn't inside my head—not yet. It was standing just outside the kitchen door, weeping in the dark, using my exact vocal inflections to beg for help. And as it spoke, the grandfather clock rang out for a third time.

*BONG.* Three chimes. An odd number.  
The fourth chime of the clock tore through the kitchen just as the shadow at the edge of the corridor shifted. *BONG.*

I didn't wait for five. I didn't wait to see my own face walk through that door.
