\[[Part One](https://www.reddit.com/r/nosleep/comments/1xmidmf/ten_years_ago_i_was_on_staff_at_a_summer_camp/)\]

Warning: animal death

Sorry it’s taken me so long to update, but I thought I should let you all know that I survived the Witch’s visit.

When she came to see me in my apartment the other night, I dealt with her like I always do. I stood in the doorway and I waited, and I waited, and I waited, and I didn’t look away, and as I looked into her eyes, I made sure that she remembered. I made sure that she remembered everything she did to us. The downside of that is that I have to remember, too.

By morning she was gone. I never remember exactly when she leaves, but it’s always before the sun comes up. It’s always with a whisper and never with a bang. I like to think that she’s getting weaker. I like to think that one day I might outlast her.

I can’t know for sure.

There are new rules now. And I’m still figuring out what they are.

But I’ll get back to what happened ten years ago. I know that’s why you’re here.

After the Witch vanished, Maria and Ava stood on either side of my bed, staring up at me, trembling like they thought *I* might turn into a cloaked demon woman. 

“What was that?” I asked.

Maria shook her head wordlessly.

“It had to be her,” Ava whispered. “But she’s never come out of the tent before. I mean, we’ve never seen her out here. I didn’t know it was possible.”

Something thudded into the door, and we all jumped. Nico burst through, Ellis right behind him. They practically fell into the room, hair tousled, searching wildly. They must have heard me scream.

“Why don’t you knock, perv?” Ava snapped.

“Because you sounded like you were *dying!”* He shot back. He looked the whole room over before his gaze finally landed on me. His eyes tracked the blood running down my neck and the pillow case I had stuffed up under my chin, and his face instantly fell. He dodged Ava and came to stand next to me.

“I think she’s okay,” Maria said softly. 

“We saw the Witch,” Ava added. “*In* our cabin.”

Ellis looked at her, and his face went grim. Nico was still just focused on me.

As I looked at Nico, I felt a measure of what might have been unreasonable anger. Why hadn’t he warned me that this thing might just show up in my room one night? Wasn’t he supposed to know everything? 

“Can I look?” Nico asked.

I nodded reluctantly and dropped the pillow case. The rough fabric tore at my skin, and I winced. He reached over and brushed my hair away from my neck. I felt a trickle of blood run down my shoulder.

Nico’s eyes widened. The reassuring smile usually present in his eyes–the certainty I had seen so many times since coming to camp–was gone. 

“Can we all just…” Maria’s voice sounded small. “Can we all just stay here tonight?”

“Yeah.” Nico nodded before anyone else could answer. Some of the determination returned to his face then, and he took a step back. “We’ll keep the lights on, too. I don’t want anything sneaking up on us.”

Before we settled in, I grabbed my first aid backpack from where it hung behind the bathroom door and headed to the bathroom to clean up. 

Leaning over the sink, I pulled my tank top down and examined the bite. I don’t know what I expected–fangs maybe–but the bite mark was unremarkable. It looked like normal teeth. 

Blood was already pooling under the surface of the skin and running out from a couple of puncture marks. She hadn’t been messing around.

How was I supposed to fight against something that could just unlock my door and hover over my bed? 

Someone knocked on the door.

“Yeah. Come in.”

Nico appeared in the doorway. “How’s it look?”

I leaned over the sink and started running water over the bite. It stung sharply and I winced. “I mean, it’s a bite,” I said. My voice sounded tight.

I unzipped the backpack and pulled out a roll of gauze. I was almost desperate to cover it. I wanted to stop thinking about it, and that would be impossible as long as I had blood running down my arm.

“Let me help,” Nico said.

“I got it.”

“Why didn’t you warn me that she might show up like that?”

“Because she never has. She’s never come out of the tent before.”

I looked up at him, trying to gauge his truthfulness.

“You know more than you’re telling me, though,” I said.

I pressed the gauze into my shoulder. It was already tender to the touch–I could feel the soreness radiating. 

He didn’t deny it, so I pushed. “Who’s Claudette?”

His shoulders slumped. “Ava’s best friend.”

“What happened to her?”

“A couple years ago, Claudette and Ava were here cabin leading. That’s when they found out about the tent. Ava wanted in right away. Claudette wasn’t so sure, but eventually they both started answering the notes. For a while it was fine, but Claudette was never really on board, you know?”

“She didn’t believe in it?”

“Not really.” 

“So what happened? Ava said she’s dead.”

“For the first part of the week, the notes were fine. The tent wanted simple stuff. Lake water. Bouquets of those little white daisies that grow around the climbing wall. I think she asked for a clump of some girl’s hair once, but she was a bully so we didn’t mind.”

“And then she escalated?”

“Yeah. I stopped taking it seriously enough. I took too long once or twice. I didn’t follow the instructions as closely as I should have. And so it came after Claudette.”

His gaze went to the bathroom door as he said her name, and there was something unspeakably sad in his expression. 

“It asked her to spend the night in the woods.”   
  
“Oh.” The request wasn’t the craziest thing I could imagine, but it was also bigger deal than anything we had been asked to do this summer–it made catching the owl and giving up an arm full of bracelets feel relatively minor.

“Yeah. I told her not to go.”

I raised an eyebrow, waiting for him to continue. If this story had a happy ending, he wouldn’t be ashamed of it and Ava wouldn’t be talking about her dead friend. 

“For a couple of days everything was fine. The tent was really quiet. But then one night, I woke up to screaming. Ava and Claudette’s cabin was just a couple doors down. I ran over, and when I got there, Claudette was dead.”

“What?” 

“Yeah. Died in her sleep. The official cause was a heart attack, if you believe it.”

“So you think…”

“I didn’t want to believe it at first. Maria thinks it was a coincidence, and Ava still wants to hold onto a reasonable explanation. But I’ve never tested the tent again.”

I think about my hunt for the owl, and I find myself second-guessing my certainty that I had done everything right. What if I misinterpreted something? What if the note hadn’t been as straightforward as I thought?

“You should have told me.”

“Probably. But things had been going well for a couple of years. I thought we had it handled.”

“Do you think…could I have done anything that made it escalate?” I asked. I wasn’t sure I wanted the answer, but I thought I needed it. 

“No,” he shook his head. “I don’t actually think it escalated at all. Asking for bracelets wasn’t escalation–Ava just didn’t want to do it. There’s a difference.” 

I nodded, and though I didn’t say anything, I wanted to slap Ava. How could she have been so careless? How could she have lived through the death of her friend and still chosen not to take this thing seriously? 

“We have to be careful now,” Nico was saying. “Really careful. We have to do *exactly* what it asks *exactly* when it asks for it. We have to get a handle on it while we can.”

I thought about Claudette’s death–how it had only taken one misstep before the Witch decided to kill. I had to hope that she was more merciful this time around.

When we returned to the bunk room, Maria and Ava were back in their beds. I glanced at Ava, wondering if she was feeling the weight of any of this, but she was just texting. Texting like it was a normal Tuesday.

I turned back toward my bed. Ellis was lying on his stomach on the floor, feet kicked up in the air, peering into the box that contained my owl. Sprawled out on the floor with his knee-high tube socks, headband, and tank top, Ellis looked like he had dressed intentionally to evoke the 70s.

“We’ll get you a mouse in the morning lil buddy,” Ellis was practically singing to the little creature. “What did you call her, Kit?”

I sat down cross-legged next to the owl in the box. I reached inside and rubbed the feathers on her head. I knew you weren’t supposed to do that, but there was some part of this one that seemed almost tame–like she was too soft for the world. That was probably what got her cut up in the first place.

She leaned into my touch, and I could almost feel her sigh.

I had been thinking of names for her all day, but nothing felt right.

“How about Sphen,” I finally said.

Ellis laughed immediately. Maria and Nico at least offered half smiles. Only Ava stayed glued to her phone.

\*\*\*  
When I woke up the next morning, Maria and Ava were still asleep, Ellis was flopped out over one of the bunks, arm hanging limply over the side, and Nico was gone.

I got up and snuck to the window, feeling a sense of dread welling in my stomach. 

Nico was walking back from the tent. The flap was unzipped, and so it looked like whatever had happened was already over. I stepped out onto the porch, hugging myself against the morning fog.

Nico walked past me, head low. I was about to say something–to ask him what the entity had wanted–when I realized he was holding his wrist. He had a piece of cloth tied around his hand, and I could see the blood oozing along his fingers.

“Tell Ava I need to talk to her,” Nico growled as he continued to his cabin.

\*\*\*

Nico and Ava had their conversation on the front porch of the guys’ cabin. I couldn’t hear what they were saying, but Nico’s face was stern, and Ava looked pale. At one point, Nico unwrapped his arm to show Ava. She covered her mouth and stepped back. A bright red cut split his arm.

Ava came back to the cabin silent and sullen. 

“What did the tent tell him to do?” I asked, half hoping that if I made her say it, she would take it seriously.

“It wanted blood.”

She didn’t say another word. Instead she pulled her bag out from under her bed and started stuffing clothes into it. Maybe Maria would have said something if she was in the room, but she wasn’t, and I didn’t. 

She hadn’t followed the rules, and Nico got hurt because of it.

Whether Nico had asked her to leave, or she had decided it on her own, I couldn’t argue with the conclusion: Ava had to go.

\*\*\*  
Nico and I spent that afternoon helping kids up and down the climbing wall. Some of them were okay climbers. You could tell they were naturally athletic or they had done it before. 

Others clearly had no idea what they were doing. They froze a few yards up the wall, let go of the holds and free-spun on their ropes. One kid started crying. I spent most of the afternoon pulling rope hand-over-fist, letting them believe they were doing well when in fact I was the one hoisting entire children up and down the wall.  By the end of the day, my whole body ached and my shoulders were burning.

My last customer of the afternoon was a little boy with curly brown hair and huge glasses. “Can I climb with these?” He asked.

“Of course.”

“Good. I can’t see the ground without them.”

His name was Jake. I’d seen him around the camp before. He was one of the nice kids. Not one of the entitled ones. I got him harnessed and clipped him in, and stepped back into belay position. 

In the distance, something stirred in the woods. As Jake took his first shaky step onto the wall, I found myself searching the trees for movement. I was sure I had seen something, but it was gone now.

I forced my attention back to Jake. “How’s it going, bud?”

“My glasses feel loose.”

“You can fix them,” I said, pulling tighter on the rope. “I’ve got you.”

Hesitantly, he let go of the wall with one hand and pushed his glasses higher up his nose.

Movement caught my eye again. This time, when I looked up, I saw her. She was standing partially behind a tree, peering out at me. At least that’s what it looked like. She was so steeped in shadows that it was hard to make out her silhouette, but her bulbous eyes shone in whatever light reached the depths of the forest.

“Nico?” I tried to whisper, but the sound barely came out. I knew he couldn’t do anything. He was helping his own kid climb.

I felt a jerk on my rope. I looked up in time to see Jake’s glasses hit the ground.

“Uh-oh,” he said.

“That’s okay,” I said, eyes still glued to the woods. “We’ll get ‘em when you come down. Are you ready to come down?” My voice squeaked.

He thought about it for a moment, “No. It’s okay. I can keep going.”

The Witch in the woods hadn’t moved, but something told me she could. Something told me she might. Something told me that if she wanted, she would be beside me in an instant. But she wouldn’t do that, right? She would stick to the shadows, where I couldn’t even be sure she was real. 

She twitched. 

More than twitched. She full-body-shuddered. 

“Hey, Bud, I actually need to bring you down,” I said. 

That got Nico’s attention. “Kit, you okay?”

Sometimes I have dreams where I know I’m asleep. There’s something coming for me–something sneaking through the recesses of my mind, reaching for me in the darkness. I always try to wake myself up. I try to get myself to scream, but I can’t open my mouth. All that ever comes out is a muffled, closed-mouth rumble from deep inside of me.

When I come to–in that moment between sleeping and waking–I hear my real voice groaning through closed lips. So even though I choose to make the sound in my dream, it is in fact my own real voice in the outside world that eventually wakes me up.

As I stared at the woman in the woods, I knew that I should call out to Nico–I knew that I should try to wake us all up–but I couldn’t make my voice cooperate.

The Witch didn’t move. 

I lowered Jake slowly from the wall.

“Kit–what’s going on?” Nico said.

I couldn’t bring myself to answer. And by the time I finally found my voice again, the Witch had dissolved into the forest.

\*\*\*

Before she left, Ava turned to me. “You can just leave, you know,” she said.

“What do you mean?”

“I mean you don’t have to keep playing the stupid tent game. You can just go,” she shrugged. “I thought we had this under control. I thought we could keep *her* under control. But it looks like we can’t. So you should leave. Let this place rot.”

I might have, if I was alone.

But I wasn’t alone. I had Nico and Ellis and Maria. I didn’t want to leave them alone either. I didn’t want to abandon them to face this by themselves.

Because while I would never say it to her face–or even to any of the others–I thought Ava was weak for leaving, even if I thought it was best for all of us. I would have respected her more if she had tried to stay, tried to fix her mistakes, and tried to help us contain the Witch.

Instead, she threw her bag into her car and peeled out, spraying gravel back at the rest of us.

\*\*\*

The tent was zipped again that night. What had felt like an irritation earlier in the week now draped heavily over our shoulders and weighed on our minds. 

“Who’s got it?” Nico asked. “I can’t go twice.” 

Nor should he have to. I looked down at his arm. The hint of a brown-and-yellow stain was forming under the gauze. 

“I’m not doing it anymore,” Maria said.

“Wait… like….”

“Like I’m not playing the game anymore,” she folded her arms.

I expected Nico to try to talk her out of quitting. Instead he nodded. “I get it. But it might not be that easy.”

I almost volunteered. Sure, I was scared, but I was part of this now, and there was no way I was going to fold up like Maria and Ava and quit. I would do what I had to do—play the part I had to play and help get all of us through this. But I hesitated, and in that tiny vacuum, Ellis spoke up.

“I’ve got it.”

I’m ashamed to say relief washed over me. Guilt was right behind. *Next time,* I promised myself. I glanced up at Nico, who was already starting toward the cabins.

“We’ll go start a fire,” he said.

\*\*\*

A few minutes later, Ellis came around the cabins and took a seat at the fire. He was holding a wine bottle, and when I leaned back in my chair and looked at the tent in the distance, I could see dark liquid pooling in the dirt, running in rivulets, picking up pine needles in its tiny river.

“Want some?” Ellis asked, pushing the bottle at me.

His expression was grim. Humorless.

I didn’t like the flavor of wine, but I felt like I should take a sip. It felt like the kind of moment meant to bind us together in common purpose or something. I took a long drink from the bottle. I tried to hand it to Nico, but he wasn’t looking at me. He was looking at Ellis.

“Is that *actually* what it wanted? It wanted you to pour wine on the ground?”

“Yeah,” Ellis said. “Of course.”

Nico looked at him for a long moment, and then took the bottle from me.

\*\*\*

We sat there drinking the wine and watching whatever we could see of the stars through the campfire smoke. Up in the trees I could see shadows moving occasionally. Every now and then I caught a glimpse of an owl rustling in a branch, shifting its weight, or tucking its head. Once I saw one swoop down and pluck a rodent off of the forest floor.

Maria joined us after a while, and Nico handed her the bottle of wine. She gave him what looked like a genuine smile and took a sip. I wondered if she would come around tomorrow. I wondered if she would get back on the tent rotation after all.

“How’s your arm?” She asked after a moment.

Overhead I caught movement in my peripheral vision. I looked up and saw a small owl sitting on the tip of a branch. Its wide moon-eyes reflected the firelight.

“Oh, it’s fine,” he said. “No damage done.”

“Are you sure you had to….”

“Of course I had to. We’ve seen what happens when we ignore the notes.”

Maria was quiet for a moment. Then she said, “What if we all just leave?” 

The owl above us blinked. It lifted its wings.

“Maria, it *was in your cabin* last night. It’s out of the tent now. What’s to say it won’t follow us? What’s to say it won’t *kill?* We have to keep her contained.”

“You don’t know that it will follow us. It’s *crazy* to just stay here and wait.”

“I don’t think we have a choice,” Nico said.

With a single beat of its soundless wings, the owl dove from the branch. At first I thought it was going for another mouse.

It took me a moment to realize it wasn’t diving for a mouse at all.

The owl struck the fire. Sparks exploded upward as the little creature writhed in the flames. And then another owl struck. And another.

I leapt out of my chair. 

“What the *fuck?*” 

Two more owls hit the fire, exploding like little bombs. 

More and more birds pelted the flames. They caught fire, screaming and thrashing. Some of them rolled out of the flames and struck the cabin porch, smoking and smoldering. 

The four of us stood around the fire, frozen in place, watching the carnage, ready to spring into action but with nothing to do. The screams of the owls turned my stomach, but all I could do was stand there and watch.

The owls stopped diving. The screaming quieted. Sharp smoke burned my nose as the birds sizzled and popped. Soon everything went quiet.

The four of us stood staring at each other.

I took a breath and almost choked on the smoke. My breathing came in short bursts after that. 

Across the fire, I locked eyes with Nico. Slowly he turned to his brother. 

“Ellis,” his voice was barely a whisper.

Ellis stared at the ground.

“What did the Witch really want?”

Ellis didn’t say anything.

My eyes watered at the angry scent in the air.

“Ellis!” Maria snapped. “Did you lie?”

“What did she ask you to do?” Nico asked again.

Ellis looked up at me. Then he turned to Nico. “It wanted Kit’s owl.” He said. “I mean, what was I supposed to do? It wanted me to kill her fucking owl.”

Slowly I sank back into my chair, shaking with fear and adrenaline. I closed my eyes. I opened them again.

I understood why he hadn’t done what he was told. I would have struggled too. But looking down at the carnage around us, with the benefit of twenty-twenty hindsight, his refusal to do what needed to be done felt a little naive. 

I would be different. I promised myself that next time the tent zipped–next time I had the opportunity–I wasn’t going to leave this up to someone else.

\*\*\*

I think that’s all I’ve got in me for today. Sometimes, during the darkest part of the night, I think I can still smell that fire. I don’t want to remember anymore of it right now.

I’ll tell you the rest of what happened later on in the week.
