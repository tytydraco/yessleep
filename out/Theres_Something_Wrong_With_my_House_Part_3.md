[Part Two](https://www.reddit.com/r/nosleep/comments/1vriree/theres_something_wrong_with_my_house_part_2/)



I almost skipped our first date.

Hannah wanted to meet at Raglan Roast, which I thought was slightly boring and, given everything happening at the flat, felt a little like salt in the wound. The cool studio apartments with mezzanine bedrooms and enormous windows—the ones I had once wanted badly enough to nearly throw a tantrum over—were right beside it. Still, it was only a ten-minute walk from my flat, which was perhaps the one genuinely good thing about the place. It was central. I could forgive quite a lot for being able to walk almost everywhere.

It was spitting when I left. Nothing unusual for Wellington, except my fake-leather jacket had a hole in one elbow that I’d been meaning to patch for just under a year, and a southerly was coming in off the waterfront. Somehow the wind kept finding that exact patch of exposed skin. By the time I was halfway there, I had goosebumps on one elbow and nowhere else, which irritated me far more than it reasonably should have.

In fact, I became so fixated on my stupid arm that I almost turned around. I wanted to go home, get into bed and absolutely not meet a girl who was so clearly out of my league that I was beginning to suspect she’d matched with me by accident. She’d given me her social media a few days earlier, which had only made things worse.

She was even cooler than I’d thought. Long, straight black hair. Chunky rings set with stones the size of dollar coins. Photos from backstage at the Opera House, washed in green light that made everything look slightly unreal. In one, she was looking up at someone outside the frame, smiling around the stick of a lollipop. Her lipstick was bright red and smudged downward at one corner. I had stared at that photograph for much too long, first because she looked beautiful and then because I found myself wondering whether she’d been kissing someone.

Which was insane. I had never met her. We’d been talking for a week. She was obviously allowed to kiss whoever she wanted, and the fact that my brain had managed to turn a smudge of lipstick into an entirely imaginary romantic rival was humiliating. I could feel myself going red just thinking about it.

By the time I reached the coffee shop, I had almost convinced myself to leave. I stood awkwardly beneath the roller door, half inside and half out, looking back towards the street and trying to decide, until the weather decided for me. The sky opened. Within seconds, rain was hammering the street, water rushing along the gutters and one of the drains already beginning to give up. There was no chance I was walking home in that, so I stayed.

Hannah wasn’t there yet. I wanted to be one of those put-together people who ordered for the other person before they arrived: the kind who remembered what someone drank and handed it over casually, as though they hadn’t spent the previous ten minutes wondering whether doing so would seem thoughtful or presumptuous. She had told me earlier that she was easy—“Just a regular latte”—so I ordered two.

While I waited, I considered putting my name in their loyalty book. You could hand over twenty dollars upfront, write your name down and use the credit for five coffees. Four dollars each, absurdly cheap by Wellington standards and exactly the kind of thing I should have been doing if I genuinely intended to stop complaining about being broke. I was still deciding whether committing twenty dollars to one coffee shop counted as financial responsibility when the coffees appeared.

I carried both cups to the back and found a table beneath the enormous Greenpeace banner with the seals on it, then checked my phone. 2:05. Hannah was five minutes late.

Five minutes was nothing. I knew that. Barely late enough to count, especially in weather like this, and I had certainly kept people waiting longer without assuming they had died or changed their mind about meeting me. At 2:07, I checked the app anyway. Nothing.

I took a sip of my coffee and immediately regretted it because I’d forgotten which one was supposed to be hers. They were identical, obviously, and there was no indent left in the foam. I turned both cups around as though one might somehow reveal itself to be untouched, then decided I had ruined the entire gesture by psychologically contaminating both.

At 2:09, I opened our messages and typed, *Hey! I’m at the back :)* Too cheerful. I deleted it. *Hey, I’m here, just at the back.* Accusatory. Delete. I had just settled on *I’m by the seal thing* when someone shook rainwater from their jacket near the counter and I looked up.

Hannah.

I recognised her immediately, which was annoying because some part of me had been hoping I wouldn’t. Maybe she would look significantly worse than her photos. Maybe she’d used pictures from four years ago and I could relax into feeling superior.

She had not.

Her hair was soaked nearly black and clung in strands to the shoulders of her jacket. The red lipstick was there, less perfect than it had been online, and she held a takeaway cup in both hands while scanning the back of the shop. Then she saw me, and her whole face changed.

That was somehow worse. She smiled, lifted her coffee in greeting and started weaving between the tables. I had just enough time to become painfully aware that I was sitting too straight, correct my posture, decide the correction looked unnatural and end up more or less where I’d started.

“Sorry,” she said when she reached me. She had a light Australian accent which was unexpected, surfacing mostly in the vowels. “I stood up front for two minutes thinking you’d done a runner.”

“I messaged you.”

She checked her phone. “Thirty seconds ago.”

“I was giving you a reasonable amount of time.”

“Heroic.”

She pulled out the chair, caught the table with her hip and knocked her coffee hard enough to send some through the lid.

“Ah, shit.”

We both reached for the napkins. Our hands collided, and I snatched mine back.

“Sorry.”

Hannah looked at me. “You didn’t assault me.”

“I know.”

Then she noticed the two coffees already on the table.

“Oh.”

I followed her gaze. Three coffees.

“I got you one.”

“So you did.”

“I didn’t know you’d already ordered.”

“Clearly.”

She sat down and surveyed the cups. “Strong start.”

I laughed, mostly because the alternative was climbing under the table. “I was trying to be thoughtful.”

“You were thoughtful.” She picked up one of the cups. “This mine?”

“That one.”

“You sure?”

“No.”

Her eyebrows lifted.

“I might have drunk from one.”

“Which one?”

“I don’t know.”

“Beautiful.”

I could feel my face getting hot. Hannah laughed.

“Eve. Relax.”

Before I could answer, she took a drink from the coffee in her hand.

I stared at her.

“There,” she said, setting it down. “Shared liability.”

That did absolutely nothing for the colour of my face.

We ended up sitting opposite each other with three coffees between us, Hannah’s leaking slowly into a nest of napkins and the spare stranded near the edge of the table. For the first few minutes, I was too aware of everything I did: where I put my hands, how often I looked at her, whether looking at her mouth when she spoke counted as eye contact or made it obvious I wanted to kiss her. Which I did, but that was beside the point.

She was easier to talk to in person than she had been over messages. Online, there was always time to think of something clever and pretend you’d thought of it immediately. In person, I tended to become aware of someone speaking halfway through a sentence and miss the rest because I was too busy deciding how I was supposed to be reacting.

Hannah didn’t seem to care. She talked quickly, changed subjects without warning and seemed to trust that I would either keep up or ask. When I said something she found interesting, she leaned forward. When she thought I was being ridiculous, she leaned all the way back. I learnt this because she did it quite often.

She told me about the one-bedroom flat she rented in Newtown. I told her about Kahurangi leaving protein shakers in the sink until someone said something passive-aggressive in the group chat. She told me she’d once taken the wrong bus during her second week living in Newtown and been too embarrassed to tell the driver, so she’d stayed on until the route looped back around. By the time the rain softened from apocalyptic to merely Wellington, I had stopped wondering what to do with my hands.

Then she asked about my thesis.

I should have expected it. I’d told her I was doing one, after all, but I still hesitated long enough for her to notice.

“That bad?”

“It’s not bad.”

“Your face reckons otherwise.”

“My face doesn’t reckon anything.”

“Your face says heaps.”

I gave her a look. She smiled and waited.

“Religious studies,” I said. “Contemporary supernatural belief.”

Her eyebrows went up.

“There it is.”

“What?”

“The face everyone makes before they ask about ghosts.”

“I was going to say witches.”

“That’s worse.”

“Is it?”

“Yes.”

I gave her the short version: I wasn’t interested so much in belief itself as in the moment before it, when something ambiguous acquired intention. Hannah listened, then frowned.

“So your brain makes a person.”

“Sometimes.”

“Because it expects one?”

“More because we’re biased towards detecting agency. If something’s ambiguous, there’s an evolutionary advantage in assuming it might be intentional.” She stared at me, so I simplified. “If the bush moves, assume tiger.”

“Right.” She nodded. “You should put that in.”

“I’ll tell my supervisor.”

“Put me in the acknowledgements.”

“For the tiger?”

“For improving the whole thing.”

She smiled, then something in her expression shifted. Nothing dramatic. She was still turning her coffee cup slowly between her hands, one knee tucked beneath her.

“My nan would hate your thesis.”

“Religious?”

“You could say that.”

Hannah’s mother had grown up in South Australia, which was where the accent came from, despite Hannah not being born there. Somewhere out past Oodnadatta. Her grandparents still lived there, on a property reached by taking one station road and then another until, according to Hannah, you had to know which particular piece of dirt belonged to you.

“That’s actual Outback, right?”

“Yeah, nah. Proper nothing.”

She flattened a napkin against the table as though it were a map and drew an invisible route north with one finger. “You just keep going. Then when you’re fairly sure nobody could possibly live any further out, you keep going again.”

“That sounds pleasant.”

“It is.”

The answer came quickly enough to surprise me. She looked towards the window, where there was nothing but light rain, parked cars and the grey wall of the building opposite.

“It’s beautiful. Just not in a normal way. Everything’s flat for kilometres, then you get these low hills like someone scraped all the colour out of the ground and piled it up somewhere else. Red, white, yellow. Burnt colours. Mostly gibber plain and saltbush. Hardly any trees. You can drive for an hour and nothing changes, then the light shifts and the whole place looks like another planet.”

“Reassuringly habitable.”

“Summer’s fucked.”

I laughed.

She’d grown up in New Zealand, but her mother had taken her and her siblings out there during school holidays. Hannah had resented it at fourteen because there was almost no reception. Once, she’d had to climb onto a water tank to send a text.

“That builds character.”

“Built resentment.”

She smiled, but she’d gone quieter. I thought at first she was bored with the subject. Then she said, “They see people out there.”

I waited for the punchline. It didn’t come.

“Your grandparents?”

“Yeah.”

“What sort of people?”

She gave me a look but answered.

Her grandfather used to see his brother David, who had died years before Hannah was born. Some kind of farm accident—tractor or truck; she couldn’t remember which. Every so often, her grandfather would come inside for breakfast and mention that David had been standing by the gate again.

“Just like that?”

“Just like that.”

“Did he know—”

“That David was dead?” Her mouth twitched. “Yeah, Eve. Pretty sure the news had reached him.”

“You know what I mean.”

“I do.”

She turned one of the rings on her finger. “That’s the weird bit. Grandad never told it like a ghost story. Nan didn’t either. It wasn’t all, *David’s haunting the gate*. More like, *David was about this morning*, and then Nan would tell Grandad he’d left the screen door open and let the flies in.”

I laughed before I could stop myself. Hannah didn’t—not because she was offended. She just looked at me, and the laugh died on its own.

“Sorry.”

“Nah. It does sound funny.” She looked down at her coffee. “Nan used to set another cup out sometimes.”

*I* stopped smiling. “For David?”

“She never said.”

Something moved across the skin of my arms, not quite goosebumps. I ignored it.

“Did she see him too?”

“Not him.”

“Who, then?”

Hannah glanced towards the front of the café. The rain had eased enough that people were starting to leave, pulling up their hoods and making optimistic little runs for the street.

“There was a woman near the tanks.”

She said it carefully. Her grandmother had apparently seen her for years, always in hot weather, usually late in the afternoon when the air above the ground had begun to shimmer. Sometimes the woman stood beside one of the water tanks; sometimes she appeared farther off, near a dry creek bed.

“What did she look like?”

“Couldn’t see much. Long dress, apparently. Never came close.”

“Heat shimmer.”

“Probably.”

I looked at her. She was turning her ring again.

“Did your grandmother think that?”

Hannah’s mouth tightened slightly. “Nan thought lots of things.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It’s the one you’re getting.”

I smiled, expecting her to. She didn’t.

Instead she said, “She still leaves the back light on.”

“For the woman?”

Hannah looked up. “No. For people coming home.”

The strangest part, to me, was how comfortably all of this existed alongside her grandparents’ religion. They were church-every-Sunday people. Bibles in multiple rooms. Grace over meals. Hannah’s grandmother had once made her throw away a horoscope because she was, apparently, “inviting things in.”

“But seeing dead relatives around the property is fine?”

Hannah shrugged. “Never said it made sense.”

“What did they think they were?”

“I asked Nan that once.”

“And?”

“She told me I asked too many questions.”

“I like her.”

“You would.”

“What did she actually say?”

For a moment, Hannah said nothing. When she smiled this time, it was smaller than before.

“She said if someone stands at your gate, you don’t need to know where they came from before you decide if you want to let them in.”

I stared at her. “That is an incredibly ominous thing to say.”

“Yeah.”

“To a child.”

“I was sixteen.”

“Still a child.”

“I thought I was very grown-up.”

“So did I. I was an idiot.”

“You turned out all right.”

I narrowed my eyes. She grinned, and just like that, she was back.

The conversation moved on after that—or Hannah moved it on. We talked about terrible lecturers, then theatre people, then a woman she’d worked with in Auckland who used to steal food from the communal fridge and deny it while actively eating it. But part of my mind stayed somewhere north of Oodnadatta.

Not because I believed Hannah’s grandparents. I didn’t. Everything she’d described had reasonable explanations: isolation, grief, expectation, familiar landscapes turning ambiguous shapes into familiar people. A dead brother seen by someone who had spent decades missing him was almost painfully straightforward. The woman by the tanks sounded like textbook visual distortion—distance, extreme heat, a human brain doing what human brains do best. If anything, it supported my thesis.

I still can’t tell you what Hannah’s grandparents saw out there. Maybe grief put familiar faces into heat and distance; maybe there really were people standing where no people should have been. At the time, I thought the distinction mattered. I don’t anymore. Before long, I would have much stranger things to explain.
