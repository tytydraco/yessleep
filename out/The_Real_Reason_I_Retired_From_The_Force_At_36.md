It’s been five years, three weeks, and two days since I retired from being a cop. I didn't retire due to my age. 

I retired because I couldn't make sense of what I saw. I was a deputy in a small town in western Massachusetts, the kind of place where the pines press right up against people's backyards and the streetlights give up half a mile outside of downtown. There was very little crime outside of petty theft, drug use, and a few deeply sad domestic violence cases.

One night I was on patrol when dispatch came through. A teenage girl, Hannah (first name only, that's all I'll give you), on her way to becoming salutatorian, had called 911, terrified. Dispatch played part of the recording. She sobbed hysterically for 20 seconds.

Then the sobbing stopped. That was worse. In a flat, hollow voice, she told the dispatcher a man was standing in her backyard, in the shadows of the big oak tree, right at the forest edge. She wasn't crying anymore. She was past crying. The last thing on the recording was her whispering, "The Muffled Curse is real. He’s here." I didn't know what she meant then. I don't know what she meant now.

I took the call. I didn't know Hannah well, but I knew her parents somewhat. They were church people, quiet, kind. She seemed like a good kid from a good family. On the drive over, I told myself she'd dropped acid or smoked too much pot. I remember actually feeling relieved by that theory.

When I arrived, the front door was wide open. Not forced. Open, like an invitation. No lights on anywhere.

I drew my gun for the first time in my career. I cleared the house room by room, my flashlight beam shaking more than I'd like to admit. Nothing. Then a motion sensor light snapped on in the backyard.

I heard a muffled scream.

I rushed outside and saw Hannah right at the edge of where the light reached, where the grass met the tree line. A large, long hand was wrapped around her mouth. The fingers went all the way past her jaw. The other arm was coiled around her waist.

I couldn't see the man holding her. He was there, I know he was there, but the shadows didn't fall on him so much as belong to him. I screamed, "Let her go or I'll shoot!"

No response. Just her eyes. I still see her eyes, silently pleading for me to help. I took a step forward, then another.

Then she screamed through the hand.

There was a crunch, loud, wet, and wrong as she folded in half backward.

Then she was gone. Pulled into the dark. I ran to the tree line, gun up, flashlight sweeping. She wasn't there. He wasn't there. The grass wasn't even bent.

How do you put that in a report? What do you say to her parents? I didn't have an answer to either question. I never will.

Her case was classified as a runaway. But I knew that night there was nowhere for her to run.

Fast forward to today. A former cop buddy of mine called. Three weeks ago, seven bones were found arranged in the woods, each cracked clean in two. DNA testing proved the bones were Hannah’s.

I used to love walking in those woods. I would stroll around for hours, fascinated by the bird calls, the rustling foliage, and how the shadows danced around throughout the day.

I haven’t been back in those woods since that night. I will never go back.
