As I sat in my van watching reels on my phone, trying to steal just a little more time after my last service call of the day, I heard the familiar ding of another call being added to my schedule.

Checking my iPad, I felt my face scrunch involuntarily.

This can’t be right. A no-heat call on a 95-degree day?

I called the office, and they confirmed it. The customer insisted it was 55 degrees inside her house.

I put the address into my phone. Of course, it was an hour away in the opposite direction of home.

Well, at least I can get some overtime in this week.

In my six years in the trade, I’d never had a call quite like this. During the hour-long drive, my mind ran through every possible fault that could cause something like that.

My prevailing theory was a low-voltage short causing the unit to run continuously. I’d seen that several times before, but I’d never seen a house get that cold.

Of course, that was assuming it actually was that cold inside and the thermostat wasn’t just going bad and reading low.

Pulling up the long gravel driveway, I didn’t see the house for at least two minutes. It was tucked deep into the woods. It had been a long time since I’d been this far out into rural Tennessee, but the house itself looked typical for the area.

I let out a quiet but audible, “Fuck,” as I threw the van into park.

I could see the condenser on the left side of the house, which meant I’d probably be crawling under the house or climbing into the attic to get to the air handler.

As I got out of the van, tools in hand, I noticed an unusual chill in the air.

Not cold, exactly. But it definitely wasn’t in the 90s like it had been back in town.

I figured a cold front must be moving in. It was supposed to storm that evening anyway.

Walking up to the house and climbing the stairs onto the decrepit old covered porch, I could now see my breath in the air.

I stopped, turned around, and went back to the van for the hoodie I kept in there year-round for working on walk-in freezers.

This is where I should have gotten back in the van and left. But I have to admit, I couldn’t overcome the curiosity welling up inside me.

When I headed back toward the house, I had my little analog thermometer out and watched it like a hawk.

From the van to the front door, I watched the temperature drop from 79 degrees to 55 by the time I reached the top of the stairs.

In my haste to grab my hoodie, and with my attention glued to the thermometer, I hadn’t noticed that the front door had opened.

I looked up and nearly fell off the porch.

A young woman was standing in the doorway.

She looked exhausted. Dark bags hung beneath her eyes, making her look older than she probably was.

I managed to squeak out, “Uh, good evening. My name is Tim I’m here to look at your furnace.”

She welcomed me inside with a quiet, almost pained, “Come in.”

I started asking the usual questions.

When did you first notice the problem? When was the last time you changed the filter? How old is the house? Do you know how old the unit is?

She told me she had only closed on the house the week before and had moved in two days ago.

Apparently, she’d gotten the place for a steal. She knew it needed work, but the air conditioner hadn’t been very high on her list of things to worry about.

She had toured the property once before buying it and said she’d instantly fallen in love with the property.

Nothing had seemed unusual during the showing. The temperature outside was normal, the house was comfortable, and as far as she knew, there hadn’t been any issues with the HVAC system.

She first noticed a chill in the air on her first night in the house. When she checked the thermostat, it read 68 degrees.

Nothing too strange. Honestly, 68 is a pretty normal indoor temperature for a lot of people.

The problem was that the thermostat was set to 72.

She explained that she normally kept the house a little warmer because she tended to get cold easily.

My curiosity instantly peaked.

I had a dozen questions running through my head, but I knew she probably wouldn’t have the answers I wanted.

So I started with the thermostat.

55 degrees.

Well, that confirms it.

This is strange.

I couldn’t hear the indoor fan running, which ruled out my theory that the AC was somehow running continuously.

In fact, nothing was running at all.

My next step was to find the furnace or air handler. Even if the HVAC system wasn’t causing the unusual temperature, it still wasn’t working.

I asked her if she knew where the furnace was located.

She had no clue.

Since I was already inside, I figured I’d start with the attic.

I pulled down the attic stairs and was immediately showered with dead bugs, dust, and dried leaves as the hatch opened.

“Shit,” I muttered, brushing the debris out of my hair and beard.

Then I noticed something else.

A faint current of noticeably warmer air was drifting down from the attic.

That stopped me for a second.

It was something I would normally expect on a summer service call, but by now I knew this wasn’t a normal call.

I climbed the ladder anyway, flashlight between my teeth and tool bag in one hand.

Taking a quick look around, I saw nothing strange or out of the ordinary.

I was greeted by a relatively nice, albeit older, air handler.

After checking power and starting my usual troubleshooting process, I found the control board was fried.

There are plenty of reasons a board like that could fail, and normally I would have stayed to figure out exactly what caused it.

Instead, I decided it was late, and whatever curiosity I’d had earlier was gone.

I’d have to order the replacement board anyway. If I was unfortunate enough to be the one sent back out here, I could dig into the cause then.

After that, my memory gets a little fuzzy.

I climbed back down, gave the homeowner a quick breakdown of what I’d found, and made my way toward the van.

The second my foot left the porch, I was greeted by the warmth of a summer evening. It still felt cooler than it should have, but compared to the house, it was almost comforting.

I tossed my tools into the van, climbed into the driver’s seat, pulled up my iPad, and started writing my notes.

When I finished, I shoved the key into the ignition and turned it.

Nothing.

My heart sank.

I tried again.

Click.

The battery was dead.

That was the moment the fear I’d been pushing down all evening finally flooded through me.

Every strange thing I’d looked past the temperature, the porch, the house, the dead control board came flooding back all at once.

And suddenly, sitting alone in that van, I wanted nothing more than to get as far away from that house as possible.

As I slowly raised my head, still in shock at my misfortune, I found myself staring at the woman in the doorway.

Unsettled and increasingly afraid, I grabbed my largest crescent wrench and hopped out, slipping it into my leg pocket on my overalls.

The sun had now fallen below the trees, and the chill in the air emanating from the house now reached my van.

I yelled over, my voice dripping with hesitation.

“My battery seems to have died.”

A reply came with a voice that wasn’t quite right.

“That’s unfortunate.”

The conversation ended there.

I’d had enough. I turned around and started moving toward the road.

The second my eyes left the house, my ears were assaulted with loud guttural screams, but I didn’t turn around. I was now in a full blown sprint.

Cursing the pack of Marlboros in my shirt pocket, I could feel my breath and body beginning to fade.

Slowing down now, believing it was all over, I flew into a coughing fit. Each cough showed in the now frigid air.

As I fell to the ground, mind fading, eyes closing, I was greeted by the sound of more than one set of feet following me, sounding closer and closer.

I lose it there.

The next thing I remember is waking up where I am now.

I’m home in my bed, and it’s cold. Really cold.

Nothing feels right.

I check my thermostat on the app.

55 degrees.

I can hear someone in the kitchen, but I live alone.
