We took out a thirty-year mortgage on Unit 13B. It was on the thirteenth floor of a newly finished high-rise on the outskirts of Chicago, all pale drywall, cheap oak laminate, and wide double-hung windows that looked out over a gray grid of strip malls and drainage ponds. We moved in together as an extended family—my older brother Mark, his wife Clara, their two kids, my mother, my teenage sister, and my wife Sarah. It took everything we had saved up to secure the down payment, but standing in that empty living room on move-in day, watching the late-afternoon sun hit the walls, it felt like we’d finally pulled our lives together.

The odd things started small enough that you’d feel stupid mentioning them out loud. The elevator never worked when I stepped inside. If anyone else in my family got in with me, the doors would slide shut, shudder, and refuse to budge until I stepped back out into the lobby. I’d take the concrete stairwell thirteen flights up while they rode up smooth and quiet. My phone camera glitched, too, but only on my face; whenever someone took a picture of me inside the apartment, my features smeared into a streak of digital noise, while Mark and the kids showed up pin-sharp. Milk curdled on the stove three days in a row before it even reached a simmer. We laughed it off as bad wiring and spoiled dairy. You have to rationalize things like that when you’ve already signed the deed.

Then the television routine started. Around one in the afternoon, my mother, Clara, and Sarah would gather on the sectional to watch a daytime soap called All Is Well. None of us had ever heard of the network or the show before, but the women got hooked immediately. It was shot on standard digital video, flat lighting, cheap sets, about an extended family living in a suburban home.

I didn't pay attention until a Tuesday afternoon when I stayed home from work with a migraine. I came out of the bedroom to get water while the show was running. On screen, the mother was arguing with the daughter-in-law over a chipped ceramic bowl, and the daughter-in-law dropped it on the linoleum. Not ten seconds later, Clara fumbled a soup bowl in our kitchen, and it shattered in the exact same pattern.

At first, I told myself it was a coincidence. But the next day, the television family sat around a dining table celebrating the younger brother’s sudden promotion at an engineering firm. Two hours later, my boss called me on my cell and offered me a senior project manager position with a thirty percent raise.

I started watching the screen with a sick, cold feeling behind my ribs. The television family mirrored ours person for person. The actors didn't look like us, but their roles, their clothes, their private arguments, and their schedules matched down to the minute. Whatever happened on their screen at one in the afternoon happened in Unit 13B by five.

I checked with the building super and the cable provider. Nobody else in the building could find the channel. When I brought a small portable TV into the apartment and plugged it into the bedroom wall, the show played there too, but the moment I carried that same set outside our front door into the hallway, the screen dissolved into dead static.

The turning point was the hammer. On a Thursday broadcast, the television husband bought a heavy, rust-spotted framing hammer with a notched wooden handle and set it on the workbench in his garage. The next afternoon, Mark came home from a neighborhood flea market, dumped his tool bag on the counter, and pulled out that identical hammer. When I asked him why he bought it, he just shrugged and said it looked sturdy.

I reached out to my friend David, a sergeant in the county police department. I didn't tell him everything—I couldn't without sounding like I belonged in a psych ward—but I asked him to run a property records search on the land our building sat on. He pushed back, told me I was obsessing over stress, but he agreed to look into the county archives.

What he found made my blood go cold. In 1977, an old single-family brick house stood on the exact plot where our building was constructed. A family of eight lived there. On a rainy night in November of that year, all of them were systematically beaten to death in their beds with a framing hammer. The only survivor was a distant relative, a man named Robert who was arrested covered in blood, but the prosecution botched the chain of custody and the case eventually fell apart due to lack of hard evidence. Robert had vanished decades ago.

David called me back two days later after digging through microfiche of old newspapers. He sent a scanned black-and-white photograph of the murdered family to my phone.

They were the cast of All Is Well. Every single actor on the screen was a dead ringer for the people murdered in that house in 1977.

I sat in my car in the parking lot, staring at the screen of my phone until the battery ran low. The show wasn't predicting our future out of nowhere; it was reenacting their slaughter, and our family had been cast in their places.

The broadcasts escalated rapidly. On Monday, the television wife announced she was pregnant, and that evening Sarah came into our bedroom holding a positive test strip, crying happy tears that made my stomach turn to water. On Wednesday, the show’s younger sister secretly met a man in an alley behind a diner; that night, my teenage sister told me she’d started seeing a guy she met online and begged me not to tell Mark. On Friday, the television plot turned dark: the family members began turning on one another, screaming accusations, the father locking the deadbolts from the inside.

The finale was scheduled to air the following Monday.

I couldn't wait. I spent the weekend scouring regional property databases, tax registries, and voter rolls, looking for any trace of Robert. By Sunday night, cross-referencing old aliases against county utility connections, I traced an address out in an unincorporated rural pocket sixty miles south—a solitary trailer on a gravel lot.

I drove out there in the pouring rain. The man who answered the door was frail, stooped, with clouded cataracts and shaking hands. When I mentioned the 1977 killings, he didn't run or get angry; he started weeping. He told me he hadn't killed his family. He had loved them. He said there was another man back then, an obsessed family friend who had spent months inserting himself into their lives, a man who worked as a television repairman and lived in their basement until they tried to kick him out. The police had pinned it on Robert because he was found holding the hammer, trying to pull it out of his brother's skull. The real killer, the repairman, had slipped away in the rain and never been named in the press.

Robert pulled an old manila folder from under his mattress. Inside was a faded, water-damaged photograph from the summer of 1976. The family was standing in their front yard, smiling. Standing slightly off to the side, wearing a mechanic’s jacket and a stiff, hollow grin, was David.

Not the David who was my friend, but the man he was decades ago. The resemblance was undeniable. The same flat eyes, the same crooked collarbone, the same set of the jaw. He hadn't aged naturally, or he had returned under a new identity, or something worse—he had stayed close to the ground, watching the land, waiting for the cycle to reset.

I left Robert's trailer and drove back toward the city at ninety miles an hour through the rain. My phone buzzed in the cup holder. It was David. His voice was completely steady over the speaker, asking where I was, telling me he had dropped by Unit 13B to check on Sarah and the kids because Mark had called him saying I hadn't come home.

I reached the high-rise just after one in the afternoon. The storm had knocked out the streetlights along the block. I sprinted into the lobby, threw open the heavy fire door, and took the concrete stairs three steps at a time. My lungs were burning, my boots skidding on the cold steps.  
When I burst through the thirteenth-floor fire door and ran down the hall to 13B, the apartment door was unlocked.

The living room was dim. The television was on, the blue light washing over the furniture, volume turned all the way up. On the screen, the killer had already entered the house. He was holding the hammer. He had Mark pinned against the kitchen counter. Clara and the children were screaming from the back hallway.

I dropped my keys and ran into the kitchen.  
David was standing over Mark, the wooden handle raised in his grip. Mark was on the floor, bleeding from the temple, struggling to hold David's wrists. In the hallway, Sarah had the kids pulled behind her, backing against the bathroom door.

I tackled David into the dining table. We went down hard against the laminate floor, knocking the chairs over, tearing through drywall. He was sixty or seventy years old on paper, but when his hands grabbed my throat, his grip was like an iron clamp. He didn't say a single word. He didn't yell or taunt me. His face was completely blank, his breathing slow and rhythmic, like he was performing routine maintenance on a piece of machinery.

My hand swept across the floorboards until my fingers closed around the hammer that had dropped during the fall. I brought it up with everything I had left.

It hit his shoulder first, then I struck again, catching him along the side of the jaw. He collapsed sideways onto the rug. I didn't stop until he stopped moving.

The police arrived twenty minutes later, called by the neighbors who heard the screaming through the vents. They took David's body away in a black vinyl bag. Mark was taken to the hospital in an ambulance; he had a severe concussion and three broken ribs, but the doctors said the skull hadn't fractured.

After hours of interrogations at the precinct while technicians sealed off the blood-soaked unit behind yellow tape, we knew we could never step foot in that place again. We never went back for our furniture, our clothes, or our kitchenware. We surrendered the deed to the lender, took the hit, and walked away from every dollar we had put into the down payment. We rented a split-level house two hours away, out in the suburbs, with a dirt yard and trees that actually grow in the ground.

Mark recovered. Sarah gave birth to a healthy girl seven months later. On the surface, the family went back to normal. We don't talk about the apartment, and we don't keep a television in the living room.  
The only thing that didn't go away was my phone.  
A month after the move, I sat on the porch while the sun was going down and opened the camera app to take a picture of Sarah holding the baby on the lawn. The screen resolved their faces clearly, soft and bright against the grass. Then I flipped the camera around to my own face.

My eyes and mouth were gone, smeared across the glass in the exact same streak of digital noise, completely static, completely unchanged.
