I hadn't seen my grandfather in eleven years when I found out he was dead.

Even saying *my grandfather* felt strangely intimate. He had always existed somewhere at the edge of the family, mentioned occasionally but rarely discussed. There hadn't been an argument between us, at least not one I knew about. We simply weren't close. He lived his life, I lived mine, and somewhere along the way enough years passed that neither of us seemed particularly interested in changing that.

So when a solicitor contacted me three weeks after his death, I assumed it had something to do with paperwork. Perhaps there was something my mother needed to sign, or some forgotten account had my name attached to it. I nearly ignored the first email.

The second one had the words **sole beneficiary** in it.

That got my attention.

My girlfriend was sitting beside me when I called them. We'd been together long enough that there wasn't much ceremony left in moments like this. She put her phone down, leaned against me and listened while I tried to understand how a man I'd barely spoken to in more than a decade had somehow left almost everything to me.

There wasn't a fortune waiting in a bank account. No secret collection of expensive cars. Most of what remained had apparently been consumed over the years by maintenance, taxes and expenses associated with one particular property.

A castle.

I actually laughed when the solicitor said it.

Not a mansion people liked calling a castle because it had a turret somewhere. An actual castle, although a relatively small one, buried deep inside a forest several hours from the nearest major town. My grandfather had owned it for more than forty years.

I'd never even heard of it.

That was the first strange thing.

The second was that nobody could give me a particularly convincing explanation for why he'd left it to me.

There were other relatives. People he'd spoken to more recently than me. People who had visited him, sent Christmas cards, attended birthdays. Yet his will was explicit. The property, the surrounding land and everything remaining inside belonged to me.

My girlfriend thought it was hilarious.

"You own a fucking castle."

"I apparently own a fucking castle."

For the next hour we sat at the kitchen table looking through photographs the solicitor had sent. Most were old, probably taken for administrative records rather than to make the place look appealing. Grey stone walls disappeared behind enormous trees. Ivy covered sections of the western side. There was a courtyard, a narrow tower and what looked like a chapel attached to the oldest part of the building.

It wasn't beautiful in the polished, postcard sense.

It was severe.

But there was something about it.

The forest surrounding the property was part of the inheritance too, several hectares of land that had remained largely untouched. According to the documents, the castle itself was centuries old. My grandfather hadn't built it or even restored most of it. He'd bought it in the early eighties from a private foundation that had held the property for decades.

Before that, it had belonged to the Church.

That was where the history became interesting.

For an unknown period of time, the castle had been occupied by a small order of nuns. The records weren't clear about when they arrived. Some documents placed them there in the late nineteenth century, while others suggested the order had existed on the property much earlier. They weren't famous. There were no stories about miracles or scandals, no tourist mythology attached to them. Eventually the order disappeared, the property changed hands, and whatever happened there became another paragraph in the history of an old building.

We didn't think much of it.

Why would we?

Within two days, curiosity had become a plan.

We weren't going there because I suddenly cared about my grandfather. I didn't feel some mysterious pull toward my ancestry, and I wasn't searching for answers about why he'd chosen me. At least, not then.

We went because an impossible opportunity had landed in our laps.

  
We could spend a few weeks there, inspect the proprty, figure out what condition it was in and decide what to do with it. Maybe renovate it. Maybe sell it. Maybe keep it as somewhere completely removed from the rest of our lives.

My girlfriend was already imagining what we could do with the interior. I was thinking about the land.

Neither of us seriously considered refusing it.

A week later, we packed the car.

The final stretch of the journey took us through villages that became progressively smaller until eventually there were no villages at all. The road narrowed, mobile reception disappeared, and the forest closed around us.

It was much denser than the photographs suggested.

The trees were enormous, their branches meeting high above the road and swallowing most of the afternoon light. Ferns and moss covered the ground between them. Every few kilometres I'd expect to see another house, a farm, anything.

There was nothing.

My girlfriend had been talking for most of the drive, but eventually even she became quiet.

"How much farther?"

I checked the navigation.

"Eleven minutes."

She looked through the window.

"Your grandfather really didn't like neighbours."

"Maybe that's hereditary."

She laughed and rested her hand on my leg.

Then we reached the gate.

Two stone pillars stood between the trees, almost completely covered in moss. The iron gate between them was already open.

I slowed the car.

"Did they say someone would be here?"

"No."

I stopped completely.

Beyond the gate, a narrow road continued through the forest.

My girlfriend looked at me.

"What?"

"The gate."

"What about it?"

I wasn't sure.

There was nothing particularly frigtening about an open gate. The property manager could have left it that way. The solicitor might have arranged it. Someone could have visited earlier.

Still, I remembered one detail from the documents.

The property had been vacant since my grandfather died.

Three months ago.

I looked down the road again.

Then I drove through.

It took another five minutes before the trees finally opened.

And there it was.

For the first time since receiving the inheritance, neither of us said anything.

The photographs hadn't captured the scale of it.

The castle rose directly from the forest, dark stone against a colourless afternoon sky, its oldest walls almost indistinguishable from the rock beneath them. Trees crowded around it so closely that parts of the building seemed to disappear into the woods. The narrow tower I'd seen in the photographs stood above everything else.

My girlfriend slowly leaned forward.

"Holy shit."

I smiled.

"Still think we should sell it?"

She didn't answer immediately.

She was staring at one of the upper windows.

I followed her gaze.

"What?"

Her expression changed.

For a second, I thought she'd seen someone.

Then she shook her head.

"Nothing."

"You sure?"

"Yeah."

She opened the car door.

"Come on. I want to see inside."

I looked at the window once more before getting out.

There was nothing there.

At least, I didn't see anything.

Years later, when I tried to remember the exact moment everything began, I kept coming back to that afternoon.

Not the first night.

Not the first voice.

Not even what we eventually found beneath the chapel.

It was her standing beside the car, staring up at a window in a castle that had belonged to me for less than a week.

Because much later, she finally told me what she'd seen.

There had been a woman standing behind the glass.

And she hadn't been looking at me.

She'd been looking at her..
