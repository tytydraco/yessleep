It’s been a little over a month since my wife died, and I still miss her like it’s the first day. I won’t go into detail, but she couldn’t fight anymore. She let herself live out her final days at our cottage, surrounded by nature and life.

When she was still alive, I would go there every weekend just to sit on the porch and play our song. The tradition continued beyond her. I often wondered if she really loved me playing the fiddle, or if she had simply been a great wife. But it doesn’t matter any more: music is all I can give her now.

I sat on a lawn chair in our yard. The fire was about to go out, and I would go to bed with it. This was our ritual: I would play for her just before she went to sleep every night I could.

With my eyes closed and the warmth of the fire just about keeping away the chill of the night, I could feel every note. I could almost see her smile once again.

“What a beautiful song,” she said, the wind carrying her whispers to my ears even through the tune.

But it wasn’t her voice. It was as if the woods themselves had spoken, the thousand voices of the forest somehow in perfect harmony.

“She’s been listening along,” the voice continued.

I stopped playing and sprang to my feet. I spun around quickly, looking for the intruder. There was nothing near me, nothing close enough for such a quiet voice to reach me. I turned my gaze toward the surrounding forest, but I could see only the outline of countless trees. Until I spotted them: two white sparks in the night. They were attached to what at first appeared to be just another tree. It wasn’t.

They were its eyes.

The figure stood only slightly shorter than the surrounding trees. I could discern the shape of its shoulders and the gap between its legs, but two great ligneous masses, akin to deer antlers, sprouted from what would be its head, allowing it to blend in with the forest.

“WHAT THE FUCK!” my body yelled.

“I am what most call evil,” the breeze answered.

“For I caused upheaval.

But to you I am a knight,

I will bring her back tonight.”

My brain wanted to run, get in my car and just speed off, but my body wouldn’t obey, paralyzed by fear.

The figure grew taller as it glided toward me, as though I were looking not at the creature itself, but at the towering shadow it cast. A gale picked up, carrying its words more clearly than before.

“So what do you say?

Shall we play her death away?”

“Wha…” I managed to let out.

“Let us brawl on the violin.

For I hear your talent true.

New life for her if you win,

But you will stake your… you.”

I was terrified by it, frozen by a primeval dread etched in my humanity. But not even the Arctic winter can extinguish the embers of love.

“That’s impossible,” I countered, forcing our reality back into place.

The shadow slowly lifted one of its long bough-like arms, exposing the claws that sprouted from its hand like branches. As it moved, the wind whistled, swirling into a twister at the edge of the firelight, just between me and it. The force from it was awesome, and I had to put a foot forward to brace myself.

From a small vortex on the ground, the phenomenon grew taller and taller, until it reached roughly the height of my neck—about my wife’s height. The storm collapsed on itself, sculpting its chaos into her form. Everything about her was perfect. Every curve and every blemish was exactly where I remembered it. This was not the dying woman she had been forced to become; it was her as she had always been within, even in the end.

She simply shook her head, a movement amplified by her long hair flowing in her own current. This was the only thing she got to do before its hooks flew through her, dispelling her in one fell swoop.

Then it stepped into the light, revealing it was but a man.

It was a tall gentleman, dressed in a tailcoat, red like the rose, with matching pants and shoes. The coat opened to reveal a pristine white dress shirt and bow tie. His face was all sharp angles, crowned by a golden mane. Only its eyes remained unchanged: two stars easily overpowering the dying fire.

“How do I know ya won’t cheat and just say you’re the winner?” I asked, any panic left in my body having burnt away at the sight of her.

He smiled and, for the first time, spoke with a voice truly his. It was almost disappointing how human he sounded, like an American trying to sound like a posh Brit.

“Why would I cheat? Victory tastes so sweet!” he exclaimed.

There was little reason to trust him, but there was nothing I wouldn’t do to see her again. Besides, if he had wanted me dead, I doubt he would have had to try.

“Alright, I’ll take ya bet,” I said, extending my palm.

Before I could even fully extend my hand, his was already in mine, sealing the deal with a firm but polite shake.

When we let go, he adjusted his bow tie before clasping his hands as though in prayer.

“Do lend me your bow, so I can start this show,” he asked.

“Ha!” I laughed. “Ya ain’t even got ya own strings?”

He groaned.

“I do seem to have lost it to someone playing a real hit.”

I chuckled again and offered him my instrument. He carefully took it from my hand, as though I were entrusting him with a baby I had been cradling. When he finally had it firmly in his hands, he let out a deep sigh of relief, and a chill breeze blew from behind him.

He placed the fiddle against his neck and raised the bow in the air. When he let it loose on the strings, a somber melody began. He played a beautiful, poignant song that reminded me of her funeral.

“Come on, this ain’t a wake,” I spat, trying to take my mind off her.

A smile spread across his face, welcoming the challenge.

I can hardly put into words what happened next. His fingers sped up, prancing gracefully over the strings, and the bow flew effortlessly, conjuring an air that shouldn’t be. Each note by itself was but a mundane vibration any old musician could recreate, but the piece they formed together was anything but. It told a story I couldn’t understand, but one so compelling that even the wind could do nothing but dance along, sweeping the surrounding trees and leaves into its rhythm. The fire could hardly survive the jig, dimming erratically and exposing the true form of his shadow.

Then the tune stopped, the final note silenced as soon as the bow left the string, as if existence itself smothered the sound before it could drift too far from him.

He simply bowed and extended his arms to offer my fiddle back.

“A beautiful tool for a true duel,” he praised.

I snatched the violin out of his hand and shouldered it. Theatrics would accomplish nothing but delay her return. As soon as I did, hell broke loose in my body, and I played like a man possessed. The strings bit into my fingers, flaying them open with each note. As my body bled over the instrument, so did my soul bleed its truth into reality. I’ve never played as I did in that moment, and I doubt I ever will again.

I didn’t care to look at him. I stared into the blackness behind my eyelids, where I could still see her, her image renewed by his powers.

As I drew the bow for the final note, the strings snapped in unison, slashing me across the face in retribution.

All three of us had just communed in the song of my life.

But it wasn’t enough.

There was no denying it, and there was no lying about it. I knew for a fact that I had lost. I had played like the greatest of men, but he had always been more.

I opened my eyes to the state of my fiddle: its neck painted red, its strings curled up near the tuning pegs. Four bloody remnants of my fingertips still clung to the fingerboard.

At least it shared my pain.

The man burst into laughter—the genuine, warm laughter of someone who had just heard the best joke of his life. He laughed and laughed, clapping his hands as he did. When he finally managed to restrain himself, he turned around and walked away.

“Wait!” I cried out. “What ’bout our bet?”

He shook his head and answered without bothering to look back at me.

“You failed when it mattered: you’re beaten and battered. I’ll let you stew before I collect my due.”
