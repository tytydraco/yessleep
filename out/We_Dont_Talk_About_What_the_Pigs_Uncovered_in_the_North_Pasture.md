Pigs are vastly more intelligent than dogs. Ask anyone who has ever worked a commercial farm or raised livestock in the deep country, and they’ll tell you the same thing. They problem-solve. They hold grudges. They recognize faces, and worse, they recognize \\\*intent\\\*. When you walk into a pen with a bucket of slop, they look at you with a hungry sort of reverence. When you walk in with a stun gun, they look at you with cold, understanding terror.

But what bothers me most about pigs isn’t their intelligence. It’s their eyes. Tucked beneath those heavy, bristly brows, pig eyes are pale, wet, and disturbingly human.

My older brother, Caleb, and I inherited seventy acres of dying land in an unnamed, unincorporated county three miles from the nearest blacktop. The property had been bleeding money since the late nineties. Our last desperate venture was a herd of forty Yorkshire-cross pigs, housed primarily in the North Pasture.

The North Pasture was a miserable stretch of land. It sat at the lowest elevation of the property, backing up against a dense, suffocating wall of black-ash trees and briars. Because it sat in a basin, the drainage was nonexistent. The mud there was always knee-deep, viscous, and black, smelling faintly of sulfur and fermented apples. We hated going down there. The air always felt ten degrees colder, heavy and still, as if the surrounding woods were holding their breath.

It was mid-November when Caleb first noticed the feed troughs were full.

Frost had already begun to choke the morning grass, locking the mud into sharp, frozen ridges across most of the property. But when Caleb came back from the morning rotation, his boots were coated in thick, steaming black muck.

"They ain't touching the corn," he grumbled, tossing his gloves onto the porch freezer. "Troughs are full to the brim. Forty head of hogs and they haven't eaten a bite of ration in two days."

"Are they sick?" I asked, pouring him a cup of black coffee.

Caleb shook his head, his expression tight. "No. They’re getting fat. Bloated, almost. And the ground down there… it ain't freezing. It’s warm. Soft like a sponge. They’ve been rooting in the back corner by the tree line. Dug a trench the size of a truck."

Pigs root. It’s what they do. But they don’t ignore a guaranteed meal unless they’ve found something vastly superior.

That afternoon, I grabbed a heavy iron shovel and walked down to the North Pasture with Caleb to fill in the trench before one of the animals snapped a leg in it. The walk down the sloping hill was quiet. Too quiet. Usually, forty hungry Yorkshires create a cacophony of grunts, squeals, and heavy bodies slamming against the corrugated iron fencing. Today, there was absolute silence.

When we reached the perimeter fence, the smell hit me.

It wasn’t the usual farmhouse stench of ammonia and manure. It was a suffocating, metallic odor, like pennies soaking in spoiled milk. I pulled my collar over my nose and gagged. Caleb just grimaced and unlatched the heavy wooden gate.

The pigs were clustered in the far corner, near the edge of the black-ash woods. They were packed together shoulder-to-shoulder, a tight, writhing sea of pale pink flesh. They weren't fighting. They weren't making a sound.

"Hey!" Caleb barked, slapping the flat of the shovel against a wooden fence post. The crack echoed sharply.

None of the pigs turned.

We waded into the muck. Caleb was right; the ground here was inexplicably warm. The heat radiated through the rubber soles of my boots. The mud didn't squelch; it sucked and sighed, clinging to my legs with an almost gelatinous texture.

As we pushed our way through the herd, the animals finally parted. They didn't scatter. They merely shuffled aside, moving with a sluggish, synchronized lethargy that made the hairs on my arms stand up. Their bellies were distended, stretched taut and shiny.

Then I saw the trench.

It wasn't a trench. It was an ulcer in the earth.

The hole was at least ten feet wide and sloping down into the dark. The dirt they had excavated wasn't piled up; it was simply \\\*gone\\\*, as if the ground had collapsed into a hollow subterranean cavity. But it wasn't empty.

Protruding from the soft, steaming black mud were thick, pale roots.

Only, they weren’t roots. They were fleshy, translucent, and pulsed with a slow, rhythmic throb. They looked like massive, calcified umbilical cords, slick with a milky sap that oozed from where the pigs had been gnawing on them.

The largest boar, an eight-hundred-pound monster Caleb had named Barrow, was standing at the bottom of the crater. His snout was buried in the mud, tearing at a thick, fibrous vein of the white tissue. With a sickening \\\*schhhh-luk\\\*, he pulled a long strip of it free. Thick, dark fluid—too dark to be blood, too thick to be water—spilled from the severed membrane.

Barrow turned his head to look at us.

His jaw was unhinged. The things he had been chewing on had somehow altered him. His gums were swollen and bleeding, and pushed through the shredded pink flesh of his mouth were rows of flat, square teeth. Human teeth. Too many of them.

Barrow didn't squeal. He opened his ruined mouth and let out a wet, rattling sigh. It sounded exactly like a man with a crushed windpipe trying to scream.

"What the hell is that?" I whispered, my voice trembling. "Caleb, what the fuck is under our farm?"

Caleb didn't answer. He was staring intensely at the bottom of the hole. Where Barrow had torn the membrane away, the mud seemed to shift and heave. A low, subsonic vibration hummed through the soles of my boots, traveling up my spine until my teeth rattled in my skull.

"We need to fill it," Caleb said, his voice flat, hollowed out by disbelief. "We just need to bury it."

We spent four hours shoveling dirt into the hole. The pigs watched us. They didn't try to stop us, but they didn't leave, either. They stood in a perfect semicircle, their pale, wet eyes locked onto us, blinking slowly. Their breathing was in perfect unison. A chorus of wet, rattling lungs.

By dusk, we had covered the exposed tissue. We locked the gate and walked back to the house in silence. Neither of us ate dinner. I couldn't get the smell of copper and spoiled milk out of my nostrils. I scrubbed my hands with lye soap until the skin cracked, but the slick, gelatinous feeling of that mud remained.

That night, the storm rolled in.

It was a violent, freezing downpour that lashed against the farmhouse windows like handfuls of gravel. Around 2:00 AM, the lights flickered and died. I was already awake, sitting on the edge of my bed, staring into the dark.

I was listening to the pigs.

Over the roar of the rain and the wind, I could hear them. They were singing.

It wasn't a melody. It was a guttural, weeping chorus of human-sounding voices, rising and falling in pitch. It was a sound of absolute ecstasy and absolute agony.

My bedroom door flew open. Caleb stood there, holding his hunting rifle and a heavy yellow flashlight. He looked pale, his eyes wide and panicked.

"They broke the fence," he said. "The gate's smashed."

"Let them go," I pleaded. "Caleb, please. Let them run into the woods. Let them disappear."

"We can't," he whispered. "They aren't leaving. They're digging."

I didn't want to go, but the thought of letting Caleb walk down to that pasture alone was worse than the terror in my gut. I grabbed my rain slicker and a flashlight. We stepped out into the freezing deluge.

The mud tried to pull our boots off with every step. The rain blinded us, stinging our faces like needles. As we crested the hill overlooking the North Pasture, Caleb swept his flashlight beam over the darkness.

The fence was entirely gone. Splintered and dragged away.

The hole was back. But it wasn't ten feet wide anymore. The entire back half of the pasture had caved in, revealing a massive, undulating landscape of pale, raw flesh. It stretched for fifty yards, disappearing beneath the tree line. The black-ash trees had sunk into the meat, their roots woven into the bloated, gray veins of whatever titanic monstrosity lay buried beneath our property. The air was a suffocating fog of steam and rotting iron.

And the pigs. God, the pigs.

They weren't just eating it anymore. \\\*It\\\* was eating \\\*them\\\*.

Caleb’s flashlight beam caught Barrow. The massive boar was half-submerged in a pool of the thick, dark fluid. The pale membrane of the earth had grown up and over his hindquarters, fusing with his flesh. Barrow's skin had turned translucent, his internal organs visible and pulsing in time with the rhythmic heaving of the buried mass.

Other pigs were sinking willingly into the meat of the earth. Their jaws were unhinged, their human teeth chewing frantically at the tissue even as it swallowed them alive. The wet, hacking weeping sounds they made were amplified by the hollow cavity of the pit.

"No," Caleb breathed. "No, no, no."

He raised his rifle. He aimed at Barrow's mutated, fusing head and pulled the trigger.

The gunshot cracked over the storm, loud and definitive. The bullet tore through Barrow’s skull, spraying gray matter and that black fluid across the pulsing membrane.

The weeping stopped. All forty pigs stopped chewing. They turned their heads, their pale, human eyes locking onto us.

Then, the ground groaned.

It wasn't an earthquake. It was a muscle spasm. The entire fifty-yard expanse of exposed flesh contracted violently. A deep, wet tearing sound echoed from the woods as the earth folded inward.

Caleb lost his footing. The mud beneath his boots—mud that had already begun to turn pale and warm—gave way.

He slid screaming down the embankment.

"Caleb!" I lunged forward, throwing myself onto my stomach in the freezing mud, reaching out over the lip of the crater.

Caleb scrambled, his fingers digging frantically into the earth. But the earth wasn't dirt. It was soft, yielding tissue. His hands slipped, pulling away ribbons of white flesh. He tumbled into the pit, landing on the surface of the beating mass.

The pigs didn't attack him. They didn't have to.

The flesh beneath Caleb split open like a toothless mouth. The thick, milky sap erupted around him, clinging to his jacket, his jeans, his face. He screamed—a raw, tearing sound of pure horror—as the ground began to swallow him. The surrounding membrane folded over his legs, fusing instantly to his skin.

I screamed his name, shining the flashlight directly onto him.

Caleb reached up toward me, his face stretched in agony. But then, his expression went slack. His eyes widened, fixing on something beneath the surface of the meat. His jaw dropped open.

He didn't scream anymore. He just began to chew.

I didn't try to save him. I couldn't. I dropped the flashlight and ran. I ran so hard my lungs tasted like blood, tearing through the briars, abandoning the farm, the truck, everything. I didn't stop until I reached the county highway, collapsing in the center line as the rain washed the mud from my hands.

The authorities went to the property three days later when I finally found the courage to speak to them. I told them Caleb fell into a sinkhole. I didn't tell them about the pigs. I didn't tell them about the teeth.

They found nothing. The police report stated that a mudslide had leveled the North Pasture, filling the basin with dense, compacted soil. They dug a few feet down, found nothing but black earth, and declared Caleb lost to the elements.

But I know the truth. They didn't dig deep enough. Or maybe, whatever is down there just pulled its head back beneath the surface to digest.

I live in a city now, three states away. I live on the fifth floor of a concrete building where the ground can't touch me. But sometimes, when it rains heavily in the dead of autumn, the wind shifts. I catch a faint whiff of copper and spoiled milk. And when I close my eyes, I can feel the slow, rhythmic vibration of the floorboards, and hear the wet, hacking sound of my brother, singing in the dark.
