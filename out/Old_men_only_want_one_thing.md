I turned twenty-nine on a Thursday, which meant I spent my birthday teaching twenty-two eight-year-olds the difference between *there*, *their*, and *they’re*. They made me a card that said HAPY BIRTHDAY MS. ELENA YOUR THE BEST TEACHER EVER, and little Dominic had drawn me wearing a crown, with arms down to my knees. I propped it on my desk where I could see it all day.

At home there was Pancake, a fourteen-year-old beagle I’d adopted the year before. He had three teeth and a heart murmur, and he greeted me every evening like I’d come home from a war. Most nights it was just the two of us, a bowl of pasta, and whatever I was grading. A most nights that was enough.

I had a father. He left when I was eight. That spring I’d made it to the final round of the school spelling bee, and I spent the whole competition watching the gym door instead of the judges, expecting him to burst in. He never did. I won anyway, on the word *necessary*. For years afterward I kept the blue ribbon in my desk drawer, in case he ever came back.

After school I bought myself a fuzzy cardigan and some perfume I’d been eyeing for months, because if nobody else was going to spoil me, I was perfectly capable of doing it myself. Then I found a bar with dark booths and photographs of dead people on the walls, and settled into a corner with a margarita and a stack of spelling tests.

I got about fifteen minutes of alone time.

“What’s a young lady doing alone in a place like this?”

Two old men stood at my table. One was tall and silver-haired, in a cream sweater, with a long, kind face. The other was shorter and rounder, with glasses that made his eyes look a little too large. They smiled at me the way people smile at a puppy in a shop window.

“Grading,” I said, holding up a test. “Somebody spelled *because* with a Z. I’m going to need a minute.”

The short one laughed, a big, delighted laugh that turned heads at the bar. “Mind if we keep you company while you grieve?”

They introduced themselves as Mark and Harold. They were eighty-five and seventy-eight, they were charming, and I was two margaritas in, so I moved my spelling tests and let them sit. Harold noticed my shopping bags and asked what the occasion was, and when I told him it was my birthday, he sang to me right there in the booth. He had a lovely voice, warm and a little old-fashioned. Mark joined in, and he definitely did not have a lovely voice. I laughed so hard and my face turned a deep read, I had to beg them to stop.

“How old?” Mark asked.

“Twenty-nine.”

Harold let out a long breath, the way you would at a vintage on a wine list. “Twenty-nine. What I wouldn’t give.”

“Harold,” Mark said mildly. Then, to me: “He means it as a compliment.”

“Don’t worry, I took it as one.”

“Good.” Mark studied my face a beat longer than was normal. “You should.”

Neither of them drank. Harold had orange juice, and Mark had something fizzy with mint. They’d given it up, Mark explained, because it was hard on the body. He glanced at my glass and smiled. “Yours can handle it, of course.”

They paid my tab before I could reach for it, and then they took me to a winery, and then a comedy club, where Harold critiqued every punchline in a whisper until I was crying with laughter into my drink. In between, they asked about my life, and they actually listened. I told them about little Dominic, who wanted to be a shark when he grew up, and Maya, who corrected my Spanish with the patience of a saint. Mark listened to every word like he was memorizing it. Harold asked follow-up questions about the shark. By the end of the night, they knew the names of half my class.

It was the best birthday I’d had in years.

At exactly ten o’clock, they both stood up at once, as if someone had flipped a switch. Mark said they’d better go. I pointed out that the show wasn’t over, and he said, warmly, that he was afraid it was for them. Harold wrote an email address on the back of a receipt and pressed it into my hand. “We’d love to see you again,” he said, and he sounded like he meant it.

“Wait.” I dug through my purse, because teachers always have stickers, and peeled off two gold stars. I stuck one on each of their sweaters. “For excellent behavior. And above-average singing. Mostly Harold.”

Harold laughed. Mark looked down at his star and touched it, the way you’d touch a medal.

“Thank you, dear,” he said.

I watched them walk away down the sidewalk, Mark’s hand resting lightly on Harold’s back, two little gold stars catching the streetlight.

My mother called the next day, and I made the mistake of telling her everything.

“Two men,” she said. “Bought you drinks. Gave you their email.”

“They were sweet, Mom.”

“Mija. Men only want one thing.”

“Mom, they’re eighty-five and seventy-eight. I don’t think they want *that* thing. I think they want someone to listen to them talk about their cholesterol.”

“Eighty-five, a hundred. They all want it.” She sighed the long, tired sigh of a woman who had raised a daughter alone and knew exactly how the world worked. “Just be careful.”

I put the receipt on my fridge, under an apple-shaped magnet a student had given me. Every morning while the coffee brewed, I looked at it and told myself I’d write tomorrow. It took me thirty-one days. When I finally sent a short, nervous little email, Harold replied in eleven minutes, all in capitals: OF COURSE WE REMEMBER YOU.

They took me to a steakhouse. I’m a vegetarian, but I hadn’t mentioned it, and Mark was so genuinely troubled when he found out that I almost ordered a steak just to make him feel better.

“You should eat properly, dear,” he said. “Protect what you’ve got.”

“I’ve got mashed potatoes,” I winked. “I’m well protected.”

Afterward, they brought me to Mark’s house by the ocean. It was old and beautiful, with a wraparound porch and rosebushes along the fence, and every shelf inside held photographs. Most of them were young women: at graduations, at birthday parties, on the beach, sitting at the very piano in the corner of the room. In some of them, Mark and Harold stood on either side of the girl, each with a hand on her shoulder.

“Who are they?” I asked.

“Our girls,” Harold said.

“Former students,” Mark said, at the same moment.

They looked at each other, and then they both laughed. “Both,” Mark said. “Some of both.”

I asked if they still saw them. Harold sat down at the piano and played a few soft notes before he answered. “Oh,” he said. “They don’t come around much anymore.” Something passed across his face when he said it, something that looked a lot like grief. I thought it must be hard, loving people who grew up and moved on.

Mark made tea, and Harold played old songs I’d never heard, and at one point Harold leaned over and examined my neck up close, like he was checking a used car for rust. “What are those little lines?” he asked.

“Harold. Are you inspecting me for defects?”

“Just noticing,” he said, and patted my hand.

At ten o’clock, the mantel clock chimed, and they walked me to my car. When I got home, Pancake sniffed me for a long time, his tail perfectly still. Then he went and slept in the other room.

I’d fallen asleep on the couch, and the next morning, my back ached.

After that, they became part of my weeks the way weather does, so gradually I didn’t notice when it happened. There were Sunday dinners and Wednesday phone calls. Harold emailed me terrible jokes, always in capitals. Mark mailed me postcards even though we lived twenty minutes apart, with neat, slanted handwriting that said things like *Thinking of you. Hope the Z-speller is improving.*

Mark started teaching me piano on Sunday evenings. I was hopeless at first, but he was patient in a way I’d never experienced. He’d put his hand over mine on the keys and say, “Again, slower,” and he never once sighed. I bought a cheap keyboard and practiced at night after grading, while Pancake snored on my feet.

One Saturday, Mark showed up with a small toolbox and fixed the hinge on my kitchen cabinet that had been hanging crooked for a year. He made me hold the screwdriver and walked me through every step. “So you’ll know how, next time,” he said. I could have watched a video and done it myself, but I didn’t tell him that. I just stood very close to him in my little kitchen and did exactly what he said.

I found my first gray hair in November. Then three. Then six. My knees started cracking when I sat down on the reading rug for story time, and the kids thought it was hilarious. They started making cracking sound effects whenever I lowered myself down, and I took a theatrical bow every time.

At a Christmas market in December, a woman grabbed Mark’s sleeve. She looked sixty, gray-faced and stooped, but she was wearing a university sweatshirt with a graduation year on it that couldn’t have been more than five years old.

“Mark,” she said. Her voice shook.

“Hello, dear.” He patted her hand with great tenderness. “So good to see you.”

She turned to me, and her grip on my wrist was surprisingly strong. “Go home before ten,” she said. “Do you hear me? *Before ten.*”

Harold gently peeled her fingers away and said, “Let’s get you a cocoa, sweetheart.” When he came back alone a few minutes later, he shook his head sadly. “Poor thing. Early onset.” Then Mark bought me a huge knitted scarf from one of the stalls and wrapped it around my neck himself, tucking the ends in like I was a little girl on her way to school.

But I didn’t like the way she’d been led off. So when they stopped at a candle stall, I slipped away and found her alone on a bench at the edge of the market, her cocoa untouched beside her.

I sat down next to her. “I’m Elena. What’s your name?”

She looked at me for a long moment, as if she were trying to remember how to answer. “Claire,” she said finally. “I’m a pediatric nurse. Well, I was.”

“I teach third grade,” I said. “Between us, we’ve got every kid in the county covered.”

She smiled. Her hands were shaking, so I took off my new scarf and wrapped it around her neck, and she touched the wool like she hadn’t been given anything in a very long time. When Mark found me a few minutes later, I turned to answer him, and when I turned back, Claire was gone. I looked for her for the rest of the night.

Right before winter break, the district named me Teacher of the Year.

There was a ceremony in the high school auditorium, with a podium and folding chairs and a slideshow that kept freezing. My mother couldn’t get off work, so she watched on a video call from her break room, her face tiny and proud on my principal’s phone. I’d told Mark and Harold about it in passing.

When they called my name, I walked up to the podium with my heart pounding, and I looked out into the audience, and there they were in the second row. Mark was wearing a suit and tie. Harold was already crying. On each of their lapels was a small, slightly crumpled gold star.

I’d prepared a speech, but I don’t remember a word of it. I remember that I didn’t look at the door once.

Afterward, Mark hugged me for a long time. “We’re so proud of you,” he said into my hair. Harold kept wiping his eyes with a handkerchief and telling strangers, “That’s our girl,” until I had to drag him away from a very confused janitor.

“The good ones are always worth the wait,” he said in the parking lot, kissing my cheek. I was beaming and told him he was a sap.

That night, I took the blue ribbon out of my desk drawer and put it on the fridge, next to the receipt.

In January, Harold took me on a picnic up the coast. Mark had an appointment, so it was just the two of us, driving north past fields of Brussels sprouts and pale winter hills. He’d packed enough food for a small army, including green grapes, because I’d mentioned once, months earlier, that I liked them better than red. I didn’t even remember telling him.

He outpaced me on the trail. Halfway up, I had to stop, my hand pressed against my lower back, laughing at myself between breaths. “I’m twenty-nine,” I wheezed, “and apparently held together with glue.”

“And I’m seventy-eight,” he said cheerfully, “and I walk every day.” Then he waited with me until I was ready, and offered me his arm for the rest of the climb.

On the cliff, with the Pacific spread out gray-blue beneath us, he told me about his two marriages and how neither had worked out, and how he’d always wanted a daughter.

“What would you have done with her?” I asked.

He looked out at the ocean for a long time. “Taught her to ride a bike. Embarrassed her in front of her friends. Kept her close.”

I didn’t know what to say to that, so I said the true thing. “I think you would’ve been a good dad, Harold.”

He looked at me so long that I got embarrassed and offered him a grape. He took it, and then he took my hand and held it, and we sat like that, not talking, until the sun started going down.

Before we left, he set his camera on a rock, ran back on the timer, and put his arm around me just as it clicked. Mark had told him to get a picture. On the way down, my knee buckled, and he caught me easily and carried my bag the rest of the way. I followed behind him, my hand on my back, wondering how a man nearly fifty years older than me could move like that, and deciding I really needed to get off the reading rug and go for more walks.

In March, I cooked my mother’s ceviche for them at my apartment. I cleaned for two days beforehand, and called my mom three times to ask about the lime juice, and she was so happy I was cooking for someone that she forgot to warn me about men.

Pancake growled at Mark the second he walked in. Pancake had never growled at anyone in his life. He’d once licked a burglar alarm technician for forty minutes. “He just doesn’t know me yet,” Mark said, and smiled at him until he slunk out of the room.

They loved the food, or they were kind enough to pretend. Harold had thirds. Mark picked up the photo of me in my cap and gown, the first person in my family to graduate from college, and held it for a long time.

“I’m sorry we weren’t there,” he said.

“We hadn’t met yet.”

“I know. I would’ve liked to see it.” He looked at the photo, and then he looked at my face for even longer, and there was something so tender in his expression that I had to turn away and pretend to check the stove.

When they left, I hugged them both at the door and said, “Love you guys.” It slipped out before I could stop it, and for a second nobody said anything at all. Then Harold said, “Oh, sweetheart,” and kissed the top of my head, and said they loved me too, and Mark squeezed my shoulder and said, “Very much.”

I sent them home with leftovers in containers labeled MARK and HAROLD in my best teacher handwriting, each with a sticky note on the lid: DRINK WATER. I MEAN IT. At 9:58, Harold emailed me a photo of an empty container. OBEYED, it said.

I sat at my kitchen table for a long time after that, looking at the ribbon on the fridge, and I don’t think I had ever been so happy.

By April, my hands were swelling at the joints, and brown spots were appearing on the backs of them. I bought compression gloves and expensive creams and told myself it was the chalk, the cold, the stress.

At recess, Maya held my hand while we watched the four-square game. She turned it over in hers and studied it with great seriousness. “Ms. Elena,” she said, “your hands look like my abuela’s.”

“Is that good?”

She thought about it. “My abuela’s really old.”

The kids around her giggled, and I did too. Then I spent my lunch break in the staff bathroom, staring at my knuckles under the fluorescent lights.

Mark, meanwhile, looked wonderful. His cheeks had filled out, and the deep lines around his mouth had softened. When I asked him what his secret was, he smiled and said, “Plenty of sleep. Ten o’clock, every night.” Harold had stopped wearing his glasses. I told them they were both aging backward, and they laughed, and Mark reached across the table and squeezed my swollen hand very gently, like it might break.

In May, I video-called my mother, and she went very quiet.

“Mija,” she said. “You look older than me.”

“Thanks, Mom.”

“I’m not joking. Come home. Tonight. I’ll buy the train ticket.”

“I’m having dinner with Mark and Harold on Friday.”

“What do they want from you?”

“Nothing, Mom. That’s the thing. They don’t want anything.”

I looked at her on the little screen, my mother, who had worked two jobs and still learned to email so she could send me recipes, and who had waited up for every phone call I’d ever promised to make. “I love you,” I said. “I’ll come home next month. I promise. I’ll make you the ceviche this time.”

“Next month,” she said, like she was writing it down.

I ended the call. For a moment, I barely recognized my reflection on the black screen.

On Friday, Pancake whined at the door when I left. He’d never done that before either. I knelt down, slowly, because of my knees, and kissed the top of his gray head and told him I’d be home soon.

Harold made vegetarian lasagna, slightly burned around the edges, which he insisted was a choice. Mark answered the door in a blue shirt I’d never seen before. His hair was darker.

“There’s our girl,” he said, and hugged me so tightly my back popped.

On the shelf by the piano, among all the other girls, was a new frame: Harold and me on the cliff, squinting into the sun, his arm around my shoulders. Someone had stuck a little gold star on the corner of the glass.

I’d made it onto the shelf.

“Took you long enough,” Harold said, grinning, and I laughed and hugged him too, and I didn’t let go for a while.

After dinner, my neck ached, and Mark offered to rub my shoulders. “I used to do this for my students,” he said, “before performances. To help them relax.” I sat on the floor in front of the couch, and he pressed his thumbs into the muscles beneath my skull. Something cracked. Then something else. My head felt heavy and warm, and I let it tip forward.

Harold stopped playing and came to sit beside me. He took my hand. His skin was smooth and warm. Mine looked like Maya’s abuela’s, thin and loose over the knuckles, spotted like a map. I looked up at him, and he looked so young. His cheeks were pink, his eyes were bright, and he looked happy.

“Time’s a funny thing,” he said softly.

“You two spoil me,” I said. “What do you even get out of this?”

Harold smiled. “Just one thing.”

“Which is?”

Mark’s hands went still on my shoulders, just for a moment, and Harold said, very quietly, “Mark.”

“Close your eyes, dear,” Mark said.

I did. The mantel clock ticked. I could feel my skin folding under his fingers.

Harold went back to the piano and played the old song about a girl waiting for someone to come home. His voice cracked on the last verse, and I opened my eyes long enough to see him wipe his face with the back of his hand.

“Harold?”

“It’s nothing, sweetheart,” he said. “It’s just a sad song.”

Mark leaned down and kissed my forehead. “You look so young when you’re resting,” he said.

“That’s a diplomatic way of saying I look terrible awake.”

“Oh, sweetheart,” he said, and he sounded almost amused, and almost like he was crying. “Not at all.”

He rested his cheek on the top of my head, and I reached back and found his hand. “Love you, Mark.”

“Love you too, dear girl.”

When the clock struck ten, neither of them asked me to leave. And I was so happy about that.

I woke up on Mark’s couch the next morning with the scarf from the Christmas market folded over my legs. The house was empty. The piano was closed, and the shelves were bare except for a single framed photo of me and Harold on the cliff, still with its little gold star. 

I checked my phone. They had emailed me this morning. All capitals, from Harold’s account.
