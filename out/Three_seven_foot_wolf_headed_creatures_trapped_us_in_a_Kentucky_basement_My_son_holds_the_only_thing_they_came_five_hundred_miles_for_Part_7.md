That single word hung in the stale basement air like smoke.

*"Boy."*

It didn't sound like a bark or a growl. It sounded like something heavy dragging across dry gravel, forced up through a throat never meant to shape English vowels.

Behind me, Sarah clapped both hands over Wyatt’s mouth, pulling him backward behind the stack of seasoned oak firewood until his back hit the concrete wall. Wyatt didn't fight her, didn't cry out he just stared up the wooden stairs with wide, unblinking eyes, his small fingers curled tightly around that braided river stone.

Bill didn't move a muscle beside the furnace. His knuckles were white around the stock of his rifle, the sights lined up dead center on the oak basement door ten feet above us.

On the other side of that door, nothing moved. Not a creak of the floorboards. Not a scrape of claws. Just three slow, heavy sets of breathing deep, ragged, and patient waiting on the kitchen landing.

"Mark," Bill breathed, barely moving his lips, his eyes never leaving the door frame. "That slide bolt's held into pine trim with two inch wood screws. If all three of 'em hit it together, it's coming off the hinges."

"I know," I whispered back, my thumb resting hard against the safety of my shotgun.

I looked down at Wyatt huddled behind the woodpile, then back at the flat stone clutched in his fist. Over those two sleepless nights on my brother in law’s couch in Cleveland, I’d burned through half my phone battery scouring every old northern legend I could find Inuit stories about the woman who took a dog for a mate, and the half human, half wolf children cast out into the woods.

In every single version of those old stories, those creatures weren't mindless beasts. They didn't hunt for sport, and they didn't break into houses without a reason. They lived by strict, ancient rules of blood, meat, and debt. Wyatt had fed the wounded one from his own bowl, and he’d accepted its stone in return. To them, the trade wasn't finished.

"Wyatt," I whispered, keeping my shotgun leveled at the top of the stairs as I took one slow step sideways toward him. "Give me the rock."

Wyatt looked up at me, his chest rising and falling fast. "Dad, no"

"Give it to me, son. Now."

Sarah pried his fingers open gently, took the smooth stone and pressed it into my left palm. It felt strangely warm, slick with oil and smelling faintly of creek mud and woodsmoke.

At the exact second my fingers closed around the stone, the floorboards at the top of the stairs groaned under a massive shift of weight.

Three slow raps echoed off the oak door again. *Knock. Knock. Knock.*

Then, the heavy iron doorknob rattled once not violently, just tested by a thick, clawed hand checking the latch.

"They're not breaking it down," Bill muttered under his breath, sweat beading along his forehead in the dim glow of the bare basement bulb. "Why the hell aren't they breaking it down?"

"Because they're waiting for him to come out," I said quietly.

I looked around the basement concrete walls on four sides, no storm cellar door, no windows wide enough for a grown man to squeeze through, just two narrow high set vents near the ceiling joists. There was no back way out. If we stayed down here until dawn, either they’d get tired of waiting and rip the door off its frame, or they’d wait us out until we had to come up.

"Bill," I said, keeping my voice low enough that it wouldn't carry up the stairwell. "How many rounds you got in that Marlin?"

"Six in the tube, one in the chamber," Bill answered, steady as stone. "Hundred seventy grain flat nose. Hit like a freight train, but if you said your twelve gauge slugs bounced off their hide..."

"They bounced off his shoulder," I corrected him, looking up at the narrow gap under the basement door where three dark shadows blocked out the faint moonlight coming from the kitchen windows. "Not his eyes. Not his throat."

I handed my shotgun to Sarah. She looked at me like I’d lost my mind, her hands shaking as she took the heavy walnut stock.

"Mark, what are you doing?" she whispered, panic cracking her voice for the first time since we left Ohio. "Don't you dare open that door."

"I'm not opening it all the way," I told her, sliding my Buck folding knife out of my belt sheath and snapping the four-inch steel blade open with my thumb. "You stay right behind the furnace with Wyatt and your mother. If anything comes down those steps, you pull the trigger and don't stop racking until it's empty."

I walked slowly up the first three wooden steps, the stairs creaking softly under my boots.

Instantly, the heavy breathing behind the door stopped. Through the inch wide gap beneath the oak panel, I saw a massive shadow drop down low one of them crouching right outside the threshold, pressing its snout close to the crack.

I stopped halfway up the stairs, holding the braided stone up in my left hand and the open knife in my right.

"I have it!" I called out, keeping my voice hard and flat, refusing to let it shake. "The stone. The boy gave it back."

Silence. Thick, suffocating silence pressed down on the stairwell. Even the furnace seemed to stop humming.

Then, from just inches behind the wood, that low, grinding voice scraped through the crack under the door.

*"Not... yours."*

My pulse hammered in my ears. Beside the furnace below me, Bill slowly cocked the hammer back on his .30-30 with a sharp, metallic *click*.

"He's eight years old," I said, taking one more step up until my face was three feet from the oak panel. "He didn't know what he was trading. You got your meat back home. Take your stone and get off this land."  
On the landing above, heavy claws dragged slowly down the outside of the door, a long, screeching scrape that raised every hair on my arms.

"Dad..." Wyatt whispered from behind the woodpile, his voice small and tight. "There's two more by the vents."

My stomach dropped.

I snapped my head up toward the ceiling joists behind me. Pressing against the narrow, frosted glass of the two foundation vents near the back wall were two broad, dark snouts and two pairs of glowing amber eyes watching every move we made from outside while the third stood right on the other side of the basement door.

We weren't holding a chokepoint. We were sitting inside a concrete box with all three exits covered.  
At the top of the stairs, the oak door suddenly bowed inward with a violent *crack* as a massive shoulder slammed against the latch, splintering the pine trim around the deadbolt.

"Bill!" I yelled, lunging backward down the steps as the steel screws began tearing out of the wood.  
Upstairs, the second impact hit and the top hinge snapped clean in half.
