I never believed in the old Icelandic stories my grandmother told me. To me, Grýla was just a regional boogeyman—a giantess used by parents to scare kids into eating their vegetables and finishing their chores. 

I was twenty-four, a pragmatic American grad student spending the winter in a remote cabin in the Westfjords to finish my thesis. I didn’t have time for fairy tales.

I should have listened.

It was December 23rd—*Þorláksmessa*. A brutal blizzard had knocked out the power three hours prior, plunging the cabin into pitch blackness save for the dying orange glow of the fireplace. The wind outside didn't just blow; it screamed, violently rattling the thick glass panes.

That was when I heard the sound that made my chest tighten.

It wasn't the wind. It was a rhythmic, heavy *scrape-thud, scrape-thud* echoing from the roof directly above my head. It sounded like something incredibly heavy dragging a blunt tool—or a massive, overgrown claw—across the corrugated tin.

I froze in my armchair, my breath catching in my throat. *An animal,* I reasoned, desperately trying to quiet the instinctual panic flooding my veins. *Just a stray sheep or a disoriented reindeer.*

Then, the smell hit me.

It drifted down through the chimney and the floor vents. It was a suffocating, sickening stench of wet dog, sulfur, and rotting, unwashed meat. It was the smell of a predator’s den.

A massive, wet *sniff* rattled the window frames.

Whatever was on the roof had migrated to the side of the cabin. I slid out of my chair and dropped to my hands and knees, ignoring the freezing chill of the hardwood floor. I crawled toward the kitchen counter, desperate to get out of the line of sight of the large living room windows.

*“Ekki hljóð,”* a voice rasped from just outside the kitchen wall.

The sound didn't feel like it came from a throat. It sounded like two massive tectonic plates grinding together, muffled by ice and dirt. My blood turned to ice. Translated from Icelandic, it meant *“Not a sound.”*

I pressed my back against the kitchen cabinets, pulling my knees to my chest. In the dark, my eyes darted around the room. They landed on the sink. A tower of unwashed dishes from the past four days stared back at me. My laptop lay open on the dining table, surrounded by empty coffee mugs and crumpled food wrappers. I hadn't cleaned. I hadn't been productive. I had succumbed to a deep, lazy winter lethargy.

*“Lazy,”* a wet, bubbling voice whispered. It didn't come from the window this time. It came from the wood stove.

Suddenly, a heavy, deafening thud shook the entire cabin. The roof groaned, the support beams crying out under an immense, sudden weight. Dust and old insulation rained down on my head. Whatever it was, it was directly on top of the kitchen skylight now.

Before I could even process the movement, a low, bass-heavy purr vibrated through the floorboards. It was so deep it rattled my teeth.

A massive shadow blocked out what little moonlight filtered through the frosted skylight. Two glowing yellow eyes—each easily the size of a dinner plate—peered down into the kitchen. Its pupils dilated as they locked onto me. It was a cat, but its face was distorted, its mouth lined with rows of needle-thin, translucent teeth. The Yule Cat.

I looked down at my clothes, my heart hammering like a trapped bird. Brand new wool socks. A sturdy flannel shirt I’d just bought in Reykjavik. The old superstition flashed through my mind: *The Yule Cat eats anyone who hasn't received new clothes before Christmas Eve.* It sniffed the glass, leaving a thick smear of gray slime, but its eyes drifted away from me. I wasn’t its target.

I was it's master's. 

A gnarled, massive hand—with skin resembling rotting black pine bark and fingernails like rusted shovels—shattered the skylight.

Shards of glass exploded into the kitchen, slicing across my face and arms. I clamped my hand over my mouth to stifle a scream. The giant hand reached down, groping blindly in the dark. Its fingers, thick as tree branches, brushed against the dining table, effortlessly snapping the wood in half and crushing my laptop into plastic shards.

From the hole in the roof, a face leaned into the cabin.

She was a towering monstrosity. Her skin was a bruised, mottled gray, hanging in loose, filthy folds around a hooked, broken nose that dripped with frozen slime. Her hair was a tangled, matted nest of frozen moss and dried blood. But it was her eyes that paralyzed me. They were milk-white, entirely devoid of pupils, rolling erratically in their sockets until they snapped forward, pointing straight down at the corner where I cowered.

She smelled me.

Her jaw unhinged with a sickening *crack*, opening impossibly wide to reveal rows of jagged, yellowed teeth coated in a thick, black sludge.

*“Krakkar,”* she croaked, her voice vibrating the air in my lungs. *Children.*

She knew I wasn’t a child, but to a creature that had hunted these mountains for a thousand years, it didn't matter. I was weak. I was idle. I was prey.

She began to force her massive, distorted torso through the shattered skylight. The heavy wooden ceiling beams began to splinter and snap like toothpicks under her weight. A massive, burlap sack, stained with old, dark patches, spilled through the opening, smelling heavily of copper and decay.

Instinct overrode terror. I scrambled to my feet, grabbed the iron fire poker from the hearth, and sprinted for the back door.

I threw it open and burst out into the blinding, sub-zero blizzard. I didn't have a jacket. I didn't have boots. The wind immediately stole the breath from my lungs, and the snow swallowed my legs up to my thighs.

Behind me, a horrific, unnatural shriek tore through the storm. It was a sound of pure, ravenous frustration. I heard the entire back wall of the cabin explode outward as she burst through the frame to pursue me.

I ran blindly toward the distant, faint lights of Route 61, my bare skin freezing in seconds. Every time I took a step, I could hear the rhythmic, heavy thuds in the snow behind me, closing the gap. I knew if I looked back, I would lose my footing, and she would shove me into that foul-smelling sack.

I somehow survived. The next thing I remember is waking up in the hospital. A snowplow driver found me collapsed on the shoulder of the highway, suffering from severe frostbite and hypothermia. The local authorities assumed a freak avalanche had crushed the cabin.

I'm writing this from a hospital bed in Reykjavik. The doctors say I might lose two toes on my left foot. But that's not why I can't sleep.

An hour ago, the nurse left the room, leaving the window slightly cracked to let some fresh air in. As I lay here in the dark, I heard a wet, heavy sniff from the third-floor ledge outside.

And then, a low, rumbling purr.
