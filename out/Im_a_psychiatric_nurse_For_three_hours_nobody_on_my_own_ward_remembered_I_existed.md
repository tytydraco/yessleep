Ferruccio walked up close enough to touch and looked straight through my face. 

He's the nurse who trained me, and for seven years we've worked nights together on a locked psychiatric ward. I was on the floor of our corridor, shouting his name as loud as I could. He ran the beam of his phone along the wall above my head, yawned, and went back up the corridor. 

My left hand was inside the fire door at the end of the corridor. The door was shut, and my hand was in it up to the wrist anyway, held by something cold on the other side. It was pulling. 

Behind me, both arms locked around my chest, was a nurse who had started on our ward that same evening. "He can't hear you," she said into my shoulder. "I can. Keep talking to me." 

That was four nights ago, at half past three. To explain her, I have to go back to eight o'clock. 

At eight that evening we did handover in the nurses' station, the glass booth in the middle of the ward, and there were four of us on nights instead of three. Nights are always two nurses and an aide: Ferruccio, me, and Giulia, our nursing aide. The fourth was Alba, a new nurse on her first shift with us, shadowing me. Nunzia, our head nurse, had called me the day before to say the new nurse had asked for me in particular. "She wants to learn from one of the best," Nunzia said, and I laughed, because her compliments usually come with a schedule change. 

After handover I gave Alba the spare ward keys. When I put them in her palm, my own hand went dead cold to the wrist. A few seconds, and it passed. I couldn't have told you what it was. 

She was quiet, and strangely at ease for somebody new. On rounds she drifted to my other side every time we passed the fire door, and she was always cold. Giulia makes coffee twice a night, at two and at half past four, and we drink it standing up in the staff kitchen, the last door before the fire door, out of little plastic cups. At two, Alba held hers in both hands long after the coffee was gone. 

Then we took our break in the station. Ferruccio put the replay of the evening's game on the television with the sound off, told the referee what he thought of him, and went to walk the corridor. Alba took the couch, pulled an old blanket out of her bag, and wrapped herself in it up to the chin. 

I said my wife leaves the coffee pot loaded on the stove for when I get home from nights, and I asked Alba if she had somebody waiting. She said she lived alone and it suited her. I figured that was just how she was. 

Then I told her, half apologizing, that she was learning the ward from a man on his way out. I'd asked Nunzia to send me back to cardiology, and my transfer request was in my locker, signed and sealed in an envelope, to hand her at seven. "Seven years," I said. "I don't think I've changed a single thing for anybody." 

Alba looked at me over the edge of the blanket. "Was there ever a night it felt worth it? Even one?" 

There was one, and I was tired enough to tell it. 

My first winter on the ward, seven years ago, we had a nursing student on her night rotation. She was twenty-one. She'd come in with a high fever, because one more missed shift and her program wouldn't let her take the exam. Around two I found her on the floor in front of the fire door, shaking. I assumed the fever had put her there. 

I got her away from the patients and into the treatment room, took her temperature and blood pressure, and called the doctor on call. He came up, looked her over, and decided she'd rest in the station until she was fit to go home. So the student went on the couch in the station, wrapped in the blanket from my locker. It was gray fleece, and I'd scorched one corner of it on our old coffee maker. 

Her teeth were chattering. "Maybe this job isn't for me," she said. When I got up to bring her some water, she said, "Don't go." 

The others covered the rounds, and I sat in the armchair beside the couch for the rest of the shift. She'd doze off and jerk awake, and every time she woke up she asked if I was still there. Every time, I gave her the same answer. "I'm here. Till it gets light." I must have said it a hundred times. 

What stayed with me was the cold. The air around her was freezing, and I felt it too, and I didn't have a fever. I reported the heating at the end of the shift. Maintenance wrote back that it was working perfectly. 

She never came back on my nights, and I never knew her name. She wasn't my student, and that year we had a lot of them. I doubt I'd know her face. What I saw that night was a kid with a fever and her hair stuck to her forehead. 

When I finished, Alba asked one question. "You never found out what happened to her?" 

"No. She'd said the job wasn't for her. I think she was right, and she quit. I hope she's okay. I hope she found something easier." 

At ten past three I went to check the fire door. Alba came with me, the blanket still around her shoulders, because shadowing means everywhere, and she stopped a full step short of it. 

I laid my palm flat on the steel above the lock, like I do every night, to feel the door sit tight in its frame. My palm sank into the steel. I could still see the door around my fingers, but there was nothing under them. Something on the other side closed on my hand and pulled, slow and steady, the way a current takes your legs, until my hand was in up to the wrist and I was down on my knees. 

The cold came in at my wrist. That door has leaked a draft for years, and I know what a draft feels like. This climbed: wrist, elbow, chest, until my teeth were chattering and I couldn't feel the fingers of that hand at all. The door has a square of wired glass at head height, and through it you see the landing of the back stairs. Looking up from my knees, I saw the landing was black, lights and all, and those lights never go off. Frost was spreading across the wired glass from the other side. 

I got hold of the door frame with my right hand. Alba threw herself against my back, both arms around my chest, hands locked, and in that same second something came down over the two of us, like a jar over a wasp. The ward was still there, but every sound in it came from far away. 

At half past three Ferruccio came down the corridor on his round, past the kitchen door, all the way to us. I shouted his name, and so did Alba. He turned around and went back. I told her to let go of me and run for him. 

"If I let go, it has you," she said at my ear. "Talk to me. I'll answer." 

So I asked the only thing I could think of. 

"Are you still there?" 

"Yes." 

"Are you still there?" 

"I've got you." 

By the watch pinned to my uniform, which I can read by dropping my chin, it was a quarter to four when Ferruccio came back down the corridor, talking to himself, thin and far off. "That can't be right," he was saying. "They wouldn't leave me the only nurse on tonight." He took out his phone to call the night supervisor, looked at the screen, and put it back in his pocket as if he'd lost the thought. 

Somewhere down the corridor a patient called out, "Nurse?" I answered before I could think. Nobody heard me. From far off Ferruccio called back that he was coming, and his clogs went the other way. 

If this thing got me, nobody would come running. There wouldn't even be a gap. The ward would carry on, Ferruccio would finish his shift, and in my locker there'd be a transfer request signed by a man nobody remembered. 

The cold went through me into Alba. I felt her start to shake against my back, and our breath came out white. When I ran out of ways to ask if she was there, I asked her anything. I asked what my wife would say when I told her about tonight. "Nothing," Alba said. "She'll have the coffee pot loaded." I asked her what she'd had for dinner. "Rice. Out of a container." Whether she owned a car. "A terrible one." Her answers got shorter and broke in the middle. Every time her voice thinned, the pull grew, and by then the door had my forearm. 

At half past four the light came on in the staff kitchen. Turning my head, I could see past Alba into the kitchen, where Giulia was setting out little plastic cups for the second coffee. Four cups. She stopped. She counted them with a finger, counted again, and asked Ferruccio, who had come in behind her, why she'd made four. He looked at the cups for a while and shook his head. They drank theirs. The other two stayed on the counter. 

Ferruccio kept coming back to our door. The third time, the frost had come through to our side of the wired glass. His clog was against my knee and he didn't feel it. He put his palm flat on the glass and kept it there a moment longer than made sense, then pulled it back and stood looking at his hand. When he walked away, his handprint stayed in the frost. 

A little after five, Alba lifted her head and spoke past me, to whatever was holding my arm on the other side of the door. She called it by name. 

"Winter. That's enough. Let him go." 

She said it the way you say the cat's name when it's on the table. Nothing answered her. The frost crept out to the edges of the glass, all around the handprint, and the grip tightened until I felt it in my collarbone. 

"Winter." And under the order, for the first time all night, I heard that she was afraid. 

Then her knees slid forward on the tiles. The door had started taking her too. 

My right hand on the frame was all I had left. I took it off and started prying her fingers apart, because she didn't have to go where I was going. I got one loose and the door took my elbow. I got a second and it took me to the shoulder, my cheek hit the steel, and she came with me. 

I stopped prying. I closed my hand over both of hers, held them shut against my chest, and started on her the way she'd been on me. 

"Alba. Answer me." 

"Yes." 

"Again." 

"Yes. Matteo. Yes." 

It was twenty past five. From then on we answered each other, and that held it. But the cold kept gaining. After a while I couldn't feel the hand I was holding her with. I kept it closed by watching it. 

My jaw locked, teeth together, and when I tried to ask her if she was still there, what came out was a noise through my nose. The pull doubled. I watched my own fingers start to open on her hands and could do nothing about them. 

She answered anyway, the question I hadn't been able to ask, her teeth rattling against my ear. 

"I'm here. Till it gets light." 

My hand closed on hers on its own. The corner of her blanket had slipped over my shoulder and hung down beside our hands. There was a scorched patch on it the size of a coin. It was the blanket from my locker. The student I stayed with all night seven years ago was Alba. 

My hand was still holding hers when the black behind the wired glass went gray with the first daylight. The grip thawed a little with every shade of gray, shoulder, elbow, wrist, until the door gave me my hand back. Whatever had come down over us lifted, and the ward came back at full volume: a toilet flushing, somebody's slippers. The frost on the glass began to run, and Ferruccio's handprint ran with it. My watch said ten past six. The door had held me for three hours. 

I turned around. In the gray light Alba was sitting on the tiles, shaking, and she was alive. She pulled the rest of the blanket off her shoulders and put it around mine. 

Ferruccio was standing over us. He'd come back to the door one more time, and this time he saw us. "Matteo," he said, and I watched it all come back to him. 

He called the night supervisor, who had the doctor on call check us both over, like anybody found on a floor: two cold, exhausted people, nothing worse. My left hand stayed white for most of an hour. Four nights later, the tips of two fingers still haven't come back. Ferruccio said that for hours he'd have sworn he was the only nurse on. Between him and Giulia, not one round had been missed. I wrote it all up in an incident report and signed it, and Nunzia read it twice when she came in. 

At handover I put the blanket back in Alba's hands. "Seven years, and you never came looking for it," she said. 

Our locker rooms are one floor down, by the back stairs, so I had to unlock the fire door to get there. My hand did not want to go near it. The key turned like any other morning. 

Alba stopped on the landing, by the window, with the blanket folded over her arm. "I asked you on purpose," she said. "On our break. I wanted to know if you remembered me." 

She'd heard from Nunzia that I was leaving, and she'd asked for my nights so she could tell me before I went. She gave me the rest in pieces. The thing behind the door first came for her the night she turned eighteen, in July. Every window in her house frosted over, in front of all her friends. She's called it Winter ever since. 

"Anyone I let close gets hurt," she said. "It starts with the hands." She looked at my left hand. "So I live alone." 

"Seven years ago," I said. "I thought the fever had put you on that floor." 

"The fever was real. The cold was Winter." 

She blew into her hands. 

"I didn't quit," she said. "I finished nursing school because of that night." 

She went down the stairs ahead of me. I didn't follow right away. She lives alone, she'd told me so twice, and I stood at the landing window trying to think who answers her. 

The men's locker room was empty. Alba waited in the doorway while I opened my locker. The envelope was on the shelf where I'd left it, furred white with frost. That locker had been shut and dark all night. The sun came through the window behind me, and where it touched the envelope, the frost began to bead and run. 

"It knows you now," Alba said. 

I left the envelope where it was and shut the locker.
