It's been nine days. My daughter is talking again, about school and the dog and what she wants for her birthday. Not about the slide.

She's seven. This was the first summer she was tall enough for the black slide, the enclosed one, the one every water park has, a tube you can't see into that comes off the top of the tower and drops you into a splash pool at the bottom. She'd been measuring herself against the height bar at the bottom of the stairs all year. The park is forty minutes from our house, twenty if you take the tunnel under the airport, and she'd talked the whole way there. She went up the stairs ahead of me, and I went around to the splash pool with her towel to wait, like a hundred other dads standing there in their wet shirts.

I wear a cheap digital watch in the water. It shows the day and the date.

The kid ahead of her shot out of the pipe and the lifeguard waved him to the ladder. Then nothing. Long enough that I looked at the lifeguard, a college kid on a stool with a whistle in his teeth, and he looked at the mouth of the pipe and took the whistle out and said, "Little ones take longer."

She came out of the pipe and didn't stand up.

The lifeguard got off his stool and waded in and lifted her under the arms and carried her to the ladder, and she was making a sound. Not crying. I've heard her cry for seven years, I know every version of it. This was a sound with no words in it, like something trying to get out of her and finding the door too small. He handed her up to me and said, "Dark part gets some of them," and got back on his stool.

I got her to the chairs. I got the towel around her. Her hands were the first thing I saw, because she wouldn't let me hold them. Her fingernails were broken off short, every one, and the tips of her fingers were raw and two of them were bleeding. I thought she'd fallen on the stairs, those metal grating stairs they all have. I thought she'd caught herself with her hands. I wrapped them up in the towel with the rest of her.

I got her a drink. She held it in both hands and didn't drink it. She would not look at the tower. Every time I asked her what happened she opened her mouth and there was air in it and nothing else. A mother at the next chair said the dark part had scared her boy too, the first time, and he'd been fine after.

I bought her a pretzel and sat her at a table by the splash pool with the towel around her and told her I'd be two minutes. She watched me walk to the stairs. She didn't make the sound. She didn't make any sound.

It's sixty-some feet of metal stairs. At the top there's a lifeguard and a dispatch light, red and green. You sit in the mouth of the tube with water running past you and the lifeguard says cross your arms and keep your feet together and waits for the light, and then you push off.

Two turns in the sun. Then black.

The black is supposed to last about a second. I've been down a dozen of those slides in my life. You go into the dark part and there's a drop, and you come out blinking. I went into the dark and the drop came and I crossed my arms tighter and waited for the daylight.

It didn't come. That was fine. Some of them have a longer dark section than others. I counted it the way you count a fall, and I got to five, and I was still going, and then I noticed the thing that wasn't fine, which was that I was slowing down. You don't slow down in a tube. The whole point of the thing is that you can't. The slope had gone out from under me. I was lying on my back in two inches of running water, moving at about the speed of a walk, and the water was still pushing.

I thought the slide had broken. I thought a section had come loose and I'd been dumped into a feeder pipe, something behind the scenes, and that any second I'd hear a maintenance guy. I said hello. I said it like a man who's walked into the wrong office.

The tube curved away on both sides where my hands could find it. Smooth. Wet. No seam, no join, nothing a flashlight would have shown me. I put my palms flat on it to stop and the water moved me anyway. I turned over and tried to crawl back up and I slid back down what I'd climbed, on my stomach, in the dark. I yelled her name. The tube took it. It was like yelling into a mattress.

I hit the light on my watch. 4:52.

I'd pushed off at 4:40 by the clock over the wave pool. I looked at that number a long time with my thumb on the light. Twelve minutes. I'd been in a water slide for twelve minutes and I was still in it, and I was still going, and I did not yet understand that the twelve was the small number.

I kept hitting it. 5:30. 7:10. The water was warm. The dark was all the way dark, not a dark your eyes come around to, and I had my hand in front of my face for a long time before I understood I wasn't going to see it. I yelled until I understood that too. The lifeguard was a tube-length behind me and a tube-length is however long it wants to be.

I slept. I didn't decide to. I woke up still moving, with my cheek in the water, and the date on the watch had gone over.

I'll tell you how I kept my head, because somebody will ask. I didn't. I never stopped trying to stop. I dragged my palms on the walls until the skin went and then I dragged my fingertips, and then I dug in with my nails, which is something your body does without asking you, and fiberglass doesn't care what your nails think. I knew by then what I'd seen at the splash pool, and what I'd called the stairs.

On the second day, by the watch, I had to go.

I was lying on my back in two inches of running water in a tube going downhill, and I had to go, and I was afraid of it in a way that makes no sense next to everything else. I was going to be behind it. Whatever I did, I was going to be sliding in the dark downhill behind it, with the water carrying it along ahead of me, for as long as the tube wanted. I held it for hours. I held it until my body took the decision away from me, the way it had with the nails, and then I lay there in the warm and the dark and listened to the water carry it off ahead of me, and I didn't yell, and I didn't cry, I just thought about my hands and whether they'd touched it. That was day two. The watch said nine hours before I thought about anything else.

A grown man in a tube in the dark. Nobody would ever have known. I'm telling you anyway. It's the only thing in here I can say in a normal voice.

By the third day my mouth was gone. My tongue had stopped feeling like a part of me. I'd been lying in two inches of water for two days without touching it, because of the chlorine, and then because of day two, and on the third day I put my face down and took some in my mouth anyway. Not to swallow. Just to have something wet in there. I held it and let it go and did that a few times, and I'd have done it again, and then I couldn't.

The tube got warm on the third day. Not the water. The walls. I'd had my hands on them for two days and knew what they felt like, and they started to feel like a car door in a parking lot in August.

Then the sound came up the pipe.

I thought it was the wave pool. I'm going to say that once so you know I tried. A wave pool has a sound, a few hundred people yelling in a rhythm, up with the wave and down. This came from ahead of me, and it came for hours before it got loud, and the date on the watch went over again while it did, and there was no rhythm in it. There was nothing in it that any person was enjoying. The water under me got hot. It started to steam, and I could smell the steam, and it wasn't chlorine anymore.

One bend was black. The next one wasn't.

The tube turned clear. All of it, all the way around, the way a straw is clear. I was lying on my back in a glass pipe, moving slow, and the pipe ran just over the heads of a crowd, and I could see out in every direction.

The light came up from under them. They were standing in water, chest deep, packed in, as far as I could see, and the water was lit red from below with no lamp in it anywhere, and every face was turned up. None of them were moving. The sound was coming from them and none of their mouths were open. There were things moving between them, in the water, and I didn't let myself look at those long enough to describe.

The heat came through the glass. The water in the tube steamed off my chest.

It stayed clear for a long time. The watch said three hours and I don't believe the watch. I was going slow enough that the ones under me turned their faces to follow as I went over, one after another, the whole way. I kept my eyes on the watch for most of it.

One of them moved. One, directly under me. It lifted a hand out of the water and laid it flat on the underside of the glass, under my back, and kept pace. I felt the heat of it through the glass and the water. I didn't look. I looked at the watch. It stayed there, keeping up, for as long as the glass lasted.

Then black again, and steep, and fast, and the drop you're supposed to get, and daylight.

Chlorine. The splash pool. The lifeguard on his stool took the whistle out and said, "Nice one."

I stood up in the pool and had to hold the ladder. My legs went out and then they came back. I looked at myself. Same man. Same shorts, clean. No beard, and I'd had one by the third day, I'd felt it. My lips weren't cracked. My mouth wasn't dry. I wasn't hungry. Whatever four days had taken out of me was back in me, or had never left, and I stood there in waist-deep water and looked at my hands, and my hands hadn't come back. Three nails gone. The tips of all ten fingers raw, two of them open and running pink into the pool. Both knees skinned through.

The lifeguard looked at me for a second longer than you look at a man who's just come down a slide. Then he turned to the pipe and waved the next kid out.

I went back to the table. The pretzel was still warm. She was where I'd left her, with the towel around her and her hands in her lap, and she looked at my face and then at my hands and she didn't make a sound, and I didn't either. I'd seen her hands at the bottom of the ladder forty minutes earlier by the clock over the wave pool. I'd called it the stairs.

My phone was in the locker. It wouldn't open for my thumb. I did the code.

We drove home. She was in the booster in the back with the towel still around her and her hands in her lap, palms up, because she couldn't close them. I drove with the heels of my hands on the wheel. No radio. Neither of us said anything, the whole way, and it wasn't the quiet of two people who have nothing to say.

The fast way home goes through the tunnel under the airport, a quarter mile of lit concrete. I've driven it a hundred times with her asleep in the back. I took the exit before it and went around on the surface roads, forty minutes instead of twenty, in traffic.

She watched me take the exit. She didn't ask why. She knows the way home as well as I do.
