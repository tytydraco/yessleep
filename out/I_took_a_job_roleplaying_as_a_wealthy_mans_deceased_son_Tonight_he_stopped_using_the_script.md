I’m an actor. Or, at least, I tell people I am when I’m filling out tax forms. In reality, I’m a guy who moves from town to town, working odd gigs and running away from a past I’d rather forget.

Three weeks ago, I found a listing on a private forum: “Seeking male actor, mid-20s, dark hair, athletic build. Immersive, private theater project. $500 an hour.”

It sounded sketchy, like the prelude to a true-crime podcast. But my rent was two months overdue, so I answered it.

The man who hired me was named Victor. He lived in a sprawling, isolated mid-century modern estate tucked deep into the hills. He was incredibly wealthy, meticulously dressed, and completely heartbroken. On our first meeting, he handed me a leather-bound binder filled with typed dialogue and a garment bag containing a tailored, expensive suit.

"My son, Julian, passed away two years ago," Victor explained, his voice entirely devoid of emotion. "I miss our Sunday dinners. I want to recreate them. You wear his clothes, you memorize the lines, and you sit across from me at the table. You play Julian."

It was deeply unsettling, but $500 an hour is $500 an hour.

For the first four sessions, it went perfectly. The script was simple, wholesome stuff. Victor would ask how my classes at Yale were going, and I would read Julian's scripted responses about his law professors. Victor would laugh at a joke written in the margin, I’d take a bite of steak, and when the two hours were up, he’d hand me an envelope of cash. I actually started to feel bad for the old man. It felt like weird, expensive therapy.

Tonight was our fifth session. The final page of the script.

I arrived at 8:00 PM, changed into Julian’s favorite charcoal suit, and took my seat at the massive mahogany dining table. The candles were lit. The wine was poured. Victor sat opposite me, looking sharper than usual.

"You look wonderful tonight, Julian," Victor said, opening his binder.

I opened mine to page 42. "Thanks, Dad. It’s good to be home. The drive up from New Haven was brutal, though."

"I imagine it was," Victor said. He closed his binder with a soft thud. He set his hands flat on the table and stared directly into my eyes. "Especially with that faulty headlight on your silver Honda Civic."

I froze.

The line wasn't in the script. And more importantly... Julian didn't drive a Honda Civic. I did. It was currently parked half a mile down his private driveway, hidden behind a row of pine trees.

I forced a tight, professional smile, trying to stay in character. "Dad, you know I drive the BMW you bought me."

"Let's drop the script, Ethan," Victor said.

Hearing my actual, real name come out of his mouth sent a jolt of pure ice straight down my spine. The warmth completely vanished from the room.

"Victor?" I said, dropping the Julian voice entirely. "What is this? If the session is over, I can just change and"

"Sit down, Ethan," Victor said. His voice wasn't angry. It was calm, perfectly measured, and entirely dead. "We haven't reached the climax of the play yet. I paid for two hours. We have ninety minutes left."

I stood up anyway, my chair scraping loudly against the hardwood floor. "Yeah, I think I'm good. Keep the cash for tonight. I'm leaving."

I walked briskly toward the heavy oak front doors. I grabbed the brass handle and pulled. Nothing. I twisted the deadbolt, but it just spun loosely in its socket. The electronic keypad next to the frame was dark.

"The house is on a smart-lock system, Ethan. Controlled entirely from my phone," Victor called out from the dining room. I heard the clink of his fork against his plate. "And before you ask, the cellular dampener in the basement is turned on. You won't get a signal."

I slowly turned around. The house felt less like a mansion now and more like a beautifully decorated concrete vault. I walked back into the dining room, my heart hammering against my ribs.

"What do you want?" I demanded, my hands trembling. "Who are you?"

"I told you who I am. I'm Julian's father," Victor said, taking a slow sip of his red wine. "Two years ago, Julian was walking home from a charity gala in the city. A driver blew through a red light at forty-five miles an hour, struck him, and kept going. Julian died on the asphalt alone."

A horrific, suffocating realization began to take shape in my throat. I couldn't breathe.

"The police never found the driver," Victor continued, leaning forward. "But a father's grief is a very powerful motivator, Ethan. It took me eighteen months, hundreds of thousands of dollars in private investigators, and a lot of patience. But eventually, we found a silver Honda Civic in a scrap yard three towns over. The windshield had been replaced illegally. The undercarriage still had traces of my son's DNA."

"Victor, please," I choked out, tears stinging my eyes. "It was dark. It was raining. I didn't see him until it was too late, and I panicked I swear to God I panicked"

"I know," Victor whispered softly. "That's why I hired you. I wanted to see the man who killed my boy. I wanted to see if you looked like a monster. But you don't. You look just like him. You even fit into his clothes."

Victor stood up, picking up the leather-bound binder. He walked around the long table, stopping just inches from me. The smell of his expensive cologne was overpowering. He opened the binder to the very last page a page I hadn't been allowed to look at.

There were no lines typed on it. Just a single sentence handwritten in red ink.

Victor turned the page toward me.

"Now, confess to the camera, or we skip straight to the epilogue."

I looked up. In the corner of the dining room, hidden behind a high-end speaker, the small green recording light of a mounted camera flashed in the dark.

Victor reached into his jacket pocket. He didn't pull out a gun. He pulled out a small, silver remote control, and hit a button. Somewhere deep in the house, the heavy sound of a garage door began to lift.

"The police are already at the bottom of the driveway, Ethan," Victor said, his smile perfectly serene, perfectly terrifying. "You have exactly three minutes to give me the performance of your life. Tell them what you did to my son. Make it believable. Because if you don't..."

He glanced down at the garment bag I had taken the suit from. Resting inside it on the floor was a heavy, rusted iron tire iron.

"...I'll have to rewrite the ending."
