100%.

I want to start with that number, because for the last three posts it has been the thing I watched instead of the things I didn't want to watch. Now it's full. I can type for as long as I like. It's the worst thing that's happened to me in here, and I need you to understand why.

Furuya came at dawn.

I knew it was him before the panel opened. Mr. Sone walks like a man in socks on a polished floor, a soft slide. Furuya walks like a carpenter: heel, then the whole foot, and a little pause before each step as if he's checking the boards. He knelt. I heard his knees crack. He tapped the front panel twice, the same as Sone, but slower, and he didn't say Hako-san. He didn't say anything.

Then the panel opened and the light came in.

I'd been in the dark so long it went through me like a nail. When I could see again he was kneeling right there, eye level, with his glasses pushed up on his forehead and a little flashlight in his teeth. He wasn't looking at my face. He was looking at the seams. Mine, I mean. The ones on my left side. He looked at them the way he looks at a dovetail joint, close and slow, turning his head a little to catch the light along the edges.

He nodded once. I've worked for him for six years and I know that nod. It means acceptable. He gave it to me the first time I lay in one of his boxes for nine hours without needing the bottle.

I tried to say his name. It came out with the echo behind it, a hair late, then again further down, and his eyes came up to my face very quickly and then went back down to the work.

Then he did something I didn't expect. He reached in, carefully, between the edge of me and the nearest layer of Mr. Arai, without touching either, the way you reach into a hot oven, and took the phone out of my right hand. He turned it over. He saw the 0%. He made a small irritated sound through his nose, a carpenter's sound, someone finding a tool put away dirty.

He went away for a minute. When he came back he had a long white charging cable that he'd threaded through something above the alcove, and he plugged the phone in. He propped it against the inside of the panel at the angle of my eyes. He picked my right thumb up with two fingers, lightly, the way you'd pick up a cicada shell, and set it on the screen.

Then he closed the panel and went upstairs.

I don't know if he's read these posts. I don't know if he did this so I could keep writing or so they could keep watching. Maybe he just can't stand a job left unfinished. With Furuya, all three could be true.

Let me tell you how I'm sitting now, so you can picture it. A lot of you asked, and it's easier to type than to think about.

I'm still sitting the way I was measured for. Knees up, back against the left wall, head tilted a little so it fits under the lid. My right side is me. I want to be very clear about that. My right arm, my right hand, my thumb, my right leg up to the hip, the right half of my face. Warm. Mine. I can feel my heartbeat in the thumb against the glass. It's fast, which I'm choosing to take as a good sign.

My left side is in layers.

It doesn't hurt. Arai was telling the truth. It's only cold, and the cold is very precise. It starts at my left hip and goes up in slices about as thick as a good shokupan loaf, and each slice is hanging in the dark a finger's width from the next, and nothing is touching anything. I can still feel them all. That's the part I didn't expect. I can feel my left hand. It's just that it's also a few centimetres to the left of where it used to be, and between each finger there's air, and between each knuckle, and in the air is her.

She's still working. I can feel her the way you feel someone sit on the far end of a bed. She did the left leg in the night while I wasn't typing, and the left arm up to the shoulder, and now she's doing the ribs again, going over them a second time, thinner. She's very tidy. She goes back and fixes her own work.

When she gets to the middle of me she'll start on the right.

I'm typing with my right thumb. You can do the arithmetic. I did it at about 6 a.m. and then stopped doing it.

Arai is still here. I have to tell you about Arai, because he did something for me last night that I've been trying to find the right words for.

When the guests were leaving, before Furuya, when it was very dark and very cold and the lines were going up my ribs, my hag got to the back of my left shoulder, the place where the bottom of her coat is inked, and stopped.

I felt her stop. All the cold in me went still. The slices hung where they were. And on the other side of me, from the gaps in Mr. Arai, his one came forward.

I can't describe that except to say the air in the box changed shape. Like when someone walks into a room behind you and you know without turning that they're taller than you. His was coming through the gaps between his slices toward the gaps between mine, and mine had stopped working and turned to face it, and there was nothing between them but about two centimetres of air and me.

They don't share. That's what he said to me in Part 3.

And Arai, whose eyes had been turned to the back wall for my sake, turned them back. He looked straight at the place where his hag was coming through. Then he took a breath.

You have to understand what that costs him. When he breathes, all his top layers lift and settle together. Every layer moves. Stillness is what keeps them lined up. He's been holding still for, I think, years.

He breathed in, and all of him lifted, and the slices swung out of line by maybe a millimetre, and his hag went back. Not all the way. But back into him, to put him right. Like a mother who has to choose between the fight in the street and the child in the road.

He held it like that. Out of line. For the rest of the night. I watched it in the green light while it lasted, every slice of him trembling a hair off true. And mine went back to work on me, and his stayed busy holding him together, and neither of them got to the other.

When Furuya opened the panel at dawn, the first thing he did, before he even looked at me, was look at Arai's alignment and frown.

I don't know how long Arai can hold it. I asked him. He said, "Long enough," with the echo running down the hallway behind him, and then he said, "Type faster."

Here's what I worked out about the house, about Sone, about what all this is for. I'm only going to say it once.

Sone collects irezumi.

Not the ink on paper. The ink on people. I've worked for the family for six years and I've seen a lot of tattoos, and I always noticed that Sone noticed them. When we're introduced he looks at your hands first, then your collar, then your face. I thought it was a yakuza thing, checking who you belonged to. It wasn't. He was shopping.

The guests upstairs aren't guests. They're other collectors. I heard them again an hour ago, a new group, the same slow kneeling. I heard one of them say Busan, and another one say hand-poked, the old way, and Sone answer with that little modest laugh, the hatching on the nails is remarkable, which he has said about me twice now, and which I am beginning to understand is the line he uses at a gallery opening.

What I don't think they understand, or maybe they do, and that's what they're paying for, is that the good work is never just ink. The old artists, the ones who did it by hand in back rooms, put something in. A name. A prayer. A promise. And if you put enough of someone into a picture, eventually the picture can do things. It can stand between your layers. It can hold you still in a display case for twenty years so a man can kneel above you with a cup of tea and say utsukushii.

Furuya builds the cases. Sone finds the canvases. The tattoos do the rest.

And we stay very, very still.

There's one more thing, and then I'm going to rest my thumb, because it's started to feel cold at the very tip and I don't want to think about why.

About an hour ago I heard Furuya's footsteps again. Not upstairs. Down here, out in the furnace room where I sat with Sone that first night. Heel, whole foot, pause. Then the sound of a tape measure. That long metal zip as it pulls out, and the snap back.

He was measuring a new box.

Sone was with him. I heard Sone's voice, through the wall, warm, unhurried. He was reading numbers off something. 38. 55. 104.

Smaller than me. A smaller woman. My first thought was relief, which I'm ashamed of. My second thought was that 38 by 55 by 104 is about the size you'd measure for someone who sits hunched forward over her work all day, who has done that for thirty years under a single lamp in a shop that smells of ice and squid ink.

Then Sone said a word I didn't need translated, because I'd heard it every time I came for a touch-up, because she said it herself every time I was leaving, with her hand on my shoulder and that four-note hum still in her mouth.

Kamsahamnida.

He was practising it. For when she arrives.

I can't warn her. I don't have her number. I never knew her name. I just paid cash and came back every year and let her hum.

So I'm going to warn you instead, and I'll keep this simple because my thumb's getting slow.

If you have old work, hand-poked, the old-school way with the fine hatching, done in a back room by somebody who didn't speak much, go and get a magnifying glass. Look at the shading. Look at it closely.

If the lines are just lines, good. Go to bed.

If they're not lines, if they're tiny and they repeat, if they spell something, don't get it lasered. Arai did that. Don't go to Umeda. Don't let anyone measure you for anything.

And don't hold still.

That's the whole secret. That's what I worked out in here and I want somebody to have it. They need you still. All of them. Sone, Furuya, the pictures on our skin. Everything in this house is built for stillness. So if you find writing in your shading, move. Fidget. Pace. Sleep badly. Be a nuisance in every room you're in. Be the kind of canvas nobody can get a clean line on.

I was the best at holding still that Furuya ever had. He told me that once, and I was proud of it for six years.

My thumb is cold at the tip now. She's crossing the middle of me. I can feel her come over the breastbone like someone stepping over a sleeping dog.

Battery's at 100%.

Arai says type faster.
