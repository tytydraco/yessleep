The air conditioner ran twenty-four hours a day in my apartment. I never turned it off. If I turned it off I'd hear the refrigerator ticking, and then I'd have to sit with the quiet, and the quiet always turned into thinking about Grelling & Associates, where I spent eleven hours a day formatting compliance spreadsheets nobody read.

I know "office job" isn't supposed to be a horror story. But there's a version of it that is. A cubicle drawer that sticks. A coffee mug with a ring of dried brown at the bottom because you stopped washing it three weeks ago and at some point it just became part of the mug. A fluorescent light that buzzes at a pitch you can't quite hear, so by 4 PM your jaw hurts and you don't know why. You don't feel anything, good or bad. You format cells. You go home. You wait for the alarm.

Two years of that.

So when the ad showed up in my feed, "Leave the desk. Work with your hands. Build something real," I clicked before I finished reading it. I wanted copper under my fingernails. I wanted to strip insulation and twist wire and be done with something at the end of a day, actually done, not "reassigned to a new project" done.

Sign-up was easy. Name, address, a $45 processing fee. The exam was remote, proctored by webcam. Two hundred questions, three hours, pass or retake in thirty days.

I did it on a Saturday. Made coffee, sat down at 8:55, opened the link. White background, blue text, a timer counting down from 3:00:00. At the top: ELECTRICAL TRADESMAN CERTIFICATION, CANDIDATE ASSESSMENT. PLEASE ANSWER ALL QUESTIONS HONESTLY.

Honestly. Weird word for a multiple choice test. I didn't think about it. I clicked Begin.

First twenty questions were normal. Ohm's law, wire gauge, NEC code references, stuff I'd actually studied. I moved through them fast. Outside my window a lawnmower was going two houses down.

Question 21 asked about grounding methods. I picked B. Question 22, insulation ratings, I picked C. Question 23:

*When you strip the sheathing from a length of Romex, what do you feel beneath your thumbnail?*

No multiple choice this time. Just a text box and a blinking cursor. I typed something about the copper being slick and the insulation having a little resistance. It ate my answer and moved on.

Question 24:

*You are holding a live wire. Not metaphorically. Your palm is wrapped around it. Describe the temperature.*

I sat there for maybe forty seconds. This wasn't in any of the prep forums I'd read. But the timer kept going, so I typed something about it being warm, not painful, like a mug left too long in the microwave.

Question 25: what does the air smell like where you work.

I typed: cold. Recycled. Like nothing has ever been alive in there. And then, for about three seconds, I actually smelled it. Not my apartment. The fourteenth floor of Grelling & Associates. Then it was gone.

I checked the timer. 2:47:00. Thirteen minutes in. That tracked, I guess.

Question 26:

*You are not qualified. You have never been qualified. Why are you here?*

I typed something about wanting to be useful. Wanting to build something that wasn't a spreadsheet. Tired of being a formatting function. It disappeared, no checkmark, straight to the next one.

*You are tired. How tired?*

I typed "tired enough to let someone else decide what I am" and then just stared at it, because I hadn't meant to write that.

Next time I looked at the timer it said 1:58:00. I don't remember the questions in between. The box was empty and waiting, patient, like it had all the time it needed and I didn't.

Question 73:

*What is the last thing you said to another human being that wasn't a transaction?*

I couldn't think of one. I typed "I don't know" and the question just sat there. Didn't advance. So I sat there too, I don't know how long, the lawnmower outside had stopped, the coffee had gone cold and skinned over. Eventually I typed that six months ago my sister asked if I wanted to come for dinner and I told her I was fine, and that was the last non-transaction I could remember.

Question 74:

*You are hollow. We can see that. This is not a criticism. This is a qualification. Do you understand?*

My hands were shaking a little. I typed yes.

Timer said 1:12:00, then 0:47:00, then 0:03:00. I don't remember answering whatever came in between but the box kept filling and emptying and at some point I realized my face was wet. I don't know when that started either.

The ones I do remember:

*If you could be reduced to a single function, what would it be?*

*You have been waiting to be used. How long?*

*Open your hands. Palms up. Describe what you see.*

I actually did it, lifted my hands off the keyboard, turned them palm up, looked. Typed: lines, a callus from the mouse, a paper cut on the left thumb from three weeks ago that never healed right. Hands that had never held anything that mattered.

Then, last one:

*You will be given a purpose. You will not choose it. You will not refuse it. This is the terms of your qualification. Type I UNDERSTAND to continue.*

I typed it. Because I was tired, and the cursor had been blinking at me for what felt like an hour, and some part of me wanted to be told what to do by something that wasn't a spreadsheet. I hit enter, the timer hit zero, and the screen went white. Not a crash, not a loading icon. Just white, deep white, the kind that looks like it goes back further than a monitor should allow.

Then, small black text in the center:

CANDIDATE QUALIFIED. WELCOME TO THE TRADE.

Then my desktop came back. Browser, email, the lawnmower starting up again outside. I closed the laptop and slept for fourteen hours.



That was five weeks ago.

The license came in the mail three days later. Real card stock, embossed, my name and a credential number, no return address, no company logo. Just: CERTIFIED, ELECTRICAL TRADESMAN, PROVISIONAL, ASSIGNMENT PENDING.

I felt good. Light, actually, for the first time in two years. Called my sister. Stood outside in the sun for twenty minutes doing nothing. Told myself okay, this is the pivot, I'll get placed somewhere, I'll come home tired for a reason.

The assignment showed up on a Tuesday. Not an email. I woke up and there was a work order sitting on my kitchen counter, printed on heavy cream card stock like a wedding invitation. An address, a time, and under task description: "Panel upgrade. Residential. Bring your own hands."

I went. Obviously I went, I'd been qualified, I'd been welcomed to the trade. Drove out to a neighborhood I didn't recognize, identical lawns, houses with numbers instead of names.

Front door was unlocked. No furniture inside, no homeowner. Just a breaker panel on the far wall, open, and a black cable running from it down into the concrete floor like it had grown there.

I reached for it.

That's when I saw the camera. Small dome unit up in the corner of the ceiling, the kind you see in office elevators, and under it a little screen, phone sized, showing the feed from it. Showing me.

Except on the screen my hand wasn't reaching. It was already around the cable. Gripping it. Knuckles white, wrist turning, feeding cable through the panel, and I could see the tendons moving under the skin on that little monitor and I could not feel any of it happening.

I looked down at my actual hand.

It was on the cord.

I don't know when it got there. My fingers were wrapped around the cable, tight, pulling, and I was stripping the sheathing with my thumbnail and the copper underneath smelled hot, exactly like the answer I'd typed weeks earlier. Like a mug left too long in the microwave.

I tried to let go. My fingers didn't loosen.

I tried to step back. My feet didn't move.

My other hand came up on its own and gripped the cord six inches below the first, and both hands worked together, stripping, twisting, feeding, like they'd done this a hundred times.

I opened my mouth to scream. What came out was a hum. Low, steady, and I recognized the pitch immediately, it was the exact hum of the air conditioner in my apartment. The exact hum of the fluorescent light on the fourteenth floor.

It lasted about four seconds. Then my mouth closed and my hands kept working and I just stood there and watched them.

That was three weeks ago.

I've done six jobs since then. I don't drive to them anymore, not really, I mean I get in the car but I don't remember most of the trip. I wake up and I'm there, hands already moving. I stand inside my own body like a passenger who isn't the one driving. The work itself is good, clean, I can see that much. Whoever's doing it does excellent work.

I'm typing this slow, one finger at a time, because my hands don't want to be doing this. They want to be on assignment. I can feel them pulling toward the outlet behind my desk, toward the nearest length of wire, the way a compass needle pulls north. I've got my palms pressed flat against the desk right now just to keep them still.

I don't know who built the system, or how long it's been running, or how many of us there are. I don't know if the guys I see on real job sites are people, or if they're what I'm turning into, or if there's still a difference.

Here's what I do know. The exam wasn't a test. It was an interview. It was a guy sitting at his kitchen table typing yes, I'm empty, yes, I'm tired, please put something in here, I don't care what, just give me a function so I can stop being this shapeless nothing in a cubicle.

And it heard that. And it said welcome to the trade.

So if you see an ad for a certification, any trade, any "build something real," and it tells you to answer honestly, and question one is about wire gauges but question twenty one asks what you feel under your thumbnail:

Close the laptop.

Go outside.

Call someone, and say something to them that isn't a transaction.

Don't type I UNDERSTAND.

I typed it because I was so tired, and somehow they knew that, they could see the hollow part, the part with room in it, waiting to be filled with a function.
