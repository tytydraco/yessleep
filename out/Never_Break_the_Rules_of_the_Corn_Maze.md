That’s what the sign read, above the maze itself. The rules were simple enough. No running, no picking or damaging the corn, stay on the paths, children must be accompanied by an adult. Just inside the maze, a four-year old was wandering around with a half-eaten apple in her hand. Somewhere in the corn an adult was saying, “Where did she go? I think I lost her!” while the four year old kept running and giggling.

None of this made my tween nephew have any desire to go into the maze.

“This looks LAME,” declared Liam, twelve going on twenty-one. At least in his mind.

It’d been like this all day with him. I told my sibling I’d watch him for the weekend and thought that bringing him to a fall festival, complete with corn maze and apple picking and jack-o-lantern carving, would be fun. Maybe even throw in a haunted hayride. So far, though, it’d been a bust. Apple picking? Jack-o-lanterns? Corn maze? Kid stuff. The only thing he’d enjoyed so far had been the pumpkin cannon, though to his disappointment it was aimed only at a rusty old tractor and not at people.

“Can I put on costumes and scare the little kids in the corn maze?”

“Absolutely not,” I said.

“So like what’s your idea of fun, then?” asked Liam. “Cause like, do you *actually* enjoy picking apples? You know they pay people to do that right?”

“I unironically do enjoy apple-picking, yes,” I said.

“Ok. So that proves you’re lame. But also… you’re paying… to pick apples… but really, they should be paying you for doing the picking. So you’re basically getting scammed.”

“I can’t wait ‘till you grow up and have kids,” I muttered. “Tell you what. Let’s do the corn maze. And if you have zero percent fun, I will never for the rest of my earthly existence ask you to try any corn-related activities.”

“How about for the rest of the day you let me have my phone.”

Not this again. My sibling begged me not to let him on his phone all day. And one of the reasons I agreed to bring him out this weekend was to get him off it. Make him “touch grass” (“Do you even know what that means?” he’d asked me. Because he’s convinced I am an “unc,” whatever that is.) But I was losing this battle.

“Fine,” I relented.

Though I had to admit, the maze did not look promising. You could easily see between the stalks to the other people meandering inside, the path was so muddy we were definitely going to get our jeans soaked, and only small children and their parents were inside. Unfortunately, just as we were about to enter, the attendant let us know the maze was closing for the day. So much for that. I reluctantly gave Liam his phone back and we returned to the car.

\*\*\*

As we left the festival, we passed a truck selling pumpkins, and then along a long and lonely stretch of road beside a cornfield, we spotted a large sign that read WORLD’S MOST AMAZING MAIZE. It was on a barn in the distance, with the letter “I” formed by a painted ear of corn and the sign’s outline showing a maze. By the roadside was a small wooden stand and various straw dolls—it looked like they were made from corn and corn husks, vaguely in the shape of people. A sign on the stand advertised the corn maze, and I pulled over to the turnout on the side of the road where the stall was.

“See that? Most Amazing Maize! Let’s see how amazing it is!” I exclaimed.

“Lucky us,” muttered Liam.

The stand had clipboards, papers with a grid of dots and the bottom marked “entrance” and the top marked “exit,” and a set of instructions along the front:

1)     Draw a maze

2)     Write the name of someone you have lost on the straw doll

3)     Their spirit will guide you through the maize

4)     When returning from the other side, follow the same path exactly, or you will be lost

5)     Be sure to thank the doll

The dolls were cheaply crafted things, but it was definitely more interesting than the other maze, with its clearly marked path to the center and the prize being you’d come out and get a free cup of cider. It was also more expensive—the corn dolls were fifteen dollars apiece. There was no one at the stall, but there *was* a bucket to put money in.

“Why write the name of someone you’ve lost?” Liam asked me.

I shrugged. “Spirit of the season I guess. Halloween. All hallow’s eve. The world of spirits and our worlds being closer. Also, it would be hard for a living person to appear to guide you.”

“Does it have to be a person?”

“Um. Probably? What else would you put?” *Please don’t say the boogeyman or Slenderman or—*

“A dog.”

“Oh.” Thank God. That seemed safe enough. “I’m sure that’s fine.”

Liam lost his dog last year. She was a copper-colored dog, named Penny, famous for getting into trouble until the day she ran out into the street and got hit by a car.

Liam drew the maze, adding in dead ends and forks but keeping the main route, to my relief, reasonably direct, with only a few sharp turns. He picked a straw doll and wrote on it and took a selfie with me by the rules. That reminded me—“No phones,” I told him, and he grumbled as I put both our phones in the car. Then we walked over to the field and the small sign marked ENTRANCE. There was no path. Just the sign, leading right into the row of growing corn.

With that, we plunged into the maize.

\*\*\*

The corn stalks loomed overhead and all around us. They were planted in orderly rows, and almost immediately as we entered, the sky was swallowed up in the giant stalks and thick green leaves, so that we had only the window of blue above, and all around, brushing our arms and faces, was the sea of green.

I wouldn’t have thought that the rest of the world could disappear so completely. But it did. Only a few yards in, following the map as we counted our steps, and I’d completely lost sight of the parking area. That didn’t seem possible, as you could turn in any direction and look between the rows, and should be able to see down to the road we’d left. But in every direction—corn.

No trace of buildings, either. The barn with the giant sign, wherever it was, had also been swallowed up by the stalks blocking our view.

“Do you think we’ll really cross over to the ‘other side?’” asked Liam. And for a moment I remembered that he was indeed still a kid.

“With how thick this corn is, I’ll be happy if we just find our way out before tomorrow morning,” I grumbled.

“What’s that? I thought you wanted to touch grass. Get out into the world. Away from technology.”

“I would like to be touching a little less corn.”

Liam giggled. “It was your idea, bro.”

It was, and as we finally emerged from the cornstalks, having roughly followed Liam’s maze (I think), I looked around, blinking.

The sky was a bright blue bowl overhead. The road we’d emerged onto—a simple gravel road—extended as far as the eye could see in either direction. Behind us, the corn field went on seemingly forever. Ahead of us across the road, another corn field stretched out without end. In every direction were towering stalks.

“Can we go back not through the corn?” asked Liam. “Like, let’s take the road.”

“Um…”

I wanted to agree with him, but the road didn’t curve. It didn’t angle. It didn’t have intersections. I was looking both ways, and the kid looked back and forth, too, and said, “Wait… where’s the barn? Where are we?”

The sky was bright. Too bright. It had been nearing dusk when we left the festival, but now there was no sun. No light source. No clouds. The cerulean sky was utterly uniform.

My gut twisted. This wasn’t right. This *couldn’t* be right. The world suddenly felt like it’d been ripped out from under me. Trying to slow my breathing, not to let him see my panic, I reached for my phone, but then remembered that it was in the car along with his. Stupid! Why didn’t I bring them?

“AJ? You OK?” he said, looking at me.

I squeezed my eyes shut. Took a deep breath. And then I said, “Let’s walk and try to find someone.”

\*\*\*

The sky remained a perfect cerulean. The cornstalks rustled in the occasional breeze. But there were no insects. No birds. No signs of life beyond Liam and me. And after walking for five minutes, no sign of any other roads or intersections or *anything.* Liam, being a kid who was on his phone at all hours at all times, hadn’t yet grokked that this was wrong. He might’ve had an inkling things were a little strange, but he was mostly just chattering, while I distracted him by asking him about slang.

“I’m probably going to regret this, but what is unc status?”

“If you have to ask you’re an unc.”

“Ok. Whatever it is, I’m assuming it means I don’t have rizz. I’m high-key old or something.”

He groaned. “Just, stop. Please. It’s painful.”

“You’re saying I’m losing aura?”

“*I’m* losing aura from listening to you!”

I smiled faintly, trying to keep up the banter while peering now and again into the corn. Looking for traces. For signs. Specifically, I was looking to see if there were insects. Broken stalks. Signs of birds or animals. Nothing so far. It was just… corn. But like how you might imagine corn if you were to paint a picture of it, pristine. Just a wall of green. Too tall to see over. And I dreaded the thought of entering again. Entering that cool green where the leaves would close in leaving only the patch of blue overhead. And the rustling…

Wait. Rustling?

Beside me, Liam froze, and both of us stood, ears pricked as the soft rustle continued just ahead of us and somewhere off to the left. A few stalks of corn seemed to sway, as if moved by an animal.

“Hello?” called Liam.

The rustling stopped, and a faint voice moaned.

“Hello?” I called also, hurrying forward. “Is someone there?”

“AJ!” Liam grabbed my arm, but I ignored him because if someone needed help we should check on them. But I slowed as I neared the edge of the gravel road, reluctant to step into that ocean of green.

“Hello?” I called again, and my voice caught at the sight of a woman lying collapsed at the base of the stalks a few feet in.

“H-help,” she moaned.

“Shit.” I started closer, but Liam pulled me back and whispered something about how, “This isn’t where we exited!” He was right, the instructions were to follow the *exact* same route in order to leave. Funny that he was being such a stickler for the rules when at the festival all he wanted to do was break them. And yet I had to admit to myself that I, too, felt deeply reluctant to enter the maize in the wrong place. Like I might lose sight of the road again, lose sight of Liam, of the way out. So I lingered at the very edge of the gravel, peering through the mess of green leaves at the fallen woman. “Hey! Can we help you? Who are you?”

“W-water…”

I tossed my plastic water bottle into the shaded green, and the woman greedily opened it and guzzled.

“Thank you,” she gasped. Her voice was raspy and parched. She propped herself up, and I caught a glimpse of a wispy blonde hair. “Thought I’d be lost out here forever.”

“How long have you been lost?” asked Liam.

“Dunno. Hard to tell time in here. I stopped by the roadside when I saw this maze and thought it would be fun. But I lost my map and my doll.”

Doll?

Struck by a sudden thought, I turned to my nephew and asked, “Liam, where is your doll?”

He looked down, guilty. “Oh. I dropped it in the maze. I got tired of carrying it and also…”

“Also what?”

“It, um… it felt like it wriggled. And it kind of startled me. But you were still walking and I didn’t want to stop and grab it.”

The doll. The doll was our guide. That’s if we believed these rules. Without the doll, how would we find our way? We’d have to follow the maze in reverse and hope we could make it.

“Do you still have the map?” I asked.

“Yeah…” He unfolded it.

“Please, take me with you!” begged the woman. “I’m… I’m not sure I can stand on my own, though. Can you help me?”

I took a single step into the corn, but Liam yanked on my wrist while my other foot was still planted on the gravel.

“We’re *only* supposed to follow the map,” he squeaked, panicky. “You’re going in at the wrong place.”

And as silly as it seemed to worry about taking a few steps in, I carefully lifted my foot up and stayed on the roadside by Liam. I called out apologetically: “Ah—can you crawl to me? Sorry, but I think we need to follow our map.”

“My leg,” she whimpered. “I think it’s… I think my ankle’s twisted.”

“What’s your name? We’ll go get help.”

“Please,” she whimpered.

“Our phones are in the car. Let me go get them and we’ll call an ambulance!” I turned and started along the gravel road. I felt awful leaving her there. The woman’s voice kept calling behind us, increasingly desperate. But I didn’t turn back. In fact, I walked faster. And one of the reasons I did, aside from trusting my gut instincts, was because I’d just remembered something.

There had been no other cars parked at the entrance to the maze. Only our own.

“Wait!” called the woman’s voice. A pale arm reached out from the corn and into the road. “Take me with you!”

The arm was followed by a second arm, this one reaching even further, bony and elongated.

“Run,” I told Liam, and we bolted.

Behind us, the woman emerged from the corn. I cast a glance back and she was crawling after us. Crawling faster than any person should be able to crawl, all but galloping on those long limbs.

We tore across the gravel, lungs bursting, and suddenly, Liam pointed to where our footsteps emerged from the corn. We plunged into the green, doing our best to follow the map. But it was hard to count the steps. Right, left, left, right—were we even going the right way?

And then Liam screamed.

He tumbled to the soil. An emaciated, thin hand gripped his ankle… And somewhere through the greenery a gaunt and pale face leered at us, eyes large and dark beneath wispy blonde hair like corn silk. Liam shrieked, kicking wildly, but the bony grip refused to be shaken, and I grabbed him, both of us screaming when—

Ferocious barking, and a ginger-colored dog slammed into the arm, teeth closing on the wrist and shaking. The emaciated figure groaned, hand opening, and I pulled up Liam and we ran.

“Penny!” Liam was screaming. “Oh my God, it’s Penny!”

The short-legged, bullish little dog bolted past us, charging down a row of corn to turn and bark, her butt wriggling and forelegs planted encouragingly. Liam immediately took off after her. “Liam!” I yelled, charging after him. The dog sprinted away.

“Penny!” he kept yelling. “PENNY!”

But we couldn’t catch her. I was more worried about the rustling in the stalks around us than I was about the dog, though Liam was solely fixated on the dog that he was certain was his. Something crawled through the stalks ahead of us, and Penny veered, taking us into the greenery, then back down another row. Leading us.

And suddenly, dead ahead of us, the corn opened out into the road. I could see the tire of my car parked beyond. Penny ran to the edge of the corn field, turned and huffed and barked. She dodged to one side as Liam came running, and he tried to stop, but I bouldered into him, tumbling us both to the asphalt. As we scrambled up, he turned and faced the corn. “Penny!” he cried. “Penny, come on girl!”

“Liam, let’s go!” I was saying, tugging his arm, desperate to get into the car and drive away.

“Penny! PENNY!”

He slapped his leg and bent forward, and the dog in the cornstalks barked and leapt out into his arms—

—but it was just a corn doll that he got, nothing but husks and ears of corn twined together.

\*\*\*

In the car on the way home, Liam was on his phone again. I wondered about the quickness with which he’d gone back to the comfort of his screen. Decided not to badger him about it. I was too haunted myself to say anything. What *could* I say? What had we even seen? The straw doll sat in his lap.

Then he said: “Do you know people sometimes go missing in cornfields? They have to use drones and thermal imaging to find them. Sometimes people get lost and die in them.”

“Seriously?”

“Yeah I’m reading about it.”

“Wow.” I pause. “So… you think that’s what happened to us?”

“No, bruh. We were on the other side. I just thought you might handle it better if you can have some kind of logical explanation for it.”

“*I* might handle it better?”

“Yeah. You’re old. Old people don’t handle new, unexplained things well. That’s why I didn’t say anything when we were on the other side. Just let you keep pretending to yourself it’s normal.”

I gave him a wry smile. All right, I had to admit that yes, I was struggling to reconcile my knowledge of established reality with… whatever we had just experienced. Especially the further we drove away from it. And by the time the sun dipped below the horizon and we were pulling up to my sibling’s home, I’d come up with a few plausible explanations. We could’ve hallucinated. Or maybe the woman really *was* lost and we should call it in…

But Liam silenced my ideas with a picture on his phone. It was the selfie of us by the rules. Me looking older and grumpier than I’d like. Liam next to me with that bored tween look. Behind him, out of the corn with the silk looking like hair, was what almost looked like a face. That might have been a trick of the light, though. A trick of the imagination. But one thing that wasn’t, and that was right there in the photo, held in his arms—a chubby little copper [dog.](https://www.reddit.com/r/QuincyLee/comments/11nxwz3/welcome_thanks_so_much_for_stopping_by/)
