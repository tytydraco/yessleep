I work at the library. I've been there long enough I know the building the way you know your own house at night. Which is fitting, because parts of it are always dark.

The shelves at the far end, where the light gives up. Nobody goes back there. The genealogies of families that ended. The atlases of countries that changed their names. Books nobody's asked for in years, some of them older than the building. And the dark shelves have spiders.

I leave them be. They keep to the dark, I keep the rest of the place lit and swept, and that's the deal we have. In all my years, no web ever crossed into the lit part of the building. That was the deal, and they kept it. A man learns to share his building with older tenants.

The first web I ever really looked at was over the county records, in the grey hour before we open. It was a good web, taut, the dew still on it from the damp that gets in. And in the middle of it, where the lines cross and knot, there was a letter.

An A. Not like an A, not a shape you could talk yourself out of. An A, by something patient. Flat across the top. And the crossbar sat a little high, the way mine does. I have spent forty years trying to get that crossbar straight and never managed it. I noticed both of those things, and then I made myself un-notice them. A letter is a letter. There are only so many ways to make one.

I stood there longer than it deserved. Silk sags, I told myself, and dust settles in lines. And I shelved the feeling right along with the records.

It was a month before the second one. I know because I wrote it down. That's the kind of man I am, I write things down.

A letter first. Then another, two shelves over. Then the letters started coming together, the way frost does, a little more every morning, and every one of them in the deepest shelf-dark. I told no one. A librarian keeps confidences for a living. Some of them are his own.

The words, when they came, were titles. Not whole titles. Usually just the first word, hung beside the book it belonged to, spelled right. I checked them against the shelf, the way I check everything. The spiders weren't making words up. They were copying the house they lived in.

Once, a web over the returns cart spelled the first word of a book that had come in that morning. Face down, under three others. I hadn't shelved it yet. I hadn't seen it yet. I told myself I must have seen it at check-in, in passing, the way you see things. I wrote the date down in the notebook, and under the date I wrote: seen yesterday, probably. It is the only probably in the notebook.

Mostly they still copy the house. Titles, shelf-marks, once a whole line of a poem I had to go look up. But last spring a web over the lending desk held a word that isn't on any spine in this building. It was the name of the town I was born in. Two counties away. I haven't lived there in forty years, and I have never once said it in this room.

The lending desk stands in the lamp-lit part of the building. It was the first web that ever crossed over. The deal had been broken since spring, and I went on sweeping around it, and I never wrote that part down.

I pulled every book off the shelves under it, one by one, looking for that name somewhere in print. It isn't in this building.

And then, this morning, I came in at six, in the grey hour when the light comes up slow, and I did my rounds the way I always do, from the door, and the dark end of the building was white.

Every web in the building had the same word.

Six hundred mornings I'd signed the day-book in that hand, right there at the front desk, for whoever came asking.

My name. Spelled right. In my own handwriting.

The A with the flat top, the way I make it. The tail I put on my g that nobody taught me and I've never been able to give up. It was hanging in silk all over the building, big letters in the big webs, small and neat in the corners.

In the biggest web, over the county records where it started, the spider was still in it. Still working. I had never seen one in a web before, not once in all these years. It was adding the tail to the g. It did not stop when the light came up. It did not stop when I stepped closer. Whatever they are, they are not afraid of me, and I stood there understanding that they never had been. The deal was never theirs. It was mine. I was the one keeping it.

A librarian doesn't destroy a text because it scares him. He catalogues it.

So now I do my rounds with a notebook. The notebook is the other ledger, the one where the building signs back. Every morning I copy down what the webs say, and the date, and where I found it. Mostly they still copy the house. But some mornings there's a word in the dark that the house doesn't own. Words out of the books I wrote myself, back before the library, the ones nobody borrows. And once, three weeks ago, over the genealogy shelves, a word I've been careful my whole life never to say out loud.

I wrote it once, when I was young, in a letter I never sent. I scratched it out before I folded the page — scratched it hard, back and forth, until the paper went thin. Nobody has ever read that word. There was nothing left of it to read.

The web had it spelled right.

I have not copied it here.

The building was only ever where I keep the most of myself in one place.

Last Sunday I was home, in my chair, and I looked up at my own shelf. The few books I keep there, the ones that are mine and not the building's. In the corner of it, small and neat, there was a web.

One letter. An A, flat across the top.

I sat with it a long time. Then I took the notebook out of my coat and I wrote it down — what it said, and the date, and where I found it. Home. I wrote the word home in the ledger in my own hand, and my hand was not steady.

I didn't take it down. I don't take any of them down, but this one was in my house. I made tea. I sat in my chair. And some time in the evening, without deciding to, I had turned the chair so I could see it without moving my head.

Tonight, when I lock up, I'm going to string a web of my own. Badly, out of plain string, between the dark shelves. It'll say: WHO TAUGHT YOU.

It came to me, writing that down, that I would be copying them to ask it. My letters, in their medium, hung in their end of the building. I wrote that down too, in the notebook, the way I write everything down. I don't know when I started.

And I'm going to leave the day-book open on the front desk. Pen across the page. The way I never leave it.

They've only ever copied, and copying isn't reading. I shelve books in languages I can't read a word of, and it's never once made me their reader. But a hand doesn't learn another hand by accident. And mine is the hand they picked.

So I'll come in at six, in the grey hour, and find out if the spiders only write — or if, like everything else in a library, they answer too.
