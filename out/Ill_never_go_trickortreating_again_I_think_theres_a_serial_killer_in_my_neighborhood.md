This year, my friend’s dad dropped us off in an affluent part of town and we had to make our way back home while gathering as much candy as we could. Along the way, we compared the candy we collected and the decorations in each neighborhood, hoping to optimize our route for next year.

If there was a next year.

The truth is that we are getting a bit old for trick-or-treating. Maybe next year we’ll hang out at my place and spend the night playing board games and scaring any kid who dares to knock on the door.

That night, however, next year did not matter. It wasn’t over until the last house went dark.

My dad is a bit more lenient, or perhaps more careless, than most, so I had been able to stay out a bit after the boys had already scurried off as their own curfews approached. I was now alone a couple of streets from my house. But even if I didn’t have to run back yet, time was running out: the streets had been ransacked, and most houses had turned their lights off after their supplies ran out.

As reality sunk in and I trudged in the approximate direction of home, I paused at each intersection to look around. If this were to be my last Halloween, I had to really make that last house count.

My salvation came in the form of a small white house surrounded by a white picket fence, its gate left ajar. I could spot one light still on behind the window, but, most importantly, a lit jack-o’-lantern sat on the porch.

The pumpkin had been carved into a classic design, with slanted, angry eyes and a mouth full of sharp teeth. It was the only visible decoration, but it had to be there for a reason.

I ran down the street, then up the stone path and staircase leading to the front door. I pounded three times on the white wooden door.

Nothing.

I waited a minute. Then two.

Nothing.

Was there a greater crime than leaving your lights on even after you stopped giving out candy?

I turned around and dragged myself back toward the street. I reached the gate and grabbed it to close it behind me.

A whisper reached my ears.

“No, please don't kill me, Mr. Ghostface,” the voice pleaded.

I spun around. An old woman now stood in the doorway, backlit by the glow of light inside the house. At first glance, she looked massive, a towering figure whose shadow stretched across the front yard to reach my feet. I blinked and my eyes readjusted. She was covered in multiple layers of cloth and wool and draped in a black shawl that covered her head and most of her torso. She was heavily hunched, and the countless necklaces and bracelets hanging from her arms and neck made it seem as though their weight was responsible for her posture.

“Happy to see kids these days still have some taste,” she said as loudly as her strained voice would allow. “It was the first horror movie I ever saw with my daughter. She loved those when she was younger.”

I walked up to her and opened up my bag.

“Hello, madam. Trick-or-treat?” I asked, the quietest I had been all night.

“It’ll be treat, dear.”

She stretched one arm out from under her shawl, and it disappeared beyond the doorway. I heard the most wonderful sound of cardboard scraping against a plastic bowl. Her hand returned wrapped around three big bundles. She dragged her limb over my open bag and let go. The candy fell with a satisfying thud. It was the good stuff: two full chocolate bars and a box of candy.

“I think I’m going to bed soon,” she said. “Do you want the rest of my candy? It’s the least you deserve for such a scary costume.”

“Of course, madam. I’d love it!” I answered.

Her lips stretched and parted, revealing the perfect whiteness of dentures.

“Ah, to be young again,” she remarked. “Come on in, I’ll get them for you.”

She slowly backed up into an open space that housed a staircase to the right of the door. As soon as I stepped inside, an odor invaded my nostrils. The house was saturated with the smell of a rainy day: that specific mix of earthy and musty aromas that my brain associated with mud and worms.

The house itself was well decorated. Not beautifully decorated, exactly, but certainly well decorated. Wooden animal carvings covered every flat surface, while paintings of forests and woodlands covered most of the white walls. A long brown rug stretched from the entranceway to a door at the end of the main corridor.

The light visible from outside emanated from a room directly to our left. She pointed me toward it.

“Please wait in the living room,” she said. “My kitchen isn’t presentable right now. I’ll be with you in a minute.”

I followed the instructions and walked through the open archway that led into the room. It was furnished with an old green fabric sofa and a matching armchair, both covered with white throw blankets. Flanked by two wooden songbirds, a vintage radio rested on a shelf right above a CRT television. I sat on the edge of the sofa, careful not to disturb the blanket. Just as I felt the wooden frame press into my skin from under the thin cushion, the television crackled to life with static.

I jumped up as the screech of gray, black and white filled the room. The static dispersed, replaced by a grainy image of a woman walking with a stoic expression. The woman was dressed in a dark business suit and stood with her shoulders squared, long black hair flowing in the wind as she passed by the camera, disregarding a microphone thrust toward her. The image then cut to a man in a suit sitting in front of a nondescript skyline.

“This is footage of Jane Doe leaving the courthouse for the final time today,” he began. “Today, she was found not responsible for the disappearances of her husband, John Doe, and their two sons, John Jr. and William. After deliberating for thirteen days, the jury deemed the evidence inconclusive.”

As the man spoke, a photo of John Doe appeared on the screen, a bespectacled, mustachioed middle-aged man. The picture looked like a mugshot: he stood in front of a white wall, staring at the camera. When the reporter finished talking, the photo gradually expanded until it filled the screen. Black and gray dots flickered across the wall behind him as the static crept back in. The man was rapidly being consumed by static. As his face was being thorn to shreds, his mouth opened wide, his lips closed back together and parted again.

The TV turned off with an audible click.

He had mouthed one of the few English words anyone could read on lips: "Help."

Then a dial turned, and the radio sprang to life.

I spun around and ran through the archway. A brown rug stretched toward a distant light at the end of the corridor. Along the walls, rows and rows of wooden animals stared directly at me. The walls were now completely hidden under continuous paintings of trees and leaves. I backed up into a wall as I tried to get back to the living room.

“Authorities are still actively searching for Anthony, who was last seen on the 13^(th) of May.” The voice of a woman reading off a report echoed from far away. “This marks the fourth similar case in three years. Authorities would like to remind citizens to make sure they know where their children are at all times and to always be on the lookout for any suspicious individual.”

I dashed toward the only way out. I ran and ran, but the door remained at the same distance, always looming on the horizon. A frame of light was barely visible from the open crack.

I reached out to grab the first animal I could, a wooden rat, and threw it as hard as I could. I watched as it crashed into the door, forcing it open. The lights inside went dark, plunging the corridor in darkness.

I couldn’t tell if I was making progress at all, but I had to keep running.

“Children,” said the whisper tickling my ear. “Always in such a hurry.”

A bulb hidden in the ceiling sparked to life, irradiating a weak yellow light.

The towering being stood at the other end. I could see what I had only glimpsed for a moment before: more than a dozen children clung to the back of an old woman, piled up one on top of another and barely hanging on to her and each other. They all stared at me with blank eyes that had sunk in deep dark circles on their pale skin. Their small bodies were packed tightly, squeezed between her body and the ceiling. It seemed a herculean feat for her to be standing, let alone walking with this mound on her back.

The scraping of their flesh tore through the night as she took a step toward me, outstretching one arm while the other remained hidden under her shawl.

I grabbed a bird and flung it with all my strength. It flew through the air with such force that its beak pierced through the white iris, lodging itself firmly in the eye socket of one of the children. The child barely registered the grievous wound, slowly letting go of its hold on the woman to put one hand up to its eye. The mass of meat on top of him held him firmly in place.

I swung my hand, hoping to find another statuette, only to recoil when I felt sharp edges bite into my palm. My eyes snapped to the object: a wooden cougar climbing a tree. Its claws were lodged in the bark; the sculpture was smooth all over.

I looked back up almost instantly.

My body slammed itself into the wall that was still only inches behind me. The woman was now standing a few feet from me, the tip of her index finger inches away from my face.

I needed to scream, but my voice died in my throat. The only sound in the hallway was the soft rustling of skin against skin as the mass of flesh bent toward me, enveloping me between her and the wall. I could feel the gaze of each and every child on my skin.

She took another step forward, and her arm reached over my shoulder.

“I’m so sorry,” she murmured.

A loud click resounded behind me, my eyes shutting themselves as they expected the source of the sound to crash into me.

My eyelids opened almost as soon as they had closed.

The woman was standing over me, white teeth tearing through the darkness.

“The chain lock gets stuck sometimes.”

The children were gone. The staircase and archway were back in their place. The animals were no longer staring at me. My hand found the door handle near my hip.

She raised her other arm, revealing a bag full of chocolate bars and boxes of candy from under her shawl.

“Happy Halloween, miss,” she said, her voice doing its best to convey excitement. “Get home safely!”
