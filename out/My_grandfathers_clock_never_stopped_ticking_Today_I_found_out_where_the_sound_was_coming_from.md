I always thought my parents were just being superstitious.

​When my grandfather passed away three months ago, he left me his house—a creaky, two-story Victorian nestled at the edge of a dense pine forest. It was a beautiful place, but it came with one rule carved into the back of his handwritten will:

​“Whatever you do, do not touch the grand grandfather clock in the hallway. Do not wind it. Do not attempt to fix it. Let it run down on its own.”

​When I moved in, the clock was already ticking. It had a deep, hollow thump-click, thump-click that reverberated through the floorboards every single second. It was annoying at first, but after a few days, it became background noise.

​There was just one problem.

​Grandfather had been dead for ninety days. A clock like that—a late 1800s weight-driven pendulum—should have stopped running after eight days max. But every night, as I lay in bed, the steady rhythm continued.

​Thump-click. Thump-click.

​By week three, my curiosity finally beat out my respect for the dead. I stood in front of the dark mahogany casing. The glass door was locked with a small, rusted keyhole. I tried to peer inside, but the glass was yellowed and covered in a thick layer of grime that seemed to be on the inside of the cabinet.

​I leaned my ear against the wood.

​The ticking was loud. Unnaturally loud. But beneath the rhythm of the gears, I heard something else. A wet, rhythmic squelch.

​Thump-click. Squeee-ch. Thump-click. Squeee-ch.

​I pulled my head back, my heart hammering against my ribs. It sounded like someone squeezing a soaked sponge in time with the pendulum.

​That night, the dream started.

​I was standing in the hallway in total darkness. The clock was ticking, but the sound wasn’t coming from the cabinet—it was coming from inside my own chest. Every time the pendulum swung in my head, my lungs constricted, suffocating me. I woke up gasping for air, sweat drenching my sheets.

​It was 3:14 AM.

​Downstairs, the ticking had changed pitch. It was faster.

​Thump-click! Thump-click! Thump-click!

​I grabbed a flashlight and a crowbar from the garage. I knew I was violating his last wish, but the psychological toll was breaking me. I couldn't live with that noise anymore.

​I walked down the dark hallway, the flashlight beam cutting through the gloom. As I approached the clock, a foul odor hit my nose. It smelled like stagnant water, old iron, and rot.

​I shoved the wedge of the crowbar into the seam of the glass door.

​CRACK.

​The old brass latch gave way, and the door swung open.

​The first thing I noticed was the smell—it rushed out, so thick and pungent I almost gagged. The second thing I noticed was that there were no brass weights. There was no pendulum. There were no gears.

​The entire interior of the clock had been gutted.

​In place of the mechanical movement, a dark, pulsing mass of tissue was wired directly into the wooden frame with rusted copper staple nails. Thick, black veins ran up and down the interior walls, vibrating with every beat.

​Suspended from the center by a severed, blackened tendon was a human heart.

​It was roughly three times the size of a normal heart, pale grey, and coated in a glistening, viscous sludge. Every time it expanded and contracted, it struck the wooden sides of the clock frame.

​Thump.

​Then a thick valve would snap shut.

​Click.

​I dropped the crowbar. It shattered the hardwood floor, but I couldn't move. I was paralyzed, staring at the wet, living mechanism that had been keeping time in my hallway for decades.

​As I watched, the flesh around the top of the heart twitched. A long, thin split opened in the muscle—a seam—and slowly parted like a pair of dry, cracked lips.

​A raspy, wet breath escaped from inside the clock, blowing a plume of freezing, foul air right into my face.

​Then, a voice crawled out from the heart's valve. It was thin, gurgling, and instantly recognizable.

​"You..." it wetly wheezed. "You opened the door... Now the time has to come from somewhere else."

​Before I could scream, one of the thick black veins snapped free from the wood like a whipped cable and lashed around my wrist. The pain was absolute—it felt like a hundred burning needles digging through my skin, straight toward my bloodstream.

​I fell to my knees, beating at the black cord with my free hand, but the grip only tightened. I felt a sudden, ice-cold pull deep inside my chest.

​Thump-click.

​The heart in the clock pulsed.

​Thump-click.

​My own heart skipped a beat, completely out of rhythm.

​I managed to grab the dropped crowbar with my left hand and blindly swung it into the cabinet, hammering the heavy iron edge straight into the center of the rotting tissue.

​A high-pitched, screeching wail echoed through the hallway—a sound like metal tearing mixed with a dying animal. Black, foul blood sprayed across my face and clothes. The tendril around my wrist slackened, leaking a thick slime, and I tore myself free, scrambling backward down the hall until my back hit the front door.

​Inside the clock, the giant heart gave one final, violent spasm. A heavy spurt of black bile poured out of the bottom, pooling onto the floorboards.

​Then, it went still.

​Total, deafening silence filled the house.

​I didn't stop to pack. I ran out into the night, bare-chested and bleeding from my wrist, and drove until the sun came up. I'm writing this from a motel room three towns over. My arm is swollen, and where the vein latched on, there are dark, purple lines branching up toward my shoulder.

​I thought it was over. I thought I killed it.

​But about ten minutes ago, as I sat on the edge of the bed staring at the wall, the silence broke.

​Deep inside my chest, right behind my ribs...

​Thump-click.

​Thump-click.

​It’s not keeping my time anymore. It’s counting down.
