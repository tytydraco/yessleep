Dog Whistle: A Prairie Horror Story

I wake early. My mind wanting to get started for the day, but my body protesting it. It’s mid November in North Dakota, and the idea of going out to start my car already has me shivering. I get up anyways, slipping on my coat and boots. Outside it’s only about ten degrees, colder than usual for this time of year. I head back inside to get ready. i get dressed and grab only what I need. I finish lacing up my boots, grabbing my shotgun and a box of shells. After everything is loaded into the car I start driving. Only stopping to get out and open and close the cattle gates. The air making the barbed wire stiff with cold. 

Eventually I come to a stop along a snow covered section line only identifiable by the deep gravel filled depressions in the Earth, long covered by snow and ice. I step out of my truck, snow squeaking beneath my boots. I look at the landscape in front of me. There's an Eerie stillness. The air's sharp clarity is only amplified by the glassy deep blue hue that is only made more intense by the snow-covered hills. I grab my shotgun, a side-by-side given to me by my dad.

 The thought of my dad sends a pang of longing straight to my chest, making it difficult to breathe. I push my feelings aside and start walking. The silence is deafening, only interrupted by the crunch of my boots and the occasional hoot of great horned owl somewhere off in the distance.

I walk for 20 more minutes until I come across a tree line, looking at the trees and then back to the cattle trail in front of me, I decide to sit down and eat. I sit at the base of an old Chinese Elm tree. leaves long gone, leaving only the bare and Spidery branches. from my bag I take out the summer sausage and Hawaiian rolls already starting to freeze. I use my pocket knife to cut the frozen meat into more manageable pieces.

The skies changed from a deep bruised indigo, the prairie beneath it stretching out like an empty frozen ocean, the truck no longer visible hidden somewhere behind the rise of a dark ridge.

 I take my time eating. The pheasants are skittish this time of year anyways, running instead of flying when you find them. I sit under the Elm tree, my thoughts continuing to lead to my father. It had been just over five months since he passed away, life around me went on, but I feel as if my life ended when his did. My therapist says I have something called complex grief. That I need to try and socialize, get out of the house and get some fresh air. People never understand unless they’ve experienced it themselves. I watched the man I loved and respected the most in this world, be withered away by cancer. He was an outdoorsman in every way. He taught me how to respect and see the natural world through the eyes of a hunter. So here I am solo pheasant hunting, carrying on his legacy while trying to feel something other than anguish making me go crazy from greif. But right now all I feel is cold.

The wind has picked up and with it a chill. The kind you can feel in your bones. In the distance a pheasant cackles, signaling me to get going. I clean up my breakfast and stand up, stretching as I do. Grabbing my gun, I crack the barrel. the wind catches in the breach, making a hollow, innocent whistling sound. I put a shell on each barrel, and the whistling ceases. I take another look outward at the dead vegetation. A rusty contrast to the crisp white snow. I continue walking following the sound of the birds.

 I walk for what seems like forever, my thighs scream from exhaustion, the snow only growing deeper, the metal of the barrel painfully cold in my hands despite my gloves. The wind is growing stronger now, roaring against my aching eardrums. Just as I feel as if I can't go any further, a slough appears in the distance. This is enough to motivate me to move forward. As I reach the edge of the slough. I carefully start moving through it. The cattails loom over me making it difficult to see where I am and even more difficult to walk. as I’m about to give up  I hear that familiar two syllable cackle of a pheasant. I swing around, gun aimed and continue walking. The bird comes out of nowhere flying up in a blur of brown.  The flap of its wings loud in my ears. Within seconds I take my aim and squeeze the trigger.

An earsplitting boom sounds out. Quickly swallowed up by the vastness of the prairie. An explosion of feathers followed by a soft thud against the ground. The air is filled with the smell of smoke and sulfur. With my heart beating in my ears. I struggle through the cattails to retrieve the bird, stuffing it into the pocket in the back of my vest. The warmth of its body a small reprieve from the bitter cold and wind. I check my watch for the time. 3:30 PM. That’s not possible. I’ve only been out for a few hours. I left my house at six this morning and it should only be a bit past nine. I struggle to understand how I could lose almost 9 hours in what seemed like a matter of minutes. I dismiss it, my watch must’ve broken, battery dead from the cold.. I force my legs to carry me out of the cattails. It’s darker than it should be, and the wind is stronger. I was entirely alone. Then a sharp, two-note whistle pierced the steady drone of the wind.

I froze.

The sound had come from the thick brush along the tree line, barely fifteen feet to my left. It was clear, melodic, and terrifyingly human—like a man casually whistling for a dog that wasn’t there.

I spun, bringing my shotgun to my shoulder as my eyes strained against the fading purple twilight. Nothing moved. Only the skeletal branches of the cottonwoods clicked together in the breeze.

“Hello?”

My voice cracked and was swallowed by the endless prairie. I waited, my heart pounding so hard it hurt. No one answered. I took a cautious step backward, my boot crunching through the icy crust of the snow. The instant my heel hit the ground, another whistle answered from the trees. It didn’t just echo the sound—it copied the exact two-note rhythm of my footsteps. Another whistle came from deeper in the darkness, closer this time. It moved parallel to me, hidden just inside the brush.

Something was matching my stride.

Tracking me through the trees.

Playing with its food.

The last trace of indigo faded from the western sky, leaving the prairie buried beneath an unnatural darkness.

No… this isn’t right. It should still be daylight.

The wind rose into a mournful howl, dragging the temperature down with it. I broke into a panicked run, my boots punching through the frozen crust of the snow. Without a flashlight, the world dissolved into shifting shades of gray and black. I strained so hard to see that phantom spots danced across my vision. I’d lost the tree line.

Or worse…

The tree line had somehow wrapped around me. Every ragged breath froze inside my throat. Then the whistling changed. It wasn’t copying my footsteps anymore. It was copying my breathing. I gasped for air. From the darkness to my left, a sharp two-note trill answered in the exact same rhythm.  
I inhaled again.

The whistle came back instantly—closer now, almost skimming across the snow. I stumbled as my boot caught a hidden tractor rut. I hit the ground hard. My twelve-gauge flew from my numb fingers and disappeared into the drift. I scrambled onto my knees, clawing through the snow until my hands found cold steel. Then the wind died. Everything went still. A long, cheerful trill sounded directly above me. Close enough that I could smell it. Not the musky scent of an animal. A stale, metallic smell—like an old copper penny held beneath someone’s tongue. My knuckles scraped against the receiver, and I lunged forward, dragging the shotgun from the snow like a drowning man grabbing a rope.  
I staggered to my feet and jammed the stock into my shoulder, aiming into the blackness where the metallic smell lingered.

“Back off!”

My scream was ripped apart by the gale. I squeezed  the trigger.

Click.

No blast.

No recoil.

Just the sickening snap of the firing pin striking frozen metal.Snow had melted from my gloves and instantly refrozen inside the action, locking the mechanism solid.

“No… no… no!”

My breath burst into white clouds as I shoved my thumb against the top lever, trying to break the action open. It wouldn’t move. The freezing November cold had welded it shut. I slammed the heel of my hand against it.

With a harsh metallic crunch, the shotgun finally gave way—but only halfway. It hung open in a crooked, useless hinge. The shells refused to eject.  
I stared through the V-shaped gap in the broken gun into the swirling whiteout. The cheerful whistles stopped. In the sudden silence, they became something else.

Click.  
Clack.  
Click.  
Clack.

A dozen mouths imitated the frantic rattle of my broken shotgun, mocking every desperate movement from somewhere only inches away. I didn’t think. I flipped the side-by-side in my hands, gripping the frozen barrels like the handle of a club. With a scream so raw it tore at my throat, I swung.

THWACK.

The walnut stock slammed into something solid.  
The impact was horrifyingly dense, like hitting a frozen sack of wet sand. The impact shuddered through the shotgun, rattling my wrists so hard the pain shot into my shoulders. I expected a grunt. A growl. Anything.Instead, the instant the stock connected, a sharp, pressurized hissing whistle burst from the darkness. It sounded like air screaming from a punctured tire, but beneath it was the unmistakable cadence of a human laugh.  
Before I could swing again, the weight at the end of the gun shifted. The stock didn’t bounce back. Whatever I’d hit was holding on. I yanked with everything I had, but the shotgun stayed locked in the darkness.

Then I felt it.

A rhythmic vibration crawled down the twin steel barrels toward my hands.

Click.

Click.

Click.

It felt like a dozen tiny, hard points tapping against the metal. Teeth. Frozen fingernails. Something climbing toward my grip, hidden behind the blinding wall of snow.

Panic won.

I let go.

The sudden release threw me backward into a drift, icy snow spilling down my collar and into my sleeves. I never looked back. I scrambled to my feet and ran. I was moving on instinct now, praying my feet were carrying me toward the ridge where I’d left my truck.

The November wind slammed into my chest, driving needles of snow into my eyes. Every step shattered the frozen crust before sinking knee-deep into powder.

And beside me…

The whistling kept pace. It had become a quick, playful trill, darting from one side of me to the other, mocking every exhausted step. Stumbling over a frozen furrow, I jammed my hand into the pocket of my canvas hunting vest. My fingers were so numb they barely felt like they belonged to me.

Loose matches.

A crumpled candy wrapper.

Then cold, crosshatched steel.

My pocketknife.

Three inches of folding blade. Intended for cleaning birds. Worthless against whatever was chasing me. To open it, I’d have to stop, tear off my gloves with my teeth, and somehow pry the frozen blade open with fingers I could barely move. A violent gust swallowed the prairie, reducing the world to solid white. 

My boot slammed into a hidden barbed-wire fence. I pitched forward. The rusted wire ripped through my hunting pants and bit deep into my shin, tearing both gloves from my hands. I hit the frozen ground hard, the breath exploding from my lungs.

As I rolled onto my back, gasping, the whistling stopped. The silence was heavier than the storm. For one impossible instant, the swirling snow above me split apart. A pocket of absolute blackness hung there, descending toward my chest. I couldn’t make out a shape. Only darkness deeper than the night around it.

Then the wind shifted. The whiteout opened over the ridge. Less than a hundred yards away, the square outline of my truck emerged through the storm. I almost cried. I could make it.

A wet whistle burst directly beside my ear. So close I felt the pressure change inside my head. I screamed. Instead of untangling myself from the fence, I threw my entire weight forward. The rusted wire shrieking and groaning as it tore through canvas, denim, and flesh. Then I was free. I sprinted toward the ridge. My bare hand clenched the closed pocketknife until my knuckles turned white. It couldn’t save me. But it was something solid to hold.

The wind shoved me from behind while the whistling circled in dizzying loops around my head. The metallic smell of old copper filled the air until it coated the back of my throat.

Fifty yards.

Thirty.

Twenty.

The truck’s windshield caught the pale blue-gray light filtering through the storm. I lunged over the ditch and landed on the snow-covered gravel road. My hand slammed against the driver’s door. The freezing metal burned my skin. Behind me, the whistling stopped.

A heartbeat later came a heavy rush through the snow. Something enormous landed behind me.  
I yanked the door open, threw myself inside, and slammed it shut. The impact rattled the truck. A split second later, something hit the driver’s side with a heavy, solid THUD that shook the entire cab.  
I was inside.

The truck smelled of my dads old tobacco, and frozen vinyl. For the first time since the whistling started, I felt something close to hope.

My hands shook so violently I could barely get the key into the ignition. I turned it. The starter groaned once. Twice. Then faded into a pathetic whine. The battery was dying. The dashboard needles twitched before dropping lifelessly to zero. The engine never caught.

“Come on…”

My voice broke as I slammed my palm against the steering wheel.

“Please… come on.”

My frantic breath hit the frozen windshield, coating it in thick white frost until I couldn’t see outside. I had trapped myself in a blind box.  
Then the whistling returned.

Not outside.

Inside.

It vibrated through the truck’s metal frame before spilling from the heater vents. A sharp, playful trill. Then a wet hiss slid across the driver’s side window. Slowly, I turned my head. The frost on the glass began to melt. A perfect circle cleared from the outside in. Right at eye level. Something was pressed against the window. Waiting for me to look back. 

Looking away, I closed my eyes. I gripped the key with both hands and twisted it forward with a desperate, breaking twist. The engine exploded to life. The dashboard rattled violently, and a wave of blessed warmth surged through the steering column. At the same instant, the automatic headlights snapped on, throwing two blinding spears of yellow light into the heart of the swirling blizzard.

Through the small circle of melted frost on my side window, the black shape recoiled instantly from the sudden glare. I didn’t look at it directly. I couldn’t bear to, but as the headlights washed over the snowbank in front of the truck, I caught a shifting silhouette. It wasn’t an animal. It was a tall, impossibly, thin shadow that seemed to have too many joints, bending backward into the dead cattails like a snapped branch.

I slammed my still numb palm against the shifter, pulling it down to drive and stomped my boot onto the gas pedal. The rear tires shrieked in protest, spinning uselessly on the frozen gravel before grabbing hold of the earth. The truck fishtailed violently, slamming me against the door panel, before launching down the section line road. I tore through the blinding whiteout at sixty miles an hour, my eyes locked entirely on the road ahead. Behind me, fading into the dark expanse of the open prairie, the frantic whistling finally drowned out beneath the steady roaring salvation of the engine.

Ten miles down the paved County highway the ground blizzard began to thin. The truck heater was on full blast, filling the cab with a stifling artificial heat that made my torn skin sting. My hands still locked in a death grip around the steering wheel were slowly thawing, turning them a painful mottled red. The adrenaline was leaving my system, leaving behind a cold, hollow exhaustion.

I risked a glance at the rearview mirror behind me. The open prairie stretched out under the pale winter moon. An endless flat void of white snow and black Fields, completely indifferent to what had just happened. There were no lights. No signs of life. Just thousands of acres of dead November grass.  
As the truck hit a smooth stretch of asphalt, the violent rattling of the gravel road ceased, replaced only by the study hum of the tires against the highway lines. I let out a shaky, ragged breath. My chest finally loosening. Then my foot froze on the accelerator.

The rhythmic drone of the tires hadn’t changed pitch, but as the sound vibrated through the floorboards and into the quiet cab, the hum began to sort itself into distinct shifting syllables. It was a clean, dual toned melody, rising at the end. A perfect, cheerful imitation of a man whistling for a dog that wasn't there. I stared straight ahead into the empty highway, realizing with a cold, absolute certainty that the wind hadn’t been making that sound. That everything that had happened was real, and my grief wasn’t making me crazy. It was the land itself. Now that it had my voice it would never stop calling me back. Just like a dog to a whistle.
