# Journals

Forgive my horrific handwriting. I must get this out quickly.

The light started sputtering around dusk. I noticed it from the keeper's quarters, watching as the beam stuttered—faltering, dimming, catching itself, then faltering again. It was the first mechanical issue I'd seen since I arrived, and, ~~God~~ goodness, was I relieved. A flickering lamp was a problem I understood. A flickering lamp meant this was still just a lighthouse, a job I could learn and perform and leave behind.

I told myself the song I'd heard this morning was nothing—a mind left alone too long with its own sparks. I'd read about auditory hallucinations in extreme solitude—sailors hearing their mothers on the wind, arctic researchers called by name across the empty tundra. The mind abhors silence, so it fills the void with whatever it has on hand: memory, language, fragments of old hymns sung in an old church. That was all.

So I climbed the stairs. I counted as I went—a habit now, a compulsion for control—and the number held steady at one hundred ninety-six. The iron door was still locked from the outside, the bolt still thrown, exactly as I'd left it. I slid it open and pushed through into the lantern room.

The lamp was sputtering. The flame inside the lens guttered low and pale, casting jagged shadows and beams across the walls. The oil reservoir was half-full, but the wick looked worn, charred at the edges. I'd never tended a lighthouse lamp before, but my father's logbook held enough instructions. He'd written them out with the same meticulous care he'd given everything else. Every*thing*, not every*one.*

I opened the logbook to Day 217 and added a new entry beneath my father's last:

*Day 1. Weather: cloudy. No wind. Oil reserves: sufficient. The light holds. I remain.*

It felt strange to write "Day 1." My father's entries stretched back decades in an unbroken chain of days, and I was starting the count over as if none of it had happened.

But I couldn’t continue his work. I wasn't my father. I wasn't Abdi. I was the spare, and I would tend this light until I could figure out how to leave it.

So I trimmed the wick. I cleaned the lens. I checked the dampers. The work was repetitive and physical and soothing, the kind of labor that empties the mind and fills the hands. I found myself humming as I worked, some old melody I couldn't quite place, perhaps from church, or from the boat’s radio on the ride here. The lamp steadied under my attention, the flame rising gold and sure, and the beam resumed its steady sweep across the black water.

I was lost in the rhythm of labor when:  
"*Selam leki. Igziabher yimesgen.*"  
I responded without thinking. "*Igziabher yimesgen. Dehna neh?*"

The words left before I realized I still knew them—before I realized I was answering anything at all. It was pure reflex, the call-and-response of a thousand childhood mornings. My father used it every time we walked through the sanctuary, anytime he walked through our home door, the greeting his father had used before him. It was a habit buried so deep I'd forgotten its roots until it bloomed.

I turned around.

The lamp was burning well, but the light was no longer confined to the housing. It was spilling out in waves—slow pulses of gold and white—wrapping around the brass, pooling in the air like liquid, coalescing into a foggy shape that hung suspended beside the window.

It was vast. It was not vast in the way a man is tall or a tower is high, but vast in the way a night sky is vast. It was an immensity compressed into a form that my eyes could only hope to perceive. Rings of light nested within wheels of gems, wheels within rims of clouds, each rim lined with what might have been flames if the light were more solid. At the center, a being of radiance and flames, like a prism made flesh—arms of brass and chitin, legs of acacia and mercury, and a head without form and void inclined slightly as if in greeting. It was beautiful, but the proportions were wrong—too long, too radiant, too beautiful to look at directly. Wings of gaseous light spread like sunshine, shattering and reforming with every pulse of the lamp.

I cowered. There’s no better word for it. My knees buckled. My hands came up to shield me. The heat of the light was intense but not painful—like the breath of a rabid dog. I tried to speak and found my throat closed around the words.

The exit was behind the figure. Between me and the door, rings of light turned slowly, eyes watching without pupils, flames burning without heat. I could not run. I could not move. I could only kneel on the floorboards and hope to wake soon.

"Yohannes," it sang.

My father's name. The voice was the same as the song—layered, harmonic, many tones woven into one—but the words were clear now. Ge'ez, the liturgical tongue, the language of the church I'd abandoned.

"He is dead," I said. My voice was a rasp, barely audible over the hums. "My father is dead."

The light flickered. For a moment, the rings dimmed, the rays of wings folded inward, the being at the center bowed. Then the hum shifted into a mourning keen, low and resonant, the sound my mother made at Abdi's funeral, the sound I refused.

When it sang again, its voice was gentler, the harmonies stripped back to a single melodic thread. "And who do you think I am, Dawit Yohannes, son of the eighth keeper, brother of the unchosen firstborn, father of the daughter who dreams of stars?"

My name. My brother. My daughter. It knew all of them.

"A—A… please… please, spare me, demon—“

The light flared. Not in anger—at least, not anger as I understand. But the rings spun faster, the wings snapped outward, the heat pressed against my body like a door slamming shut, and I felt myself shoved backward by the sheer force of attention. My feet slid across the floorboards. My back hit the iron door. It swung open behind me, and I stumbled onto the landing, and the voice followed me out, kind and firm, like mother when I burnt my tongue on tea she warned about. It sang a single phrase over and over in too many words and too few notes:

*Try again, little wick. Try again. Try again. Try again.*

The iron door slammed shut. The bolt slid into place from the inside. The lamp steadied through the open door at the base of the tower, burning golden and calm, as if nothing had happened.

I sat on the landing for a long time. My hands were shaking. My face was wet, and I was too afraid to learn the source. The song was still, quieter now but persistent:

*try again try again try again*

And I knew I would have to. Not tonight. Not tomorrow, maybe. soon. The angel wanted something, and it had pushed me out to make me think. To make me try.

I went back to the keeper's quarters and opened father’s journal—blank. Not a single writing in any page. As though it were newly bound and sold on the markets. I wailed and threw it in anguish, then found the Bible. I searched my father's annotations for any mention of light. I needed to know what I was speaking to. I needed to know what my father had spoken to, during all those years alone in the tower.

*Who weeps for dead men? Who asks after his sons by name? Who greets a stranger in the language of his childhood, with the same words his father used at the threshold?*

Not a demon then. Something more. Angelic, perhaps, but what man could trap an angel? Perhaps this was something I don't have a name for yet.

*The light holds. I will try again.*

*—*

I spent the morning trying to make sense of my father's Bible. The annotations were dense, scattered, written in three different languages and a handwriting that deteriorated as the pages turned. Some notes were in English, crisp and academic. Some were in Amharic, cramped and hurried. A few were in Ge'ez, which I can still sound out and comprehend at a child’s level. My father had circled, underlined, and drawn lines connecting passages that seemed unrelated until I read them three times: light. Pride. Fallen. Bound. Holy but twisted, or never holy to begin with. I couldn't tell. The annotations contradicted each other.

*Helel is a title, not a name.*

Another, written later in a shakier hand, said *Helel is the name. He is the title now.*

I didn't know what that meant. Perhaps I still don't.

After three hours, I gave up. The Bible was too dense, too old, too encoded in a family history I'd spent my life avoiding. I couldn't decipher my father's theology from the outside. If I wanted to understand what was in the lantern room, I would have to ask it directly.

So I climbed the stairs again. One hundred ninety-six steps, then six steps to the iron door. It was unlocked this time—or never locked in the first place. I pushed it open.

It was sitting in the chair at the writing desk, reading the logbooks, and its body was a concert of light.

Instruments—not musical instruments in the most human of senses, but bones and flesh that produced sound and light—floated in a constellation that cohered into a shape roughly the size of me. Strings of gold and silver wound through a torso made of compressed air. Pipes of bone curved into shoulders and arms. A great bellows of azure fire expanded and contracted where lungs might have been. And its hands—too many to count—appeared and disappeared as it turned the pages, each one forming a different instrument as though perfectly evolved for worship: a harp, a horn, a flute, a drum, fingers that were bows drawing across strings that were veins. Its face was a nebula, with no eyes I could see but a sense of attention like a hand on my throat.

It hummed as it read, a new melody I'd heard too long ago, but now I could see the music radiating outward from its body in waves, pulsing through the air like ripples on water. The logbook in its many hands was the one my father had kept, the one that ended.

"It's curious," the being sang, its voice a chord of flutes and reed pipes and timpani. "Your father noted the weather every day. Every single day, for nearly fifty years. Even when the weather never changed. The sea was flat and the sky was grey and nothing moved except the light, and yet your father wrote." It turned a page, and a ripple of harps cascaded through the room. "Is that what it is to be mortal? To record sameness as if the recording makes it matter?"

I didn't answer. I was still standing in the doorway, one hand on the iron frame, ready to close the door, or ready to leave it open.

The being looked up from the logbook; the stars in its face shifted, and I felt the attention refocus on me. "I asked you a *question*."

"I don't know what it means to be mortal," I said. My voice was steadier than I expected. "I've only been doing it for thirty-four years."

A pause. Then a sound I can only describe as…a bright, arrhythmic cascade of chimes and bells and the plucked strings. It was warm. It was surprised. It was the first time anything in this world had seemed genuinely delighted by something I'd said in years.

"You’re not what I expected, Dawit. Your father was so *serious*. So meticulous. He never made jokes like that." It set the logbook down and folded several of its hands together, while others continued to trace idle patterns in the air, leaving trails of silver music behind them. "I’ve been trying to learn how to speak to you. It's been a very, very long time since I spoke to anyone who was not already halfway to me. Your father was far along by the time we began our conversations. You are... fresh. Words feel different in your mouth, and you do not yet know how to listen with anything but your ears."

It leaned forward. Its bellows flared, and the pipes along its shoulders emitted a low chord.

"Tell me about Yohannes. What happened to him? I felt him stop, but I could not see it. The light only shows me what it touches, and your father was dark."

I swallowed. The question was strange—it knew my father was dead, it had wept for him the night before, but it didn't know how, even being one floor above where it happened.

"I was hoping you could tell me," I said. I didn't bother to hide the edge in my voice. "He burned while standing. There was ash on the floor below and a note telling me to sweep it into the reservoir. That's all I know. That's all anyone knows."

The being was silent for a long moment. The nebula dimmed and the tower gave me the first true silence since I’d docked. When it spoke again, its voice was a single cello note.

"*I* didn’t know. I felt him stop, but I didn’t know how." A pause. "I would have liked to say goodbye. He reminded me of someone I knew a very long time ago."

I was still standing in the doorway, still ready to run, but something in the grief held me there. It wasn't performing. It wasn't manipulating. It was mourning a man it had spent fifty years learning to speak to, and I had interrupted that mourning by trying to exorcise it.

"Have you figured out what I am yet?" the being continued, and the music returned, lighter now, almost a tease. "You ran out of here last night with a question on your tongue."

I shifted the Bible in my hands. "I still have questions."

"Ask."

"Are you a god?"

The chimes laughed again, more like a flourish this time, like an orchestra tuning their instruments. "No. No, no, no. Next ti—“

"Are you an angel?"

It tilted its head, continuing its reading. "Closer, but not quite."

"Are you Sargon?"

The being froze. All of its hands stopped moving at once, and the nebula sharpened into something almost like a face—features of light and shadow, a brow of condensed starlight, a mouth that moved when it spoke. Its features looked almost human now, draping with a beard and tied hair. "*Šarrugi of Akkad?* The babe pulled from a river in a basket of reeds?" It tilted its head, and the snares along its spine shook with what I can only describe as amusement. "That's a new one. No, little wick, I’m not Sargon, though the comparison is kind enough. He was a remarkable man—conquered Sumer, built an empire, and spent his old age writing letters to his daughter about the proper way to store grain.” It turned back to its reading, “I liked him."

"Isaiah compared you to him," I said, flipping through the Bible to the passage I'd found that morning. "In the fourteenth chapter."

"Yeshayah," the being said full of affection, the way you say the name of an old friend. "Poor man. He was always the dramatic. He saw me *once*, in a single vision, and spent the rest of his life trying to find metaphors big enough to hold me. He couldn't, of course. So he spoke of the biggest thing he knew: a king. A conqueror." The being's nebulae clouds shifted into something like a smile. "You're halfway there, Dawit. Keep going."

I looked down at the passage and my father's pencil marks. "Helel?”

"Yes."

I read the verse aloud, my voice unsteady: "How you have fallen from the sky, Morning Star, song of the Morning."

The being laughed again—not chimes this time, but something deeper and scratchier, almost human. "That's a *terrible* translation. 'Song of the Morning'? Is that what they're teaching now? Good Lord, no, no. Let me give you the real one." It straightened in the chair, and its voice shifted into a formal register, the words sang like a hymn:

*"Helel, who rises up earliest, how you have fallen down from heaven; you who has wounded the nations, fell down to the ground."*

It continued from memory, its many hands tracing the words in the air in letters of verdant light:

"Who said in your heart, 'I shall ascend into heaven, I shall exalt my throne above the stars of the heavens; I shall sit on the mountain where the gods meet, on the sides of the north. I shall ascend on the highness of the clouds; I shall be like the Highest.'”

Helel paused. The green letters, scripts unlike any I’d seen in this life, hung in the air, then dissolved into a fine mist of crimson. When it sang again, its voice was quiet, stripped of performance.

"Do you know why I said that?"

I knew the answer the church had given me. The answer drilled into me since childhood, the answer my father had given his congregation before he left them for this tower. "Pride," I said.

"No," Helel said, no longer in song. "Love."

The word hung in the air between us, and for the first time, I saw something that I recognized—a love so fierce that the loving itself became sin.

"I loved the Morning," Helel said, and its voice was a single viola now. "With a love that wanted to be near—not to overthrow, but to be *one*. I wanted to ascend because I wanted to be close, closer than ever.” Two stars, supernovae in their galaxies, turned to catch my eye, “Do you understand? When you love something, you do not want to worship it from a distance. You want to sit beside it. You want to become part of its light."

The bellows of its lungs expanded, and the pipes along its shoulders released a chord that made my teeth ache like tasting the purest of sugars.

"I succeeded," it said, lowering all of its hands into a dewy mist. "I am the light now. Complete. Eternal. And it is the loneliest in all creation."

I couldn't speak. The Bible was heavy in my hands, and my throat was tight. Somewhere in the back of my mind, I thought of Meron. Her small hands molding her slime that stuck to the tablecloth for weeks. Her gap-toothed laugh drenching my cheeks in saliva and joy. Her cries when I leave her in the fall, and the way I cry too, in the car, where she’ll never see.

"You know this love," Helel stated. "You would burn for her. You would climb any height. You would ascend beyond the stars if it meant holding her again."

I didn't say it was wrong. I turned and walked out of the lantern room, and it let me go, shutting the door behind me like a gentle mother for a sick child.

The fish traps were full today. Three of them, silver and small, with those pale milky eyes that saw nothing and all. I gutted them on the rocks and thought about what Helel had said. I opened the Bible again to Isaiah 14, read the passage over and over, searching for a lie.

I couldn't find it. The words were there as It spoke them. And I—standing on a black rock with fish guts on my hands and thoughts of my summer daughter—could not say I would not do the same.

I remember the first time Meron called me "Baba." She was barely one, learning the shape of words, and she looked up at me from her high chair with oatmeal smeared across her cheek and said it like it would be her entire world: "Baba."

I cried in Sarai’s arms that night. I didn't cry when Abdi seized or when father burned—I still haven't truly, if I’m being honest—but I cried when my daughter named me.

I remember the last time I saw her, nearly a month ago now. She was standing on her mother’s porch, waving with both hands, and shouting "Don't forget the seashells, Baba! The ones that sing the waves!" I promised I wouldn't forget. I promised I would bring her the biggest shells I could find.

Why haven't I looked for any yet?

If someone told me I could never see her again unless I climbed a tower and burned to become a light that never died—

I would climb. I would burn. I would become anything for her.

I understand Helel better than I want to.

*The light holds. I remain another day.*

*—*

I decided not to climb today.

The light was burning steadily when I woke—I could see its beam through the window, pale gold against the orange morning, still sweeping its arc across the flat water like a spotlight searching a prison crowd. No sputtering. No dimming. Whatever Helel needed, It didn't need me today.

But I needed a day where I didn't think about fallen lights or Morning Lights or the way my father's handwriting deteriorated in his final entries.

So I went fishing. Not with the traps this time. I found a reel in the storage closet, buried behind a crate of oil tins—an old Shimano, scratched and corroded but still functional. I recognized it immediately—my father's reel. He'd brought it with him when he left for the lighthouse, I guess. Or maybe it had been here longer than that, passed down through the generations of keepers like everything else in this place.

I held it in my hands for the time between the sloshes of waves, running my thumb over the worn grip. And I remembered…

I must have been nine, maybe ten. Abdi was twelve, all long limbs and easy confidence, the kind of older brother who made everything look effortless, and made sure you felt it. Our father took us to Lake Tana, a rare trip, just the three of us. He'd been distant for weeks—he always got distant, cycling through periods of attention and distant gazes—but that day he was present. He taught Abdi how to cast, patient in a way he rarely was, guiding his arm through the motions.

He showed me how to bait the hook without piercing my thumb. We caught nothing all morning, but it didn't matter. We sat on the rocks and ate injera wrapped in newspaper and watched the pelicans dive for his scraps. He laughed when one snatched a piece. He frowned when one dove without catching.

I remember Abdi caught the first fish after lunch—a tilapia, wriggling and precious. He held it up like a trophy while our father clapped him on the shoulder. I remember the jealousy—not of the fish, but of the shoulder clap. The way our father looked at Abdi the same way Abdi looked at the fish. The way our father looked at me the same way I looked at the bait.

I remember, later that afternoon, our father staring out at the water with distance again. Abdi asked him what he was looking at. He didn't answer for a long time. Then he said, "The sun on the water. It looks like a path, doesn't it? Like you could walk out on it."

Abdi laughed. I didn't. I stared at the light on the water and wondered where our father went when his eyes emptied like that. I know now. He was in this lighthouse, even then, already burning before the flames touched him.

My line jerked. I snapped back to reality so fast I nearly dropped the reel. Something had taken the bait—god, it was heavy, heavier than the silver fish I'd been catching in the traps. The line screamed off the spool, and I braced my feet against the rocks and started reeling.

We fought for ten minutes. The water churned, black and thick like burnt sugar, and I caught glimpses of a pale and vast shadow beneath the surface. When I finally hauled it onto the rocks, I barely understood what I was looking at.

A manta ray—easily a meter across, its great dark fin-wings spreading across the wet stone like a shadow given flesh. It was beautiful. Its skin was bright and flushed, almost translucent, and I could see veins of something that glowed faintly. It thrashed once, weakly, and its mouth opened, and I heard a song.

Not the beautiful layered harmonies of Helel's voice. This was a thin, anguished keen, like a string instrument tuned too tight and played by someone who didn't know the melody. It was pain and confusion. It was a creature that had never been meant to sing deciding to sing anyway.

I knelt to pull the hook from its mouth, and that's when I saw the fish—a small silver one, the kind I'd been catching in the traps—clutched in the ray's jaw. The ray hadn't eaten it. It had been holding it. And the fish was singing too—a smaller, higher keen, harmonizing with the ray's deeper note. Two voices, braided together in incomprehensible anguish.

The fish's eyes were the same milky blue I'd seen before, but this time, beneath the milk, I saw a faint lavender glow. The same color as the veins in the ray's wings. The same color as the light that pulsed through Helel's nebula-face when It spoke of love.

I stumbled backward. The ray thrashed again, and I grabbed it by the edges of its wings—the skin was warm, feverish—and tossed it back into the water. It vanished into the black with a final flick of its tail, its song cutting off mid-note.

I sat on the rocks for a long time, my hands still slick with whatever coated the ray's skin. The water was flat again. The light was still sweeping its arc. Nothing had changed.

I went inside for breakfast: Bread, a heel of cheese, and water from a canteen that tasted faintly of minerals. I was sitting at the desk, chewing mechanically, when I noticed my reflection in the window.

The sun was behind clouds, the room was dim, and my fingertips were faint. Just at the tips, where the skin meets the nail, I saw a pale luminescence, the color of moonlight on still water. I held my hands up in front of my face and watched them. The glow pulsed, very slightly, in rhythm with my heartbeat.

I told myself it was the oil from the lamp. I'd been tending it two days ago, trimming the wick, and the residue must have gotten on my skin. That happened; that was a thing that happened to lighthouse keepers constantly.

Then I told myself it was the fish. The fluid from the ray's wings, some bioluminescent bacteria, completely natural, completely explainable.

Then I told myself it was the stress. The low sleep, the nightmares, the isolation. My mind was playing tricks on me, projecting fears into my body.

I looked at my reflection again. The window was dark enough to cast a faint image back—my face, my shoulders, my hands resting on the desk. And my eyes—

My eyes caught the light in a way they shouldn't have. Not the flat reflection of a human cornea, but pale and lavender.

I closed my eyes. I counted to ten. I opened them again.

The glow was still there—fainter now, maybe. I couldn't tell anymore. That's the thing about being alone in a lighthouse—you lose the ability to tell the difference between what's real and what you're afraid might be real.

I'm writing this down because writing is the only thing that still feels real, still tied to my old world. The world where fathers didn't burn standing up. The world where fish didn't sing. The world where your reflection didn't glow back at you with borrowed light.

And I keep thinking about something Helel said yesterday. 'Your father was far along by the time we began our conversations. You are fresher.'

Far along into what?

I want to write more about my hands, my eyes, the glow that I'm still not sure I saw. But I've changed my mind. I want to write about Meron instead.

She called me before I flew out. Sarai put her on the phone even though it wasn't my day, because Meron had had a nightmare and wanted me to sing the song I used to sing when she was small—the one my mother sang to me, *Octopus’ Garden*. I sang it to her over a crackling international line while she sniffled and calmed down, and when I finished, she said, "Silly Baba, you sound like a lullaby when you're not singing!”

I didn't know what to say to that, but I've been thinking about it all day. Maybe Meron heard something in me that I'm only now starting to recognize.

I'm going to read more, then sleep. I think tomorrow I'll climb the tower again. I have more questions for Helel. And I think I'm afraid It’ll have answers.

*The light holds still.*

*—*

I need to sleep. Tomorrow I'll… maybe I’ll confront It. Maybe I’ll search the keeper's quarters more thoroughly—there has to be something my father left behind, some explanation. I'll update when I can.
