The first time someone asked me how many people were in the raft, I said eight. That was how many the rescue crew took aboard, and I was trying to answer the questions correctly. A woman had scissors against my neck, cutting through the collar of my shirt. Someone else was asking whether I could feel my feet. When the man with the clipboard asked again, I gave him the same number. I had been counting to eleven all night.

I was thirty-four then, a science teacher. I'd spent the previous Saturday helping my sister move because I found it easier to give someone an entire day than say no. Until that night, I thought my inability to refuse people was a kind of generosity. I had fairly flattering explanations for myself.

The Caroline was a small passenger vessel making an evening crossing to the Maine mainland. Our departure had been delayed by weather, but we went. Forty minutes later, the engine stopped and the lights went out. I was concentrating on keeping a sandwich down, with both hands pressed against the seat in front of me.

A deckhand came into the cabin carrying life jackets. His name was Owen Shaw. He told us to put them on and remain seated while he checked the deck. Someone asked whether we were sinking. He said, "Put it on first, please," and helped an elderly man with his straps. Then water ran along the aisle, and everybody stood up together.

Owen came back with blood running into one eye and sent us toward the stern. One raft canister went over without anybody holding its line. Another opened beyond the stern, its canopy appearing behind the spray. People were heading toward it when the boat rolled hard enough to put the railing underwater. A woman beside me was pulling her daughter out from under a folded bench. I helped lift it. All three of us went into the water.

I couldn't breathe properly. My life jacket held my chin up while water hit my face. Someone inside the third raft, still tethered beside the hull, dragged me to its opening, and I climbed across people to get in. A young man in a kitchen jacket pulled the girl in after me. Her mother followed. Owen came last, dragging one leg; his boot caught on the boarding ladder and he made a sound that stopped everybody talking. He told a man with a closely shaved head which line to cut, and we fell away from the Caroline.

There were eleven of us. I counted because the girl asked me to. Michael Hayes, Miriam Tate, her daughter June, Ben Torres, his baby Sophie, Owen Shaw, Victor Kane, Arthur and Ruth Bell, Leah Sethi, and Nico Alvarez. June was sixteen. Sophie was seven months old; Ben kept repeating that as though it might help us find something she needed.

Sophie's infant life jacket held her so awkwardly she couldn't turn her face into her father's chest. Leah helped him position her and got her against his chest beneath his open coat, with the infant jacket secured around the layers. She wrapped them both in a survival blanket. Miriam held the torch. June reached between people's knees to retrieve a little knitted hat floating on the floor.

The raft was meant for eight, according to the number inside the canopy. Two inflated tubes surrounded us, one above the other. Even with our knees drawn up, we overlapped. When somebody moved, somebody else had to move first. Water washed through the entrance and collected beneath our legs. Our socks stayed wet even when the floor was nearly empty, and the air inside smelled of rubber, vomit, and soaked wool. If I reached for anything, my elbow went into somebody's ribs. After a while I learned to ask before moving, then hated myself for being irritated when someone asked me.

Victor, the man who had cut us loose, spread us around the perimeter. Owen talked Nico through deploying the sea anchor, and we stopped turning so violently. We found a bailer, sponges, a pump, flares, water packets, and a repair kit. There was no radio. Owen had lost the separate grab bag getting out.

"The boat's got a beacon," he said. "That doesn't depend on me holding a radio. Keep that opening shut. You're getting the whole sea in." Victor and Miriam secured the flap with cord where a fastening had torn. Victor used his belt on another section, then checked that the loose end couldn't hit Sophie. He could decide what to do. I was still waiting to be told where to put my hands.

My watch still worked: 8:17. I checked it repeatedly, expecting the passage of a few minutes to have changed our situation. Owen said we'd been clear of the boat for less than half an hour. It felt longer, and I resented him for correcting me.

Leah, an off-duty paramedic, examined Owen while Ruth held his head. His leg was badly injured, but she was more concerned about his chest and abdomen. He'd been struck by shifting deck equipment. She asked where it hurt. "Depends where you're touching," he said, and Arthur gave a surprised laugh.

Arthur was seventy-six, with a cut behind his ear and bruising across his ribs. Ruth told Leah he took heart medication, then told Arthur not to look at her like that because it was relevant. He said he wasn't looking at her like anything. They sounded like people arguing in a pharmacy. I felt better for a few seconds.

Nico, twenty-two, had been working in the galley. He apologized for bleeding on Leah while she examined his split lip. "I don't charge for that," she said. He laughed, then started crying without changing his expression. She kept a hand on his shoulder until he could talk again.

We worked. We bailed, checked the tubes, huddled, and used whatever we could to insulate people from the floor. When Victor suggested discarding our shoes, Owen said the weight wasn't worth losing protection for our feet. We shifted whenever a low side took water. Sometimes it helped. There are things we did properly that I need to record without pretending they cancel anything.

Ben gave Owen his fleece, keeping his coat around Sophie. Owen protested, and Ben said, "Man, you've got one pant leg," as though they were friends arguing about a dress code. He tucked the fleece around Owen himself. I hadn't asked him to do it, and Owen hadn't asked either.

June had cut her left hand getting out. While Leah dressed it, Miriam said she was being brave. "Could you stop doing the dentist voice?" June asked. Miriam looked offended, then laughed through her nose and handed me the torch. When June learned I was a teacher, she asked whether we'd have to introduce ourselves with an interesting fact. I said I was considering a seating plan. She said we'd have trouble with the fire exits.

We laughed too hard at that. Ruth kept pushing more blanket over Arthur while he pushed it back. Owen apologized for being sick on my pants. Every time we stopped working, our teeth began chattering, so we talked about what would happen when we got in. Not whether.

Victor opened a water packet for Ruth and held it while she drank, turning it so she wouldn't cut her lip on the edge. He wanted his wife called before she saw the news. Nico said his mother would make this about him not answering his phone. Miriam asked June whether she'd eaten since lunch, then said, "Well, that was a useful question." June had eaten half a bag of something her mother had called nutritionally criminal.

They'd been looking at a photography course. Miriam objected to paying for accommodation when there was a course at home. June said the shared bathroom wasn't the main attraction. Later, I heard her offer to study locally if it would stop Miriam worrying. "Don't decide your life in a rubber boat," Miriam said. June said it had a roof. Her mother brushed the hair off her forehead. June pulled Miriam's sleeve down over her bare wrist and told her she needed the blanket too. They sat quietly together.

The top tube had a soft patch where it had scraped the Caroline. We took turns pumping it up. A repair plug slowed the leak, but the damage was wet and awkward to reach. Owen explained that there were separate chambers; we hadn't lost all our buoyancy. When Victor looked at the capacity marking, he said, "Eight isn't a trapdoor. Doesn't give up at nine. We've got damage. We deal with the damage."

Owen was forty-five and had worked the crossing for nine years. While Nico took a turn pumping, he told us his daughter had just started at a grocery store and already thought she could run it. He'd helped her practice counting change with a jar of coins. June said the register did that now. "So she told me," Owen said. "After forty minutes." He asked about the photography course and wanted to know whether June still had to buy film. They discussed it while I squeezed water into the bailer. For a little while he wasn't a casualty we were trying to arrange around; he was somebody's dad, getting things slightly wrong.

I left that out of my first account. I said Owen agreed we were overloaded. He had said that too. It took me a long time to admit I had selected the part that suited us.

An aircraft passed beyond the canopy. We heard it before seeing a light behind the rain. Victor fired a flare, and for a few seconds every face was red. The engine faded. We kept the entrance open too long, trying to find it again, and a wave dumped water across our legs. Victor said it would come back. Owen said it might not have seen us. We asked both men to repeat themselves.

Victor asked Owen whether the beacon could have gone down with the boat. Owen said it was supposed to release, but he hadn't seen it happen. "Supposed to," Victor repeated. Owen said it was designed for exactly this, that people would be looking once the boat was overdue even without it. Victor asked when overdue became long enough to worry. Owen didn't know. Victor was forty-nine, ran a roofing business, and later said he was used to being responsible for crews. At the time I only heard a man insisting on definite answers when the one person with relevant experience couldn't give them.

Ben asked how far we could drift before daylight. Owen started explaining currents and wind. Victor interrupted to ask whether we could paddle toward shore. Owen told him we'd exhaust ourselves without knowing which way shore was. It was sensible advice. We stayed where we were, but Victor kept looking toward the blank canopy as though somewhere beyond it was a direction Owen refused to supply.

Arthur died before we heard another engine. He stopped answering Ruth, and Leah had us shift to get him flat. She gave breaths and tried chest compressions, bracing him against the yielding floor while we held his shoulders. She made us keep the light on him. There wasn't room to work properly, and she kept asking for space we didn't have. When she finally stopped, Ruth was holding his sleeve out of the water. She asked whether he was unconscious. Leah answered quietly. Ruth nodded, then said his neck was bent and asked us to turn him a little.

Victor waited until another wave came through the fastening. Then he said we needed to talk about weight. Leah told him not now. He said Arthur couldn't be helped and the rest of us could. Ruth looked at him without seeming to understand. When he said we couldn't keep her husband aboard, she said he wasn't taking up any more room than ten minutes ago.

Ruth offered to hold him outside until rescue. Owen said we needed the line and couldn't have a body against the entrance. She gripped Arthur's jacket anyway. "There. You don't have to do anything. I'll do it." Her fingers were too swollen to close properly around the fabric. Victor asked me to help lift him. I said we'd be respectful. Ruth asked whether I thought she was concerned about our manners.

We let her get tired of holding him. Nico suggested giving her time. Victor asked how much, and Nico looked down at the bailer. Eventually Leah took Ruth's hands. She didn't tell us to proceed, but she didn't release them when Victor and I lifted Arthur.

I held him under the shoulders. His head rested against my wrist. As we slid him through the opening, Ruth said, "He's frightened of deep water," in the same reasonable voice she'd used about his tablets. We removed his life jacket first because Victor said we might need it. His wedding ring caught on a fold of the blanket. I freed it so we could keep moving.

His body went away from my hands, then the raft lifted and I couldn't see him. Ruth asked which direction he'd gone. Nobody answered. I gave her his life jacket. She kept it on her lap until we were rescued.

Less water came through afterward. The sea had changed, or our position had, or we'd gained a little height. We couldn't measure it. Victor pressed the upper tube and said, "There. Feel that." Ben felt it. I didn't, but I nodded.

Victor assigned shifts. Miriam and I bailed, Nico pumped, Ben helped when he could. Leah kept checking people. Owen gave instructions. Ruth held the sponge without using it until June quietly put her hand over Ruth's and squeezed the water out.

I helped Victor count who was working. Owen apologized for not sitting up. Victor said he wasn't accusing anyone, then asked if Owen could at least hold the entrance clear with his good foot. He tried, moving a little, waiting for the pain to ease, then moving again. Leah told him to stop.

Water collected around him. We bailed it out. Ben looked at the place Owen occupied and quietly asked Leah how long someone could survive with internal bleeding. She said you couldn't tell by looking. Ben said he was only asking.

"Why are you asking me quietly? He's right there," she said. Ben became angry, saying she didn't need to make everything an accusation. Sophie stirred, and he lowered his voice so quickly the rest of us did too. Owen asked Nico to bring him the pump.

He managed a few strokes lying down before he had to stop. When Victor reached for it, he held on. "Give us a minute. It's the angle." Nico took over without making him release the handle. Owen began telling us things nobody had asked: where spare line was stored, how to use the signaling mirror after dawn. He kept checking that we were listening.

June folded her coat beneath his head. Miriam protested that she needed it herself. June said she'd rather not have his ear in her knee. Owen thanked her and asked her name again, although he'd already used it. I think he wanted her to keep talking.

By midnight the tube had softened again. We pumped and bailed. I could grip with my whole hand but couldn't pinch the edge of a packet. Victor said we couldn't sustain this until morning. Owen said we didn't have to wait until morning to be found. Victor asked whether he could guarantee that. Owen closed his eyes.

I suggested giving it another hour. I thought I was opposing what was happening. Instead I'd agreed to a deadline after which Owen could be reconsidered. Victor checked his watch and said, "All right." Owen looked at me. I couldn't tell whether he was grateful or afraid.

Before the hour was up, water folded the canopy inward and filled the floor. Sophie made a thin noise Ben couldn't soothe, then stopped crying altogether. He uncovered her face and said her name, gently at first, then loudly enough that everyone looked. Leah put two fingers against the baby's neck and asked Ben to keep talking to her. Sophie opened her eyes, but her usual fussing didn't return. Ben asked Leah if she was dying. She said she needed warmth and that he had to keep her protected from the water. He asked again. Miriam told him to help bail if he wanted his daughter warm. He shoved the bailer against Owen's boot, apologized when Owen cried out, then said, looking at Victor, "We can't even move in here."

The proposal was to put Owen outside on a line while we stabilized the raft. Nobody called it throwing him out. Leah said the water would kill him. Owen said he couldn't hold on. Victor said we'd tie him. Owen asked who would pull him back.

"We will," I said. "When we've got it cleared." I hadn't considered how we'd lift an injured man back over the tube. Giving the plan a return made it possible to keep discussing the departure. Owen asked me to promise. I said we'd get him in. He asked me to look at him when I said it, so I did. His hair was plastered over the cut above his eye. I remember wishing he would wipe it away.

Leah wanted two people pumping while Nico tried the repair from another angle. Victor said Owen was in the way. Nico said we could move him across, and Miriam began making room. A practical solution was happening in front of us. We let Victor interrupt it.

"And when that doesn't work?" he asked, repeating it until we were discussing what might happen afterward instead of trying. Owen pulled on the overhead line. "Just move me, then. Come on. I'll help." Ben told him to stop rocking the raft.

Owen swore at him. He sounded as though he might fight us, and I felt a cowardly relief; anger was easier to resist than a man politely asking to remain alive. Victor told him to calm down. June said he didn't have to be calm. Miriam put a hand on her arm and said, "Let them talk." I picked up the loose line Victor handed me.

Owen saw it. "Michael, don't." Then, to Ben: "I got her out. Took her off you so you could climb. Remember?" Ben stared into his coat. He said Owen didn't need to do that, meaning remind him. Owen said, "Then don't make me."

Leah put herself between Owen and the opening. Victor reached across her, and she struck his hand away. The raft tipped. I shouted at her to stop, because she was the one who might listen. Miriam caught a canopy support. Nico was still saying we could move Owen across.

When the raft settled, Victor and Ben lifted him. Owen caught my cuff. For a second I thought he was helping. Then he said, "Don't let them," and I knew what he was asking. I pulled my sleeve free and looped the line through his life jacket's lifting point.

They lowered him outside. He kicked once with his good leg and struck the entrance. His injured leg hung at an angle I couldn't bear to examine, and he tried to bring both knees toward his chest when the next wave reached him. Leah tried to get past Ben. He pushed her back and said he had a child. He said it as though we had forgotten what a child was.

Owen's head appeared beside the tube. He said he couldn't breathe when the water hit him. Victor told him to lean back. Owen said he couldn't, then called Nico, who crawled to the opening and tried to lift his arm over the edge. Two of Owen's fingers found the orange seam. He held it until the raft rose, stretching his arm straight; his mouth went below the water and his fingers slipped loose. When he surfaced, he was coughing hard enough that he couldn't get my name out. I watched the place where the rope crossed the tube instead of his face. I could have moved my hands from the rope to Owen.

Victor told me to close the flap because another wave was coming. I pulled Nico back by his jacket. He struggled upright and tried again. Someone shouted that we were going over. I tightened my grip on Nico, and he stopped fighting long enough for Victor to draw the flap down.

The line moved against my palm. It wasn't the sea moving it evenly; Owen was pulling in short, frantic bursts, with pauses long enough that I kept thinking he'd lost his grip. I shortened it, thinking that would keep Owen's face near the raft. Every hard pull made the wet rope saw across the crease of my fingers. Leah demanded we bring him back. Victor said to wait for the next lull. I repeated that. I don't know how many lulls we let pass.

When Nico opened the flap, the line ran beneath the raft. We pulled; Owen's jacket came against the lower tube without his face appearing. Leah made us keep trying. We couldn't position him to lift. Eventually Victor cut the line. I had let go before he did. The rope left a pale, numb strip across my palm, and for several minutes I kept closing my hand around nothing.

Ben said Owen had probably been dying anyway. With Owen gone, I could move my right knee without pressing into anybody's side. Nico got both hands onto the pump, and Ben shifted Sophie higher against his chest. I noticed the relief in my cramped leg before I understood how badly I wanted it to mean we'd done the right thing. June said he'd been talking. Ben told her she didn't understand his injuries. "I understand he asked you to stop," she said. Then she looked at me. "You said you'd bring him back."

I said I'd tried. She looked at Nico's torn jacket. I followed her eyes and began explaining the wave. She asked where the bailer had gone. She had stopped expecting an answer to the other thing.

We didn't become efficient afterward. Ben couldn't fit the pump connection. I lifted the sponge without squeezing it. Ruth asked whether we were keeping Owen's jacket too. Ben put the fleece he'd lent him behind his back. Leah spoke only when she had to.

Nico worked on the damage while I held the torch. Victor said we were letting heat out. Nico said he needed to see. Victor asked whether he'd take responsibility if the baby got colder. I lowered the flap. Nico stared at me, then worked through the smaller opening.

June was shivering hard enough to make her jaw ache. Her coat was soaked. Miriam tried to put her own around her, but June couldn't work her injured hand through the sleeve. They argued over which arm went first with an intimacy that made me turn away. I had no right to feel intrusive after what they'd watched me do.

"When we get in, don't let them put me in the paper," June said. Miriam offered to fight the newspaper. "I mean it. They'll use my school picture, where they made me hide my teeth." Miriam said there was nothing wrong with her teeth. "I know. That's why I don't like it."

One of her canines sat behind the other teeth, crowding the corner of her smile. Miriam said they could take another picture when they got home. June said that wasn't the point, then leaned against her. Leah asked me to tuck a blanket around June's legs.

I asked about the course because I didn't know how to apologize for anything that mattered. June liked printing in a real darkroom, seeing whether she'd done it right before somebody corrected it on a screen. She probably wouldn't go away, she said. Miriam said, "You can go wherever you want once we've got you dry." June rolled her eyes at me. I told her she had time to decide. She asked if I thought we'd get in. I said yes.

The hand beneath June's dressing had swollen, and she could no longer straighten two fingers. Leah checked her, asking questions June answered with increasing irritation. She said she knew her own name. Miriam said she could be rude again when she was warm. Then June noticed water entering through a fold below the damaged fastening. Nico helped her tuck it outward. It helped. Victor said, "Good girl," and she told him her name was June. He said he knew. She tried to keep bailing afterward, first with the sponge and then with the bailer braced against her knee. Each time she wrung out the sponge, her injured fingers opened too slowly to catch it again. "I've got it," she told me when I reached over, even though the sponge had dropped between our feet. Her hands weren't working, and she kept trying to rest her head on her knees.

Leah wanted June moved beside Ben and Sophie for shared warmth. Victor objected to moving everybody. Miriam said she'd go too. I suggested waiting for the next waves to pass. They passed. I didn't bring it up. There were nine of us now, and Victor kept looking at the number overhead.

He said we were still overloaded. Ben said water was climbing inside his coat. Leah said we could float if we maintained the raft. Victor asked why she hadn't fixed it. She said she was keeping people alive. He said that was what he was doing. I looked at my hands as though that could keep me outside their argument.

Nico offered to work on the repair from outside, tied on. Leah wanted us to improve his access from inside instead. Victor said we couldn't experiment with people's lives. "But you can put them out?" June asked. Miriam told her to save her breath. "No. He said we'd be all right after Owen. We're not."

Victor said he wouldn't discuss it with a child. Miriam said he'd been happy for the child to repair his raft. Ben asked us to stop arguing while Sophie couldn't get warm. When Leah reached to examine her, he turned away before letting her. He only trusted the person treating his daughter when she agreed with him.

A wave entered while the flap was raised. June slipped sideways. Miriam caught her head. Victor shouted for everyone to work; June picked up the bailer with both hands and managed one scoop before dropping it. She tried to retrieve it with her bad hand, apologized when she couldn't, and pushed herself upright against the tube. Ben stared at the bailer between her ankles. "She can't even sit up," he said. Miriam asked what he meant. He said he meant what he'd said.

Victor said nobody was blaming June. Leah said, "Then stop looking at her." I moved the torch away from her face. That made it easier for them to continue.

"You're not doing that," Miriam said. Victor said we had to be realistic. "No. Use the words. What are you saying?" He looked at Ben, then said somebody had to go outside until we were stable. Miriam pointed to where Owen's line had been cut. June looked down at her jacket and began fumbling with it. Leah caught her hands. "Keep it on. You're staying here."

Miriam immediately offered herself. "Give me the rope." She reached toward Nico. He shook his head. "She's smaller. I'm heavier. You want weight out, take mine." Victor said she was keeping us afloat. Ben said he needed her help. She looked at him. "You need my help?" She offered to hold Sophie so he could go outside. He closed his coat and didn't answer.

I said nobody should go anywhere yet. Miriam turned to me with such relief that I began qualifying it: we needed to calm down, avoid a struggle. I heard myself turning a refusal into a request for everyone to behave while the decision was made. Leah said, "Then tell them no, Michael. Help me move her."

There was room beside her if I shifted. I could put June between us. I began to move my knees, and Victor said that if we all decided individually where we wanted to sit we'd overturn. I stopped. That is one of the moments I can replay without any trouble. My feet were already underneath me. All I had to do was finish moving.

Miriam began explaining June's weight, trying to subtract her wet clothes, searching for a number they'd accept. She pointed out the repair June had helped make. "Mom," June said softly. Miriam stopped listing her qualifications, as if she'd suddenly heard herself.

"Have me," Ruth said. Victor told her not to start. She said Arthur was gone and she was willing. He said she didn't know what she was saying. When she leaned toward the opening, he put her back. He could protect someone firmly when he'd decided to.

Ben said Ruth wasn't making trouble. June looked at him. "I'll be quiet. I won't say anything else." She straightened herself, took her weight off Miriam's shoulder, and held both feet still so she wouldn't knock the equipment bag again. She was trying to demonstrate that she could occupy less of our attention. I wanted Leah to say something powerful enough to stop us, or Miriam to become too frightening to approach. I wanted an engine. A girl was bargaining away her right to speak, and I was waiting for somebody else to supply the reason we shouldn't kill her.

Nico raised the flap to try the repair again. "Hold her there," he said. "I'll do it." He meant keep June inside. I held the torch for him. Then the raft dropped, his head hit a support, and the light went out. When I got it working, Ben was holding June's jacket and Miriam had both hands around his wrist.

"Let go," she said. He said she was pulling him onto Sophie. She wasn't touching the baby. Victor took June's other shoulder. Leah caught his forearm, but she was pinned between Ruth and the tube. He shoved her away. Nico struggled to turn back, one foot caught beneath the equipment bag.

I could have taken Ben's wrist. Instead I caught Miriam's upper arms because she had risen onto her knees and I could see the raft leaning. I told her she'd tip us. She tried to slip under my right arm rather than push against it, and I brought that arm down across her shoulders. She twisted toward me and said my name sharply, as though I'd mistaken her for somebody else. I moved my hand higher on her sleeve, where the wet fabric gave me a better grip.

June looked over her shoulder. "Mr. Hayes?" She'd called me Michael all night. I don't know why she changed it. I know what I heard, because I'd heard children use that voice when somebody was hurt in the playground and an adult finally arrived.

I told her to stay still. Miriam drove forward, and I pulled her against my chest. She wasn't trying to hurt me. She was trying to reach June's coat. Her fingers caught a fold and lost it when I tightened my arms. She tried to pull one wrist through the narrow space between my elbow and my ribs; I squeezed my elbow shut without thinking, the same way I'd hold a child who was about to fall. I knew the difference. I kept holding her.

"Look at me," Miriam said. I thought she meant me and began explaining that I was keeping everybody safe. "June. Look at me. I'm coming. Hold the side." June caught the inside rope with her good hand. Everything stopped because they couldn't lift her without hurting her fingers. I was grateful for the delay and did nothing with it.

Miriam offered to sit on the floor and do every shift if they'd leave her daughter alone. She sounded businesslike, trying to arrange something they could accept. Victor told June to release the rope. She shook her head and looked at me. I could still have let her mother go.

Ben worked June's fingers loose one at a time. Sophie was crying inside his coat. He kept saying, "I'm sorry," in the voice he used to soothe her. June asked him to stop. When her hand came free, she tried to catch his sleeve, but Victor already had an arm under her knees.

Miriam threw herself backward to get free. I braced my heels and held her harder. My forearm was across her chest, and I felt her taking shallow breaths because she couldn't expand against it. She clawed at my wrist, trying to lift it enough to get past. June disappeared below the opening. Water came in where her legs had been. Miriam's head struck my chin, cutting my lip. For years I could describe that injury precisely while calling everything before it a confusion.

June's hand came over the tube. Miriam stopped fighting my whole body and concentrated on one arm, working her wrist beneath mine. Victor wanted to close the flap. I told him to wait. I said it several times. I didn't release Miriam.

Nico reached the opening as the raft lifted. He caught her fingertips, or thought he did; afterward he couldn't be sure. The raft came down and his arm was empty. Leah pushed past to look. Victor pulled her back. Miriam called June's name in the voice you use when someone's in the next room and ought to answer.

There was no line attached to June. Some accounts said she'd been secured and accidentally separated. Nobody secured her. The rope Nico had offered Miriam lay beside the pump. We'd stopped even pretending she was coming back.

Miriam struck my face with the arm she'd freed. I released her then. She crawled toward the entrance, and I caught her jacket. She bit my hand. Leah and Nico held her when I let go. They were trying to keep her alive. I'd used those words less than a minute earlier.

We searched with the torch. Ben thought he saw orange, but when we turned there was nothing. Miriam gave directions, telling us to shine farther left. We obeyed with a care we'd refused her while June was inside.

I don't know when June died or what she saw after losing contact. People offer possibilities: confusion, unconsciousness, the cold. I'll accept almost anything that shortens the interval. She was alive when we put her out. That's the part we witnessed.

The searchlight found us less than half an hour later. That comes from the rescue records and what could be established afterward. My watch said 2:43. We'd been afloat for almost seven hours. Miriam was still pointing the torch back along our drift, with Leah's arm around her waist.

The light passed over us and returned. A loudspeaker told us to remain seated. Miriam tried explaining about June to a boat that couldn't yet hear her. A smaller rescue boat approached from the larger vessel, backed off, repositioned, then came close enough to pass a line.

A man in a helmet asked about injured people and children. Ben lifted Sophie. Leah told him a girl was in the water. He relayed it immediately, and another light began searching away from us. Miriam answered his questions about June, including ones nobody had asked: her height, the cut on her hand.

Sophie and Ben went first with a rescuer supporting them. Ruth released Arthur's life jacket long enough to be helped across. Nico told the crew about Owen. Victor gave directions until someone told him firmly to sit still. Leah stayed with Miriam until there were people ready to receive her. Miriam said she needed to see the water.

I was last. A man at the entrance told me when to move. I put the bailer inside the equipment bag, wanting to leave it tidy. He told me to forget it and give him my hand. I said there'd been eleven of us. He said he knew, they were looking, and I had to move now.

I wasn't waiting until everyone else was safe. They were already safe enough to be taken away from me. I heard engines, radios, people doing their jobs. Someone would put a blanket around me and ask whom they should call. I'd answer to my name as though I were still the person my family expected them to have found.

I didn't want to cross. The rescuer took my wrist, and I stepped when he told me. Later a report called me the teacher who stayed until everyone was off. My sister saved it. When I asked her to throw it away, there were other reports she couldn't bear to open.

At the hospital I said we'd lost three people. Lost was the word we initially used. Leah corrected it before any of us spoke together. She said Arthur died aboard and Owen and June were forced into the water. Nico agreed. We didn't successfully arrange a silence. Several of us separately hoped for an account we could live inside.

My first statement put weather between every action. A wave came, Miriam stood, the raft leaned, I held her. The investigator asked what Ben and Victor were doing. I explained June's condition. He asked again. Finally I said they'd lifted her. He asked where they'd put her.

The Caroline's beacon had worked. It floated free, and the first alert came within minutes of the sinking. Crews launched while we were fastening the canopy. They had to establish the numbers aboard and expand their search as we drifted apart. The aircraft we'd heard had been searching. There was no record its crew saw our flare.

Other people were found in the raft we'd seen beyond the stern. Rain, wind, darkness, and our drift made finding us difficult. The beacon wasn't attached to our raft. Knowing where a boat sank wasn't knowing where we'd be hours later. Nobody responsible claimed rescue had been guaranteed.

Owen might have died from his injuries despite treatment. June needed urgent care. The raft was damaged. Victor repeated every danger in his defense. The recovered raft still had one fully inflated buoyancy chamber. An examiner couldn't say how long we'd have stayed afloat with eleven. He could say the rating didn't establish that three people had to leave.

He asked what repairs we'd tried, then why we'd stopped trying to reposition Owen inside. I said the conversation had moved on. He waited, and I heard how it sounded.

I was suspended from teaching. A colleague brought my things: pens, a mug, a photograph of thirty children outside a museum. I stood at the side looking irritated that they wouldn't settle down. One boy had asked to change groups because another kept taking his hat. I'd said we'd sort it out inside. I couldn't remember if I checked. I spent an afternoon searching old emails about a hat, a question I might still answer.

Victor sent a letter saying none of us had been ourselves. He reminded me of Owen's injuries, Sophie's weakness, the way Miriam nearly overturned us. He didn't mention the times the raft leaned because he moved. I read it enough to soften the creases. It would be another lie to say I found nothing useful in it.

Ben's wife came to some proceedings. Once she brought Sophie, walking by then but tired enough to be carried. I recognized how Ben supported her head. She lived because people kept her warm and found her medical care. I could be glad until he said we'd all done what we had to do. Then I couldn't look at them.

Ruth died the following winter. Her family asked us not to attend the funeral. Nico messaged me, then blocked my number when I asked how he was. Leah hasn't spoken to me outside the process requiring it. I used to imagine a final conversation among us, some acknowledgment making it shared. They're entitled to keep me out.

June's father, Paul, asked to see me three years after the sinking. He and Miriam separated when June was small. He'd supplied the photograph the papers used. Not the school picture, he told me, before explaining why he'd come.

We met at my lawyer's office. For a while I thought agreeing might count for something, a reason to be ashamed before he arrived. He put a notebook on the table but didn't open it. He asked whether June knew Miriam had offered. I said she'd been beside her. He asked whether she'd heard Ruth. I thought so.

Then he asked if she could see her mother when she went over. I began explaining the crowding. "Please," he said. "I've heard about the raft." I told him she'd looked at me. He asked why. I said we'd talked, she knew I was a teacher. He rubbed the notebook's edge.

He asked what she'd said. I repeated what I remembered, including my name. He wrote it down and asked how long I'd held Miriam. I couldn't give a number. I showed him where my arms had been. He looked at the space in front of my chest, then closed his notebook.

Before leaving he asked if June had smiled during the night. I told him about the seating plan. He nodded in recognition before his face changed. I'd given him something he wanted. It didn't make him able to remain in the room.

Miriam hasn't asked to see me. I've seen her giving evidence and in corridors. Once she put her hands in her coat pockets, and I remembered June rubbing them between her own because Miriam had been pretending she wasn't cold.

I still catch myself saying we had no information, thought help wasn't coming, feared losing the raft. Those statements leave out people speaking. Owen told us about the beacon. Leah explained immersion. Nico offered to repair the tube. Miriam offered herself. June asked us to stop. We had information from people we'd decided not to hear.

I've admitted my part. There are legal words for it now, and a sentencing date. It's taken years. I don't expect the end of that process to change what happens when I hold someone's arm to help them over a step, or hear a parent calling a child just out of sight. I keep my hands in my pockets more.

I remember June rolling her eyes at me more clearly than I remember leaving the raft. She had shifted her knees to give me room while I tucked the blanket around her, and she'd smiled when I said she had time to decide. Later, holding Miriam, I noticed that her sleeve had ridden up. June had kept pulling it down over her wrist earlier in the night. I pulled it down for her without loosening my grip.
