“No break in the ulna, hairline fracture in the humerus,” I said, attaching the X-ray to the display chart. 

“You need to be a little bit more careful on the playground. Can’t say I blame you; when I was your age, my mom called me eggshell because I was always getting injured.” 

The young boy cracked a smile and giggled. 

The boy spoke up for the first time since the initial questionnaire, “I was playing tag with Bryson, and then I fell.” 

“Well then, you and Bryson should make sure that you are watching where you are running; hopefully you at least weren’t tagged,” I said, cracking a grin. 

“No, I got tagged; that's when I fell. I almost dodged him, though; I am the second fastest at school.” He said, pointing to his Superman shirt.

“Oh no, did he push you?” I asked

“No, he barely got my shoulder. I got really heavy, and I fell when I tried to chase after him.” 

“What do you mean you got really heavy? Like you felt lightheaded?” I rephrased the question; sometimes when you need to speak to kids, you forget what they can understand. 

“When you ran, did stuff feel different in your head? Maybe the playground was spinning?”

“No, like my mommy’s blanket.” 

I turned to the mother sitting in the corner of the room. She looked at me with a raised eyebrow and tilted her head. She looked back to her son, 

“What do you mean, sweetie, which blanket?”

“The blue one you and Daddy have.”

“Oh, I see. He’s talking about the weighted blanket we have.”

“So you say that when you ran, it felt like you had a heavy blanket? When did the feeling start and stop?” I asked, now crouching down in front of the young boy.



“It’s still here. It’s see-through, but I am wearing it right now!” 

The mother leaned forward in her chair. “Honey, what do you mean you are wearing it?” 

“Look, I’ll show you, Mommy.” He stood up on his chair. Before I could stop him, he hopped off, luckily landing on his feet. His mother looked back at me; I knew exactly what she was thinking. The sound this kid made when his feet touched the ground was not what you’d expect from  55 pounds falling 2 feet. This was a thick thud, a thud that you’d hear at a bar when a heavyset man had drunk too much. 

“Come here, take your shoes off.” I doubted the shoes would be the defining factor, but I had to make sure. Just normal foam sneakers, with the lights included. No metal, no weights. 

A nurse threw open the door. “EVERYTHING OKAY?” 

“Yes, sorry. Our little superhero was showing us how high he could jump.” The mother said.

I stood up and walked to the other side of the room. 

“Come here, buddy, let's take your weight.”

The red digital screen started up, 20lbs, 30lbs, 40lbs, 50lbs, 60lbs, 70lbs, 80lbs, 90lbs, it kept going. The nurse was still peeking in, and I could hear her mumble under her breath. 143lbs. The final reading was 143lbs. Broken scale; had to be. The only possible way. Once he stepped off, I waited for the scale to re-zero. 185lbs, my exact weight. The scale didn’t appear faulty.  
“Try it again,” I said. Same reading, 143lbs. 

No, there is an explanation here: density. There have been cases of bone density reaching nearly twice the average. Doesn’t explain why he claimed to have suddenly started feeling this way, but that's the only current explanation. 

“Order a DEXA scan; see if you can push this one ahead,” I said to the nurse. The scan would allow us to confirm overall bone mass as well as muscle tissue. I already had doubts that this would be conclusive, but at least we can start to rule out possibilities. 

“Okay, buddy, we are going to give you a super scan. You are going to go with Mommy here, and the nice nurse will take you two to the second floor.” The boy and his mom stood up, and she took her son's hand. She looked at me, followed by an affirming nod, and told her son, 

“Say goodbye to the nice doctor, sweetie.” 

“Bye-bye,” He said, waving his small little hand. 

I opened the door and politely waited as Nurse Alvarado began to escort them away. As the young boy walked by me, he touched my forearm,   
“TAG,” He said, giggling. I smiled, and in a playful tone I responded, 

“Grrrrr, you got me! We will have to continue this game another time.”   
  
The group walked towards the elevator as I stayed behind. I had other patients to attend to, and the scan would take roughly an hour, assuming that we had a few more urgent cases ahead of the boy. Another one of my nurses, Nurse Matthews, stepped in front of me, 

“Young girl seemed to have an allergic reaction to pistachios. Minor hives, no swelling, runny nose.” 

“Okay, can you get an allergy panel ready? I’ll be there in a moment.” I said. He nodded and walked away. 

I walked towards my office to quickly grab a glass of water; I stopped. I stood for a moment and took another step forward. Have I always been able to feel this? The air gently touching my eye? Not the feeling of an eyelash that was stuck, the feeling of a gentle pressure, almost unnoticeable. I only felt it when I began to walk, like moving through a canopy of the world's finest silk. I am stupid; it’s the same effect as when someone tells you to think about your breathing. I’m clearly paranoid and overanalyzing every possible sensation. 

“MATTHEWS, run the panel without me. I’m going to take my lunch.” He nodded from across the hall and walked into my patient's room. 

Walking into my office, I opened the mini fridge under the desk and took out my morning leftovers. I pulled out a plastic fork and began to dig into the cold and soggy hash browns. My blood sugar must be low; I feel a lot weaker than normal. The fork is heavier than I remember, and my elbow- yes, my elbow is harder to lift. I ate the same breakfast I had for the past year, no variation whatsoever. Why would my blood sugar change so drastically? 

My office chair began to creak, the noises it would make when first sitting down. The piston began lowering ever so slightly, only noticeable as my knees could now clear the desk. He was correct: a weighted blanket. What started as a silk had gone from a bathroom towel to a thick comforter. Every inch of my body had this slight pressure, but it was growing. Any tilt in my head became a fight to properly straighten it back out. My breathing, the added weight on my chest, every exhale more labored than the last. A heart attack? No, my heart was beating loud and fast, but steadily. My hand, resting on the mouse pad, began to sink into it. A visible deformation appeared along the edges of my fingers. What the fuck is happening to me? What is this feeling? 

“MAUUUHTTTEWWWWWS!” I screamed, unable to properly form my words with weight inside of my throat. For the first time in my life, I could feel my vocal cords; I could feel them struggle to displace the air surrounding them. Matthews threw the door open, his eyes wide with shock. 

“OH MY GOD, ARE YOU OKAY?” He ran to me. I now lay on the floor, arms spread wide; a broken chair lies beside me.

It has been a week since I first felt the pressure. After Matthews rushed in, he was able, alongside 5 other nurses, to successfully put me onto a gurney. I was immediately brought to the ER 3 floors down, where they proceeded to automate nearly every vital bodily function I had. The rate at which the pressure had increased plateaued, with my body weight being a record 946 pounds. Well, at least according to an industrial scale which they had to bring from the loading dock. I can’t move my limbs anymore; I still have some movement in my hands and fingers. That’s how I can write this. Every keystroke feels like moving a solid lead pipe through molasses. The only good news I’ve heard is that the young boy’s exam came back completely normal. Had to be a faulty scale, they said. I don’t believe that, nor does his mother. Aside from the hairline fracture, he’s as healthy as any other boy his age. I write this story today, trying to find people who are experiencing what I am. We’ve had numerous doctors, specialists, scientists- nothing. They have no explanation, no reason, no cure. And so I ask, does anyone else notice that the air has gotten heavier?
