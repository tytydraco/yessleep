My grandpa passed away recently and as such I had been drafted by my parents to help remove his personal effects and items from his home. I was tasked with starting in the attic and working my way down.

When I got up there it was as you'd expect an 86 year old man's attic to be, filled with clutter and junk from decades gone by. I started with hauling the larger items like bulky boxes of clothing, some jewellery containers, an old-timey sewing machine and such. 

Whilst moving a particularly heavy shelving unit I knocked some clutter off the top of it and with a thump, an ornate wooden lockbox rolled to a stop at my foot. Normally I'd have kicked it to the side and continued hauling but something about this box caught my eye. Something drew me towards it in a way I haven't felt before.

I picked up the box and as you'd expect, it was locked. One thing I noticed right away though were deep scratch marks all around the keyhole which typically I would chalk up to years of a key being shoddily pressed in at the wrong angle, but they grooves were *deep*, too deep for a key scratch.

This is where things start to get weird. I took the box to my parents and asked if they knew what it was and upon locking eyes with it, my fathers face turned white as a ghost. He held his composure as he lied to my face about having no idea what it was, so I chucked the box into my hoodie front pouch so I could study it later.

I'm a lock pick enthusiast so it wasn't very hard to gain entry. Sat at my desk I opened up the box and was greeted with a red velvet inlay with two items within. A leather-bound journal and a brass pocket watch. Both looked stained and dishevelled.

I picked up the journal and flipped to the first page.



*Oct. 9th, 1878. Cheyenne River.*

*"Came upon a dead man in a dry wash, not a mark on him, and the coyotes wouldn’t go near. Found a watch in his vest, stopped at five exactly. I ain’t gonna set down what I seen when I took hold of it. Next I knowed I was layin’ in the dirt and the damn thing was tickin’, and I never wound it. Nobody had to tell me the terms. I knowed ’em like I know my own name: five in a year, or I go under and five more with me, and not a one of ’em will ever know why.*

*Walked off a good half mile and found it in my fist. Traded a skinner at the fort for this book, so I aim to keep a tally here and not lose count. I’ve took to eyeing the men in camp different. That watch runs quieter when I do.*

*E. Varney"*



Then the second page had 3 names on it with crosses through them along with this passage.



"*Feb. 2nd, 1879. Fort Pierre.*

*Three of ’em now. I’ll not set down how. Walt, Ezra, the widow. I wash my hands till the skin cracks and I can still feel the warm of it. I was a soldier once and thought I knowed what it was to be sick over a killing, but this is a different sickness. This one don’t pass.*

*I said it out loud last night so’s the dark could hear me: no more. Then I took the watch and went down to the river with a notion of settling the matter my own way, and ending it where it started. I’ll not write what I done there. I will only say I woke on the bank at first light with the cold in my bones, breathing, and the watch ticking in my fist like it had been waiting on me. It ain’t done with me, and I reckon I know now what it meant by five more. I can’t even leave it the cheap way without the debt going to somebody else.*

*It’s took to ticking louder since. Yesterday I looked at the cook a long while, and I liked what I was thinking. That’s the part I can’t abide. Not the doing, but the liking. I put the watch in the river Tuesday and this mornin’ it was in my coat, dry, and the hands set to five 'o' clock.*

*E. Varney"*



A final entry.



"*April 12th, 1879. Fort Pierre, in the jail.*

*The judge read it out as four, and he had it right. I never argued. I hang Friday at sunup. I do not fear the rope.*

*What I fear most is the count. The watch asked for five and I fell one short, and I have never heard of a debt that dies with the man who owes it. I do not think it will let me go. I think I will be left somewhere between, neither under nor risen, still hearing it tick. I tried to take the watch with me once and it would not be taken. Now I wonder if it was only waiting for a better door.*

*Amos, I am leaving you this book. I have no land to give you and no name worth carrying, only this. Do not touch it and do not let it be touched.*

*E. Varney"*



The pages after contained various old news clippings, one of which detailed the day Varney died.



**"THE PIERRE WEEKLY SIGNAL**  
*Saturday, April 19th, 1879*

**“THE FIFTH-HOUR FIEND” MEETS HIS END AT FORT PIERRE**

*Elias Varney, styled by the territorial press “The Fifth-Hour Fiend” for the hour at which the bodies were invariably found, was hanged at sunup yesterday before some two hundred, for the murders of four persons in this vicinity. He mounted the gallows unassisted and declined to speak. The physician pronounced him dead at seven minutes past six. The prisoner’s pocket watch, held by the sheriff, was heard by several to cease ticking the very instant the drop came, and has not been heard since. His effects are to be forwarded to a son, one Amos Varney, near Deadwood, the county declining to keep them."*



It's safe to say this set my mind racing. I stared at the watch resting on my desk. There are so many questions unanswered such as how did a western pocket-watch make it's way to my grandfathers attic in London? Did the Sheriff become cursed after contact? What does my father know? Is it reversible in *any* way?

Unfortunately though, I haven't been entirely honest in my retelling. You see, in the excitement of picking the lock, the journal was **not** the first thing I picked up...

It's currently set to 5 'o' clock and won't stop ticking...
