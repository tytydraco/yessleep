The Cascade Motor Inn sits on the old highway spur outside a logging town I'm not going to name, because there are maybe four hundred people left in it and I don't want any of them finding this and knowing exactly which one of us is telling it. I've worked the front desk there for six years, ever since the mill cut its second shift and half the town went looking for work that didn't require you to still have a mill. It's a slow job. Nine rooms, most of them empty most nights, the kind of place that survives on hunters in the fall and nothing much the rest of the year.

I want to tell you about Room 6.

Room 6 doesn't get booked. Hasn't in the six years I've worked there, and according to Deloris, who worked the desk for twenty years before me and still comes in to gossip and drink the bad coffee, it's never been booked in her memory either. It's not blocked off officially. It's on the board same as any other room, key hanging on the same pegboard. People just don't ask for it. When I asked Deloris why, once, early on, she got a look on her face like I'd asked her something rude and changed the subject, and I let it go the way you let things go in a town this small, where everybody's got one thing they don't discuss.

I found out why on a Tuesday night in October, nine years to the day, I checked the register later (I'll get to that) since the last time it happened.

A man came in a little after eleven. Older, maybe sixty, dressed like he'd stepped out of a photograph, not costume-old, just *wrong*\-old; the cut of his coat a couple decades off from anything you'd see walking around today. He didn't say much. Asked for Room 6 by number, which nobody does, we always just ask for "a room." Paid cash. Signed the register in handwriting so neat it looked typed.

I gave him the key without thinking twice about it, because it was eleven at night and I was tired and the coffee maker was broken and none of the wrongness about him had caught up to me yet. It caught up to me about ten minutes later, when I glanced at the register to file the carbon copy and saw the date he'd written next to his signature.

It wasn't tonight's date. It was nine years ago, to the day.

I told myself it was a mistake. People write the wrong date on things all the time, especially older folks, especially at night. I decided I'd mention it to him gently if I saw him again, and I went back to the desk and didn't think much more about it until the temperature in the lobby dropped so fast the window fogged from the inside in about four seconds flat, and a smell came through the vents that I can only describe as a nosebleed, thick and coppery, the kind of smell that makes your own mouth taste like a bitten cheek.

I looked at the key rack out of some instinct I can't explain. Room 6's key was back on its peg.

He'd had it maybe eleven minutes.

I did not go check on him. I want to be honest about that, because I think about it more than almost anything else that happened that night, I did not go check on him because some very old, very quiet part of my brain told me that if I walked down that breezeway and knocked on Room 6's door, whoever answered would not be him, or would be him in a way I did not have any interest in seeing.

Instead I called Deloris, at close to midnight, and she picked up on the second ring like she'd been waiting for the phone to go off for six years, which, as it turned out, she had.

She told me to leave the key on the peg and not go near that end of the building until sunup. She told me the register would show his name in the morning and not before, and that if I went and looked at the room right then, through the window or otherwise, I would see something I would spend the rest of my life trying to unsee. She told me this in the same flat, tired tone you'd use to tell someone the ice machine's broken again, and something about that flatness scared me more than anything she actually said.

I stayed at the desk. I did not go near that end of the building. Around 3:40 in the morning, and I only know the time because I was watching the clock do almost nothing else for four hours straight, I heard a door close, very softly, down the breezeway. Not slammed. Not loud at all. Just the particular, final click of a latch catching.

I did not go look.

In the morning the key to Room 6 was back on the peg, though I never saw anyone return it. The bed inside, when I finally worked up the nerve to check with Deloris standing next to me, hands in her cardigan pockets, was made up perfect, hospital corners, like housekeeping had just left it, except we don't do hospital corners, our housekeeper never has, not once in six years, and there was a smell in that room, faint but there, of cold pennies, that took two days and every window open to air out.

The register, when I checked it that morning, showed his signature under last night's actual date, in the same too-neat handwriting, like the wrongness had corrected itself somewhere between midnight and dawn. Deloris pulled the old registers out of the back office, she'd kept every one going back thirty years, out of some instinct I understand a lot better now, and showed me the same name, same handwriting, entered every nine years almost to the day, going back as far as the records existed to go.

She wouldn't tell me what happens if you go and knock. She said she didn't know, not really, only that two people had, in her time at the desk, and neither one of them talked about it after, and one of them didn't stay in town much longer.

I asked her what the man asked for, all those years he'd checked in, whether he ever said anything about where he was headed or where he'd come from.

She said the same thing every time, apparently, right after he signed. Not to her, exactly. More like he said it to the room.

*You found it. Now you belong to the stay.*

I still work the desk. Room 6 is on the board same as the others, key hanging on its peg. It still doesn't get booked, and I've never once had to explain to a guest why, because nobody ever asks for it, nobody except the one man, once every nine years, right around eleven at night, in a coat that's a couple decades off from anything anyone else is wearing.

That was three years ago now. I've got six years left, if the pattern holds, before I have to sit at that desk again and decide whether to hand over the key.

I already know I will. I think that's the part that scares me most, not the room, not the cold, not the smell. It's that some part of me, the part that took his cash and slid the key across the counter without a second thought, already knows exactly what it's going to do next time, and it isn't going to ask the rest of me for permission.
