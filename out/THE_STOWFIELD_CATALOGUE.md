I'm writing this at the counter. Pat's in the back, pricing.

Pat owns the shop. Secondhand books, the Lanes, Norwich, since 1988. Two rooms, a radiator that only works on Thursdays, and a cat that isn't ours but comes in to sleep on Military History. Pat prices everything in pencil, top right corner of the flyleaf. Everything ends in a seven. £3.70. £7. £17. Pat says sevens sell. Pat's sevens have a little tail at the bottom, like they're about to walk off.

I've worked here on and off since I was seventeen. I can tell you the price of most things in the shop without looking. That's the job, mostly. That and carrying boxes.

On Tuesday Pat came in with a packet of custard creams and said, "I've bought a basement."

"Whose?"

"Stowfield library. Shut since the nineties. Council's finally selling the building. Wants the basement cleared by Friday. Everything in it, one price." Pat ate a biscuit over the Penguin Classics. "You'll take the van tomorrow."

"Where's Stowfield?"

"Past Wymondham. Sort of. Left at the sugar beet."

"That's not a direction."

"You'll find it. Don't read anything. Just box it."

That's Pat. Pat doesn't believe in reading stock. Reading stock is how you end up keeping it.

\*

The van is a white Berlingo with a sliding door that only slides one way. It has opinions about hills. Norfolk has no hills, so we get on.

I left at nine. The satnav found Wymondham and then lost interest. After that it showed me sitting in a blank grey field with an arrow pointing nowhere. The real fields weren't much different. Flat, brown, ploughed in long lines that went to the edge of the fog and kept going. A crow on a gatepost. Another crow on the next one. It might have been the same crow.

I found the sugar beet. A heap of it, taller than the van, on a concrete pad by the road. I turned left. The road narrowed to one lane and grass up the middle. I drove for twenty minutes and didn't pass a car.

The sign said STOWFIELD. It leaned. Someone had shot at it once, a long time ago, and the holes had rusted.

The town was one street, a church with a square tower, a shut pub called the Plough, and a post office with a poster for a 2019 jumble sale in the window. The library was at the end. Red brick, tall windows, PUBLIC LIBRARY 1891 carved over the door. A council notice was taped to the glass from the inside. The tape had gone yellow.

A man was waiting on the step. Seventies, flat cap, keys on a bootlace round his neck.

"You'll be from Norwich."

"That's me."

"Mr Dunn. Caretaker. Was." He unlocked the door. "There we are."

He said that after everything. I learned that quickly. He showed me the toilets. "There we are." He showed me where the light switch was for the stairs. "There we are." He stopped at the top of the basement steps and didn't go any further.

"You not coming down?"

"I don't go down."

"Bad knees?"

He looked at me for a while. "There we are," he said, and went to sit in his car.

\*

The basement was long and low and cold enough that I kept my coat on. One bulb worked out of four. It smelt of damp paper and something sweeter underneath, like apples left in a drawer.

Most of it was what you'd expect. Reader's Digest condensed books, swollen with damp. Forty years of the Eastern Daily Press tied in bundles. A globe with the USSR on it. A box of children's books with the date stamps still in, last borrowed in 1986. Pat would want those. Pat can sell anything with a date stamp to a certain kind of person from Hampstead.

I boxed for two hours. My hands went grey with dust. I ate a sausage roll from the garage at Wymondham sitting on a box of Catherine Cooksons.

The card catalogue was at the far end, against the wall, under the bulb that worked.

It was oak. Sixty small drawers, five across, twelve down, each with a brass label holder and a little brass pull. The labels were typed. A to Z, then the alphabet again, then again in a different typeface, then handwritten, in pencil, on card gone brown.

Pat had said leave the furniture. But I wanted to see what they'd catalogued. You can tell a lot about a library from its catalogue. Mostly how much they had on beekeeping.

I pulled out drawer B. It was full. Each card had a hole at the bottom with a brass rod through it, to stop them falling out.

The first card said:

BELL. St Aldric's, tower. Rung at night, approx. 40 strokes. Tower locked, key with vicar, vicar in bed. 12.ii.1951.

Typed. The e was slightly raised, the way old typewriters do it.

The next one:

BELL. As above. 13.ii.1951.

And the next:

BELL. As above. Vicar present in tower. Rope did not move. 14.ii.1951.

I flicked through. There were dozens. Then BIRDS. Rooks, Church Lane, all facing west. None feeding. 9.x.1934. Then BOY. Seen on Mill Lane, wet through. No rain. Not known. 2.vi.1962. Then BREAD. I didn't read BREAD.

I opened D. DOG, black. There were a lot of those. I opened L. LIGHTS. Over Stowfield Fen, moving against the wind. I opened W. WOMAN, in the reading room, after closing. Did not answer. That one was dated 1979 and the handwriting was neat, blue-black ink, slanted.

I stood there with the drawer in my hands and laughed. It came out louder than I meant it to.

It was a joke. Obviously. Somebody's project. Some bored librarian on a Wednesday afternoon in 1955 deciding Stowfield needed a folklore index, and then someone else carrying it on, and someone else, the way people do with crosswords left in staff rooms. Every small town has someone like that. Our local history society in Norwich once spent a year cataloguing every pub sign with a horse on it.

Then I got to the back of the drawer.

LIGHTS. Over Stowfield Fen. Seen from the Plough car park by three persons. 3.iii.2029.

I read it twice. I checked the front of the drawer to see if I'd got the dates the wrong way round. I hadn't. They went up. 1934, 1951, 1979, 2003, 2011. Then 2026. Then 2029. Then a card dated 2041 that I didn't read because the bulb flickered and I put the drawer back quite quickly.

I sat down on the Catherine Cooksons.

It's a joke, I thought. A good one. Somebody had a sense of humour and a lot of spare card. You'd put a few future ones in the back for whoever found them. You'd want them to sit down on a box and feel stupid. Fine. I felt stupid.

I got up and opened K, because I'd already decided it was a joke and I wanted to see how far they'd gone with it.

KNOCKING. Residence, no rear entrance. At rear. Three, three and three. Not answered. 26.xi.2026.

That was the next day. I remember thinking that was a nice touch.

I slid the brass rod out. It was stiff and it squeaked. I took the knocking card and two others. One from B, the bell, 1951, because Pat likes a bit of local history at the till. And one from R that I picked without much reading. I pushed the rod back in. I put the three cards in the inside pocket of my coat.

I don't know why. I think I wanted something to show Pat. I think I wanted something to show anyone.

Mr Dunn helped me load the van. He stood at the top of the basement stairs and I carried the boxes up to him, and he carried them out. It took an hour. He didn't say much.

"Why'd it shut?"

"Nobody came." He put a box of Reader's Digests on the van floor. "There we are."

I asked about the catalogue as I was getting in. Who'd done it.

He was quiet for a bit.

"Librarians."

"All of them?"

"Every one." He tapped the van roof twice, the way you do to send someone off. "Mind the fog past the beet."

\*

I got back to Norwich at half five. Pat had gone home. I left the boxes in the van and went up to the flat.

The flat is above the shop. It's got a front door at the top of the stairs and, at the back, a doorway that's been bricked up. It used to go out to a fire escape. The fire escape was taken down in the nineties. The landlord calls the bricked-up doorway a feature. It's got a coat hook on it.

I had beans on toast and watched a programme about canal boats. I went to bed at eleven.

At ten past two I woke up. I didn't know why. The flat was quiet. A taxi went past down in the Lanes and then nothing.

Then three knocks. Then a pause. Then three more. Then a pause. Then three.

They came from the back of the flat.

I lay there. I said to myself, pipes. The building's old. The heating clicks. Next door's student has a boyfriend who works nights.

Three. Three. Three.

It was knuckles. It wasn't pipes. Pipes don't wait.

I got up and put the big light on. I stood in the hallway and looked at the bricked-up doorway with the coat hook on it. My dressing gown was hanging there. It was moving very slightly, the way things do when someone's just walked past.

The knocking stopped.

I went and found my coat in the kitchen and took the cards out of the pocket. My hands weren't working very well. I dropped one. I read the knocking card standing up under the light.

Residence, no rear entrance. At rear. Three, three and three. Not answered. 26.xi.2026.

It was the twenty-sixth. It had been since midnight.

I didn't sleep. I sat on the sofa with the telly on mute and the canal boats going past until it got light. At seven I put the kettle on and made tea and didn't drink it.

Then I read the third card. The one I'd taken from R without reading.

RIVER. Smell of, in upstairs room. Window shut. Mud on sill, inside. 27.xi.2026.

\*

I went down and opened the shop. I didn't know what else to do. I priced a box of Agatha Christies. I sold a Collins bird book to a man who wanted to argue about the price. I was glad of him. He stayed twenty minutes.

Pat came in at eleven with a sausage roll and asked how Stowfield was.

"Foggy."

"Get it all?"

"All of it. Except the furniture."

Pat nodded. "Good."

I nearly said something. I had the cards in my pocket. I had my hand on them. But Pat had already gone into the back to look at the boxes, and I heard the first one being opened, and then a low whistle, and then, "Date stamps. Lovely."

I slept in the shop that night. I told myself it was so I could start early. I took a sleeping bag down and laid it out between Travel and Military History. The cat that isn't ours came and lay on my feet.

I didn't go upstairs once. I want to make that clear.

At about four I woke up and the shop smelt of the river. Not the clean smell of the Wensum in spring. The August smell. Low water. Green and brown and cold, the bit of the bank where things get caught.

The cat had gone.

The smell was coming down the stairs.

I sat with my back against Military History and listened. There wasn't any sound. That was the worst part. I'd have preferred a sound.

When it got light I went up. The flat was cold. The bedroom window was shut and locked. On the sill, inside, was a line of mud. Wet. Grey-brown. Not much. About the width of a forearm. As if something had leaned there for a while, looking out over the Lanes.

I didn't touch it. I shut the bedroom door and went back downstairs and sat at the counter until Pat came in.

\*

Mr Dunn's phone number was on a paper tag on the van keys. Pat must have written it there. I rang it from the shop phone while Pat was out buying milk.

It rang a long time.

"Yes."

"Mr Dunn. It's the man from Norwich. The basement."

Nothing. Then, "You took some."

I hadn't said anything about cards. I hadn't said anything at all.

"Three."

"Read them?"

"Yes."

"And?"

I told him. Not all of it. The knocking. The mud. He listened. I could hear a clock on his end, a loud one, the kind that's in every nan's front room.

"They don't stay put," he said, "unless they're filed. That's what she said. The last one."

"The last what?"

"Librarian."

"Where is she?"

"Went down in eighty-seven. October. To do the cataloguing. Told me she'd be an hour." The clock ticked. "Didn't come up."

"What do you mean, didn't come up?"

"I mean what I said. Lights on. Coat on the chair. Police came. Went down. Came up. Said there was nobody there. There we are."

I waited for him to say more. He didn't.

"Who's been writing them, then? Since eighty-seven?"

The clock ticked a long time.

"I don't go down," he said, and put the phone down.

\*

Pat came back with the milk. I was still standing by the phone.

"You look terrible."

"I took some cards," I said. "From the catalogue. At Stowfield."

Pat put the milk in the little fridge under the counter. Pat didn't look at me. Pat took the van keys off the hook and put them on the counter, next to my hand.

"Best take them back, then," Pat said.

That was all. Pat went into the back and I heard the pencil start up again on a flyleaf.

\*

It took me longer to find it the second time.

Wymondham, then the blank grey field on the satnav. The fog was thicker. I drove for an hour looking for the sugar beet. When I found it, it was smaller. Half the heap had gone. There were tyre marks in the mud around the pad. I turned left.

The single lane. Grass up the middle. No cars. The sign, leaning. The rusted holes.

I don't know what time I got there. My phone said four, and then it said nothing at all.

The street was empty. The post office had its blind down. No lights in the Plough. Mr Dunn's car wasn't outside the library.

The front door was unlocked.

I went in. It was colder than before. The rooms upstairs had been cleared by someone. The shelves were empty and the light from the tall windows lay on the floorboards in long grey squares. I could hear the van engine ticking as it cooled outside.

I put the stair light on. It worked. I went down.

The basement was empty. Bare floor, the marks where boxes had stood. The globe was gone. Even the Catherine Cooksons were gone, and I'd put those in the van myself, so I don't know where that box went. I haven't checked. I'm not going to check.

The catalogue was still there, under the one bulb that worked.

I took the cards out of my pocket. I'd kept them in an envelope. My hands were shaking, which made the envelope rattle, which made me feel stupid.

B first. I slid out the drawer and pulled the rod. The cards were tight. I found 12.ii.1951 and 14.ii.1951, and between them was a gap. Exactly one card wide. As if the drawer had been holding the space.

I put the bell back. I pushed the rod through.

R. RIVER. The gap was there too. In it.

K last. KNOCKING. There was the gap, and I slid the card in, and the rod went through all the holes in one go, smooth as anything, and I stood there with my hand on the drawer and breathed for what felt like the first time in two days.

That was when I saw the new card.

It wasn't on the rod. It was standing up at the front of the drawer, loose, leaning against the brass plate. The card was white. Not brown like the others. New. The pencil was soft and dark and hadn't smudged yet.

I didn't read the heading. I want to be honest about that. I could have. I didn't. I put my thumb over it.

I read the date. 7.xii.2026.

I looked at the seven for a long time.

It had a little tail at the bottom. Like it was about to walk off.
