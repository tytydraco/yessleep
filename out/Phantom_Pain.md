I only met my extended family once I left my parents. 

My parents were isolated from the rest of my family due to their religious differences: my mother converted to christianity once she met my father, whilst the rest of her side of the family follow an idiosyncratic mix of beliefs belonging to distinct ancient pagan religions. This meant my parents were never interested in presenting me to my grandparents.

I can remember the first time I heard news of my grandparents as if it was yesterday: someone was calling our home, and since my parents were going out, I was the only one who could pick it up.

On the other side of the line, I heard the voice of an old woman asking to speak with my mother, and once I said I was her child, she started sobbing, and whatever she was there to talk about was forgotten, as she presented herself as my grandmother. She said her and my grandfather were having problems with the retirement home my parents sent them to, and asked when they could pick up the phone in order to talk with them.

Before I could say anything else, my parents came back home, and once they discovered I was talking to my grandparents, my mother took the phone out of my hands, and started screaming to my grandmother, saying she did not want her in her life again.

I was surprised with my parents' reaction, but even more surprised knowing they were in contact with my maternal grandparents: they never talked about them, to the point I stopped questioning who they were, but that day brought those questions back. I asked for more information, and since they refused to tell me anything, we had a huge discussion that day, in which I exposed to them how I resented them for isolating me from my family. This fight was the last straw for us, and my parents even threatened to throw me away from home.

I decided that day that I did not want to live with my over controlling parents anymore, and since I was a legal adult, I decided to leave home and visit my grandparents at their retirement home, and maybe learn something new about myself, or at least learn what made my parents resent them so much.

I searched for more information on my grandparents around the house, and discovered the address of their nursing home: it was in a big city in the neighboring state, pretty different from the town I lived in for my entire life. I did not thought this was a bad change of scenario, thought, and I did not plan to live in my town anyway: my hometown was a particularly dead and unattractive place, with an old population serving to decorate a scenario full of decrepit building and an unusual amount of abandoned factories that once made the place a thriving, yet ugly, industrial center.

I took my things, and left home when my parents were out for work. I left a note explaining my reasoning, but I did not say I was going to visit my grandparents, as they could use it to find me.

After some days of travel, I finally reached the big city in the neighboring state, and started making my way to the retirement home. This city was a true breath of fresh air: the architecture was old without being dilapidated, with plenty of buildings with a sophisticated Victorian aesthetic, mixed with new and gigantic skyscrapers serving as testament for that city’s importance as an international metropolis. A very different world from my isolated and conservative hometown.

Once I got to the retirement home, I presented myself to the workers, and one clerk guided me towards my grandparents.

The clerk was a pretty friendly woman, and we engaged in a short conversation as we moved towards my grandparents. Unfortunately, I could not get immersed into our dialogue fully, as I realized how terribly managed that nursing home was: I noticed how the other clerks were screaming at the elders they were supposed to take care of, how dirty everything was, and how plenty of the elders there had marks in their body alluding to beating and other types of aggression. The clerk noticed my discomfort, and clearly embarrassed on the behalf of her coworkers, said she was sorry.

We finally got to my grandparents room, a bedroom with strange egyptian idols in the shelfs, and became surprised with how receptive they were: I always imagined them to be difficult people to deal with, since that would explain why my mother left them, but they were actually pretty heartwarming in person.

My grandfather was a skinny, but tall man with a missing right arm and in a wheelchair. He was silent, and often communicating using groans rather than words, but he had a shy smile that showed a far more inviting inner life. My grandmother, on the other hand, was a pretty talkative old lady, often stumbling upon her words as if the topics in her head were fighting for attention, but she was as welcoming as my grandfather.

We started talking, and it was as if they knew me for my entire life, and this was only one more visit after years of not seeing each other. I sincerely never felt as seen by anyone like I felt that day. The clerk excused herself, but my grandfather called her to the side, and whispered something to her ear that made her pale. She looked at my grandparents, and they both said she should take a day off from work, as she seemed tired.

The clerk seemed nervous, and after murmuring something to my parents and saying goodbye to me, she left the building.

I was intrigued by that, but I did not want to embarrass my grandparents with questions during our first day together, so I decided to ignore it for now.

 

As the hours went by and the evening started its slow journey towards the dark night, I started talking with my grandparents about my work, saying that I was glad to have left my job at my hometown, as my boss was a terribly abusive man. My grandmother scoffed at me playfully, and mocked my complaint against my boss. After that, she started telling a story about her and my grandfather when they were younger, a story that still lingers in my mind to this day.

I got interested in what she was about to say, so I left her to ramble the story for how long she wanted.

In the story, my grandmother revealed to me that she was born and raised in the same hometown as I was, and lived there when it stopped being a rural area and became an industrial powerhouse, way before the industry left the settlement as jobs were shipped overseas.

She told me that  my grandfather was one of the workers that maintained it going, one of the thousands of young men that were expected to work for 16 hours a day, 6 days a week.

My grandparents were recently married, and they expected that the factories would increase their economic gains, but they were mistaken: not only my grandfather was receiving little for his cooperation, the factories killed the soil quality, preventing the villagers from engaging with the agriculture that sustained them before industry took over.

The factory owners would pay little for the work, and after they bought most of the town’s services, even the worker’s salary was going back to them. 

My grandfather was working overtime when a terrible accident came upon him: his right arm was crushed by rotating gears, mutilating the limb. He was taken to a healer, and they decided to amputate the arm. Being now disabled, my grandfather was unable to work in the factory again, and the factory owner, fearing the repercussions of his factory being closed for work violations, decided to use his resources in order to create a smear campaign against my grandfather, using my grandparents religious beliefs to put the other workers against them, accusing my grandmother of being the cause as to why so many accidents were happening with other workers as well.

My grandmother took care of my grandfather by using her newfound fame as a witch to open her own fortune telling cabinet. Still, she said people were not as open to mysticism as they are today, and the same clients who would visit her at night would join the angry mobs that would protest outside her house for the deaths and accidents of her loved ones at the factory. 

One day, as the deaths and accidents in the factories became too much to handle for the villagers, they joined themselves into a mob, and attacked my grandparent’s house with fire. My grandmother showed to me the scars left by this incident, saying that she tried her best to defend my grandfather with her own body since he still was debilitated due to a fever that he acquired after the accident in the factory, but said it was ultimately useless, as my grandfather inhaled too much smoke, and his heart stopped beating.

She ran away with him to the forest, praying to many gods that the villagers would not follow them, and when she was certain no one else was around, she started doing everything she could in order to resuscitate my grandfather.

She said he was dead for hours before she could bring him back, and this made my skeptical side doubt the narrative she was telling me. I asked what she did to resuscitate him, and she blushed, getting angry and secretive in a way she never was during our other conversations. When I pressed her more for answers, she limited herself to saying “She applied the wisdom of Isis”. She refused to elaborate further, and stopped telling the story, as if she suddenly realized I was not supposed to know what happened.

My grandmother started pondering alone, and stopped talking about her experiences. I did not press her further, afraid to distance her from me, and we started talking about different things entirely. My grandfather remained silent, and strangely gloomy, as if my grandmother’s tale about their early years and bac experiences in my hometown had affected him deeply.

As the day was coming to an end, my grandfather asked me to take him outside for a while: he was in a wheelchair, and wanted to see the sun set that day. My grandmother, on the other hand, wanted to stay in the bedroom, so it was only him and I.

As we left the room and I could see my grandfather with stronger lights, I noticed his body was full of marks similar to the other elders.

Once we were alone on a balcony, he started talking with me, explaining the rest of the story my grandmother did not tell me.

My grandfather confirmed that he was brought back, but he also refused to tell me the details of the method my grandmother used to resurrect him.

Police were called due to the house fire, and my grandparents were found in the woods. My grandmother knew she had nowhere to go except the village, and due to that fact, she swallowed her pride and publicly converted to catholicism, which seems to have eased the hostility the villagers had towards them. The church helped her rebuild her house, and plenty of villagers would go on to ask for forgiveness for taking part in the fire. She pretended to forgive them, but in secret, she maintained their names and faces so she could, when the opportunity came, get revenge against them.

One day, people around the town got the news that my grandfather’s former boss, the factory owner who ruined their name, was found dead in his home. His body was found contorted, denoting a violent death. His eyes were wide open, and the mark of someone’s hand could be found around his neck, supposedly the hand that suffocated him to death.

His death was only the first of thousands of other similar cases that took the village by surprise: the mysterious strangler claimed thousands of victims, including the lives of my grandmother’s old church congregation.

Once he stopped his story, he looked at me, more serious than ever, and asked me if I understood what was happening in that retirement home. I nodded, realizing the abuse that was taking place.

My grandfather then said that, if I can understand what is going to happen, that I should not be here when the sun finally sets down, and should back away to never come back. 

His commentary sent shivers down my spine, but he quickly turned back to his original calm tone of voice, and we chatted for a little bit before I took him back to his room.

After all that was said and done, I left the retirement home, as my grandfather’s commentary still lingered in my mind. I promised I would do my best to take them out of that place, and maybe contact some relatives in order to get a better place to live.

We gave our goodbyes, and I left my grandparents.

Once I was away from the retirement home, I heard news of what happened: I saw news about a mysterious attack that claimed the lives of all the clerks. They were all found deceased with signs of being strangled to death. The elders , on the other hand, were left alone.

Some of the elders reported seeing the killers: a spectral arm who strangled the clerks to death. Unfortunately for them, the police did not take those reports seriously, and the “strangler ghost” became a short lived urban legend that quickly died out in the city once public interest in the case mellowed.

I am still not certain what happened that night, but I know it was not an ordinary event in any way, and I take the accounts of the elders more seriously than the police did.

I still visit my grandparents often, as they have changed to a better nursing home after the events that took place in their old one. Something inside me knows they were involved in what happened in that retirement home, but not only I have no desire to harm my relationship with my grandparents due to my suppositions, I could not even prove they were responsible in any way, so why bother? 

I have seen how the clerks were treating the elders, and even though I do not approve of killing in any capacity, I will not shed tears for elder beaters and condemn whoever decided to do justice with their own hands.
