My sister wants me to apologise for what I did at her wedding. Not to her. To Mum. Mum had been dead for five years when Erin got married, and this Sunday will be the first time since the wedding that all of us are supposed to sit around Dad's table together.

I haven't apologised. Erin says that's why we're still not speaking properly, although we speak enough for her to tell me that we're not speaking properly, which is very Erin. Dad has stopped calling altogether, and Callum messages me sometimes but never about this. He sends pictures of the dog, something stupid he saw at Tesco, that sort of thing, and once at about eleven at night he sent me a photograph of a pizza he'd burned so badly the pepperoni had curled into little black cups. I think that's his way of saying he doesn't hate me, and since he was the groom, I suppose that counts for something.

Erin got married last October in a converted farm outside Ludlow, all exposed beams, gravel and fairy lights. Mum would have hated the place. She would have complained about the gravel in her heels, said the lights made it look like a garden centre and asked why anyone had paid good money for chairs that didn't match. She also would have loved every second of telling people those things.

Mum was not an easy woman to describe after she died because everybody started editing her. She became warmer, more patient and less likely to ring you three times because you hadn't answered the first two. At the funeral, my Aunt Diane gave a speech about how Mum could make anybody feel welcome, and I sat beside Erin thinking about the Christmas Mum made Diane cry because she'd brought shop-bought trifle after being specifically told not to bring dessert. Both versions were true.

She could be generous to the point of embarrassment, remembered birthdays other people forgot and once drove forty miles at midnight because Erin had been dumped and wouldn't stop crying. She could also enter a room where six people were having six perfectly good evenings and, within ten minutes, make all six evenings about her. We loved her, and sometimes we needed a rest from her. Dad had managed thirty-two years of both things, which was longer than any of us.

The empty chair was Erin's idea. She mentioned it about three weeks before the wedding while we were sorting table decorations in her kitchen, with me tying cream ribbon around jam jars because she had decided flowers looked more expensive if you put them in something that used to contain pasta sauce.

"I'm leaving Mum a place."

I kept tying the ribbon.

"At the top table?"

"At the end."

"Okay."

"You think it's weird."

"I didn't say anything."

"You tied that bow so tight you've strangled the jar."

I looked down. She was right.

"It's not weird."

"It is a bit."

"It can be a bit weird."

She smiled. We were technically discussing a chair, but we both knew we were discussing who had got to have a mother at the important parts of her life. I'd had Mum at my university graduation, my twenty-first, my first flat, various breakups and the year I briefly thought I wanted to become a teacher and she bought me a personalised planner before I'd even applied. Erin was twenty-four when Mum died, and Callum didn't meet her until the following year.

I said the chair was fine.

On the morning of the wedding, Erin put Mum's photograph on it herself. It wasn't a formal picture; Mum was in sunglasses on a beach in Cornwall with one hand holding her hair out of her mouth. Somebody had cropped Dad out of it years ago after an argument about him disappearing to go fishing on the second day of the holiday, although he had kept the photograph anyway. Erin tied cream ribbon around the chair back, put white flowers on the seat and then moved them to the table because, as she said, nobody could sit there with flowers on it.

She noticed me noticing.

"Don't."

"I'm not."

"You've got your face."

"What face?"

"The face where you don't say the thing because you want credit for not saying it."

That annoyed me because it was accurate, so I said the thing.

"Mum isn't actually going to need the chair, Erin."

She looked at me for a second.

"I know, Lucy."

I felt horrible. She left to find the hairdresser, and I stood there holding the flowers she'd taken off the chair without knowing what to do with them. That was our first argument of the day. The second happened after dark and involved our dead mother telling my sister that I'd spent the evening jealous of her.

There were ninety-three people at the wedding. The ceremony was outside after a week of rain, but the clouds broke just long enough for Erin to get married in actual sunlight. Callum cried before she'd made it halfway down the aisle, and Dad held himself together until the vows before making a noise like he'd hurt his back. By the time everybody went into the barn for drinks, most of us already looked as though we'd been crying for hours.

Mum's chair was at the far end of the top table. Someone had poured prosecco into the glass in front of it, and Aunt Diane was standing there when I went inside. She touched the stem and looked at the photograph.

"Your mum would've complained they didn't fill it properly."

"She'd have complained it was prosecco."

"True. She'd have wanted champagne."

"She couldn't tell the difference."

Diane looked at the photograph.

"Don't say that where she can hear you."

Then the chair moved. It slid perhaps four inches and made that ugly wooden scrape across the floor that makes everybody nearby clench their teeth. A little boy was under the next table doing something complicated to a napkin, and the nearest member of staff was on the other side of the room. Diane and I stared at the empty space behind the chair.

"Uneven floor."

"Probably."

Neither of us checked. That's important, because anywhere else I think I would have crouched down and looked at the legs or pushed it back to see whether it moved again. Instead I was at my sister's wedding, wearing shoes that had already removed most of the skin from my right heel, while somebody behind me asked where the toilets were. The chair had moved, and we let it.

Dinner started at six. Dad was sitting next to Mum's chair, with me on his other side, then Erin, Callum and the rest of the wedding party. About halfway through the starter, Dad laughed at something none of us had heard and turned toward the empty chair. His expression was ordinary enough that for a second I thought I'd missed part of a conversation.

"What?"

There was a pause.

"No, she hasn't."

He looked at me.

"Have you?"

"Have I what?"

"Still got that blue coat?"

The fork in my hand touched my plate. I had owned a blue duffel coat when I was nineteen, and Mum despised it because she said I looked like Paddington Bear. I kept it partly because it was warm and partly because annoying your mother is one of the few reliable pleasures available to a nineteen-year-old. It had been in a vacuum bag under my bed for years, and Dad wouldn't have remembered it.

"I think so."

He turned back toward the empty chair.

"She has."

Dad smiled at something beside him, and his eyes went wet.

"Dad?"

He put one finger up to tell me to wait. The gesture irritated me so badly that for a moment it overpowered everything else, because he was shushing me so he could listen to my dead mother. Then his face folded, and he covered his mouth with one hand.

Dad is not a theatrical crier. At Mum's funeral he dealt with it by going outside every twenty minutes and finding something wrong with the cars, and for most of their marriage he treated emotional conversations as problems that might resolve themselves if he went to put the kettle on. That afternoon he reached toward the empty space beside him, stopped with his hand in mid-air and curled his fingers as though somebody had taken them. Watching him, I understood how easily thirty-two years of habit could survive five years of death.

The whole table went quiet.

"Erin?"

Dad nodded because he couldn't speak. Aunt Diane stood so quickly her chair went backward.

"Mare?"

Dad looked at her and then at the space beside him. Diane came around the table and stopped a couple of feet from the chair. Her expression changed, and I watched recognition arrive.

It wasn't surprise. It was recognition, the same face you make when you're collecting somebody from a station and suddenly pick them out of a crowd. Diane stared at empty air for two or three seconds, and then her mouth opened.

"Oh, you bitch."

She started laughing and crying together. People further down the room noticed, but nobody could explain what was happening because Diane had both arms around somebody I couldn't see. Her cardigan bunched between her elbows as if a body were pressing against her. Dad laughed through his tears.

"Look at you."

There was nothing there for me.

Erin wasn't watching Diane. She was staring at Mum's chair with her mouth slightly open, and when I said her name she stood without looking at me. Callum reached for her hand, but she stepped around him and walked toward the end of the table.

"Mum?"

That was when people started getting their phones out. I remember hating them for it more clearly than I remember being frightened, because something impossible was happening to my family and half the room had decided their first responsibility was to film it vertically. I snapped at the nearest man to put his away. He did, probably because I was a bridesmaid and looked furious.

Erin stopped in front of the chair and stood there for several seconds.

"You came."

Her whole face changed. I had never seen my sister look that happy, which made everything that followed harder. She leaned forward and rested her head against empty air, except it wasn't empty because her cheek stopped and her hair flattened against something that wasn't visible.

One of her earrings was pushed sideways. People around us started crying, and somebody behind me kept saying, "Oh my God," as if repetition might make it useful. Callum didn't say anything. He stood behind his chair watching his wife hug someone who had been dead since before he met her.

I went to him.

"What can you see?"

He shook his head.

"Nothing."

That helped more than it should have.

"Me neither."

"Is this..."

He didn't finish.

"I don't know."

Erin pulled away from whatever she was holding and laughed at something none of us had heard.

"No, she didn't."

Dad grinned.

"She absolutely did."

That was the first time I understood there was a conversation happening from which Callum and I were completely excluded. Diane was crying too hard to speak, Dad kept answering questions nobody else heard, and over the next ten minutes my cousin Molly and Uncle Ray began doing it too. There was no dramatic moment when it spread; people simply started making room for somebody I couldn't see.

They shifted their chairs when she passed. They looked at the empty air over one another's shoulders and waited before replying. Staff didn't seem able to see her, but guests kept guiding them around the space she occupied until the movement of the room bent around her without anybody discussing it. For perhaps twenty minutes, I was jealous.

I'm not proud of that, but it was there. Dad could hear her voice, Erin could touch her, and Diane was swearing at her affectionately while I stood within feet of my mother and had nothing. Five years is enough time to lose stupid details, and I couldn't remember whether the tiny brown mark beside her left eye had been above or below the corner. I could remember facts about her voice without being able to hear the voice itself in my head.

For a few minutes I would have done almost anything to hear her call me Lucy-Lou again. I moved closer to Dad and watched the glass in front of Mum's photograph. The prosecco level dropped while nobody visible touched it, one swallow and then another. I remember counting them.

Erin caught my wrist.

"She wants you."

I looked at the chair, and my throat hurt.

"Tell her I'm here."

Erin's smile slipped.

"She knows you're here."

"Okay."

"Lucy."

"What?"

Her eyes moved slightly to my left. They tracked upward as though someone had stepped close enough to stand almost shoulder to shoulder with me.

"Just look at her."

"I am."

"No, you're not."

I turned my head toward the place Erin was watching and tried harder than I knew how to try. I stared at the floorboards for a shadow, at the glass behind us for a reflection and at my own sleeve to see whether fabric moved where something touched it. Erin's eyes followed a face beside mine, blinking when that face apparently moved, and I saw nothing except the room beyond her.

"I can't."

Erin's expression became pity. Mum had come back from the dead and somehow, within an hour, I was already the difficult daughter again. I left before I said something I couldn't take back.

Callum followed me outside. We stood beside a stack of empty beer barrels behind the barn, and he offered me a cigarette even though neither of us smoked. Through the wall we could hear the reception restarting itself: music, cutlery and people talking too loudly because they'd just witnessed something none of them had language for.

"Do you think it's her?"

"Yes."

I'd expected myself to hesitate, but I didn't.

"Really?"

"I don't know what else would know about the coat."

He nodded and looked at the ciga+rette.

"I wish I could see her."

That surprised me.

"Why?"

He gave me a look.

"She's Erin's mum."

I liked him very much at that moment.

"But I also want my wife back."

It sounded selfish until we went inside.

Mum had taken my seat, although my actual chair was still where I'd left it. Dad had shifted down, Diane had pulled another chair over, and Erin was sitting beside Mum's place with her whole body turned away from Callum. The best man was waiting beside the microphone because the speeches had been due to start ten minutes earlier, but nobody at that end of the table seemed to remember him.

Callum sat beside Erin.

"Love, they're waiting."

She held up one finger, the exact same gesture Dad had used on me.

"One second."

It was not one second. Whatever Mum was saying kept the end of the table listening until the best man eventually cleared his throat into the microphone. Erin looked annoyed at the interruption, and Callum saw it.

Mum used to do that at family events. At my eighteenth birthday she spent most of dinner telling everybody the story of giving birth to me, including details I had specifically begged her not to share. There always came a point when the event developed a centre and the centre was Mum. Seeing the same thing happen after she was dead should have comforted me more than it did.

The speeches finally started. Callum made Erin cry, Erin kissed him, and for a little while the room belonged to them again. Dad sat with one hand resting palm-up beside Mum's chair, and once or twice his fingers closed around hers.

The first dance was at eight. Erin and Callum got through perhaps a minute before she looked over his shoulder at someone behind him. She said something, he answered, and then she stepped away while the song kept playing.

Erin lifted her right arm. Her hand settled at shoulder height, and her left hand closed around invisible fingers. Her wedding dress drew inward at the waist as though a hand had settled there. Then my sister started dancing with our mother.

I couldn't see Mum, but I could see every place she touched. Erin's sleeve flattened beneath a hand, her fingers curved around fingers that weren't visible, and a shallow pressure moved across the fabric at her waist. They danced slowly because Mum had never been a particularly good dancer. Dad made a sound behind me, Diane leaned against him and people cried openly.

So did Callum. So did I, because it was beautiful and because Erin had wanted this for five years. The song ended and she kept dancing.

A second song started, then a third. People drifted back toward the tables while Erin stayed in the middle of the floor with her head resting against someone's shoulder. By the fourth song, hardly anybody was watching except family. Callum went to the bar.

I followed him.

"She's missed her."

"I know."

"It's one night."

"I know."

He rubbed his forehead.

"I sound like an arsehole."

"You don't."

"Her dead mum comes to our wedding and I'm jealous."

"You're allowed to be jealous of the dead."

"Are you?"

"Apparently."

He laughed once. Erin came over a moment later, flushed from dancing and happier than she'd looked all day.

"Mum wants us upstairs."

"Us who?"

"You and me."

Callum looked at her.

"Oh. Sorry. She just wants a minute with us."

"It's your wedding."

"I know."

He smiled, but it took him a second.

"Go."

She kissed him and took my hand. I didn't want to go, but I went.

The bridal room was above the barn. My shoes were in there along with six makeup bags, three half-drunk glasses of champagne and a plate containing one abandoned sandwich. Erin closed the door behind us and then opened it again.

"Mum says it's stuffy."

I nearly answered, Of course she does. Erin sat on the edge of the bed, and the mattress dipped beside her exactly where Mum would have chosen to sit, hip against yours and knee touching because she treated personal space as something other families had invented. I stayed near the door.

Erin looked beside herself.

"No."

There was a pause.

"Don't."

Her smile disappeared.

"What is she saying?"

"Nothing."

"Erin."

"She thinks you're angry."

"I am angry."

"Why?"

I gestured downstairs.

"Your husband has spent half his wedding waiting for you."

Erin's face hardened.

"Mum's been dead five years."

"I know that."

"I get one night."

"You don't know that."

The words came out before I could stop them. Erin looked toward the dent in the mattress and then back at me.

"What does that mean?"

"I mean nobody knows what this is."

"It's Mum."

"I think it's Mum too."

"Then what are you doing?"

There wasn't a good answer. Trying to make sure our dead mother behaved herself at a wedding sounded ridiculous even in my head. Erin looked beside her again, and whatever expression she saw made her sit straighter.

"Don't say that."

"What?"

She looked at me.

"Mum says you're jealous."

For a second I thought I'd misheard.

"Of what?"

Erin's eyes moved toward the mattress.

"Of me."

I laughed, but there was nothing funny in it.

"That's convenient."

"Lucy."

"What exactly does she think I'm jealous of?"

Erin hesitated.

"She says you're angry because I can see her."

I stopped smiling.

"Anyone could work that out."

Erin listened. Her face changed again, this time from anger to confusion.

"What?"

"What did she say?"

"She says you counted."

I felt cold.

"Counted what?"

"The drinks."

Neither of us spoke for a moment. I had stood beside Dad watching the prosecco fall and counting the swallows in my head, and I hadn't told anyone. Erin looked unsettled now too.

"She says you watched every time she drank because you wanted proof."

I couldn't think of an answer.

"Lucy?"

"Keep going."

Erin didn't want to.

"She says you wanted her to call you Lucy-Lou."

My hand tightened around the door handle.

"Did you tell her that?"

"Tell who?"

"Anyone. Did I ever tell you that?"

"No."

The mattress beside her shifted. Erin's eyes went to it, and I had the sudden certainty that whatever was sitting there was enjoying this.

"She says you always do this."

"Do what?"

"Make things ugly when people are happy."

I felt every muscle in my face tighten.

"She said that?"

"Lucy—"

"Did she say that?"

Erin listened again.

"She says you were like this at my twenty-first."

"My appendix burst at your twenty-first."

"You complained the whole evening."

"I went to hospital."

That stopped her. Then, horribly, Erin laughed and covered her mouth.

"Sorry."

The mattress shifted again, and Erin tried not to laugh.

"Mum says you did always need to make an entrance."

That was when I knew it was her more completely than I had when Dad mentioned the coat. It was the cruelty of it, perfectly measured and impossible for her to resist once she knew she had an audience. I opened the door.

"Come downstairs."

"Lucy."

"It's your wedding."

"Mum isn't finished."

"She never was."

Erin stood.

"Fuck you."

I went downstairs alone. Callum was waiting near the bottom, and he knew instantly from my face that something had happened. He started up the first step.

"Don't go up there."

He stopped.

"Why?"

"Because right now you're the only person she can't talk into agreeing with her."

He didn't ask which 'she' I meant.

Erin came down with Dad and Diane about ten minutes later. Callum went to her before she reached the bottom.

"Can I have my wife for five minutes?"

Erin blinked.

"What?"

"The photographer wants us outside before he loses the light completely. Then cake, then we leave at eleven. Remember?"

She glanced toward the empty space beside Diane.

"Can we do it in a minute?"

"We've been doing things in a minute for two hours."

Dad looked uncomfortable. Diane looked at the floor. Erin's expression tightened as though Callum had embarrassed her.

"It's one night."

Callum stared at her. I saw him recognise his own words coming back at him.

"Fine."

He stepped aside. Erin went straight back to Mum's chair.

The photographer got them for three pictures before Erin looked away from the camera and started laughing at something beside him. In the fourth, Callum is looking directly into the lens while Erin is turned toward empty air. He told me later that the photographer asked if they wanted another try, and Erin said they had plenty. They didn't.

The cake was supposed to happen next. Instead Dad laughed at something Mum said about Diane's new kitchen, Diane called her a cow and three cousins dragged their chairs closer. Mum thought Dad's beard made him look homeless, Molly shouldn't have gone blonde, and Callum's suit was nice but the trousers were too tight. People laughed harder than the jokes deserved because every insult proved something; each mean little comment was evidence that she was really there.

Callum stopped laughing first.

Around half ten, he sat beside me.

"We're supposed to leave at eleven."

I looked at Erin. She was holding Mum's invisible hand.

"Does she know?"

"I've told her twice."

"What did she say?"

"She said we can leave tomorrow."

I turned to him.

"What?"

"We lose the hotel, apparently."

"Callum."

"I know."

"No, that's not—"

"I know."

He looked exhausted. Across the room, Erin stood, Dad kissed the top of her head and headed for the doors.

"Where's he going?"

Callum watched him leave.

"Getting her overnight bag."

I stared at him.

"She's staying here?"

"With her mum."

The venue closed at midnight. Staff were already stacking glasses, someone had turned the main lights on and half the guests had gone home. Erin was still in her wedding dress, sitting beside an empty chair because our dead mother wanted more time. Whether Mum wanted the time or Erin wanted it stopped seeming like an important distinction.

I walked over. Erin looked up before I said anything.

"Don't."

Dad came back through the doors carrying Erin's overnight bag.

"Lucy, leave it."

"Dad, the wedding's over."

"Exactly."

"Callum is waiting."

"She'll go when she's ready."

"No, she won't."

I'd said it too loudly. Everyone around the table heard me, and Erin stood so fast her chair scraped backward. Dad's face closed in the way it used to whenever Mum and I reached the point in an argument where he knew choosing a side would only make it worse.

"What is wrong with you?" Erin asked.

I looked at Mum's chair. The cushion was compressed by perhaps an inch, enough that I could see the shape of weight on it. Then I smelled her perfume.

Mum had worn the same perfume for as long as I could remember, something floral and expensive that she'd spray too heavily before walking through the house, leaving it behind. I hadn't smelled it in five years. Now it surrounded me so completely that my eyes filled and the chair blurred. For a moment all I wanted was to crouch beside her and ask where she'd been.

I wanted to tell her I still had the blue coat. I wanted to hear her say it made me look ridiculous. I wanted my mum so badly that I almost hated everyone in the room for having had her all evening.

Then Erin said, "Mum thinks you should go."

I looked at her.

"Go where?"

"Home."

It was my sister's wedding, and now my dead mother was throwing me out of it.

Dad caught my arm when I moved around him.

"Lucy."

I pulled free and went to the empty chair. Erin understood before anybody else.

"Don't you fucking dare."

I pulled the chair out. Something resisted, not like a leg catching on the floor but like somebody on the seat had planted both feet and refused to move. I yanked harder, and the chair came with me.

Diane screamed my name.

I turned around and sat down.

I didn't reach the cushion. My thighs struck two solid thighs beneath me, warm through the fabric of my dress, and my back trapped an arm against the chair. My weight settled against a torso I couldn't see. For one impossible second, I was sitting on my dead mother.

Everyone reacted at once. Dad shouted, Diane covered her mouth and Erin lunged toward me. I nearly stood.

Then a woman's voice spoke beside my left ear.

"Lucy-Lou. Get off me."

Five years of trying to remember that voice ended in four words.

Something hit me across the face.

My head turned and my lip caught against my tooth. I tasted blood, and the room went completely quiet. I put both hands on the sides of the chair.

And I sat harder.

The body beneath me pushed back. I felt one knee twist under mine and a shoulder shove against my spine, then the resistance disappeared all at once. I dropped the last inch onto the cushion hard enough to jar my teeth.

Dad stumbled backward. Erin made a sound I had heard once before, five years earlier in a hospital corridor, and looked at the space behind me.

"Mum?"

Nobody answered.

"Mum."

I touched my cheek. Five red marks were already rising across it. Erin looked at them, then looked at me.

"You made her leave?!"

Nobody spoke to me for the rest of the night. The staff brought the cake out after midnight because someone had paid extra for sparklers and they didn't want them wasted, and Erin and Callum cut it in an almost empty room. There are photographs, but I'm not in them. In the photographer's last picture of the first dance, Erin's dress is pulled inward at the waist beneath a hand that isn't there.

The phone videos are worse in a quieter way. They don't show Mum, and there is no translucent woman in the background or face appearing in a mirror. They show people making space for nothing, Diane hugging air, Dad holding an invisible hand and Erin dancing with her arms positioned perfectly around an absent body. In one clip, when I sit in Mum's chair, I stop several inches above the cushion and stay there for almost a second before the camera jerks sideways.

Erin and Callum eventually went to the hotel. Dad drove home without saying goodbye, and Diane called me a vicious little bitch in the car park before bursting into tears and hugging me. I think she hated me, and I think she was relieved. Those things can be true at the same time.

My cheek bruised badly enough that I had to explain it at work. I said I walked into a cupboard, which nobody believed, but nobody asked twice. Erin didn't speak to me for seven weeks, and Dad hasn't spoken to me since Christmas. I saw him once in Sainsbury's and he went down another aisle.

Callum sent the burned-pizza picture during week three.

Erin finally rang in January. She didn't shout, which would have been easier. We talked for nearly an hour about her honeymoon, my landlord, Dad being stubborn and Callum getting the dog he'd wanted and discovering that dogs apparently still need walking when it's raining.

Then she said, "I need you to apologise."

I said I was sorry.

"Not to me."

I didn't answer.

She told me they'd been setting Mum a place at Sunday lunch. Dad still poured her a drink sometimes and left the chair pulled slightly away from the table. She hadn't come back. Erin thought that was because of me.

"Mum held onto that stuff. You know what she was like."

I did.

"If you apologised, she'd know she was welcome."

I asked Erin whether she wanted Mum back.

"Yes."

She started crying after she said it. Of course she wanted her back. So did I.

That's why I haven't apologised.

Erin is four months pregnant now. She told me last week, and we both cried again. She said she wants me there when the baby comes and wants us to be a family again.

Then she said, "I want Mum to meet the baby too."

I've spent five years wishing my mother could have seen the things she missed. I've spent almost a year knowing that, under the right circumstances, she might.

Yesterday Erin sent me a photograph of Dad's dining room because she wanted to show me that he'd put my usual place back at the table for Sunday. Mum's chair is still at the end, pulled slightly away as though someone has just stood up, and Dad has moved the chair beside it to leave a gap. Erin says that's where the high chair will go when the baby is old enough.

Sunday lunch is in two days. I'm trying to decide whether I can sit across from that chair without wanting to sit in it again.
