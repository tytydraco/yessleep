If you bought a cast iron skillet from a Goodwill in the last week, please don't cook with it. I just got off the phone with the store. It's already been sold. They can't tell me who bought it, and apparently there isn't anything they can do to contact you, so this is my last resort.

The skillet is old, black, heavy…honestly, it looks like any other cast iron skillet you’d see in your Grandma’s kitchen if you were unlucky enough to grow up in the sticks like I did. This particular skillet belonged to my grandmother, and to a lot of other people in my family for a very long time before it belonged to her.

If you do have it, I'm not asking you to give it back. Actually, I don't want it back.

I just need you not to use it, and I need you to make sure nobody else uses it either.

The fact is, if I don’t tell you everything, you might make the same mistakes I did. I just got home a few hours ago after spending 5 days in a psychiatric hospital, and the skillet was part of the reason I ended up there.

That's also why my sister gave it away. She thought she was helping me. 

Before somebody goes searing any pork chops into that thing and accidentally starts this all over again, let me explain.

I realize there is a slight problem with my credibility here. All I ask is that you read to the end before you make up your mind.

Like a lot of shitty beginnings, this story starts with an end. My grandmother died. 

I’m not the oldest grandkid, but I am the oldest granddaughter, and with that came a closeness to her that’s still hard for me to talk about, even after everything that’s happened. You see, my grandmother, who I called “Nana,” was the embodiment of Southern charm and timeless grace; she was the last and best relic of a dying era that, aside from her, I’m quite happy with staying dead. Poor choice of words, I guess, considering. 

Much like her life, there was nothing particularly remarkable about her death, with one notable exception: she died exactly two weeks after the death of my grandfather, my “Opa”, her husband of over 50 years. While he’d been dealing with health struggles on and off for years, Nana was the picture of health. As heartbroken as I was at the sheer density of these losses, even I could see how achingly fitting an end this was to their love story. 

Then came the will. Nobody in my family has much in the way of money, not for generations, and that tends to make the question of inheritance much simpler. Divide anything of monetary value amongst the kids, and give the grandkids and any other still-living close relatives some mementos to cherish. And that’s what Nana did.

So when I got the old box, taped up to hold its integrity from too many uses over too many years, the finality of her loss hit me way harder than the wake or funeral had. I unpacked it slowly, carefully folding the old newspapers wrapping its contents for no reason other than it slowed the process. Inside, there was the cast iron skillet, and inside of it, wrapped in a grocery bag to protect from the oil glaze, was an envelope with “Melanie” written in Nana’s perfect cursive. I stared at my name, moving my fingers over the ink from her pen for at least a minute before I took a deep breath and opened it. 

I expected a long, heartfelt goodbye letter, but instead, there was just this message:

*Melanie - This is yours now.*

*You'll have to find your own way of washing it. Mine won't work for you. You'll know when you've found the right one.*

*Once you do, don't change it.*

*There is more I could tell you, but knowing more won't make it safer.*

*Only use it when you have no other choice. Never use it for something you can afford to lose.*

*I hope none of this ever makes sense to you. Forgive me.*

*Love Always,*  
*Nana*

I called my Dad immediately. After half a ring, he picked up: “Did you get your box yet?”

Hello to you too. “Yeah, yeah I did. I got her pan, I mean her, you know, cast iron skillet. And a super weird note? She told me I need to wash it carefully, like in a special kind of way, or-”

“Oh yeah, of course.” he cut me off. “You can’t just wash those things like any other dish, not even like the nonstick stuff, it’s more than that. Maybe look it up. I’m glad you got it, that thing’s been passed down to the oldest girl in the family for *forever*.” I heard engine noise in the background. 

“Are you driving? Why don’t you call me when you get-”

“No it’s okay I got a sec to talk. Anyway, not havin’ daughters of her own and everything, and sure she loved your Mom, but blood’s blood and all that.” At this point, he didn’t even seem to be talking to me. “Ah *shit*, that’s your uncle calling now, gotta figure out puttin’ that house on the market and whatnot. And kiddo - shippin’ on that box weren’t cheap, so happy early birthday.”

“Okay, well thanks, and please be care-” 

Dialtone. 

Helpful, as always. 

The one kernel of advice in that exchange wasn’t a bad idea: look it up and see what others had to say about special ways of washing a cast iron skillet. I found people who never use soap, insulted by those who never use water, further insulted by those who heat the pan at 500 degrees then scrape it with a special chainmail scrubber, and so on. Never go on the internet for advice.

Despite how militant these people were about their cleaning method, nobody used any language even close to the ominousness of Nana’s note. But it sounded like, for all its weirdness, she trusted me to figure it out. 

So I stopped worrying about it, and put the note out of my mind. 

My boyfriend Josh got home the next day from his business trip, and when I showed him the pan, he thought it was charming and sweet. 

“Ima make ya some delicious tenda steak, chil’” He said with a sickly over-the-top southern drawl while stroking his chin like he had a beard. 

I pretended to be hurt. “Shut up, my *Nana* just died, have some damn respect.” 

“Oh please, you know I think your Nana was probably the *second* greatest southern woman on Earth. Let’s make her proud and give this baby a go!” 

In our house, whoever cooks, the other person does the dishes. I didn’t have any “chain mail” (who the hell does?) and the idea of using soap scared me to death 100 angry blog posts later, so I used a method somewhere in between insane and careless. I sprinkled about a tablespoon of kosher salt into it, added a few drops of warm water, then used a fresh sponge to scrub away the mess.

Days went by, and my little sister asked to come over for dinner. I made cornbread, one of the few family recipes I actually know, thinking it might make Morgan feel better. She was always the more emotional of the two of us, and she was still inconsolable over Nana and Opa. It had been days now, and she’d taken off work for “at least the next two weeks.”

To be honest, I was horribly annoyed, and it took everything in me not to shake her as she sat bawling uncontrollably at our kitchen table. Josh was happy to escape into the kitchen to clean up while I manned the sob factory. 

Feeling equal parts abandoned and emboldened by his absence, I finally raised my voice, “Good *God*, Morgan, get your shit together! I’m sad too, I get it, we’re *all* sad, but grandparents die, and honestly, we had more time with them than most other people our age. Can you get a grip?” I tensed my jaw and looked away, knowing I’d just put even more fuel into her fire. The girl can’t even tolerate cursing in her presence, let alone being yelled at. 

Instead, I was met with silence.

I looked up at her, and she was staring back at me with her red and tear-streaked face, eyes wide but no longer watery. “You’re right.” she said smoothly. “Grandparents die.” She smiled wide, and what was left of the tears on her cheeks slipped into the corners of her upturned lips. “And honestly, we had more time with them than most people our age.” 

I narrowed my eyes at her while she finished her food. The sounds of phlegmy weeping had been replaced by meek grunts of satisfied eating. “This is *really* good, Mel.” She looked up at me with her smiling mouth full of cornbread.

She left soon after, and I went into the kitchen to help Josh with the rest of the dishes. Turns out, he was done with everything except the last plate Morgan had been eating from. He took it from me and rinsed it while I tried to recount how weird she’d acted.

“Morgan, weird? Go figure,” was all he said. 

But it bothered me. I called her the next morning between meetings to check in, and when she didn’t answer, I got worried. Then she sent a text, saying: 

*At work, can’t talk now. Everything ok?*

I furrowed my brow at the message and responded, *Yeah, just checking in. You’re back at work? Already?*

A few seconds passed, then: *Of course! I just needed to get my shit together.* 

An unsettling thought passed through me, and as soon as I got home, I went to the junk drawer where I’d put Nana’s note and looked at it again.

I turned to Josh. “Last night, when you washed the skillet, my Nana’s, how’d you do it?”

“Just hot water and that new sponge you set aside for it. People get downright territorial over that shit, but, oh God, I didn’t mess it up, did I?”

“No, no, that’s…that’s probably fine. Hot water. Sponge. Which side?”

“The kinda abrasive side?”

“Okay. Good to know.” I rolled the thought around in my head like a marble. “So I’ve been thinking. Roger, from work? I was thinking of inviting him over for dinner. You know, talk over the promotion he keeps dangling in my face. Does this Friday work?”

“Oh? Yeah, that sounds fine. What did you want to make?”

“I’ll wash up” I said too fast, realizing it wasn’t what he asked. I swallowed and forced a calm tone. “If you can cook. Anything, really. The steak you made the other night was fantastic, can you do that?”

He smiled warmly. “If it finally gets you that damn promotion, you bet.”

So I invited Roger, my asshole boss, over for dinner that Friday.

Josh went all out on the steaks, and I tried not to hover while he cooked Roger's in Nana's skillet. By the time we sat down, I was already feeling stupid about the whole thing. Roger ate every bite, complimented Josh twice, and spent most of dinner being exactly the same Roger I'd spent years working for.

That didn't mean anything yet. Whatever had happened with Morgan hadn't happened until after Josh washed the skillet.

The moment Roger cleaned his plate, I practically jumped out of my chair.

“I've got the dishes.”

Josh looked at me quizzically. “I know. But, right now?.”

“Yep.”

He gave me a funny look, but I was already gathering plates.

This left Josh alone at the table with Roger, which I realized about three seconds too late was perhaps not the kindest thing I'd ever done to either of them.

“So,” I heard Josh say while I turned on the faucet. “You guys been pretty busy at work?”

“Always.”

“Yeah.”

Silence.

I bit my cheek to force my focus on the task in front of me and picked up the skillet.

Hot water. The same sponge Josh and I had already used, abrasive side.

I did exactly what he'd told me he'd done after dinner with Morgan. I even stopped once to add more force to get as close to his washing method as possible. The whole thing took maybe ninety seconds.

Behind me, Josh had somehow moved the conversation to road construction. God help us.

I rinsed the skillet, set it down, dried my hands, and went back to rescue him.

Roger was checking his phone.

I sat down across from him. “So, while I've got you here, can we talk about the senior position?”

His eyes came up from the screen. “Melanie.”

I knew that tone. “I know, I know. But I'm already doing the work.”

“And you're doing it well. We've had this conversation.”

I waited. Roger took another sip of his drink. Nothing.

“What exactly am I still missing?”

“Experience. Time.”

I pushed a little harder. Roger didn't budge. He gave me the same polished speech I'd heard in his office about continuing to develop and revisiting the position down the road.

By the time he left, I was irritated enough that I'd almost forgotten why I'd invited him in the first place.

Josh shut the door behind him. “Your boss sucks.”

“Yep.”

He headed toward the kitchen, but I stayed where I was.

I'd cooked for Morgan in that skillet. Josh washed it. She changed.

Josh cooked for Roger in that skillet. I washed it exactly the way Josh had. Roger hadn't changed at all.

So whatever Nana meant by finding my own way of washing the skillet, apparently hot water and the rough side of a sponge weren't it.

Feeling defeated over the next day, I hardly registered when Josh asked me to make some vegetables for the church potluck. He'd been trying for months to get accepted into something called the Knights of Columbus, a Catholic men's organization that did charity work and, as best I could tell, gave the older guys in the parish an excuse to wear matching regalia and have meetings.

In fairness, I was raised Southern Baptist. Catholicism was still new territory for me, and I was making an honest effort not to judge anybody else's strange church traditions after growing up with enough of my own.

Whatever the Knights meant to me, they meant a lot to Josh. The problem was me, his live-in, definitely not a wife, girlfriend. Nobody had ever been outright cruel about it, but a few of the more traditional members had made their disapproval clear.

So, in typical Josh fashion, he'd volunteered us to bring baked BBQ ribs, shepherd's pie, and a vegetable dish, then discovered the morning of the potluck that our oven couldn't accommodate his ambitions. He all but begged me to take over the vegetables on the stove side of the battlefield while he handled everything else.

I'd given up on Nana's skillet as anything more than a very good piece of cookware, but it was at least good for that. I put it on the stove, got it hot enough to smoke, and seared the Brussels sprouts until the cut sides browned and the loose leaves crisped black around the edges.

We arrived with enough food to cover half the serving table. The potluck itself was uneventful. People ate. I got asked a few times how I'd made the Brussels sprouts. 

It wasn't until people started clearing their plates that Josh tried again. A few of the Knights were standing near us, and I saw Josh breathe deeply before nodding toward one of them.

“So, Frank,” Josh said, “you guys ever going to let me join, or am I going to have to keep feeding you first?”

A few of them laughed.

Josh smiled. “Come on. I know our situation isn't exactly what you guys would prefer, but I'm here every Sunday. I volunteer all the time. I'm trying to do things right. That ought to count for something.”

He'd said versions of that before. Usually, somebody changed the subject.

Frank looked at him. “You're here every Sunday.”

Josh's smile held, but only just.

The man beside him nodded. “Every Sunday.”

“And you volunteer,” another said.

“All the time.”

Frank glanced at the others. “He's trying to do things right.”

“He is.”

“I've always thought that ought to count for something.”

Josh looked at me, gobsmacked. I don't think he noticed it.

Frank nodded slowly, as though Josh's words had become more convincing now that they were coming out of his own mouth.

“You're here. You volunteer. All the time. You're trying to do things right.” He clapped Josh on the shoulder. “I don't see why we can't get this moving.”

“Neither do I,” someone said.

And that was it.

Months of resistance reduced to a few men standing around with paper plates, handing Josh his own argument back to him piece by piece.

Josh was ecstatic. He was still talking about it in the car. “Did you hear Frank? He's the one who's been weirdest about the whole thing.”

“Yeah.”

“And *Tom* backed him up.”

“Yeah.”

“I told you they'd come around.”

I turned toward him. “Did you do the dishes before we left?”

He paused. “What?”

“This morning. While I was getting ready.”

“Yeah.”

“All of them?”

“I think so.”

“The skillet.”

“What?”

“Nana's skillet! Did you wash it?”

“Yeah. Why?”

I didn't answer.

I felt the seat belt tighten against my chest as I sat forward.

It wasn't the hot water. It wasn't the sponge. It wasn't *how* the skillet was washed at all.

It was Josh.

I wish I could say I listened to Grandma. I wish I could say I was careful, that I understood I was messing with forces I didn’t fully understand, that I kept a cool head. But then I wouldn’t be here, typing this warning to you.

Things escalated quickly after that.

It didn’t take long to get my well-deserved promotion; just a slice of skillet cookies on Roger’s desk, with a note that it was leftover from Jamie’s birthday party. Jamie’s birthday was 4 months ago, but I’d bet dollars to donuts he wouldn’t have the slightest inkling. And wouldn’t you know it, the entitled prick scarfed it down without a second thought. A 60-second conversation later, and I’m the new senior editor. Like I said, it was much deserved, so don’t go throwing him a pity party.

And there were other things too. I convinced my best friend Susie to finally re-enroll in school and finish her degree with some lamb chops. I persuaded Josh’s sister to finally leave her cheating husband with pan-seared wings. I even got old Cousin Tina to lay off the sauce with the almighty power of home fries, surely saving her liver. I was helping people, and they didn’t have to know it. What the hell would you do if you were given this kind of power?

There were some slightly more selfish things too, I admit. Like inclining my neighbor to keep his tree branches trimmed neatly to his side of the fence with a mac and cheese casserole at his doorstep, or swaying that city council to vote to install streetlights on our road after leaving a Dutch baby in the City Hall kitchenette. 

All I had to do was keep Josh washing the pan. He’s always been a great partner: happy to help around the house whenever needed. At first I assumed he figured I had anxiety about messing up my own piece of Nana’s legacy, but after a week of cleaning it every day, he started asking questions. I didn’t have time to cook for us *too*, so I was asking him to both manage dinner and do dishes, which was a big no-no. Without answering any of his questions, I agreed I’d step up and take charge of dinner every night, as long as he could keep doing the dishes. *All* of the dishes.

And I was true to my word. That’s when things got worse. 

For one, I realized that, even though Josh’s hands were the key to keeping the magic going, his mind was just as susceptible to it. The first time was an accident. I wanted to see a band he couldn’t stand. We’d had the argument before, so when I brought it up over dinner and he suddenly shrugged and said, “Sure, could be fun,” I knew exactly what had happened. I should have been horrified.

Instead, I bought the tickets.

I told myself I wasn’t crossing any major moral boundaries. I kept it light, you know, like the concert, or trying that new restaurant he kept turning his nose up at. And I think he was better for those experiences, really I do. But they weren’t his decisions. I can see that now.

It wasn’t just him. Eventually, people at work stopped eating the little treats I was leaving them; I guess once it stops being occasional, it stops really being a treat.

So I started taking inventory of the refrigerator, examining their various Tupperware containers, taking note of what they brought in and how much of it.

Carol brought leftovers almost every day. Greg meal-prepped. Roger usually brought whatever his wife had cooked the night before. I started remembering the containers, the portions, even stupid little details, like whether somebody sprinkled cheese on top or packed their sauce separately.

Then I'd go home and make my version. If Carol brought a slice of frittata on Tuesday, there was a decent chance she'd bring another slice on Wednesday. If Greg had chicken and rice, that was easy enough to reproduce. I didn't need it to be flawless; I just needed them not to look too closely.

The next morning, I'd get in early. I'd dump out the real lunch and put my recreation in its place. Same exact arrangement in the container when I could manage it, same amount of food. Everything I made touched Nana's skillet.

And in exchange, I was getting volunteers to help with my workload, drama-free office friends, and coworkers who were, for the first time, *truly* giving it their all.

“Sure, I can stay late.”

“You know what, Melanie's right.”

Little sentences. After a while, I stopped hearing how strange they were. I was in a management position now, so it didn’t seem as out of pocket as it feels writing it now.

This was taking a lot more time than just the hour or so each night I had been spending on these little projects, so I convinced Roger to start letting me take off earlier in the day, no questions asked. He liked that cornbread even more than Morgan did.

And even though I was still cooking dinner every night, even though I hadn’t told him about any of this, Josh sensed something. I should’ve noticed the signs sooner. 

Whatever the skillet did to people, I don’t think it was meant to be done to the same person over and over again.

He would agree with me, parrot my words, but every once in a while, something underneath seemed to surface.

The bedroom was the first time I couldn’t pretend not to see it. I’d wanted to paint it green for years, something he’d always been opposed to even though it’s our *mutual* favorite color. I fed him dinner, brought it up again, and by Saturday morning we were standing beside each other with rollers in our hands.

We’d finished almost half the room when his roller stopped.

“This looks awful,” he said. He was staring blankly at the wall.

Then he dipped the roller back into the tray and started painting again.

“Josh?”

“What?”

“What…what did you just say?”

For a second, there was something in his face I hadn’t seen in weeks. Then it was gone.

“I said, it’ll look good when it’s done.” Then he rolled another stripe of green over the wall. 

So when he finally told me he was done washing the damn skillet, I shouldn’t have become as angry as I did. It was a Tuesday night. I remember because I’d made pork chops for a woman from corporate who was coming in Wednesday morning to decide which of three departments would get another full-time position.

Josh was at the sink when I brought him the skillet. He looked at it, then at me. “No.”

I laughed. “No what?”

“No pan tonight.”

“Very funny.”

“I’m serious, Mel.”

There were already dishes drying in the rack; it was clear he’d washed most of them. All of them, actually, except the skillet.

“Josh, come on.”

“I’m tired.”

“It takes you like thirty seconds.”

“Then you wash it.” He dried his hands on a towel. “I’m going to bed.”

“Josh.”

“I said no.”

Something cold opened in my stomach. “You *always* do the dishes.”

“And you’ve gotten weird about this one.”

“I’m not weird about it.”

“You hand it to me every night. You *stand* there until I wash it. Last week I left it soaking in hot water while I took a shower and you came upstairs to remind me.”

“I thought you forgot!”

“I *did* forget. Because it’s a pan.” He started out of the kitchen.

I followed him. “Okay. Fine. I’m sorry. But can you please just wash it tonight?”

“No.”

“Why?”

He turned around. “Because I don’t want to.”

There are answers that become terrifying only when you’ve forgotten the other person is allowed to give them.

I looked down at the skillet in confusion, then back up. “Please.”

“No.”

“I really need you to.”

“For what?”

I should have told him then. Instead I said, “It’s Nana’s.”

He looked hurt. “Don’t do that.”

“Do what?”

“Use your dead grandmother to make me feel like an asshole because I don’t want to wash your pan.”

“That’s not what I’m doing.”

“Then tell me why.”

I couldn't. The woman from corporate had eaten the pork chops. She'd smiled when I told her how badly we needed the position. She'd said she understood. Tomorrow morning she would sit down with Roger and make her decision.

The skillet had to be washed before then.

“Josh, please.”

He shook his head.

“Just tonight.”

“No.”

“Thirty seconds.”

“Mel.”

“*Twenty* seconds, then. Run it under the water. Scrub it. Put it down.”

“I said no.”

“Why are you doing this?”

His mouth fell open. “Why am *I* doing this?”

“You know it matters to me.”

“I know you’ve decided it matters for some God-forsaken reason.”

“Then why can't you just do this one thing for me?”

He didn’t even respond to that, just scoffed.

I was screaming before I realized it. “Just wash the damn pan, Josh! Do it!”

The kitchen went silent. Josh stared at me.

I was breathing hard, but I couldn’t hear it; instead there was a whine in my ears like you get in an airplane, or when you have a head-cold.

“Give it to me,” he said. He held out his hand. The tone was softened. “Give me the skillet, Mel.”

“No.”

“I’m putting it away.”

“No.”

“Then put it down.”

He reached for it, I pulled it back. That's all I meant to do: just get it away from him.

But it was heavy, and I had both hands around the handle, and when I pulled it back my arm came up with it. Over my shoulder. Like a bat.

Josh flinched. I froze. The skillet was still behind my head. His hands were up.

I don't know how long we stood like that. Finally, I lowered it.

“Oh my God.”

Josh didn't move.

“I wasn't going to hit you.”

He looked at the skillet. “I know.”

“I wasn't.”

He still had his hands up.

I set it on the counter so quickly it hit hard enough to make us both jump.

“Josh, I swear.”

“I know.”

“Then stop *saying* it like that.”

He looked at me for a long time.

Then he walked past me.

“Where are you going?”

He didn't answer.

“Josh?”

I heard the bedroom closet open. I followed him and found him pulling his overnight bag from the shelf.

“Are you serious?”

“Yeah.”

“Josh, I didn't do anything.”

He stopped packing. “You didn't?”

“I mean, I didn't hit you.”

“No. You just almost hit me with a cast iron skillet because I wouldn't wash it.”

“I pulled it away from you.”

“You raised it over your head!”

I sat on the edge of the bed.

“Please don't leave.”

“I’m going to my mom’s.”

“For how long?”

“I don't know.”

The panic came back immediately. I wish it was because my boyfriend was leaving me. It wasn't.

“Wait.”

Josh zipped the bag.

“Wait, wait. Before you go.”

He looked at me. I could hear my own voice before I said it. I knew how it would sound, but it didn’t stop me.

“Can you please just wash the skillet first?”

His face went completely still.

Then he picked up his bag and left.

I didn’t go to sleep, and I had no intention of going to work the next day, corporate bitch be damned. Shortly after Josh left, I drove the six hours to Nana’s house.

The charming cottage with Opa’s meticulously maintained yard and Nana’s expertly crafted garden were just starting to show signs of neglect. The hedges had gone shaggy. Weeds had pushed up between the stones along the walk. Half a dozen tomatoes hung split and overripe on the vines, opened up for bugs to feast on.

Opa would have hated the hedges.

Nana would have mourned the tomatoes.

Uncle Charlie was standing in front of the porch when I pulled up, just like I’d asked him to be when I called at five that morning, an hour earlier.

“Well, look who it is.” He put his hands on his round waist and feigned surprise. “Girl, you’re lucky I was already needin’ to take care of Momma and Papa’s house ‘fore the realtor takes a good look at it.”

“You said on the phone you were sure everything’s still inside?”

“Everything ‘cept what she already willed away, if that’s whatcha mean.”

I walked past him toward the porch steps. He caught my shoulder.

“Melanie, don’t you take this the wrong way now, but you don’t look so good. Don’t look *yourself*, I mean. You sleepin’ alright?”

“Sleeping fine, Uncle Charlie. Just, I need to look around in here. See if there’s anything that can help me.”

“And what’s it you’re needin’ help with?”

I was immediately exhausted trying to think of how to explain it without sounding crazy.

“Did Nana ever talk to you about her cast iron skillet?”

Charlie blinked. “Her what?”

“The skillet she left me. Old cast iron. Did she ever tell you where it came from?”

“Melanie, your Nana owned ‘nough cast iron to armor a tank.”

“This one was different.”

That wiped the smile from his face. “Different how?”

“I don’t know yet.” 

He studied me for a second, then unlocked the door.

The house smelled wrong without them. Not the way you’d expect, rotten or dusty, just closed. Stale. Opa’s reading glasses were still beside his chair. Nana’s ceramic birds still lined the kitchen window. A grocery list remained on the refrigerator beneath a peach-shaped magnet.

I forced myself not to read it.

“If you’re looking for old family stuff,” Charlie said behind me, “Momma kept all that genealogy mess in the office.”

“What genealogy stuff?”

He laughed. “Oh, Lord. You never got trapped into that conversation?”

I hadn't. Apparently Nana had spent years tracing our family back through births and deaths, marriages, property records, newspaper archives. Three plastic tubs were shoved beneath Opa’s desk, along with binders thick enough to stop bullets.

Charlie helped me pull them out.

“You holler if you need me.” Then, mercifully, he left me alone.

I started with Nana and worked backward.

Her mother. Her grandmother. Her great-grandmother. I was looking for women.

It took less than an hour to find a mention of the skillet.

An instruction had been copied from the margin of an old family recipe, with Nana’s handwriting beneath it identifying the woman who'd written it.

*Dinah is to scour this one. No other hands.*

I found Dinah somewhere else. Not in the genealogy, as I expected. In an estate inventory.

She was fourteen years old when someone in my family put a price beside her name.

I sat with that for a while, the Hardee’s biscuit I’d scarfed down for breakfast on the road threatening to fight back. There are things people like to call complicated when enough time has passed. A child's name beside a dollar amount isn't one of them.

I kept reading. Within a few years of emancipation, that branch of the family began losing what it had. There were records of reclaimed debts. Repossessed land. Letters blaming politicians, freedmen, each other, God. Nothing blamed a skillet, at least on paper.

The next instruction I found was decades later.

*After washing, scour with earth from the kitchen garden.*

That one seemed almost laughably safe. Then came the fire.

It started in the barn during a dry August and spread fast enough that the family barely got out. The house burned. The barn burned. By morning, nearly everything they owned was ash. The family had no money left to recover. Creditors took what remained of the property.

Another generation: *Rinse only with rainwater.*

Then a drought. A historic one, according to the article Nana had printed. Crops withered. Wells failed. Families left.

Then: *After washing, set before all the family men for prayer.*

There was a photograph clipped to that one. A woman and her smiling daughters surrounded by her husband and three grown sons.

The next document was a newspaper account of a mine collapse. All four men were among the dead.

I stopped. The papers covered the floor around me. My eyes locked on the words I’d retained from each: Dinah, farm, rain, men.

Different women. Different instructions. Different catastrophes. But the same ending.

Whatever made the skillet work eventually became impossible. Some of those women had years, one had decades. Enough time to trust it, to build a life around it, to look at every woman who came before and think:

*Mine is easy. This one is easy.*

I thought about Josh standing at our sink. Thirty seconds. Hot water and a sponge. I had thought I was the lucky one. I’d thought it was easy.

Then I remembered Nana’s note.

*You'll know when you've found the right one.*

*Once you do, don't change it.*

I understood the first half now. It was the last line that frightened me.

*I hope none of this ever makes sense to you. Forgive me.*

I went through everything again looking for Nana’s version. Nothing. But I had my suspicions. There was no instruction naming Opa, nor recipe with *Karl washes this one* scribbled underneath it. No confession from Nana explaining what she'd done. But she'd been married to Opa for more than fifty years. And now I understood why she might have believed her version was easy, too.

Charlie found me sitting on the floor sometime later. “Jesus, Melanie.”

I hadn't noticed I was crying. He lowered himself into Opa’s desk chair.

“You find what you was lookin’ for?”

“I think so.”

“That good or bad?”

I looked around at the papers. “Bad.”

He waited.

I don't know why I said what I did next. Maybe because I hadn't slept. Maybe because Josh was gone. Maybe because I'd just watched two hundred years of my family repeat the same mistake.

“Nana knew.”

“Knew what?”

“That when Opa died, she was going to die too.”

Charlie’s face softened. “Oh, honey.”

“Not like that.”

“They were married fifty-three years. I know two weeks sounds quick, but sometimes when folks have been together that long-”

“I don't mean she died of a broken heart.” I wiped my face. “I mean I think she knew she *couldn't live without him.* Literally.”

Charlie stared at me. Then he shook his head. “You know, you keep talkin’ like your Nana had never been sick a day in her life.”

“As far as I know, she hadn't.”

“Well, that's because all that happened before you were born.”

I looked at him. “What did?”

“Cancer.”

For a second I thought I'd misheard him. “Nana had cancer?”

“You really didn't know that?”

“No.”

“Huh.” Charlie scratched his chin. “Guess there wasn't much reason for anybody to tell you. It was ancient history by the time you come along.”

“When?”

“Long time ago. Your dad was still a youngin’.”

“How bad?”

“Bad enough they wasn’t givin’ her much to be hopeful ‘bout.” He paused. “Then she heard about this new treatment they was trying. Chemotherapy.” 

“Chemo?”

"Wasn't like it is now. It was still new enough that her doctor didn't think much of the idea.”

“She changed his mind.” It wasn’t a question.

Charlie smiled. “You met the woman, you know how she can get. She kept after him. Probably drove the poor man half crazy. Eventually he agreed to try it.”

“Did it work?”

“She's your grandmother, ain’t she?”

I didn't smile.

Charlie shrugged. “Cancer never came back. She never had another serious problem until the end.”

“Did she know the doctor personally?”

“Not that I know of.”

“Did she ever have him over?”

Charlie frowned. “For what?”

“Dinner.”

“I haven’t the slightest idea.”

“Did she cook for him?”

He gave me a strange look. “Melanie, your Nana cooked for everybody.”

My eyes dropped to the papers around me.

Charlie followed my gaze, but thankfully didn't ask.

After a moment he said, “Funny, though.”

“What?”

“Your Opa had his own miracle later on.”

I already knew which one he meant. “The transplant?”

“Yep.”

That story I knew. Opa's health had been failing for years. Then came the transplant, the hospital, the long recovery. I remembered balloons in his room. Nana crying. Somebody bought him a T-shirt about being made of spare parts that he wore until the lettering cracked.

The transplant was the reason we'd gotten so many more years with him.

“He almost didn't get it, you know,” Charlie said.

“I know it took forever.”

“No, I mean they weren't going to give him one.”

I blinked. “What?”

“Initially, anyway. He wasn't exactly their dream candidate. Too old, too many other little things wrong with him.”

That part I had never heard. “But…he was on the transplant list.”

“Sure. Way down there.”

The room was spinning. “What changed?” I heard myself ask, though I felt like I was watching the conversation from outside my body.

Charlie shrugged. “Your Nana. She did the same thing she always did. Wouldn't take no for an answer. There was some doctor or committee they needed to reconsider him, and eventually they did.”

“Who?”

“Honey, I don't remember.”

“Did Nana meet them?”

“I'm sure she met somebody.”

“Did she cook for them?”

Charlie stared at me again. “Why do you keep askin’ me that?”

I didn't answer. I looked down at my knees to try to stop from hurling.

Nana had cancer. Her doctor said no to chemotherapy. Nana changed his mind. She lived.

Decades later, Opa needed an organ. The people deciding whether he should get one said no. Nana changed their minds. He lived.

And suddenly the ugliest part occurred to me.

Opa got an organ. Someone else didn't.

I don't know who that person was. I don't know what happened to them. I don't want to.

I only know Opa came home.

And if Opa had been Nana's Josh, then every time he stood at the sink afterward, he wasn't only preserving whatever she'd done to save *him.*

He was preserving what she'd done to save *her*.

I thought about every photograph I'd gone through that morning in those bins, in scrapbooks next to the binders of Nana’s findings. Nana tucked against Opa's side. His arm around her waist. Fifty-three years of birthdays and Christmases and vacations and ordinary Tuesdays.

Nana loved him. I have no doubt about that. But sometime after the transplant, his continued existence had become something else too. A condition of hers. I wondered if she was thinking about that when she fought to keep him alive.

*Only use it when you have no other choice.*

Charlie was still talking. “Truth is, those two got more borrowed time than anybody I've ever known.”

Borrowed time. Fourteen days apart, they’d died. My family had found that beautiful, myself included. And suddenly Nana's last plea to me made sense.

*Forgive me.*

I left Nana's house sometime in the late afternoon. I sat in the car for a minute before starting it and finally looked at my phone.

Five missed calls from Roger. A text from Susie: *I can't afford this, Mel. I don't know what I was thinking. I'm dropping the classes tomorrow.* I stared at it. Then, on a hunch, I opened Facebook. Josh's sister had posted a picture twenty minutes earlier. Her and her husband. Smiling. His arm around her. The caption: “*Sometimes love deserves a second chance.”*

I didn't need to call Roger back. I knew what was happening.

Instead, I drove straight home, then to Josh's mom's house with the skillet on the passenger seat.

He wouldn't let me inside. I told him I understood now. I told him I was sorry. I told him he only had to wash it one more time, that I could fix it.

He called the police.

I called Morgan.

When the cops arrived, I was still sitting in the driveway with the skillet in my lap.

You can imagine how that conversation went.

Morgan got there before they took me away. One of the officers handed her my purse, my phone, and, after some discussion, the skillet. It wasn't evidence of anything. Just something they weren't going to let me take with me.

My sister called me at the hospital later. She said she'd water my plants and clean up the house so I wouldn't have anything to worry about when I got home. I thanked her.

I got home this afternoon. The plants were watered. The house was spotless. The skillet was gone. I called Morgan. She'd donated it to Goodwill.

So now we're caught up.

If you bought it, you don't need to panic yet. You can cook with it. You can wash it. Maybe nothing will happen for weeks, or months, or even years. Until one day you wash it the right way. Someone who ate from it will agree with you when they shouldn't. Then somebody else will.

You'll figure it out.

And when you finally understand what you've got, you'll look at whatever ridiculous little thing the skillet requires from you and think exactly what Nana thought. Exactly what I thought.

*This one seems easy.*
