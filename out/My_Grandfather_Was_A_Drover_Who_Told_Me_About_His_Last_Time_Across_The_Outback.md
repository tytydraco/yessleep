I should start by saying that this story isn’t about me. It’s about my grandfather, a man with a whole life of better stories than I would ever have and ones he loved to tell even as he slowly forgot when, where and what had happened. 

He’d regale us with tales about how he became Western Australia’s best sheep sheerer, how he’d ride past my grandmother’s grocers and spend half his money each day just to talk to her, stories about his adventures travelling through the outback, stories he’d tell over and over again as he’d forget he’d told them before and sometimes ones he’d remember like he’d just lived it.

For all his memories and stories, there was only one story he’d ever told us that gave me chills. A story he'd ask us to remember for generations, even if it’s never believed. I don’t know if I truly believe it either but maybe someone here has been told a similar story told by their great grandparents. 

The story of his last ride across the vast red desert.

When my grandfather, a man who I will refer to as Blue, was in his 20s he worked as a drover, something some of you may know as a sort of Australian cowboy who’d drive cattle across the country, surviving some of the most brutal conditions for months at a time. 

This was normally to drive cattle to markets from rural farms or bring them to new farms when cars weren’t an option— a team of drovers is what you needed when these distances could involve getting up to a thousand heads across the hostile terrain covering 70% of the country. 

Considering this size, you’d see most of this cattle roaming over vast properties instead of behind fences and that meant rounding them up on horseback and taking them the same way.

Blue’s team had been small, it wasn’t too many heads and was only expected to be a good two or three weeks on a fast route from Alice Springs. Four men, four hundred cows and six dogs. Coming from a rural town meant there wasn’t much of a crew and two of the men hadn’t done more than farm work with the youngest of them being about sixteen and the boss of the crew being their father. 

They were the boss, the kid, Irish and Blue. “ The boss was strict, no nonsense and eager to teach the kid how to do some man’s work. The “Irishman” was a roamer who’d picked up a few jobs in town, an Arrernte aboriginal bloke. I’d been herding since I could get on a horse.” He’d say but there was always a sadness to his words, “ They… They were good men. Men who rode out into the ocean and never returned.”

If you’d ask someone to imagine the desert they conjure up images of endless sand dunes and the unrelenting sun drying up their throats from the thought alone. Just like a dying man wandering the wasteland sees the mirage of welcoming waters and palm trees hanging over an oasis, yet that figment of the imagination will forever be just out of reach of the dying man. 

His lips will crack and globs of blood will stick to the frayed edges but not even that will satiate his thirst, his feet will blister and wear down to bone where they touch the fiery sands— he’ll never reach the mirage. 

The outback is not a place of endless sand but dust. A Red Sea of dust that crawls across the horizon with waves of shimmering heat telling you that this land is not God’s domain. This vast, beautiful and brutal place at the very edge of our world belongs to something older. 

The crew got their gear together and saddled up, heading off at dawn with the waves of their families seeing them off and six dogs bounding and barking ahead of them to tell the sea they were coming.

Blue knew from the first sign of clouds overhead that this drive was moving with a bad omen following behind them, a feeling he couldn’t shake even when the boss said it would cool off the dogs and cattle. 

Rain was a good sign, water meant they’d have more places to rest and drink if the river beds weren’t dried up. 

The temperamental weather and only two real seasons to rely on often meant it would be nothing but dead trees and dirt in the dry season and during the wet season you see it come to life. Water was a blessing but all of those empty slopes and bends from the rivers aren’t called ponds, pools or lakes— they’re called billabongs even when they’re full. Dead rivers. Except even when the land was alive, a dead river was never truly dead.

The first few days were slow because of the showers. The rain would pour down and soak their clothes and the dust would stick to them like mud one moment and then their drying skin would be left to the flies and blood suckers. 

The cattle never wanted to cross the smallest of creeks and add time that the impatient boss wasn’t happy about. They pitched half a tent, hoping the branches from dead trees would still be dry enough to make a fire and settle into their swags under the blanket of clouds hiding the sky. 

The cattle would wander but never stray too far, the dogs would pile up near the fire like a mass of furry limbs on a six headed beast— the last dog, Little Dog, always slept at my grandfather’s feet.

The fourth day started another bad omen. If not for the rain, the route they would’ve taken around the gorges, jagged mountains and cliffs used for years and it was one Blue was familiar with— straying could mean days of wandering and getting turned around through open deserts or bush land with sudden drops behind sparse trees, dry yellow grass could mean a sudden blaze and rain could mean floods— none of which is easy to navigate with four hundred heads. 

The route they’d planned to take was drowned, they couldn’t get the cattle across. 

Whether they wanted to or not, they’d need to go off the beaten path. 

They’d argued over it that night but The sun came up bright and the four men got to work planning a new route through Luritja Country bushland the reluctant Irishman said had been burned off— something often done to control the spread of violent bushfires that eat the regions when dry lightening hits dry grass that’s left unfed. 

They could avoid most of the large canyons spanning the region but these burns would mean they’d be travelling through ash and smoke for a week. 

The route would be dangerous, dark and difficult for the cattle but the boss— the man with the final say— said a quicker path could cut the journey in half and waiting for water to dry up along the usual route means a few weeks could become a few months.

They sat there arguing. 

The boss and the Irishman were at odds, “ That Country ain’t gonna have drinking water, just wet ash.” Irish said, “ It’s either a quick trip through burnt bushland, dry desert and fire or waiting months to even get back to Alice Springs.” The boss shouted, barely able to keep his voice down.

“ Then we go up. Go round Uluṟu way.” Irish said over his tin cup of tea, “ The kid can see the big red rock.” He added. Blue and the kid didn’t have much say in the matter but the burnt bushland route was the path they’d eventually settle on with Blue being the reluctant deciding vote.

The crew set on the new trail under through a wide canyon with high walls of red rock that looked dark with the rain and while sparse shrubs and jagged rocks lined the sides the walls seemed to wave like a constant stream had passed through and worn even the highest points down to a smooth, bloody surface. 

Over their 19 hours of travel through the valley it opened up to outstretched arms of rock where what might be their last pool of clean water rested in its bed. The red canyons showed no signs of travelled paths through the large stretch; the cattle roamed while the four men slept, the distant mountains were shadowed by a thick cloud of black smoke— soon to be above them.

The fourth night began with the same routine— the four men built a fire, cooked some of the food they’d packed and Blue quietly ate his meal under the weight of the brewing tension between the boss and the Irishman, one drinking his dinner from a flask and the other focused on checking the supplies for the days ahead. 

The dogs kept their distance, sitting far from the fire the four men sat around with their eyes on the cattle wandering down the nonexistent path. “ Blue, you think it’s okay we’re going out there?” The kid said from his seat beside the man, he hadn’t touched his food much. 

“ It’ll mostly be burnt old trees but someone had to go through and burn it so we might come across some black fellas with better food than this shit.” Blue grumbled and looked down at his own sorry excuse for porridge and sausages, an already unappetising combination but it’s good for the cold and it can get very cold at night. 

“ I’m gonna toss this to the dogs. Finish your dinner, kid.” Blue got to his feet just for the kid to stop him, “ Blue, can I set my swag up next to yours? Dad’s been drinking.”

Blue rolled out his pack after another hour or so talking with the Irishman, he’d always found the man’s input to be a lot more helpful considering how much time they’ve spent out in the bush but he’d feel a lot more comfortable knowing that what he told the kid about the tribes still being close was true— Will they be alone out there? 

The Irishman laughed and said they’d be a week ahead of them at least and that they’d probably find it funny if they saw a bunch of white fellas all covered in ash and scared. “ The boss-man thinks my type are superstitious but we’ve been walking through this land for longer than bossman. We’ll get there quick if we go where the others went but quick doesn’t mean safe and just because others left doesn’t mean the land is empty.” He joked and laughed at Blue’s nervous frown. 

He gestured to the river and held up his hands like his long fingers were the claws of a beast, “ Don’t piss in the billabong or a bunyip gonna grab ya.” He said and with a pat on Blue’s shoulder he kindly added “ We might run into some dingoes but burnt ground is dead ground, Blue.”

Blue rolled out his swag and tried to get himself comfortable, whistling over to Little Dog to come over. Even with his faithful companion curling up at his feet it did nothing to sway the growing doubt in his mind. 

Bad omens aren’t something everyone believes in but unfamiliar ground comes with a particular unease that twists in your gut because a little part of your brain is saying “ You can’t see it but something is there. 

You don’t know what’s around every bend and behind every tree because you’ve never seen them before. 

You don’t know where or what but something, somewhere was wrong and he just couldn’t see it yet. 

For that moment as he laid there with his eyes on the blanket of clouds, he’d started to have that very feeling. The crickets, the idle chirps of distant creatures and the rattling he had expected to hear from the rain hadn’t come. The long stretch they’d been travelling had been completely silent. 

The wind wasn’t sweeping across the sky with whispers and howls, the fire didn’t crackle— and not a single breath or thumping heartbeat came from anyone inside of Blue’s camp. Four hundred and fifteen living, breathing and sleeping beasts were drowned out by the sound of his own heart and one other sound, a sound from so far away and yet he swears he could’ve heard it right against his ear.

Dry bark snapped under the weight of something’s foot down the winding path of blood soaked walls of smooth canyon rock they’d herded to cattle through.   
He knew it could’ve been the echoes of a dogs paws walking over dead grass reverberating off of the canyon’s wide arms— but he couldn’t explain the slow, creeping movement of something walking on two legs.

The noise drew closer.

What started off as the calm tha-thump of his heart was barely contained in his chest, throwing itself against his ribcage and thrashing with a rhythm so fast that the noise should’ve been drowned out by it— but the footsteps were so clear and so close.

Snap.

He didn’t move an inch but he strained his eyes to look at the crew sleeping close enough to the fire that even with their faces shrouded in shadows he knew those silent, sleeping bodies were still wrapped in sleeping bags safe in the light. He could see Little Dog at his feet and to his right was the kid.

Snap.

The closer the sound got to him the clearer each little bend and breaking twig, the slight rustle of dry grass and the unusual gait of those two feet became. How close had it gotten? 

It sounded very slow compared to a heart straining in his chest and a land so quiet he could hear each muscle in his body tense and twist— it was far too close, impossibly close, close enough that the path felt as if it had been only a few steps away.

Snap.

A fire is warm and bright. Fire lets you see what’s around you but light has always had a flaw, it could never light up the unfamiliar darkness around you— you exist in a small cone of light with no real way for eyes that have adjusted to it to see what’s lurking off in the shadows. 

What is out there however, can see you. 

He can’t see what’s standing just out of view where not even his peripheral vision can make out in the darkness but he knows it has stopped. He can see the flames flickering with little shadows across the kid’s sleeping face but he can’t see what’s standing near their feet.

Something was there and his entire body was screaming in the silence saying that this something he can’t see is staring.

His stomach twisted into painful knots and his lungs shrivelled and cried for air when he held his breath but he didn’t move. 

His mind raced between thoughts of just what is lingering there unseen, what if the kid moves and if this something is malevolent— how would he even stop it? Is it simply a person standing above them or is it the contorted branch of limbs that stand so tall on its skeletal feet that the dark skies he knows are clouds are now hidden by this unseen mass overhead. 

His eyes felt dry and his vision blurred but Blue didn’t dare to close them even as they watered in an attempt to save themselves.

Snap.

The tall thing on shambling limbs continued its journey toward the thick clouds of ash in the far distance and just beyond the herd. He swallowed the sandpaper in his throat and finally allowed air into his lungs, his tense muscles eased and after blinking away his blurry vision he was able to look at the kid staring at him with wide eyes.

“ Blue… I don’t think we should go through the dead place.”

The sun came up but as he stared up at the dark omen hanging above them, the smoke-covered morning left the sky in a perpetual night. Irish and the boss argued but their path had been marked and the boss wasn’t turning back.

Blue downed his coffee and spat out the grit from the old tin cup, shaking it out and hooking it back to his pack. “ Kid, how’s the herd lookin’?” He joined the kid up on the high rocks to get a look over the four hundred heads, thinned and spread wide across the open plain, “ They went wandering… We’ll have to spend extra time rounding them all up.”

 The crew packed the remains of their camp, readied on their horses with their hats on and rode off beneath the black clouds to urge the cattle toward the chores earth where only nightmares awaited them.

The air was humid, the ground still hot beneath the red dirt and the clouds threatening to rain down ash water above them. 

The day was spent pushing the cattle through soot and a crumbling blanket of black earth that kicked up with the dust, stained their clothes and leaving them with no markers but the blur of moving bodies and true charred skeletons of gum trees a breath away from collapsing into the mud. 

Progress felt slow with little way of knowing how far into the smoke they’d travelled without Irish’s direction, Blue still felt they’d been going in circles for days.

“ How far do you think this stretch goes, Irish?” Blue said, but the man just laughed. “ We’re gonna hit the worst tomorrow. Gonna get better soon with the rain but it’s gonna be blacker than black when we get further.”

Blue leaned forward on his saddle and scratched at the thick coat of sweat and grime clinging to him like a new skin, “ Why’d it be harder? Shouldn’t it open up?”, “ This is where they started burning. Far over there is where they stopped. Gonna be hard on the cows”.

The boss’s whistle echoed through the smoke and his booming voice followed, signaling an open area with water and the soft break from the blinding smoke. “ We set up camp here. The cattle can drink and we can head on in the morning. We won’t be able to see anything at night.”

The camp was quick, all too eager to cool off in the billabong and too exhausted to do more than eat their meals and crawl into their swags. The kid stuck close again with Blue that night and all seven dogs returned to their sleeping pile of limbs and blankets of fur warming one another in the cold night. 

The boss drank down his dinner and sat by the fire with no signs of turning in. No stars were visible overhead and the fire felt like a blur behind the smoky air. 

As much as he wanted to sleep it wasn’t until the rain began to pour and filled the empty earth with noise that he felt he could sleep without the dream that had haunted him the previous night.

The rain crashed down on them with full force, the dogs barked at the sky and the cattle joined the cacophony of sound that seemed to keep everyone awake but the drunken boss. “ Blue, when do you think the rain is gonna stop? Dad says rai—“ lightning cracked and the kid jumped, terrified and shaking Blue with his eyes on the black beyond the fire.

“ Blue, I saw something out there, out with the cattle”. Thunder rumbled and Blue rubs his face as if he could wipe the exhaustion away, “ You sure it wasn’t a cow?” He said but he isn’t sure he believes it, everything about this drive had felt wrong. Each choice they’d made felt wrong and each warning ignored.

The dogs howled and the cattle cried under the heavy rain and Blue finally got to his feet. He never thought he was much of a brave one but the kid was scared and he wasn’t going to get a lick of sleep. 

“ I’ll have a look.” He pulled on his boots, took one of the lanterns from the burned out fire and headed off toward the animals barely visible in the darkness. Irish and a few of the dogs were already standing off by the edge of the camp, wary just like him. 

“ The lightning has the cattle spooked, the kid thinks something else is out there causing it.” He says, eyes still on the smoke. “ Last night… Me and the kid heard something. Didn’t see anything but it’s the kids first time on a drive.” He says and at the next strike of lightning Irish points out the quick flash of a distant figure.

“ I think it’s ghosts.”

“ …Ghosts?”

“ Little Men. Tall spirits my grandfather told me cause mischief to keep us from playing in the bush. They cause trouble but we can shoo them away.”

Blue swats at the insects drawn to the dim source of light in the smoke but all Blue can do now is stare out for any signs of this creature. 

“ How… How do we get rid of Little Men? So the kid can sleep?” He cautiously says but Irish just laughs and pats his shoulder, “ White fellas believe every story they hear don’t they? We don’t go out there. This is why kids like you get lost in the bush, Blue.”

Blue frowns and follows Irish back towards what’s left of the fire but for the kid’s sake he leaves the lantern between their swags. 

He isn’t drenched but the night air makes him shiver even under the warmth of the blankets. “ There’s nothing out there worth worrying about. Get some sleep.”, “ I know it’s a funny thing to worry about but dad says even a snapped twig makes me jump like a girl. I couldn’t ask him to go check because I was scared. Thanks, Blue”.

“ I got on my first drive when I was real young, pissed my pants the first night because I thought one of the dogs was a dingo.”

My great grandfather felt the need to follow the boss when the path they were driving changed, he kept out of the brewing tension between Irish and the boss and what he said his biggest regret was— he never told the kid he believed something was out there.

None in the crew woke up feeling rested, they felt sapped of their energy and sick from the layer of dust clinging to their throats. Blue washed his face and shaved by the billabong, he brewed the coffee with the billycan and went out with a few of the dogs to check on the cattle. The air was thick with smoke but the rain had washed the worst of it away, leaving the blood soaked sand visible through the scattered herd.

Blue stepped over hooves and mangled limbs, meat torn free and the hollow husks of large bodies like a road of blood and bones leading to Irish and the other dogs. “ Christ, what happened to the cattle?” Blue stressed, eyes down on the charred skulls of the cows half buried beneath the ash. “ …How… How many heads?” Blue covers his mouth less to avoid the foul smell but to keep the coffee from coming up his throat. “ Twenty, thirty? Half the big horns.”

“ What the damn hell happened to my cattle!” The boss shouts, raging like a bull with only Irish in his sights. A punch is thrown before there’s any chance of talking, another swung that Irish catches and only releases when the boss relents. 

“ You did this so we’d turn back, ay? Savaged half my bloody cows!” The boss spits and Irish holds his hands to take a step back, hoping it’ll calm the man before he does anything worse than a punch. “ If I was gonna go I would’a gone and left you white fellas in the smoke. I got money needs same as you, boss man.”

“ Boss, he couldn’t have done something like this on his own. No dogs or dingos could’ve either.” Blue says but the tension is thick as the smoke. “ We don’t need anyone dying when we already lost this many.” He adds, looking at the festering pile of torn heads and swarming flies. “ We should round up the rest and get moving, boss”.

“ This was those black fellas who burned the place off. Your type did this, Irish.” The boss shoves past the man, soothing his anger with his hip flask and shouting out a stream of swears once he’s just another blur in the dust and smoke.

“ We should’ve gone Uluṟu way? Boss man and his son aren’t built for driving. They don’t listen to warnings, ay?” Irish rubs his jaw and whistles for the dogs to follow on his way back to camp.

The crew packed camp and the three discussed the situation, the boss had no plans on backing down. Blue was able to talk the stubborn older man into taking a path around the gore to keep the horses from spooking. Truthfully he just didn’t want the kid to see the mess. 

The dogs barked and howled, the cattle cried out and soon the herd was heading deeper into the black. Moving was slow with so little sight and it was only with bandanas tied tight around their faces that they could breathe more than ash. 

They stopped infrequently by stagnant pools of red mud and black water to keep the cows hydrated but few would touch it. If not for the distant whistling cutting through the fog of ash, Blue isn’t sure he’d have known any of the crew were even there at all.

By eleven hours the ever present sounds of a moving herd was broken by a guttural wail torn from one of the cows that ended just as quickly in weak gurgles and a trail of blood dripping like sprinkled breadcrumbs barely seen against the red ground by the horse’s hooves. “ Christ,” Blue slowed to the gory sight of another cow crying, throat torn. He whistled out into the black and got down to check on the large beast, the animal was dying but once he’d taken down his rifle he offered what bit of comfort he could with a soothing hand. “ Hold on, mate. It’s alright. I got you, it’s alright.”

The sound of a gunshot echoed across the blind desert as far as the distant canyons with another following from down the stretch. Another cow cries out where he can’t see, another from behind him where none of the cattle should be. 

“ Boss! Irish!” He shouts out once he’s on his horse, heading toward the gunshots with scuffed footprints lining his way to another down beast. A ring of red dirt surrounds the fallen cow where something has broken the thick layer of scorched grass and black snow. The blood and footprints lead to the next beast and sporadic gunshots but neither beast had been shot. “ Boss! Irish!”

A sharp crack whips past his ear with a deafening noise and his horse jumps, she bucks when a piercing pain clips Blue’s calf and goes straight through the saddle, “ Jesus! Hold on, girl!” 

He hits the ground hard and the frantic brumby bolts into the dust, leaving him coughing in the ash and listening out for the source of the gunshots and the incomprehensible shouting in the distance. “ Boss! Irish!” He forces himself up and follows the only thing he can, the trail towards the next slain beast and whatever monstrous thing in the black that is chasing them down.

A loud cry hits him from one of the horses and he knows it’s close but everything else, the dogs barking, the cattle crying, it’s all so far away it could be across the whole sea. “ I’m coming girl, it’s alright. I’m—“ Blue stills as he sees the kid’s brumby lying dead and twitching with the kid’s scattered pack, “ Christ.” He swipes up the kids rifle, checks to see if it’s been shot and begins to chase the large trail cutting through the ground and ending by stones. 

The injured kid he finds half dead and trying with weak limbs to tie his belt around his broken leg. “ Kid, where are the others?” He kneels down and moves their hands, he has no idea if the kid can even answer him in this state but he’s broken enough bones falling off horses he’s had to learn how to patch up what he can and this kid surely can’t. “ Come on, talk to me kid”.

“ D-Dad s…saw s-something knock down one of the c-cows. He said Irish done it. They got yelling and dad pulled out his rifle. He said the… the black fellas done it. I didn’t— Blue, I didn’t see a person. Irish told me to run so I ran, he said he was gonna calm down my dad… h-h-he…” the kid goes quiet, coughing and sputtering until Blue gives him a drink from the canteen, “ Go slow kid. I don’t want us staying here too long if something is out there… But Irish and Little Dog are deep in the smoke”. 

The kid a breath he can and nods, he’s drenched in sweat and soot, streaks running down the dirt on his cheeks and eyes a blistering red from the tears. “ Something went past the h-horse and it fell o-on m-my leg… It kept on in dad and Irish’s way. I s-saw the grass that wasn’t dead a-and the rocks, Irish said it’s a g-good sign… good sign a…”.

My grandfather never told us what had happened next, he just said he heard a twig snap by his good ear and everything in his body told him it was the beast that lurked beyond the swags that first night down the new path, he swung back with the rifle and took a shot into the nothingness when something brushed past his back. 

The kid’s laboured breaths became rasping gurgles and choking, the thrashing and the fear of a boy suffocating through a torn throat with barely enough to hold his head up. Blue didn’t want to turn around, he knew. He knew what killed the cattle had just ripped through the kid’s neck and left him bleeding out.

He could never bring himself to say what he did but with a gunshot from the kid’s rifle my grandfather picked up the pack and continued toward the billabong and the one beast he knew had been sending the blind stretch into terror with a rifle and a drink in his hand.

The dogs barked through the smoke and a whistle for Little Dog helped him reach the waters edge and his injured horse by dusk.

“ Boss! Ir—“ Blue shuts himself up as the smoke clears not to stagnant black water but to the boss with his rifle head against Irish’s back like an executioner. Irish’s blood muddy boots stand at the precipice of the billabong, the dogs growl beneath red dirt with none approaching and half injured or down, all black with soot like devil dogs baring their teeth. 

“ Boss! Irish isn’t doing this! No human can do all this, even a tribe!” Blue forces himself to remain calm and keep his rifle lowered, finger off the trigger but hands shaking like a leaf. 

“ There’s something—-“, the boss snaps with a wide eyed scowl full of fury, paranoia and fear, “ It’s dead ground, Blue! It has to be these savages! He lured us into a trap! They— They’re trying to steal my cattle!” The boss steadies his rifle again and prepares to shoot “ If we kill one of them the others will come out! We sick the dogs on them and gun them down! We’re nearly through the smoke. We take out the savages and we’re nearly through!”.

“ No one tryin’ get you, bossman. You just shooting like a madman at smoke.” Irish says, loud but calm in the face of what’s about to be his end. “ The cattle already scattered and you killed one of the horses. Best we gonna do is ride out of here fast as we can”. 

Blue can see the belt wrapped around Irish’s arms and he knows if he doesn’t get to the man then the madman ready to pull the trigger is going to shoot them before whatever beast is out there has the chance to do what it did to the kid.

A sharp wind brushes past Blue’s back, running somewhere unseen in the black dust blocking out the desert behind them. A twig snaps and the paranoid boss feels it too, swinging his rifle wildly in search, “ There! You see that, Blue? It’s one of those black fellas trying to take my cattle!”. 

Blue grips his gun and takes a silent, cautious step toward the water and Irish, “ It isn’t people out there, it’s something else. We made something mad, something old”.

A twig snaps and another of the cows cries out in the smoke, hitting the red sands with a wet thud before the impossible beast’s footsteps echo behind the crew once more. A twig snaps left and the boss shifts his rifle in panic, a wailing cry cut off in a gurgling slash to the right. 

“ They’re everywhere! Th— Blue, they’re all over the place!” The boss points his gun toward Blue, finger shaking violently against the trigger. “ Boss, calm down! We need to find the horses!” Blue shouts and the boss’s gun swings toward another sound.

Blue keeps his gun trained on the man lost in his own insanity, he’s nearly by Irish when the gun fixes on him. Irish makes his move, slamming his shoulder into the boss and driving him backwards until they both hit the red dirt in a plume of dust and ash. 

A deafening shot rings out but Blue can’t see more than a flash of light and something drop. 

A sharp wind kicks up a thick cloud sends the air into darkness with a cold pain ripping across Blue’s back and the panicked scream of the boss echoing across the silent stretch shrouded in shadows and full of carnage. “ What are you!! What the fuck are y—“ a slash, a choking gurgle of blood and he knows the sound he hears in the black is the boss hitting ground.

“ Irish… Christ, Irish…?” Blue crawls despite the agony, dragging himself through the ash until he finds his wounded friend. The dogs howl and whimper, Little Dog licks the sweat from Blue’s cheek as he reaches out to grab the body half in the black pool of rainwater. He stretches out and the ribbons of cut skin on his back opens to the fist beneath his tattered shirt but he reaches the belt tying Irish’s arms and drags them onto the banks.

 “ Christ, Irish, come on… You can’t be— You can’t be dead too.” Blue fumbles to remove the belt and pulls the man over onto their back but Irish is gone. A bull crack right in the skull. There’s only red dirt and the bloody banks of the billabong, the only life in an endless sea of black surrounding them.

Little Dog licks Irish’s hand and gets the attention of her companion ready to accept his face. He draws his eyes to her and the remaining hounds before following her gaze down to the red mud washing away from Irish’s hands. 

Red dirt stains Irish’s skin as if he’d been covered in it before his body hit the billabong and the dogs. Everything had been blackened by the ash and dust, the coals spread across the burned ground and soot coating their clothes.

My grandfather didn’t know how or why it worked, he’d assumed when he and the kid had slept it was the light of the fire but it wasn’t, it was the red covering their body after the days of travelling through the Red Sea. 

He coated his body in the muddy red sands, he painted the dogs fur and he covered the surviving horses as quick as he could, praying it worked.

He stopped only to help the dying beasts he’d come across and lead the horses and dogs for the night through the ash, half dead by the time he reached its end and the world became a red sea and canyon waves of bloody rocks.

Vultures circled and the sun blistered the little skin he had left on his back but he had made it.

Despite the boss’s hate, his collapse at the tribe’s feet was met with kindness and a guide to town once he could ride. He said he’d returned home and married Daphne soon after and in his years since he never stopped his searches for the wandering owners of the land who’d saved him just as Irish had in the dead ground.

He never joined another drive but he could never give up travelling the outback with his bride and that despite her thinking his stories were silly superstitions he’d sometimes see the roaming cattle and see the lights of a drover’s campfire resting at the billabong’s.

I tell you this story not just because of the dangers of the beautifully brutal red oceans or a warning to those who wish to cross it and think of ignoring a bad omen but for you to remember the drovers who perished on the rivers and canyons, gone but not forgotten, and that small crew of drovers who were swallowed by the sea.
