It was a few years ago now—exactly two months—when I took a job at a Burger King in Buenos Aires to help pay for my studies. I had just moved to the capital from the countryside. An uncle let me stay with him for a while until I landed the position and moved in with a coworker from the same restaurant. We became close friends pretty quickly.

The pay was terrible, but it was enough to cover tuition and the occasional small treat. What I hated most about the job was having to kick out the homeless people who showed up during the late-night shift. They bothered both the staff and the customers. Even though the place did decent business, management never wanted to hire security. They said it wasn’t necessary—if things ever got out of hand, we could just call the police.

That changed one night around three in the morning. Only two customers were in the restaurant. A homeless man walked in, went straight up to a heavyset regular who always came in at that hour, and asked for a burger or some money. When the guy said no, the man spat in his drink. A fight broke out. It only ended when the police and an ambulance arrived.

After that, they hired overnight security.

That was the first time I saw him. Mr. Leonel.

He was in his mid-forties. His head had this square, stretched shape, and his face was lined with deep wrinkles that shifted with every expression. But it was his eyes that really got to me. They were so sunken it looked like they might disappear into his skull at any moment. Only the heavy bags underneath seemed to be holding them in place.

He held out his hand and said quietly,

“Hey… you know where I can change?”

His handshake was firm. I pointed toward the employee room and he walked off without another word. Karen, the manager on duty, came over and admitted he’d given her an immediate bad feeling. The same kind of darkness I’d felt.

When he came back down in uniform, he looked even more defeated. He wore a wrinkled white shirt with the security company’s logo and a pair of dirty blue pants. He clocked in and took his place near the entrance, ready to make sure no more homeless people caused trouble.

Just looking at him stirred up this strange mix of laziness and irritation in me. That night I was on cleaning duty. A customer had spilled soda near where he was standing, so I had to go mop it up. I could feel his eyes on my back the whole time. His hands were clasped behind him, like he was waiting for something. I started mopping faster out of nerves and dropped the mop right at his feet.

He bent down with difficulty. His stomach hung so heavy it looked like one hard shove might make everything spill out. He handed me the mop and smiled. I saw his teeth—rotted and full of cavities. He was wearing small earbuds, and from them came a sweet, almost painfully loud melody: *Livin’ in the Sunlight, Lovin’ in the Moonlight*.

He put the earbud back in and stared off into nothing with that dead expression that unsettled me so much. I gestured for him to take it out. I wanted to talk to him. Something in me felt bad for judging him so quickly just because of how he looked.

Before long we were having a real conversation. He was smart and well-spoken, but every word carried this deep sadness underneath. Days went by and our friendship grew. I ended up telling him personal things—about my father’s death, a breakup, how much I hated the job. The others gave me a hard time about it. They said he was disgusting, that he probably watched messed-up videos or even ate people. Just stupid stuff. They judged him only by his appearance.

One day while I was cleaning the sidewalk outside, I heard the same song again at that ridiculous volume. I asked him how he could stand it without complaining. He gave me a gentle smile, despite the teeth.

“Honestly, I don’t even know. I just know I like it. It eases the pain—the kind of pain I’ve carried deep inside for years. I’m really grateful we talk, that you keep me company on these long shifts. Loneliness is the worst wound a man can feel. My life wasn’t easy. I grew up with abusive parents, got bullied from the time I was a kid because of how I looked, and loneliness never left me. I did find love once. She saw past this body. We had two kids—the most beautiful little angels anyone could ask for. A car accident took all three of them. Ever since then, I’ve only listened to this song. It was my kids’ favorite. The volume is never high enough to make it feel like they’re still with me. I’m telling you this because you’re the only person who’s actually come close and wanted to talk to me for real.”

I didn’t know what to say. I just felt this enormous sadness for everything he’d been through. Behind us stood Carlos, my coworker. He’d heard the whole conversation. He made a face of pure disgust, turned around, and walked away without a word.

Leonel asked me not to tell anyone. I didn’t.

A few days later I walked into work and heard everyone whispering about him. They were saying he’d killed his family, that he’d been in prison, that he was a pervert who stared at customers and drooled. I knew it was Carlos. He’d twisted everything he’d overheard.

They asked me what I thought and why I still talked to him. I got scared. Scared they’d start making things up about me too. Scared of being left alone. All I said was,

“Yeah… maybe he is a little weird.”

That was it. I went to the changing room with tears in my eyes and a heavy guilt in my chest. I just hoped Leonel would never find out what people were saying.

The days passed. He didn’t show up Monday. Not Tuesday either. Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday—nothing. On Sunday I saw him walk by like a shadow. He didn’t say hello. He didn’t speak. Just the song blasting from his earbuds while he moved his head in small, shaky motions and whispered, “We’ll see each other soon… we’ll see each other soon…”

He had a rough beard, food stuck to his face, and a nauseating smell, like he hadn’t bathed all week. I walked over, wanting to apologize. He looked at me, gave me that same gentle smile, and nodded slightly.

“Everything’s going to be okay. I’ll be right back—just going to the bathroom.”

He went into the customer restroom on the lower floor. Ten minutes passed. Then twenty. At twenty-five the manager started asking why he was taking so long—there was a homeless guy outside and they needed security. At forty-five minutes we were banging on the door in a panic. After an hour the police were forcing the lock.

When they finally got it open, we saw Leonel’s body on the floor. He was pale. His wrists were destroyed. He hadn’t found anything to cut with, so he’d used his teeth. He’d bitten down until he tore through flesh and tissue and reached the veins. On his face was a wide smile. And from the earbuds, still playing at full volume, came Tiny Tim’s voice singing *Livin’ in the Sunlight, Lovin’ in the Moonlight*.

I never forgave myself for staying silent that day. No matter how he looked on the outside, all he wanted was to feel less alone. His appearance was stronger than any words for the people around him.
