# I’ve been delivering mail for eleven years.

# In that time, I’ve been bitten by six dogs, threatened with a shotgun twice, and found a dead man sitting on his porch in the middle of July.

# But nothing has ever scared me as much as the little white house at the end of Briarwood Lane.

# And until last Thursday, I thought I understood what was wrong with it.

# I was wrong.

# I work for the postal service in a small town in North Carolina. I’m not going to say exactly where because, frankly, I don’t want anyone looking for this place.

# My route is mostly rural. Long driveways, old farmhouses, trailers tucked behind trees. The kind of places where people wave when you drive by.

# Briarwood Lane is different.

# It’s a narrow road surrounded by thick woods. There are seven houses along it.

# At least, there are supposed to be seven.

# The eighth house sits at the very end.

# A small, white, single-story home with peeling paint, black shutters, and a rusted mailbox.

# 184 Briarwood Lane.

# I first noticed something strange about it three years ago.

# Every Tuesday, without fail, there would be a single envelope in my sorting tray addressed to that house.

# No return address.

# No postage.

# Just the address written in shaky black handwriting.

# And every Tuesday, I delivered it.

I never questioned it. Mail gets weird sometimes.  
Then one Tuesday, I noticed something written on the back.  
**PLEASE DON’T KNOCK. HE THINKS YOU’RE HIS SON.**  
I laughed when I saw it.  
Some kind of prank, I figured.  
I put the letter in the mailbox and drove away.  
The following Tuesday, another envelope arrived.  
Same handwriting.  
Same address.  
This time, the back said:  
**HE SAW YOU LAST WEEK.**  
I stopped laughing.  
I looked toward the house.  
Someone was standing behind the living room window.  
An old man.  
At least, I assumed he was old.  
His face was pressed so tightly against the glass that his nose looked flattened. His mouth hung open, and his eyes were fixed on my truck.  
He wasn’t blinking.  
I raised my hand.  
He raised his.  
But something about the movement was wrong.  
His arm moved several seconds after mine.  
Like a delayed reflection.  
I drove away.  
For the next few months, the letters kept coming.  
Every Tuesday.  
And every week, the message changed.  
**HE’S GETTING BETTER AT REMEMBERING YOUR FACE.**  
**DON’T LET HIM HEAR YOUR VOICE.**  
**HE KNOWS WHAT TIME YOU ARRIVE.**  
Then one week:  
**HE’S BEEN PRACTICING YOUR WALK.**  
That was the first time I considered telling my supervisor.  
But what was I supposed to say?  
Someone was sending creepy letters to an old house?  
Instead, I started delivering the mail as quickly as possible.  
I wouldn’t look at the windows.  
I wouldn’t look at the front door.  
I just opened the mailbox, shoved the envelope inside, and left.  
Until last Thursday.  
It was raining hard.  
The kind of rain that makes the woods look black even in the afternoon.  
I was running behind schedule, and when I reached Briarwood Lane, I noticed the road was completely empty.  
No cars.  
No birds.  
Not even the usual barking dogs.  
When I reached 184, the mailbox was already open.  
Inside was a letter.  
That wasn’t possible.  
I hadn’t delivered anything yet.  
I picked it up.  
It was addressed to me.  
Not just my first name.  
My full name.  
And underneath it:  
**MAIL CARRIER — ROUTE 17.**  
My hands started shaking.  
I opened it.  
Inside was a single piece of yellowed paper.  
It read:  
*You have delivered 156 letters.*  
*He has eaten 155.*  
*The last one was for you.*  
I stared at those words for what felt like a full minute.  
Then I heard knocking.  
Three slow knocks.  
From inside my mail truck.  
I was standing outside it.  
The driver’s door was open, and the cab was empty.  
At least, it had been.  
Knock.  
Knock.  
Knock.  
The sound came again.  
This time from behind the driver’s seat.  
I leaned forward.  
There was something moving in the dark space behind my seat.  
Something pale.  
Something folded into a space far too small for a person.  
Then I heard a voice.  
My voice.  
“Don’t look at me yet.”  
I stumbled backward.  
The thing behind the seat began making a wet, cracking sound.  
Like someone bending their fingers backward.  
Then it spoke again.  
“He’s almost finished.”  
I ran.  
I didn’t think about the mail. Didn’t think about my truck.  
I just ran down Briarwood Lane until my lungs burned.  
Behind me, I heard the truck door slam.  
Then the engine started.  
I looked over my shoulder.  
My mail truck was following me.  
Slowly.  
The headlights were off.  
Someone was driving.  
I could see their silhouette through the windshield.  
They were wearing my uniform.  
My hat.  
And they were waving.  
Not like a person waves.  
Their arm was moving back and forth mechanically, the elbow bending in the wrong direction.  
I ran into the woods.  
I stayed there until dark.  
Eventually, I heard the truck drive away.  
When I finally made it back to the main road, I called the police.  
I told them someone had stolen my mail truck.  
I didn’t mention the house.  
I didn’t mention the letters.  
I definitely didn’t mention the thing wearing my clothes.  
The police found the truck parked outside the post office that evening.  
The keys were in the ignition.  
The mail had been delivered.  
Every single piece.  
According to my supervisor, I had returned to the office at 4:37 p.m., handed in my scanner, and clocked out.  
He even showed me the security footage.  
I watched myself walk through the back entrance.  
I watched myself hang up my keys.  
I watched myself smile at the camera.  
And then I watched myself turn my head almost completely backward.  
Not my body.  
Just my head.  
Until I was looking directly into the camera over my own shoulder.  
The footage cut out.  
My supervisor said it was a camera glitch.  
I told him I needed some time off.  
He agreed.  
That night, I searched for 184 Briarwood Lane online.  
Nothing.  
No property records.  
No tax history.  
No satellite images showing a house at the end of the road.  
I checked an old county map.  
Briarwood Lane ended at number 182.  
There had never been a number 184.  
I called a retired mail carrier who used to work my route.  
His name was Frank.  
He’d been delivering mail in the county for thirty-two years before retiring.  
When I mentioned Briarwood Lane, he went quiet.  
Then he asked me something I’ll never forget.  
“Did it start sending letters yet?”  
I didn’t answer.  
He sighed.  
“Listen carefully. Whatever you do, don’t go back there.”  
I asked him what lived in that house.  
He started crying.  
Not quietly.  
He was sobbing so hard I could barely understand him.  
Finally, he said:  
“It doesn’t live in the house.”  
“What?”  
“The house is its mouth.”  
Then the phone went dead.  
I tried calling him back.  
No answer.  
I searched his name online.  
Frank had died in 2019.  
Seven years ago.  
I know what you’re thinking.  
Someone was messing with me.  
A prank.  
A hallucination.  
Anything.  
I wanted to believe that too.  
Until yesterday morning.  
I woke up to three slow knocks on my bedroom door.  
I live alone.  
I sat up in bed.  
The knocking came again.  
Knock.  
Knock.  
Knock.  
Then a voice from the hallway.  
My voice.  
“Time to get up. We have mail to deliver.”  
I didn’t move.  
The doorknob turned.  
Slowly.  
Then stopped.  
For several minutes, there was silence.  
Eventually, I heard footsteps moving away.  
I waited until sunrise before opening the door.  
There was an envelope on the floor.  
No postage.  
No return address.  
Just my name.  
Inside was a photograph.  
It showed me sleeping in my bed.  
The photograph had been taken from the ceiling directly above me.  
On the back, someone had written:  
**HE DOESN’T NEED THE HOUSE ANYMORE.**  
I packed a bag and drove to a motel two towns over.  
That’s where I’m writing this.  
I haven’t slept.  
About twenty minutes ago, someone knocked on my motel room door.  
Three slow knocks.  
I looked through the peephole.  
A mailman was standing outside.  
He had his back to me.  
His uniform was soaked.  
Water dripped from his sleeves onto the carpet.  
Then he spoke.  
“Package for you.”  
It was my voice again.  
I backed away.  
He knocked three more times.  
“Sir, I need a signature.”  
I didn’t answer.  
He stood there for almost ten minutes.  
Then I heard him laughing.  
Softly at first.  
Then harder.  
Until the laughter turned into choking, wet gasps.  
Finally, he walked away.  
I waited another fifteen minutes before checking the hallway.  
There was a cardboard box sitting outside my door.  
It was small.  
About the size of a shoebox.  
The label had my name and room number.  
Underneath, in red ink, someone had written:  
**RETURN TO SENDER.**  
I should have left it alone.  
I know that now.  
But I opened it.  
Inside was a postal service uniform.  
My uniform.  
Covered in dried blood.  
Underneath it was a plastic bag containing eleven human teeth.  
And a handwritten note.  
*You’ve been very good at delivering things.*  
*Now it’s your turn to be delivered.*  
I dropped the box.  
Then I noticed something else.  
At the bottom was a photograph.  
It showed the little white house at 184 Briarwood Lane.  
Except the house wasn’t white anymore.  
The siding was dark red.  
The windows were boarded shut.  
And the front door was wide open.  
Standing in the doorway were dozens of people.  
All wearing postal uniforms.  
Some were missing eyes.  
Some had mouths stretched impossibly wide.  
Others looked almost normal.  
But every single one of them had my face.  
On the back of the photograph was one final message.  
**WE’VE BEEN TRYING TO MAKE ONE THAT LASTS.**  
**YOU’RE THE FIRST ONE WHO MADE IT ELEVEN YEARS.**  
I don’t know how long I’ve been sitting here.  
About five minutes ago, I heard a mail truck pull into the motel parking lot.  
Then another.  
Then another.  
I looked through the curtains.  
Eight trucks.  
Eight men standing beside them.  
Every one of them looked exactly like me.  
They weren’t looking at my window.  
They were looking at the motel room next door.  
Room 184.  
I hadn’t noticed the number until now.  
One of them walked up to the door and knocked three times.  
A moment later, someone answered.  
I heard an old man’s voice.  
“Is he ready?”  
“Almost,” the mailman replied.  
Then the old man said something that made my stomach drop.  
“Good. The last one lasted eleven years. I don’t want another disappointment.”  
I backed away from the window.  
That’s when I noticed the envelope lying on my bed.  
It hadn’t been there before.  
My name was written across the front.  
Underneath it were two words.  
**FINAL NOTICE.**  
I opened it.  
There was a single photograph inside.  
It showed me sitting on this motel bed, holding this exact envelope.  
Behind me stood an old man.  
His face was pressed against the back of my head.  
His mouth was open.  
Wide enough to fit my entire skull.  
I dropped the photograph.  
Then I felt something warm drip onto my shoulder.  
I haven’t turned around.  
I can hear him breathing.  
And every time I stop typing, he whispers the same thing.  
“Keep going.”  
I asked him why.  
He leaned closer.  
His lips brushed my ear.  
“Because the next one needs to know what happened to you.”  
I don’t understand what he means.  
But there’s something wrong with my screen.  
The words I’m typing keep disappearing.  
And underneath them, new words are appearing on their own.  
The same sentence.  
Over and over.  
**YOUR ROUTE HAS BEEN ASSIGNED.**  
**YOUR ROUTE HAS BEEN ASSIGNED.**  
**YOUR ROUTE HAS BEEN ASSIGNED.**  
I just heard a mail truck start outside.  
The old man has stopped breathing.  
And now I can hear someone in the parking lot calling my name.  
It’s my voice.  
But younger.  
Confused.  
Asking where he is.  
Asking why he’s wearing a postal uniform.  
**Asking why there’s a handwritten note on his dashboard.**
