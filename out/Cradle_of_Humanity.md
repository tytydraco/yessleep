My name is Nikolai. I am an archaeologist by trade, fifty-six years old, and a man who has long outlived his own convictions. I have a deep, jagged scar running across my jaw, a permanent reminder of a life I tried to leave behind. I force myself through rigid physical conditioning every single day, keeping my muscles hard and ready, but it feels increasingly pointless. I feel the creeping age settling deep in my very bones. Every morning, the dry air makes it just a little harder to breathe. It is getting impossible to pretend the decay has not already taken hold of me.

 

I was born in The Soviet Union. When the Union collapsed, I stayed just long enough to watch the scavengers arrive. I saw foreigners flood over our borders to carve up the motherland. They took our natural resources for pennies, but worse, they carved up our people.



For every man who drank himself to death in those bleak years, there were many more young girls lured to horrid fates in foreign ‘beauty pageants’, an easy way to repackage trafficking to the innocent such that they would come of their own volition, like moths to the flame.

 

Watching that brutal, desperate scramble destroyed every ideological pillar I ever possessed. It killed my religious convictions right alongside them. Before the fall, I was a deeply devout Orthodox Christian. The horrific violence of those days stripped the faith entirely out of me. God abandoned us to the wolves, and I abandoned Him in return.

 

I fled south to Turkey, choosing to bury myself in antiquity because the dead demand absolutely nothing of you. By two thousand and two, I was granted Turkish citizenship and a place at the university.

 

I work alongside a new generation now, still in archaeology. My current team consists of four younger colleagues, true-born Turkish men and women who possess an exhausting, untainted optimism I lost decades ago.

 

Our latest venture started on a bus in Iraq. We were riding out of Nasiriyah in a cramped, rattling transport, pushing away from the south and heading deeper into south-central Iraq. The past few weeks in the city had been gentle, not exactly unexpected, but surprising perhaps.

 

But as we drove further north into the arid wastes, leaving that warmth behind, the atmosphere inside the cabin shifted. My younger colleagues sat a few rows ahead, laughing and reviewing our survey maps, completely oblivious to the isolation swallowing us. Our destination was a newly uncovered cluster of ancient Christian ruins found north of the city.

 

The ruins we were seeking were an academic impossibility. Preliminary dating of the masonry and the few recovered artifacts placed the site in the very early first century. If the data held true, it drastically changed the entire accepted historical narrative of how Christianity spread.

 

It meant early disciples, or perhaps some forgotten, older offshoot of the faith, had pushed deep into the cradle of Mesopotamia decades before anyone previously thought possible. For a man who had entirely lost his faith, it was a bitter irony to be tasked with unearthing its hidden origins.

 

 

The bus groaned to a halt just outside a small, dust-choked town near Batha. This was our stop for the night.

 

Nasiriyah had been full of life and hospitality, but this place felt somewhat more hollowed out. The few locals who watched us unpack our gear from the undercarriage did not smile or approach us. They stared with saddened eyes, whispering among themselves in hushed Arabic.

 

 

My younger colleagues did not notice the shift in the atmosphere. Emre and Kaan were too busy arguing over who had to carry the heavy surveying equipment, while Zeynep and Elif took photographs of the dying light hitting the low mudbrick buildings.

 

We secured rooms in a small concrete guesthouse for the night. It was a dreary place to rest. Yet, it was absolute luxury compared to what I had been through, thirty odd years ago, in Moscow. Tomorrow, we would be sent drivers and cars, courtesy of the Iraqi Archaeological Society, to take us to our final staging area.

 

Our actual base camp was to be near an abandoned village perched right on the edge of the excavation zone.

 

I left my cramped room and walked through the dusty streets with Kaan. The setting sun bathed the town in a harsh, bleeding orange light. As we navigated the narrow dirt alleys, the truth of this place finally settled over me. The residents were not hostile at all. They were just as hospitable as the people in Nasiriyah. But their warmth carried a heavy, unsettling weight.

 

"They are looking at us strangely, Nikolai," Kaan muttered, adjusting his glasses and keeping his voice low. "Do you think they resent us being here?"

 

"Look closer, Kaan," I replied. "That is not anger in their eyes. It is pity. They look at us the way a priest looks at a dying man."

 

Before Kaan could process that thought, a voice called out to us. Near the edge of the town square, a local man waved his weathered hands. He was old. His face was carved by the desert sun beyond my own fifty-six years, lined with deep, dark creases. He was tending a small open fire, and the rich, smoky smell of roasting fish filled the evening air. He was preparing Iraqi carp, their national dish.

 

"Welcome, welcome!" the old man called out in thick, broken English, gesturing frantically to the woven rugs around his fire. "You are the scholars, yes? Sit. Eat."

 

"Thank you, uncle," Kaan said, his natural enthusiasm returning instantly. He practically dropped onto the rug. "Is this masgouf? It smells incredible."

 

The old man nodded slowly, turning the many spiked fish over the glowing coals. "Yes. Masgouf. You eat well tonight." He paused and looked up at me. His dark eyes were filled with that exact same sorrow I had seen in the alleys. "Tomorrow, you go to the old stones in the north. The abandoned place."

 

"That is the plan," I said, offering a polite nod. "We are here to study them."

 

The old man shook his head, keeping his eyes fixed on the fire. "Some stone should stay buried, Russian man. The earth eat them for good. But we will not speak of dark in night. Eat. Drink tea. You are my guests."

 

I thanked him again and told Kaan I needed to stretch my aching knees. The boy was already seated, entirely unbothered by the old man's warning, and instead helping him cook.

 

I stepped back from the fire and let my eyes wander. Nestled between two crumbling stone homes was a building that made me stop in my tracks. It was a church. Judging by the stark concrete architecture, it was built no earlier than the nineteen fifties. Finding a relatively modern Christian church sitting out in the open in this remote stretch of the desert was entirely unexpected.

 

A low chuckle escaped my throat. The sound was dry and devoid of real humour.

 

"What is it?" Kaan asked, looking up as he fanned the flames.

 

"Irony," I said, pointing down the alley. "We travel thousands of miles and plan to dig through tons of hostile earth to find ancient Christian ruins, and there is a parish sitting right next to our hotel."

 

Kaan laughed and went back to his task.

 

A strange compulsion took hold of me. For a man who had abandoned his faith in the gutters of Moscow, the sight of that cross should have meant absolutely nothing. Yet the heavy shadow of the building pulled at me. I walked away from the warmth of the fire, away from the laughter of my colleague, and approached the heavy wooden doors of the sanctuary. They were unlocked. I pushed them open with a heavy groan and stepped into the dark.

 

 

The air inside was stale and heavy with the scent of old dust and melting wax. The wooden doors slammed shut behind me with a resounding thud, cutting off the sounds of the town outside. My eyes took a moment to adjust to the dim light filtering through the grime coated windows, aided meekly by a bunch of weak incandescent bulbs. The sanctuary was small and entirely empty save for a few wooden benches near the back walls.

 

Two figures stood near the front of the nave.

 

One was a tall Caucasian man, looking to be in his early thirties. He possessed an unnatural level of physical fitness, radiating an aura that made my own failing joints ache in jealousy. He was dressed all wrong for the Iraqi desert. He wore a heavy trench coat, dark sunglasses, and a bowler hat.

 

During my younger days in The Union, I had seen men who carried themselves with that exact same intimidating posture. He gave off the distinct, chilling vibe of a spy, but there was something fundamentally stranger about him that I could not immediately identify.

 

The second figure was an ancient, wizened man standing at his side. He was impossibly old, far surpassing both my own age and the man cooking carp in the square. His skin was folded and draped over his frail bones in deep, permanent ridges. Looking at his frail posture, I joked to myself that it was possible he was old enough to have personally witnessed the construction of the ancient ruins we were traveling north to excavate.

 

He leaned heavily on a bright yellow walking stick that stood out vividly against the drab grey concrete of the church interior.

 

The loud creak of the door hinges had drawn their attention. Both men turned their heads to stare at me as I stood frozen in the entryway. I braced myself for a confrontation, my muscles tensing out of pure habit, but the ancient man simply offered a kindly, disarming smile. The expression was completely warm and punctuated by an utter lack of teeth.

 

Beside him, the tall man in the trench coat reached up and tipped his bowler hat in a polite greeting. The motion was incredibly stiff, rigid and mechanical in a way that defied normal articulation. Yet, despite his bizarre attire and unnatural movement, my instincts did not scream at me to turn around and flee. I felt no deep, primal gut feeling of wrongness. The scene before me felt strangely domestic, just two bizarre travellers sharing a quiet moment in a forgotten house of worship with another traveller.

 

"Welcome, friend. Please, come in and sit."

 

The greeting came from the ancient man. He spoke in exceptionally fluent English, carrying a crisp, educated accent that felt entirely out of place in a crumbling desert town, but still sounded Iraqi enough. I immediately noted the fluency. The tall man in the trench coat nodded, gesturing toward one of the benches nearby.

 

I approached slowly and took a seat across the aisle from them. Above us, the old incandescent bulbs hanging from the ceiling flickered with a weak current. Yet, as I settled onto the bench near the ancient man, the dimness retreated. A strange, comforting warmth radiated from his presence.

 

 Sitting near him made the heavy shadows lift, making everything in the dusty sanctuary feel brighter and somehow better. The persistent, gnawing ache in my knees even dulled to a quiet throb.

 

"Are you two Christians?" I asked, looking between them. "The only Christians left in this town, perhaps?" Even as I asked, I knew the young man was clearly not a resident, and winced at what I said.

 

The ancient man chuckled. The sound was rich and full of surprising vigour for someone with such a fragile frame. He shook his head, leaning his weight onto his bright yellow walking stick.

 

"No," the old man replied, his toothless smile remaining firmly in place. "I believe in something older. Much older."

 

I thought about the history of the region, the birthplace of the Abrahamic faiths. "Ah, you are Jewish then?" I asked.

 

The old man gave a gentle, noncommittal nod, his eyes crinkling at the corners in a friendly manner. He did not elaborate further, simply letting the quiet of the church fill the space between us.

 

I turned my attention to the tall man in the sunglasses. "And you?"

 

The younger man slowly shook his head, the brim of his bowler hat tilting with the same stiff motion from earlier. "I do not subscribe to such things," he said. His English was just as flawless as the old man's, carrying a perfectly neutral, unaccented cadence. "I am merely an observer of the region."

 

A cold prickle of unease finally brushed against the back of my neck as he spoke. I realized exactly what had unsettled me about him. He was articulating his words with perfect clarity, but his lips barely moved. The flesh of his jaw remained almost entirely rigid, parting only a fraction of an inch to let the sound escape. It defied basic human anatomy.

 

I stared at his static mouth for a long moment, my mind struggling to process the impossible mechanics of his speech. My instincts, honed by years of surviving the brutal collapse of The Union, should have demanded I stand up and walk right out the door.

 

But the oppressive dread never manifested. Despite the unnaturalness of the tall man and the impossible age of his companion, the atmosphere remained completely peaceful. They were bizarre, but they were friendly. They just felt like two quiet travellers sharing a sanctuary, and for a fleeting moment, I was entirely glad to sit in the quiet dark with them.

 

I introduced myself simply as Nikolai. The two strangers accepted my name with polite nods, and the younger man asked what brought a foreign academic to such a desolate corner of Iraq.

 

I explained our upcoming expedition north to the newly discovered ruins. They listened with an intense, quiet focus. Then, they began to ask questions. The younger man inquired about our stratigraphy methods and ground-penetrating radar anomalies with a shocking level of technical expertise.

 

The old man asked, no, spoke about specific early structural motifs and the geological composition of the mortar we expected to find. Their questions were so apt and immensely well-educated that I actually had to stop speaking for a moment. I paused, looking between the two of them, wondering exactly who these people could be to possess such specialized knowledge.

 

The old man seemed to notice my hesitation. His eyes twinkled brightly in the dim light of the flickering bulbs. "Uruk is nearby, no?" he asked gently. "Not interested in visiting?"

 

My eyes widened, and a genuine smile finally broke across my scarred face. In all the planning and the stress of the journey, I had completely forgotten our proximity to the true cradle of civilization. I nodded eagerly. Seeing humanity's oldest city would make me incredibly happy. I had personally been one among many excavating the great Neolithic settlement of Catalhoyuk back in Turkey, but Uruk held a legendary status that called to the very core of my profession.

 

The conversation then shifted away from antiquity. The younger man in the sunglasses tilted his head slightly, asking about my personal life. He inquired bluntly if I was born in The Union.

 

I recited the short, sanitized version of my history. I spoke of the collapse, my subsequent departure, and finding a new life among the academic circles of Istanbul.

 

The younger man listened without blinking. He paused for a long, heavy moment when I finished. "I was there in Afghanistan," he stated flatly.

 

I frowned, quickly calculating his apparent age. He looked to be in his early thirties. But it was possible he was in his forties. "As a child?" I questioned. "During the invasion?"

 

He raised his gloved hand in a wide, sweeping gesture that felt overly rehearsed. "Something like that. But I do not blame you nor your country. Afghanistan suffered a lot worse after."

 

He lowered his hand and leaned slightly forward. Even behind the dark lenses of his sunglasses, I could feel the intense weight of his gaze locking directly into my eyes.

 

"Do you mourn your country, Nikolai?" he asked.

 

I thought of the breadlines, the bitter cold, the violence, and the absolute loss of my faith. I thought of the Motherland that had birthed me and then bled to death in front of me. A Union of peoples that was now many lesser countries. I nodded slowly, offering him an honest confirmation.

 

I waited for his response. I expected a word of sympathy, a shared anecdote of loss, or perhaps a philosophical observation. Instead, the young man had absolutely no reaction. He offered no follow-up question. His face remained a rigid mask, completely devoid of empathy or understanding. He just went perfectly still.

 

The abrupt, lifeless halt to the conversation left me completely baffled. I shifted uncomfortably on the hard bench, unsure if I had somehow offended him.

 

The tension shattered a moment later when the ancient man chuckled loudly. The rich, vibrating sound echoed off the bare concrete walls of the church. He waved his wizened hand dismissively toward his companion.

 

"Do not mind him, Nikolai," the old man said, his toothless smile returning. "That is simply how the young man is."

 

The old man's eyes twinkled again in the dim, flickering light of the church.

 

"When you visit Uruk, remember me, yes?" he asked softly, his voice carrying that strange, comforting warmth. "Old Sham from this small town."

 

"I will remember, Sham," I replied. I then turned my attention to the tall man in the trench coat. "And you, friend? Who do I have the pleasure of speaking with?"

 

The man did not blink behind his dark sunglasses. His jaw barely parted as the perfectly unaccented words slipped out. "Al Muraqib."

 

I accepted the name with a nod. Just as I opened my mouth to ask him another question, a loud, sudden knocking at the heavy wooden entrance doors startled me. The sharp sound echoed violently through the quiet sanctuary.

 

"Nikolai! Are you in there?"

 

It was Kaan. His voice carried through the thick wood, tight with a distinct note of concern.

 

I spun around to look at the main doors. A second later, I snapped my head back toward the benches. I had spent enough time listening to desert folklore to half expect the two strange men to have vanished into thin air the moment I looked away.

 

But they were still there.

 

Sham and Al Muraqib looked at me and gently motioned toward the entrance, urging me to answer. They then turned away and began to slowly make their way toward a small side door near the altar. The ancient man leaned heavily on Al Muraqib's rigid arm, putting the rest of his fragile weight onto his bright yellow walking stick. They looked entirely mundane, just two travellers quietly taking their leave.

 

I gave them a final, respectful nod of farewell. "I am coming, Kaan!" I shouted back.

 

I walked down the short aisle and pulled the heavy main doors open. Kaan stood outside in the dusty evening air, looking entirely confused as he peered past me into the gloomy interior.

 

"What are you doing sitting inside a dark church all by yourself?" Kaan asked, wiping a layer of sweat and grit from his forehead.

 

"I am not by myself," I said, stepping aside to let him see inside. "I was just having a fascinating conversation with a duo."

 

I turned and pointed toward the front to show him the two men.

 

They were not there. The space near the altar was completely empty.

 

I stared for a moment, feeling a brief spike of confusion. Then I remembered the small side door. It was left standing wide open, revealing the alleyway outside. The dry wind was gently pushing against the wooden frame.

 

A quiet chuckle escaped my lips. I shook my head, feeling foolish for letting the local superstitions briefly cloud my judgment.

 

"Well, they were here," I said with a smile. "They are just shy. They probably left through that side door when they heard you shouting."

 

Kaan shrugged, accepting the explanation without a second thought. "If you say so. Come on," Kaan said, waving his hand toward the centre of the settlement. "I actually came to get you because the food is ready. The others are already at the fire."

 

We walked back out to the town square together. The scene waiting for us was a beautiful contrast to the desolate, dusty alleys. A large, roaring fire illuminated the faces of a dozen people. Emre, Zeynep, and Elif had arrived from the guesthouse. They were sitting cross-legged on thick, brightly coloured rugs, completely surrounded by the old man's extended family and several curious townsfolk.

 

It was an absolute feast. The old man who had invited us earlier was currently pulling multiple large, split carp from the wooden spikes stationed near the hot coals. Woven baskets sat scattered around the edges of the rugs, overflowing with towering stacks of fresh flatbread. Over a smaller set of coals, his sons were carefully turning small iron spits holding roasted game birds, plump desert partridges glistening with fat and dark spices.

 

"Eat, my friends," the old man urged, his weathered face splitting into a huge, welcoming grin. He laid the fish onto a communal platter in the very centre of our gathering. "Eat well. Welcome to our home."

 

We did exactly that. The meal was chaotic, messy, and entirely wonderful. The language barrier was immense, but it simply did not matter. Emre tried to mime a story about our bumpy bus ride down from Nasiriyah, exaggerating the potholes by throwing his hands in the air and bouncing violently in his seated position.

 

The local children pointed at him and erupted into fits of pure, unfiltered laughter. Elif shared pieces of sweet Turkish delight she had brought in her travel pack, trading the candies for small glass cups of dark, cardamom sweet tea poured by the host's teenage grandson.

 

It was a moment of profound humanity. Yet, amidst the laughter and the passing of plates, I caught it again. The host's wife handed Zeynep a piece of tender roasted bird, and for a fleeting second, her warm smile faltered. Her dark eyes filled with that exact same heavy pity I had seen earlier in the streets.

 

She looked at the young woman with a quiet grief, a look reserved for those walking towards a sudden end. The look vanished a moment later, replaced by a polite nod, but it chilled the warm tea in my stomach.

 

I tore off a piece of bread and dipped it into the rich oils of the fish, deciding to distract myself. I thought of the two men in the church. I actively considered asking our host about old Sham first. The ancient man leaning on that bright yellow stick had to be a known fixture in such a small, isolated community. Everyone in this town probably knew Sham. The younger man in the trench coat was the true anomaly.

 

"Uncle," I said, catching the host's attention across the fire. "Do you know a man in town named Al Muraqib? A tall fellow. He wears dark glasses and a western coat."

 

The old man paused with a piece of fish halfway to his mouth. He frowned deeply, his wrinkles bunching together in absolute confusion.

 

"Al Muraqib?" he repeated, testing the syllables on his tongue. He shook his head firmly. "No name. That is not a name."

 

"I just spoke with him," I insisted gently, pointing back toward the dark alley. "In the church down the road. He introduced himself as Al Muraqib."

 

The host spoke rapidly in Arabic to his grandson, who looked equally puzzled. The teenage boy pulled out a smartphone and tapped the screen, opening a translation application. He typed for a moment and then leaned across the rug, holding the screen up for me to read.

 

The bright white text on the screen was stark and clear. It read simply, 'The Observer'.

 

I stared at the bright screen for a second, and then a quiet, genuine laugh rumbled deep in my chest. I shook my head, thoroughly amused by the sheer theatricality of it all.

 

"What is so funny, Nikolai?" Elif asked, leaning over to hand me another cup of tea.

 

"Nothing," I replied, taking the warm glass by the rim. "Just a very secretive, very dramatic young man in the church. He told me his name was 'The Observer' instead of giving me a real answer."

 

"Maybe he is a spy," Emre joked loudly, his mouth full of bread. "Keeping an eye on the dangerous archaeologists."

 

The locals did not understand the exact words, but they understood Emre's jovial, teasing tone. The host chuckled along with us, reaching over to clap Emre warmly on the back. The fire cracked loudly, sending warm orange sparks dancing up into the dark desert sky.

 

I smiled and took a sip of my sweet tea. I completely dismissed the strange encounter, chalking it up to the eccentric behaviour of an odd traveller with a flair for the dramatic.

 

The night deepened, and the heavy meal gave way to music. Someone brought out a traditional skin drum, and the rhythmic, steady beating echoed off the mudbrick walls of the square. A few of the younger men in the village began a traditional local dance, stepping and clapping in a wide, energetic circle around the fire.

 

Emre and Kaan eagerly joined them, stumbling over the unfamiliar footwork but laughing uproariously. Soon, Zeynep and Elif were pulled into the fold by the local women, their bright voices mixing with the deep chanting of the townsfolk.

 

 

I stayed seated on the woven rug, nursing my cardamom tea and letting the heat of the fire seep into my aching joints. I watched them all, feeling the vast distance between my weary bones and their youthful exuberance.

 

As the music swelled, my thoughts drifted back to the dark sanctuary of the church. The ancient man with the yellow walking stick still lingered in my mind. He had felt so vibrant, radiating a warmth that had physically soothed my pain.

 

I leaned across the rug and caught the host's attention once more.

 

"Uncle," I called out over the rhythmic clapping. "What about the other man with him? The ancient one with the bright yellow walking stick. He told me his name was Sham."

 

The old man paused, his hands resting heavily on his knees. He looked genuinely confused again, squinting at me through the firelight. "Sham?" he repeated slowly.

 

Then, a profound change came over his weathered face. His dark eyes went incredibly wide. He did not shrink back in fear, nor did he recoil in terror. Instead, he looked exactly as if a bolt of thunder had just struck the earth right at his feet. It was a look of absolute, unadulterated awe.

 

"Old Sham spoke to you?" the host asked, his voice dropping into a breathless, tight whisper. He leaned forward, gripping the thick wool of the rug. "He spoke words?"

 

"Yes," I replied, suddenly feeling a cold knot form in my stomach. "He spoke to me in perfect English. We had an entire conversation."

 

The host stared at me, his mouth hanging slightly open. He shook his head in pure disbelief. When he spoke again, his voice carried the bright, hushed tone of childish wonder.

 

"That is impossible, Russian man," the old man said hastily, his broken English tumbling out in a rush. "Old Sham has sickness of mind. He forget everything. He been deaf and completely mute for many, many years. He only sit in sun and stare. He do not hear, and he does not speak."

 

I sat perfectly still. The warmth of the tea completely vanished from my throat. I pictured the wizened man standing in the church, leaning on his yellow stick, his sharp, twinkling eyes meeting mine. I remembered his rich, vigorous laughter echoing off the concrete walls.

 

"He spoke," I insisted quietly. "He told me to remember him when I visited Uruk."

 

The host looked up at the dark sky, shaking his head in sheer amazement. He looked back at me, his face glowing with a reverent curiosity. "Then who was other man? Who was Observer, Al Muraqib to make silent Sham speak?"

 

I had no answer for him.

 

"Sham has been in this town since before I was born," the host continued, leaning closer to me, his eyes shining with awe. "My father told me Sham was an old man when even he was little boy. They say he carried rifle in the first great war against the British. Maybe he is even older than that. Nobody truly know his age. We just feed him and care for him because he belong to the town, to our family."

 

I looked away from the fire, my gaze drawn past the dancing bodies and back toward the dark alley where the church sat hidden in the shadows.

 

The local drums continued to beat loudly, and my colleagues continued to laugh in the firelight. Yet, sitting there in the warmth of the crowd, the desert night suddenly felt unfathomably ancient, cold, and entirely outside the realm of logic.

 

I retired to my room that night expecting the usual chorus of aching joints and restless thoughts. Instead, I fell into a deep, profound slumber the moment my head touched the thin pillow.

 

I woke up the next morning completely shocked. It was the best sleep I had experienced since the early nineteen eighties. My mind had been filled with quiet, kind dreams that left me feeling inexplicably restored. The heavy, persistent pain in my knees had even faded to a dull whisper.

 

We bundled our gear into the rugged SUVs that had just arrived that morning. A large, happy crowd of locals gathered in the morning heat to wave goodbye. I scanned the faces carefully, hoping to catch a glimpse of the ancient man with his yellow stick or his rigid companion.

 

"Do you see him, Nikolai?" Kaan asked, leaning out the window of the vehicle.

 

"No," I replied, scanning the smiling crowd, interspersed with the same quiet expressions of pity lingering on the edges of the group. "Neither of them are here."

 

Emre let out a loud groan from the last row, he liked sitting with the equipment that wasn’t in the boot. "I cannot believe it. You are telling me we just missed out on meeting the oldest living human on the planet?"

 

"I told you last night, Emre," I said, as our quiet Iraqi driver began putting the vehicle into gear. "You were too busy cooking to come to the church."

 

"If the stories are true, the man fought the British before our grandparents were born," Elif chimed in, sounding deeply disappointed. "I would have loved to at least take his photograph."

 

As we drove away from the settlement and out into the vast, blinding expanse of the northern desert, Zeynep pulled out a stack of briefing papers. She began explaining the sheer scale of the upcoming dig to Kaan, though she was clearly reciting the facts more for her own benefit.

 

"Do you actually understand how massive this site is, Kaan?" Zeynep asked, tapping her finger against a printed satellite image. "This is not just a handful of old walls. The preliminary ground-penetrating radar scans show a subterranean structure that rivals the footprint of ancient Babylon itself."

 

"It is just an early church, Zeynep," Kaan teased, though he looked impressed by the charts.

 

"It is an entire buried complex," she corrected him sharply. "There are over three hundred archaeologists from dozens of nations converging on these coordinates right now. It is a logistical miracle."

 

"And it is all spearheaded by Arthur Pendelton," Emre added, leaning forward from his row. "The man is a living legend. He pioneered those new isotopic dating methods and single-handedly uncovered that lost Phoenician port city off the coast of Lebanon. Working under his banner is going to make our careers."

 

"He is a brilliant man," Zeynep agreed, her voice full of absolute reverence. "A true innovator."

 

I gripped the overhead handle tightly, keeping my eyes fixed on the dusty road outside the window. I absolutely despised Arthur Pendelton.

 

My hatred had nothing to do with professional rivalry or his staggering institutional budget. I hated him because I knew exactly what kind of monster he hid behind his tailored suits and academic prestige. A few years ago, a brave independent investigative article exposed his private life. During the chaotic, starving years of the Soviet collapse, Pendelton had travelled to Moscow under the guise of academic diplomacy.

 

He used his wealth and western influence to prey on several deeply vulnerable Eastern European women, nay, girls who were desperate to escape the ruin of our motherland. He bought them, abused them, and walked away with his sterling reputation entirely intact. The academic world completely ignored the exposé, brushing it all under the rug to protect their golden boy.

 

I never forgot it. I was being driven into the heart of the desert to work under a man who embodied everything that had destroyed my home.

 

"He is a pathetic swine who preyed on the young girls of my Motherland," I said, my voice cutting sharply through the dry air of the cabin.

 

“Svoloch”, I hissed.

 

I have lived in Turkey for over three decades, and I practically never spoke Russian anymore. I prefer to let that language, and the life attached to it, remain entirely buried. For me to curse in my mother tongue was a rare, bitter outburst that violently shocked the younger archaeologists.

 

The enthusiastic chatter in the vehicle died instantly. The heavy silence in the SUV was deafening, save for the hum of the engine and the crunch of tires on sand driven by our quiet Iraqi guide at the wheel.

 

The young women exchanged a dark, knowing look. Their bright, optimistic expressions hardened into something cold as the memory of that buried article surfaced in their minds.

 

Kaan, sitting in the front passenger seat, reached over and placed a steady hand on my thigh.

 

"You are right, Nikolai," Kaan said softly, breaking the tense quiet. "He might be famous, and he might have all the grants in the world. But you have always been an infinitely better person. We are proud to work for you, with you."

 

Zeynep and Elif nodded in firm agreement, their previous reverence for the American titan completely shattered. I let out a long, shuddering breath and offered Kaan a small nod of gratitude. I might be an aging, broken man who lost his faith, but I appreciated the loyalty of my Turkish colleagues more than they could ever know.

 

We drove in heavy silence for another hour, until we reached the mostly abandoned village on the outskirts of the dig site.

 

I looked past the driver's shoulder and the decaying mudbrick homes. Beyond the village, resting in a massive depression in the earth, the true scale of the excavation finally came into view.

 

It did not look like an archaeological site. It looked like a royal occupation. Rows of massive, climate-controlled geodesic dome tents gleamed in the brutal Iraqi sun. There were lines of heavy diesel generators humming loudly, massive satellite communication arrays pointing toward the sky, and a fleet of pristine transport trucks parked in perfectly straight rows.

 

There were even elevated wooden walkways constructed over the sand to keep the boots of the researchers clean. The sheer luxury of the setup was ludicrous for a site this remote. It was an obscene display of wealth.

 

Kaan pressed his face against the glass of the window, his eyes wide with disbelief.

 

"Where in the world did the Iraqi archaeological society get the funds for a camp like this?" Kaan asked, completely bewildered by the scale of the operation.

 

Zeynep stared at the sprawling, arrogant compound. The admiration she had held just an hour ago was entirely gone.

 

"They didn't," Zeynep spat, her voice thick with disgust. "Pendleton funded it all himself… well, himself and Emirati money." She finished.

 

We stepped out of the SUV, our boots sinking into the loose sand just short of the pristine wooden walkways. The heat hit us instantly. It was almost a weight bearing down on our shoulders. Kaan and Emre stood beside the vehicle, completely speechless at the sprawling camp.

 

"Dr. Nikolai of the Istanbul team?" a voice called out over the low, constant hum of the heavy diesel generators.

 

Two figures walked briskly toward us down the elevated wooden path. They were both British, dressed in spotless khaki uniforms. The older of the two extended an eager hand as he approached.

 

"Dr. Thomas Aris, Oxford," he said, offering a bright, highly practiced smile. "And this is my colleague, Dr. Eleanor Vance. We have been assigned to receive your team and get you settled in. I must say, it is an absolute honour. Your work on the Catalhoyuk excavations in Turkey completely reshaped our department's understanding of Neolithic social structures."

 

I took his hand. His grip was remarkably soft for a field researcher, but his enthusiasm seemed genuine. Eleanor shook my hand next, echoing his praises. I introduced my younger colleagues, keeping my own words incredibly brief. I had absolutely no patience for academic flattery.

 

"It is quite the setup, isn't it?" Eleanor noted, catching the begrudging awe still plastered across the faces of my team. "Pendleton spares no expense. Come along. We will show you to your quarters and give you a quick lay of the land on the way."

 

We followed them onto the wooden walkways. The sheer amount of money poured into this patch of dirt was genuinely nauseating. It was not a pursuit of history. It was a corporate conquest.

 

Thomas stopped halfway down the main thoroughfare and pointed toward a massive, rectangular depression excavated near the very centre of the camp.

 

"That is Sector One," Thomas explained, raising his voice slightly to be heard over a passing transport truck. "We believe it is the primary nave of the structure. We have cleared about forty feet of sand and debris, and we still have not hit the foundation level."

 

I walked to the edge of the safety railing and looked down into the pit. A cold knot immediately formed in my stomach. The exposed walls were constructed of massive, tightly fitted blocks of dark, unyielding stone. There were no visible mortar joints. It mirrored the cyclopean masonry of much older, deeply pagan cultures.

 

Zeynep leaned over the metal railing right beside me, her eyes scanning the dark, imposing stone. "Are there any inscriptions? You said it was a Christian site."

 

"It is," Eleanor answered, stepping up beside us. "We have uncovered several early crosses and distinct Christian iconography carved into the upper archways. But that is where things get complicated. Mixed right in with the Christian carvings, we found a decent portion of Mithraic elements. There are bull-slaying reliefs and a host of other minor pagan iconography scattered throughout the vestibules."

 

"A syncretic temple," Kaan muttered, adjusting his glasses. "That is highly unusual for this region."

 

"The iconography is only half the mystery," Thomas added, his bright smile fading into a look of deep, unsettling confusion. "The working estimates from our isotopic analysers came back yesterday. Factoring in the masonry techniques and the organic material trapped in the stone pores, we have a preliminary date of construction. It points to a mere ten years after Christ died."

 

I stared down into the abyss of the excavation. That timeline was an absolute impossibility. Christianity had barely formed as a localized sect in Jerusalem at that point. To find a massive, deeply fortified complex hundreds of miles east in the heart of Mesopotamia, blending the new faith with ancient Mithraic rites, defied every established law of history.

 

We continued down the path, the roar of the generators drowning out the quiet, ancient wind of the desert. We passed several heavily fortified tents. Armed men stood at the flaps, their rifles resting easily in their hands. Private security contractors. Pendleton was guarding his prize with mercenaries.

 

"Your quarters are right up here," Thomas said, finally stopping in front of a large, gleaming white geodesic dome. "Pendleton has called for a mandatory briefing in at sundown for all new arrivals."

 

I stared at the pristine white structure. It felt completely sterile. And yet, as I looked around, never did I feel so unnerved as then. The site was all wrong. I did not feel such horror even in 1991. I was trembling, and I hoped my gut was wrong.
