I'm sixteen years old, and three days ago, my younger brother disappeared from our house. I know that sounds like the kind of thing you'd expect to see on the news, but the weirdest part isn't that he's missing. It's that nobody else seems to remember him. Not my parents, not our neighbors, not even the people who supposedly watched us grow up together. I've spent the last seventy-two hours trying to find someone who can confirm that my brother existed, and every time I think I'm getting somewhere, I find something that makes me question whether I actually know anything about my own life.

My brother's name is Eli. He's fourteen, about five feet six, and has a small scar above his left eyebrow from when he fell off his bike when we were kids. He's the kind of person who leaves empty cereal bowls in his bedroom and borrows your things without asking, then acts offended when you want them back. We argued constantly over stupid things, mostly about the bathroom, the television, and whose turn it was to take out the trash. A couple of weeks ago, he stole twenty dollars from my desk and spent it on snacks at the convenience store down the street. I remember being furious with him. I remember telling him that if he touched my money again, I'd tell Mom. He laughed and called me a snitch before running upstairs.

I remember all of this so clearly that it physically hurts when I try to explain it to someone and watch them look at me like I'm making everything up.

On Monday morning, I woke up around seven because I had school, and the first thing I noticed was how quiet the house was. Normally, Eli would already be awake, playing videos on his phone at a volume that made it impossible to ignore him. Sometimes he'd knock on my bedroom wall just to annoy me, even though I had told him at least a hundred times to stop doing that. That morning, there was nothing. I figured he had overslept, so I went into the hallway and walked toward his room to wake him up.

Except his bedroom wasn't there.

I stood in front of the wall for several seconds before I understood what I was looking at. The door that had been there for as long as I could remember was gone, along with the light switch and the little dent in the wood near the handle where Eli had accidentally slammed his skateboard into it. There was just a plain, freshly painted wall, the same color as the rest of the hallway. I even ran my hands across it, looking for some kind of seam or hidden panel, because my half-asleep brain had decided that was a more reasonable explanation than an entire bedroom disappearing overnight.

I went downstairs and asked my mother where Eli was. She was standing at the kitchen counter, making coffee, and she didn't even turn around when she answered.

"Who's Eli?"

At first, I thought she was messing with me. My mom has a pretty dry sense of humor, and Eli and I had been getting on each other's nerves all weekend, so I assumed he had done something to get himself grounded. I laughed and asked her to stop being annoying because I was going to be late for school. She turned around then, looked at me for a moment, and asked if I had slept well. When I told her I was talking about my brother, she put her coffee down and said, very calmly, that I was an only child.

I remember feeling embarrassed before I felt scared. I thought maybe my parents had planned some elaborate joke, and I was the only person who hadn't gotten the message. I checked the living room, the garage, and the backyard, calling Eli's name as I went. I even checked behind the shower curtain because he had hidden there once when we were younger and scared me so badly that I nearly threw a shampoo bottle at his head. I found nothing, obviously, and when I came back downstairs, my father was sitting at the kitchen table reading the news on his phone.

I asked him where my brother was.

He gave me the same answer.

That was when I started getting angry. I told them both that this wasn't funny anymore and that I needed to know where Eli had gone. My father looked genuinely confused, while my mother kept asking me to sit down and eat something. I refused and grabbed my phone to show them pictures of us together. I knew I had hundreds of them because I was always taking pictures whenever Eli did something embarrassing. There were pictures of us at the beach, at birthday parties, in the back seat of our aunt's car, and in our living room during Christmas. I opened my gallery and searched through years of photos.

Every picture was still there, but Eli wasn't.

In one photo, I was sitting on the couch holding a controller, laughing at something just outside the frame. In another, I was standing beside our Christmas tree with my arm raised as if I were taking a selfie with someone who wasn't visible. There was even a picture from my birthday where I was leaning toward the empty space beside me, smiling so widely that my cheeks looked painful. I remembered that photograph. Eli had been making a ridiculous face behind the cake, and I'd been trying not to laugh while our mother took the picture.

I checked the date and then checked it again. It was the correct day. I searched my messages, my contacts, my social media accounts, and every old conversation I could remember having with him. There was nothing. No contact, no messages, no photographs with his face, and no evidence that anyone named Eli had ever been part of my life.

I didn't go to school that day. Instead, I called the police and told them my fourteen-year-old brother was missing. They arrived a little after lunch, asked me for his full name, birthday, school, and a description, and then spoke privately with my parents. When they came back, one of the officers asked whether I had been experiencing stress or trouble sleeping. I told him I wanted to know why he was asking me that instead of looking for my brother. He exchanged a glance with his partner before explaining that my parents had reported no missing child and that their records listed me as an only child.

I could barely hear him over the sound of my own heartbeat.

Before leaving, the older officer gave me a card and told me to contact a trusted adult if I continued feeling distressed. My mother thanked him at the front door, then watched the police car disappear down the street. When she turned back toward me, she had an expression I couldn't quite understand. She looked tired, but there was something else underneath it, something almost like relief.

That night, I stayed awake until nearly two in the morning. I couldn't stop thinking about the wall where Eli's bedroom should have been. I knew that if I went downstairs and looked at it again, it would still be there, but I couldn't convince myself to stop checking. Eventually, I left my room to get a glass of water, and as I passed the hallway, I heard three soft knocks coming from behind the wall.

I stopped walking.

A few seconds later, I heard them again.

Then a voice whispered my name.

It was Eli.

I recognized his voice immediately, down to the slightly scratchy sound he made when he was tired. I put my glass on the floor because my hands had started shaking so badly that I was afraid I'd drop it. I pressed my ear against the wall and whispered his name, barely expecting an answer.

"Ellie?"

I started crying before I could stop myself. I asked him where he was, whether he was hurt, and why I couldn't find his room. He answered in a whisper, telling me that he was still inside and that I needed to be quiet because Mom might hear us. I asked him what he meant, and he went silent for so long that I thought he'd disappeared again.

Then I heard something scraping against the other side of the wall.

It sounded like fingernails dragging slowly across wood.

"Eli, what's happening?" I whispered.

He took a shaky breath.

"I think Mom's trying to make everyone forget me."

I wanted to ask him what he meant, but I heard a bedroom door open upstairs. The scraping stopped immediately, and Eli's voice became so quiet that I had to press my ear harder against the wall to hear him.

"Don't let her find out you remember."

My mother's footsteps moved across the upstairs hallway. I backed away from the wall and hurried into my bedroom, locking the door behind me. I sat on the floor until morning, listening for footsteps outside, but nothing happened. When I finally went downstairs, my mother was making breakfast as if the previous night had never occurred. I asked her whether she'd heard anything strange, and she smiled at me and told me I looked exhausted.

I decided then that I needed proof that didn't depend on photographs or messages. If Eli had been real, somebody had to remember him. Our neighbors had known us since we were little, and the woman across the street used to babysit us when our parents worked late. I went over to her house that afternoon and asked whether she remembered my brother.

She frowned and said she didn't know what I was talking about.

I described him in detail, including his scar and the bike accident, and she listened patiently before telling me that she'd only ever seen me playing outside alone. She even laughed a little and said I had always been a quiet child who preferred drawing to playing with other kids. I left before she could ask any more questions.

On the way home, I passed the little convenience store where Eli had supposedly spent my twenty dollars. I went inside and asked the cashier whether he remembered a boy who came in almost every afternoon to buy snacks. The cashier said he saw hundreds of kids every week. When I described Eli, he shook his head and told me he couldn't help.

I was beginning to wonder whether I was losing my mind when I remembered the box of childhood belongings in our attic. If there was anywhere my parents might have missed something, it would be there. I waited until they went grocery shopping and climbed upstairs, digging through old clothes, school projects, and toys I hadn't touched in years. Most of it was ordinary stuff, but near the bottom of the box, I found a folded piece of paper covered in crayon drawings.

It showed two children standing in front of a house. One was a girl with messy hair and a yellow shirt. The other was a boy with black hair and a small mark above his left eyebrow.

Underneath them, written in my childhood handwriting, were the words, Me and Eli. Best friends forever.

I sat on the attic floor and stared at it for a long time. I didn't know whether to laugh or cry. It wasn't a photograph, but it was something. I had drawn him. I had written his name. I hadn't invented every memory of him, had I?

I turned the paper over.

There was another sentence written on the back in pencil, so faint that I almost missed it.

If you forget him, find the little door.

I read it several times before I noticed the date in the bottom corner. It was from eleven years ago, when I would have been five years old.

I didn't remember writing it.

I looked around the attic, trying to work out what the little door could mean. The only door in the room was the one leading back to the stairs, and the old wardrobe in the corner was too small to hide anything larger than a few coats. Still, I walked over to it and opened both doors. The inside smelled like dust and damp wood. I pushed the coats aside, expecting to find nothing, but there was a narrow wooden panel at the back that I was certain hadn't been there before.

It was about the size of a cupboard door, with a small brass handle near the bottom.

I pulled it open.

Beyond it was a dark space that looked much bigger than the wardrobe should have allowed. I couldn't see the walls or the floor, only a narrow strip of darkness stretching away from me. The air coming through the opening was cold enough to make my fingers ache.

Then I heard someone breathing.

"Ellie?"

I knew that voice.

I dropped the drawing.

"Eli?"

"You're late," he whispered.

I grabbed the handle and pulled the door a little wider. I couldn't see his face clearly, but I could make out a small figure sitting somewhere in the darkness. I asked him how long he'd been there, and he told me he didn't know. I asked him why nobody remembered him, and he didn't answer.

Instead, he asked me whether Mom was still outside.

I told him she was at the grocery store.

He began to cry.

"She told me you'd forget me eventually," he said. "She said it would be easier if nobody remembered either of us."

I didn't understand what he meant. I asked him to come out, but he shook his head. He said there was something behind him, something that had been following him through the dark for as long as he could remember. He said it looked like a person when it stood far away, but whenever it got close, its face was wrong.

Then I heard footsteps on the stairs.

My mother had come home.

She appeared in the attic doorway carrying a grocery bag, and when she saw me standing in front of the little door, the bag slipped from her hand. An orange rolled across the floor. Neither of us moved to pick it up.

For several seconds, she just stared at me.

Then she whispered, "You weren't supposed to find that."

I asked her what had happened to Eli. I demanded to know why she kept pretending he had never existed, why our pictures had changed, and why I could hear him inside the wall. My mother began crying, but she didn't look at the little door. She looked directly at me.

"Sweetheart," she said, "you have to understand something."

I waited.

"You were never supposed to come back."

I felt a strange coldness spread through my arms. I looked down at my hands and noticed that I could see the floor through the tips of my fingers. At first, I thought the light was playing tricks on me, but when I raised my hand toward my face, the skin looked thin and transparent, like an image that hadn't loaded properly.

My mother reached for me, screaming my name, but her hand passed straight through my shoulder.

I stumbled backward toward the open door.

Inside the darkness, I could see more clearly now. There were children sitting along the walls, dozens of them, all silent and motionless. Some were very young. Others looked close to my age. A few wore clothes that seemed old-fashioned, and one little boy was holding a stuffed rabbit that looked exactly like the one I'd had when I was five.

At the very back of the room sat a little girl with messy hair and a yellow shirt.

She looked exactly like me.

She was staring at the doorway with the patient expression of someone who had been waiting a very long time.

Then she smiled.

"You're finally home," she said.

I woke up in my bed the following morning.

My mother told me I'd had another nightmare. She said I'd gone into the attic, fallen asleep, and frightened myself with something I'd found in an old box. She seemed so normal that I almost believed her. Almost. But when I climbed upstairs to check, the wardrobe was gone. There was no little wooden door, no drawing, and no box of childhood belongings. The attic looked as though nobody had ever stored anything there.

I went to school anyway, hoping that being around other people might make me feel less alone. My teacher asked why I hadn't been in class for two days, and when I told her I had been looking for my brother, she gave me the same concerned expression the police officer had given me. During lunch, I approached my best friend and asked her whether she remembered Eli. She looked confused and said she'd never heard that name before.

By the time I got home, I was beginning to accept that something was seriously wrong with my memory. Maybe I'd imagined him. Maybe the drawing had been part of some childhood game I'd forgotten about. Maybe there was a perfectly reasonable explanation for all of it.

Then I looked at the family photographs on the living room wall.

There was a picture of my parents holding me as a baby. Another showed me on my first day of school. A third was from my sixteenth birthday, taken just last month.

I wasn't in any of them.

My parents were smiling at the camera, standing beside empty spaces where I knew I had been. In the birthday photograph, my mother was holding a cake with sixteen candles, and my father had his arm stretched around somebody who wasn't there.

I checked my phone.

My contacts were gone. My messages were gone. My social media accounts didn't exist anymore. When I searched my own name, the only results I could find belonged to other people.

I ran outside and knocked on the neighbors' doors, begging somebody to tell me they knew who I was. Nobody recognized me. One woman threatened to call the police because she thought I was trespassing.

I went back to my house and tried the front door, but it wouldn't open. Through the window, I could see my parents sitting at the kitchen table with a little girl between them. She was wearing a yellow shirt and drawing on a piece of paper.

My mother kissed the top of her head.

My father laughed at something she said.

They looked happy.

They looked like a family.

And the little girl looked exactly like me.

I'm writing this from the sidewalk outside my house. I don't know what I am anymore, and I don't know whether anyone will believe this story. I just needed to tell someone before I disappear completely.

A few minutes ago, I heard three knocks coming from inside the house.

Then I heard Eli's voice.

He was calling my name from somewhere upstairs, telling me he'd finally found a way out.

I wanted to run inside. I wanted to see him, to grab his hand, and to make sure we could never be separated again.

But then I heard another voice.

It sounded like mine when I was little.

It came from the other side of the front door.

And it whispered, very clearly, "Don't let them remember you. That's how it starts."

I don't know which voice is telling the truth.

I don't know whether my brother was ever real.

But I just looked through the window again, and the little girl at the kitchen table has stopped drawing.

She's looking directly at me.

And she's holding up a piece of paper.

On it, in my own handwriting, are four words.

THERE WAS NEVER AN ELI.
