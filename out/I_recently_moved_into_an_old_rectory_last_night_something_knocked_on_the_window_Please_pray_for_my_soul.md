I’m typing this out on my phone in the dark, with my screen brightness dragged all the way down to zero. I’m huddled under a heavy, scratchy wool blanket in the absolute dead center of my living room floor. I can't turn on a single light. If I flip a switch, the glare will hit the glass, and it will know exactly where I am standing. Right now, the pitch black is the only thing keeping me alive, and I need advice fast. The sun dipped below the tree line about an hour ago, and the sounds are starting back up.

A month ago, I honestly thought I’d struck gold. I bought the Old St. Jude’s Rectory—a massive, decaying Victorian property sitting right on the lip of the parish woods. The local Catholic diocese had left it rotting and abandoned since the mid-to-late seventies, which explained why the price tag was practically dirt cheap. I knew it would be a financial nightmare to restore, but I work from home as a freelance graphic designer. I thrive on total isolation. A giant house with warped, wavy antique glass, drafty hallways, and floorboards that groaned like old bones felt like the perfect place to disappear into my work.

The rectory shares a cracked gravel driveway with the church itself, which sits about a hundred yards away through a dense choke of pine trees and wild brambles. St. Jude’s is a total ruin now. Its stone spire collapsed decades ago, looming over my backyard like a jagged, broken tooth suffocated by poison ivy.

The first few weeks were quiet, though the air inside the house always felt incredibly heavy. No matter how many windows I propped open, a thick, stagnant smell lingered in the corners. It tasted like burnt tallow, damp basement earth, and a faint, metallic tang that reminded me of copper pennies. I figured it was just eighty years of trapped dust and dry rot. I set up my main office on the third floor—a converted attic space with a single, massive arched window that looks straight down into the overgrown graveyard and the ruined church beyond.

By the third week, the silence started to feel less like a luxury and more like a physical weight pressing against my ears. Sound didn't move normally through the house. If I dropped a screwdriver on the second floor, the noise didn't echo; it just went thud, as if the plaster walls were actively swallowing the vibrations.

To break up the monotony, I started digging through the cellar. It was a freezing, damp labyrinth of exposed fieldstone and packed dirt. Tucked away under a rotted wooden workbench, half-buried under rusted iron tools, I found a couple of heavy galvanized steel trunks. They were packed tight with old parish archives.

Most of it was useless junk: yellowed tithing ledgers from the Great Depression, crumbling Latin missals, and water-damaged baptism certificates. But at the very bottom of the second trunk, wrapped tightly in a stained, stiff linen altar cloth, was a thick journal bound in peeling black leather. It belonged to Father Thomas, the last parish priest who lived here before the diocese abruptly shut the whole place down in 1975.

The early entries were incredibly mundane. He wrote about the leaky roof, the dwindling collection plates, and how the brutal winters were killing the old boiler. But as the pages went on, his elegant, flowing cursive started to degrade into a jagged, frantic scrawl. The ink was smeared in places, like his hands were shaking violently while he wrote.  
One specific entry from that final spring made my chest tighten. The ink had scratched so hard into the paper it nearly tore through:

*"The seal of the confessional is absolute. It is a vow before Almighty God. But what happens to the sins that are too grotesque for Christ’s grace to wash away? What happens to the absolute filth whispered into the dark of the box? I can feel them now. They do not evaporate into the air. They stick to the stones of the nave. They crawl through the tall grass. Lately, I can hear them scraping against the rectory walls at night, begging to be let back in. They are heavy. They want a chest to sit in. They want a mouth to speak through."*

I shook my head, letting out a nervous laugh in the cold cellar. I figured it was just the classic story of a lonely, isolated country priest losing his mind to isolation madness. I took the journal up to my office, tossed it on the corner of my desk, and forgot about it. God, I wish I had burned it right then.

To understand how things got this bad, you have to understand the layout of this town. Blackwood Valley isn’t a place people move to; it’s a place people get stuck in. The local economy died when the lumber mills shut down in the late sixties, leaving behind a tight-knit, insular community where everyone knew your grandfather’s secrets.

As I kept reading through Father Thomas's journals over the next few days, a darker picture of the parish began to take shape. St. Jude’s wasn’t just a church for the community; it was the final dumping ground for everything the town tried to hide.  
Father Thomas recorded anonymous fragments of confessions that haunted him. He didn’t name names, but the patterns were clear. There were entries about a local farmer who watched his brother sink beneath the mud of the marsh and never reached out a hand to pull him out. Entries about a prominent town councilman who secretly set fire to a competitor's barn with the livestock still trapped inside. Infanticide, hidden abuse, poisonings disguised as sudden illnesses—the journal was a catalog of human rot.

*"They come to me on Saturdays,"* Father Thomas wrote in June of 1975. *"They kneel in the dark behind the screen. They whisper their filth, I give them three Hail Marys, and they walk out into the sunshine feeling light as feathers. But the weight doesn’t vanish. I can hear the floorboards of the church groaning under the phantom mass of what they leave behind. The air in the sanctuary is thick with it. It smells like copper and rancid suet. Yesterday, during the Liturgy of the Eucharist, I looked into the shadow behind the altar and saw a shadow that didn't belong to any pillar. It was tall. It was hungry. It was feeding on the unsaid."*

The townspeople eventually stopped coming to St. Jude’s altogether. Not because they lost their faith, but because they claimed the church felt 'wrong.' By August, Father Thomas was completely alone in the rectory. The local grocery delivery boy refused to drive up the gravel path, leaving crates of canned goods at the edge of the county road instead. The power lines to the property cut out during a minor storm that summer, and the diocese never bothered to send a crew out to fix them. They simply let the parish fade into the woods.

And I, like an idiot, thought I had bought a peaceful historical landmark.

**The Town Archive and the Missing Ledger**  
The modern town of Blackwood didn't want to talk about St. Jude’s. Two days ago, before the weather completely turned, the heavy silence of the rectory drove me out of the house. I needed to cross-reference Father Thomas’s journal with public records, if only to prove to myself that his frantic writings were the product of a collapsing mind rather than cold reality.

I drove down into the valley to the local municipal library—a cramped, water-damaged brick building that felt only slightly more modern than the rectory itself. The air inside smelled of vinegar and old pulp. When I asked the elderly archivist, a woman named Martha whose family had owned the local dry goods store for three generations, about the closure of St. Jude's in 1975, her entire demeanor shifted. Her hands stopped sorting through index cards. She looked at me, her eyes tracking the mud on my boots, and asked exactly where I was living.  
When I told her I bought the old rectory, she didn't offer a polite smile. She pulled her cardigan tight around her chest and pointed toward a dusty shelf in the back corner labeled *Local History: 1960–1980*.

"People around here don't talk about that winter," she whispered, her voice cracking slightly. "We don't look up that hill. When Father Thomas stopped coming down into town for supplies, the bishop sent a young deacon up there to check on him. The boy found the church doors chained from the *inside*, but every window in the rectory had been broken out. Not from stones thrown from the yard, mind you. The glass was pushed outward. Like something inside grew too big for the rooms."

She left me alone in the back stacks. I spent three hours digging through microfilm copies of the *Blackwood Gazette* from the autumn of 1975. What I found made the hairs on my arms stand up. There were no obituaries for Father Thomas. There were no police reports detailing an investigation. Instead, there was an official decree from the diocese stating that the parish had been 'spiritually deconsecrated' due to an uncontainable blight.  
Tucked into the back of a 1975 county real estate ledger, I found a loose, yellowed police log sheet that had been deliberately omitted from the official archives. It was an entry from October 14, 1975—exactly fifty years ago. A local deputy had responded to a call from the rectory. The deputy wrote that he couldn't step onto the front porch because the air was too cold to breathe, and that he could see 'a black shape in a Roman collar' standing perfectly still inside the second-story glass, watching him without moving its head. The log ended with a single handwritten note from the sheriff: 

*Do not send men back up St. Jude’s Lane. Let the dark keep what it took.*

I drove back up the hill in a daze, the tree branches arching over the gravel road like ribs. When I stepped back into the house, I realized something I hadn't noticed before. Every window on the ground floor had tiny, ancient iron hinges at the top of the frames. They weren't built to slide up and down. They were built to swing outward, as if the builders knew that one day, the pressure inside would need an escape.

By the fourth week of living here, the house began to actively reject me. It sounds crazy to say out loud, but houses have a rhythm, and the rectory's rhythm turned hostile.

It started with the water. The plumbing in the master bathroom on the second floor began to run brown, emitting a smell so violently foul I had to tape the drain shut with heavy-duty plastic. It wasn't the sulfur odor you get from well water; it smelled like stagnant pond water mixed with old hair and raw decay. When I called a local plumber from the next town over, he asked for my address. The moment I said "Old St. Jude’s Rectory," there was a long, static-filled silence on the line before he simply hung up. He didn't answer when I called back.  
Then came the auditory distortions. I’d be sitting in my third-floor office, completely focused on a design layout, when I’d hear the heavy oak front door on the ground floor slam shut. I’d grab my flashlight, creep down all three flights of stairs, only to find the deadbolt locked tight and the chain still engaged.

One night, while frying an egg in the kitchen around midnight, I clearly heard the sound of footsteps shuffling directly above my head on the second-floor landing. They were heavy, unhurried paces, accompanied by the distinct, rhythmic scratching of fabric—like the thick wool of a long coat dragging against the wainscoting.

I stood there with the spatula in my hand, staring up at the water-stained plaster ceiling. The footsteps marched directly to the door of the old master bedroom, stopped for a full two minutes, and then simply evaporated into the silence.

I convinced myself it was just the structural settling of an unheated, century-old Victorian timber house. I told myself that the isolation was playing tricks on my mind, that I needed to take a weekend trip back to the city just to hear traffic and see crowds. I promised myself I’d pack a bag the next morning.  
I never got the chance.

Last night, around two in the morning, a massive summer thunderstorm rolled through the valley. The lightning was violent, throwing long, skeletal shadows of the window frames across my office walls. I was deep into a heavy rendering project, the cold blue glow of my dual monitors providing the only light in the attic.

Then, right over the rumble of the thunder, I heard it.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

It was a sharp, brittle sound. It wasn't the soft, rhythmic brushing of a pine branch against the siding. It wasn't the chaotic, heavy splatter of rain. It was the unmistakable, solid thud of a hard human knuckle striking glass.

Every muscle in my body locked up. I stared at the dark pane, my pulse hammering in my throat, before the sheer reality of the layout hit me.

My office is thirty feet off the ground. The exterior brick wall is completely flush, dropping straight down to a concrete patio below. There is no balcony. There is no fire escape. There is no ledge. The nearest oak tree is fifteen yards away across the lawn. There was physically nowhere for a human being to stand.

I forced myself out of my chair, my knees feeling like jelly. *A bird,* I muttered out loud, just to hear a human voice. *A disoriented bird got caught in the storm and hit the glass.*

I took three slow steps forward. The air in the attic suddenly plummeted, the temperature dropping so fast I could see the faint puff of my own breath. Another flash of lightning ripped across the sky, turning the night bright white for a fraction of a second.

In that split second, I saw a silhouette.  
Hovering directly outside the third-floor glass was a tall, unnaturally elongated shape. It wore the tattered, dripping outline of a black Catholic cassock. Its shoulders were hunched forward at a sickening angle, and where its face should have been, there was only a hollow, shifting smear of pure shadow that seemed to drink the light from my computer screens.

The thing raised a pale, skeletal hand—the fingers were far too long, with yellowed, cracked nails—and pressed a knuckle flat against the glass.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

As it knocked, a thick layer of icy frost bloomed across the window. But it wasn't forming on the outside where the storm was raging. It was rapidly spreading across the *inside* of my office. Right where the entity’s fingers touched the exterior pane, three greasy smudges appeared in the frost on *my* side of the glass, mimicking the movement from within the room.

I didn't scream. My throat completely closed up. I scrambled backward, tripping over the wheels of my desk chair and slamming hard onto the floorboards. The creature didn't move. It just hung there in the empty air, thirty feet up in the pouring rain, staring down at me through that featureless void of a face.  
I dragged myself backward on my hands and knees, tore the attic door open, and practically threw myself down the stairs in the pitch black. I didn't stop running until I hit the ground-floor living room, cramming my back against the interior wall furthest from any glass. I sat there shivering until the sun finally came up, flinching at every creak of the house.

This morning, the storm cleared out, leaving behind a bright, hot summer day. The sun streamed through the lower windows, casting beautiful, deceptive bars of gold across the hardwood. For an hour or two, I managed to convince myself that I’d just had a severe panic attack, a waking nightmare brought on by too much caffeine, lack of sleep, and reading those godforsaken journals.

I forced myself back up to the attic, determined to look at the window in the cold, rational light of day.  
The window was completely dry. But when I got right up to it, my heart sank into my stomach. Etched deep into the antique glass—not scratched into the dirt or dust, but physically gouged directly into the physical glass itself—were three deep, jagged grooves. They looked like they had been carved with a diamond cutter or a shard of flint.  
I sank into my office chair, my hands shaking so badly I could barely turn the pages of Father Thomas’s journal. I thumbed frantically toward the back of the book, seeking out the final entries from September 1975. The handwriting here was barely legible; it looked like the tracking of an active seismograph during an earthquake.

I found his very last entry, dated just forty-eight hours before he vanished into thin air. I had completely missed it the night before:

*"He has broken out of the confessional box entirely now. The stones of St. Jude's can no longer hold him. He mimics the shape of the cloth, but he is nothing more than the living rot of our parish's unconfessed cruelties. He cannot cross the threshold unless you look him in the eyes and acknowledge what he carries. Do not look. Do not answer.*

*If you hear him testing the panes, kill the lights immediately. If he captures your gaze through the glass, your eyes become his doorway. He will pour the collective filth of a hundred dead men into your skull, and you will become the vessel. I can hear him at the bedroom window now. My candle is dying. May God have mercy on my soul, because I cannot keep my eyes closed much longer."*

That was the end of the book. The remaining fifty pages were completely blank, except for a dark, circular grease stain on the very last sheet that smelled faintly of old tallow.

I spent the rest of the afternoon in a state of absolute, paralyzed panic. I tried to pack my bags, but every time I walked near the front door, a wave of profound, unnatural lethargy would wash over me. My limbs felt heavy, like they were filled with wet sand. A voice in the back of my mind—smooth, oily, and completely foreign—kept whispering that there was no point in running. 

*Where would you go?* it asked. *The roads are slick. The woods are deep. Just stay inside where it's dry.*

By 6:00 PM, the sky began to turn a bruised, sickly purple. I knew I couldn't handle another night in this house with the power on. If a light flickered, it would create a reflection. It would give the thing a surface to work with.

I walked down to the main electrical breaker in the hallway and threw the master switch. The entire house dropped into a dead, suffocating silence. The refrigerator stopped its low hum. The digital clock on the stove died. The only sound left was the frantic, erratic thumping of my own heart against my ribs.

I dragged my mattress into the dead center of the living room floor, as far away from the windows as humanly possible. I wrapped myself in a heavy wool blanket, gripped Father Thomas's black leather journal to my chest like a shield, and stared straight down at the floorboards between my knees.

The sun dropped below the tree line at exactly 8:42 PM.

The cold arrived five minutes later.

It didn't drift in like a draft; it dropped like an anvil. The temperature in the living room plummeted from a humid eighty degrees down to what felt like absolute freezing in a matter of seconds. I could see the thick, grey plumes of my own breath rising in the dark, hanging in the air like smoke.  
Then, the testing began.

At 9:15 PM, I heard the first sound. It was a low, heavy wet thud against the back mudroom door at the far end of the house. It sounded like a massive sack of grain being dropped against the wood.

*Thud.*

I squeezed my eyes shut, pressing my forehead against my knees.

Ten minutes later, the sound shifted to the kitchen window. The old wood-framed glass rattled violently, the iron latches buzzing like angry hornets as something heavy leaned its weight against the pane.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

Then the dining room window. Then the small decorative glass pane in the downstairs bathroom. It was circling the house, checking every single sheet of glass, looking for a crack, looking for an eye, looking for a way in.

And just a few seconds ago, it found the living room.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

It’s right behind my head. The living room window is a massive bay window that looks out onto the gravel driveway. I am sitting less than three feet away from it. I can hear the structural wood of the frame groaning under immense pressure, the ancient mortar between the exterior bricks crumbling and clicking as it falls onto the bushes outside.

Along with the knocking, there’s a wet, garbled whispering bleeding straight through the glass. It doesn't sound like one person. It sounds like a stadium filled with people, all speaking at the exact same time, their voices overlapping and fighting for dominance. They are crying. They are whimpering. They are screaming out horrible, violent secrets into the dark.

*“I buried the money under the floorboards…”*

*“I let him drink the poison…”*

*“I left her in the woods to freeze…”*

The voices are trying to force me to turn around. They are calling my name now, mixing it into the chorus of dead sins. They want me to look through the glass to see who is speaking. They want me to acknowledge the weight.

My phone battery is at 3%. If anyone out there knows how to seal a house, or how to get rid of something like this, please tell me. I can’t leave the center of the room. The air around the perimeter of the walls feels like solid ice.

**UPDATE**

My battery is at 1%. This is it. This is the last update I’ll ever be able to post.

The glass didn't just rattle this time. It cracked. A loud, sharp *snap* like a rifle shot echoed right behind my ears. I didn't turn around, but I can feel the change in the air pressure. The thick, rancid smell of old copper and rotted meat is pouring into the living room through the fracture, so heavy and dense I can literally taste it on the back of my tongue.

The frost is spreading across the floorboards now. I can hear the wood grain popping and freezing as the ice creeps toward my bare feet like frozen fingers.

The voices aren't a mumble anymore. They have stopped whispering. They are shouting. They are screaming through the split in the glass, a chaotic chorus of old townspeople confessing to arsons, affairs, and unspeakable violence, all bleeding together into one deafening, wet, agonizing roar that is vibrating the teeth in my jaw.

And then, a new voice broke through the chaos.  
It wasn't old. It wasn't distorted by time or static. It was loud. It was clear. It sounded exactly like my own voice.

The voice whispered a specific date from three years ago. October 14th. The night of the heavy storm on the dark county road. The rainy night I told myself I’d just hit a deer. The night I looked in my rearview mirror, saw the crumpled shape lying in the ditch, and hit the gas anyway. The night I never called the police, never told a single human soul, and buried deep down in the dark of my chest so I could keep living my comfortable, selfish life.  
*He knows.*

The entity doesn't just carry the parish’s old sins. It uncovers yours. It tracks the hidden rot inside your soul like a shark smelling blood in the water. It didn't come here by accident. It followed the scent of what I did.

I’m staring down at my dying phone screen, and in the dark, reflective glass of the black screen, I can see the window behind me. I didn't turn around, but the reflection showed it anyway.

The tall, tattered black cassock is standing right inside the broken frame. The shifting, faceless void of shadow is leaning directly over my shoulder.  
It’s pressing its entire hollow face against the shattered pane now, the remaining shards of glass slicing into its pale, bloodless skin without making it bleed. The wood of the frame is completely giving way.

If I don't say it out loud, it’s going to tear through the glass and drag me into that dark void. Father Thomas was right. It doesn't just want to terrorize; it wants a mouth to speak through. It wants a vessel to carry the weight of everything this town—and I—have hidden.

I can’t let it inside my head. I can’t let it take my mind. I have to give it what it wants. I have to let the weight out.

I’m setting the phone down on the floorboards now.  
   
The screen is flickering.

I’m opening my mouth.

I’m going to confess.
