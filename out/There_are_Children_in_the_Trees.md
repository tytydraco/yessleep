I've read a lot of stories on here about people hearing things in the woods. 

Voices, laughing, footsteps that match your own pace and then don't. I never had much of an opinion on whether any of it was real, because I'd spent probably four hundred nights of my life alone in a tent in the backcountry and nothing had ever once made me feel like I wasn't alone out there except, you know, actually being alone out there. I want to say that up front because I think it matters. I wasn't a nervous hiker. I'm not a nervous person. I need you to understand that before I tell you what changed that.

This was in the Ozarks, a section of national forest I won't name for reasons that will make sense by the end, on a solo overnight trip in late September. I'd hiked that forest a dozen times before, but always the same loop off the main trailhead, the one with the sign-in box and the gravel lot that fills up on weekends. 

This time I wanted something quieter, so I'd found an old spur trail on a paper map at a gas station two towns over, barely marked, no name I could find online, that supposedly connected to a creek crossing about seven miles in. I'd never set foot on it. I left the trailhead around two in the afternoon. Clear day, cool, that first-real-hint-of-fall smell in the air that I love more than almost anything.

The spur trail started easy enough, a soft, needle-packed path winding down through pine and scrub oak, and for the first hour or so I was in the exact headspace I go looking for on solo trips, no phone signal, no particular thought in my head, just the rhythm of my own boots and my pack settling into place on my shoulders. 

I passed a dry creek bed around mile two, a couple of old fire rings someone had built and abandoned who knows how many years back, the ordinary debris of a trail that doesn't see much traffic. I remember feeling smug about it, actually, pleased with myself for finding somewhere quiet this late in the season. 

The trail narrowed as the pines gave way to hardwood, oak and hickory mostly, canopy thickening overhead, and I didn't think about any of that as a bad sign. I think about it now, obviously. At the time it just felt like good hiking.

Around mile four, I noticed the gravel had gone strange under my boots.

I don't know how else to describe it except that it stopped sounding right. Trail gravel has a particular crunch to it, sharp, uneven, and what I was walking on for a stretch of maybe a hundred yards had a smoother, duller give, almost like walking on packed sand instead of stone. I remember looking down once, seeing pale, small, round little fragments mixed in with the dirt, and thinking, in the completely unbothered way you think about things when your brain hasn't caught up to a threat yet, that it must be some kind of local mineral deposit, chalky limestone maybe, worn down fine. 

I did not stop to look closer. That's the detail I keep returning to, because if I had crouched down right then, in full daylight, I think the whole rest of the night might have gone differently. I might have turned around.

By mile six, my legs were done arguing with me, and I finally sat down on a flat rock just off the trail to eat something and get the weight off my shoulders before the last push to the creek. It was the first time all day I'd actually stopped moving, and it's strange how much more a place tells you once you're not walking through it anymore. 

Sitting still, I noticed the ground around the rock was covered in the same pale fragments I'd been half-registering for two miles, thicker here, undisturbed, the way things settle when nobody's been stepping on them yet. I picked one up without really thinking about it, turning it over between my fingers while I chewed, waiting for my brain to file it under something ordinary the way it had all afternoon.

It didn't file it under anything ordinary. It was too smooth on one side and too rough on the other, a texture no stone has, and it had a faint curve to it, almost a shape, and there was a second, harder ridge along one edge that my thumb kept finding before I let myself understand what my thumb already knew. I picked up a second one. Then a third, from a few feet away, because some part of me needed more than one data point before I'd believe the first. All three had that same curve, that same small ridge, and by the fourth one I was no longer telling myself a story about limestone. 

I was looking down at a scatter of small, root-blunt teeth, some yellowed with age and some so white and fresh they looked wet, spread thick around the rock I was sitting on and thinning out in both directions along the trail, like something had been losing them steadily, one at a time, over a very long walk that ended, or began, right where I'd chosen to stop and rest.

I want it on record that every instinct I had told me to turn back right then, and I ignored it, because it was getting dark, I was six miles down a trail I'd never hiked before, and turning around felt riskier than just pushing on to the creek like I'd planned. I recognize now that's exactly the kind of reasoning something patient would want me to land on.

I made camp near the creek anyway, a hundred yards or so past the rock, close enough that I could still see the pale scatter behind me if I turned my headlamp that way, which I made a point not to do. I told myself a story about some kind of old ossuary or animal kill site that I did not believe even as I was thinking it.

I ate dinner fast and without much appetite, some dehydrated pasta thing I barely tasted, and I remember being almost aggressively normal about the rest of my routine, hanging my food bag, brushing my teeth with water from my bottle, zipping myself into my sleeping bag at what was, for me, an unusually early hour. I wanted the day to be over. I left the tent flap half-unzipped out of habit, the way I always do, enough to see a slice of sky and gauge the weather without having to actually get up, and I lay there for a long while listening to the creek and telling myself that tomorrow, in daylight, the teeth would turn out to be something explainable, deer bone fragments, some quarry runoff, anything.

It was right at the edge of sleep, that loose, drifting point where you're not quite thinking in full sentences anymore, that something in the sliver of sky through my tent flap snagged my attention and pulled me back up out of it. I lay there for a second not sure what had bothered me, and then I understood it was the stars, or what I'd assumed were stars, that little wedge of black canopy gap visible past the unzipped flap. Something about the number of them, or the way they were arranged, low and close together instead of scattered high and even the way stars always are, didn't match what I expected to see, and I found myself sitting up on one elbow, watching that same patch of dark for a long, uneasy minute before I made myself unzip the flap the rest of the way and look properly.

I'd been out at night in deep canopy more times than I can count, and I know what a clear sky looks like glimpsed between leaves: cold white points, motionless, far away. What I was looking at instead, high up in the black shapes of the branches around my camp, were dozens of small points of light with a faint warmth to their color, something closer to candlelight than starlight, and as I watched, several of them blinked, all at once, the way a hundred small pairs of eyes blink together when something below them moves.

I put my headlamp on its brightest setting and pointed it straight up, and for one full second before my brain refused to process it correctly...

I saw a child.

Small. Younger than ten by the proportions.

Sitting on a branch far too thin to hold real weight, legs dangling, watching me with an expression of mild curiosity. 

Its skin in the beam of my light was bone white, and its mouth was open slightly in something I first read as a smile before I understood it was full, edge to edge, of small, close-set, needle-fine teeth. When I swung the light wider, shaking hard by then, I found not one child but a dozen at least, spaced through the branches of three or four trees ringing my camp, all facing down, all watching, all with those same warm, unnaturally steady points of light where their eyes should have been.

I understood, with a kind of sick, sliding clarity, exactly what had been scattered across that quarter mile of trail, and exactly why the gravel had felt wrong under my boots hours before I'd had the courage to look at it.

I did the only thing my body would let me do, which was get up, get my pack half-zipped with shaking hands, and start moving back down the trail the way I'd come, headlamp aimed at the ground because I could not make myself look up again.

I didn't get thirty yards before I saw her standing in the trail.

I want to describe this as carefully and as honestly as I can, because I've gone over it so many times since that I'm afraid of embellishing it into something cleaner than it actually was. 

She was standing perfectly still in the middle of the trail, facing me, and she gave off a faint pale light of her own, the same warm, wrong glow as the eyes in the branches, like something under her skin was lit from within rather than reflecting my headlamp. 

She was tall, taller than any woman I've stood in front of, with the same bone-white skin as the small ones in the trees, stretched over a frame that was recognizably a woman's, shoulders narrow, waist tapering, hips full, thighs heavy and rounded in a way that should have looked soft and instead looked dense, like something built rather than grown, her breasts full and unremarkable in shape but faintly luminous at the skin the same way the rest of her was, so that even that ordinary part of her carried the wrongness the rest of her body did. 

Every proportion was pushed very slightly past what a human body actually does, so that looking at her felt like looking at a technically correct drawing of a woman made by something that had only ever studied the shape from a distance. She was unclothed, and there was nothing performative or inviting in it. It read the way an animal's nakedness reads, simply the way she existed, without shame or intention either way. 

Her eyes, when I finally made myself look at her face, were a flat, milky blue, the color of a blind dog's eyes, and behind her shoulders, folded close against her back, I could see two long, veined shapes, moth wings, far larger than the small folded ones on the children in the branches, twitching very faintly with a texture like dust caught in a light beam.

She did not move toward me at first. She only stood there, watching, with an expression I can only describe as patient.

I turned to run back toward the creek crossing, thinking I could cut wide of her through the brush, and that's when the smell hit me.

It came out of nowhere, no breeze that I could feel to carry it, and it was so specific and so overwhelming that my legs actually slowed down on their own before my mind caught up to what was happening. I was surrounded by the smell of my childhood home. 

Not close to it, not similar to it, exactly it, down to a note of my mother's hand soap and the particular mustiness of the hallway closet where we kept winter coats, a smell I hadn't consciously thought about in over twenty years and would not have been able to describe to you an hour earlier if you'd asked me to. 

It hit some part of my brain that had nothing to do with fear, and for three or four seconds I wasn't running from anything. I was seven years old standing in a hallway that no longer exists, and my whole body went warm and slow and heavy in a way that had nothing to do with exhaustion.

My legs gave out somewhere in that fog. 

I remember the ground coming up slower than it should have, like I was moving through water. Even as I was going down, I heard a sound start up all around me in the branches. Not laughing this time, but a low, even hum. Dozens of small voices pitched together in a single sustained note that reminded me, absurdly, of the sound an electric car makes idling at a stoplight. It was felt more than heard, vibrating up through the ground into my chest.

I saw her walking toward me while I was on my knees in the dirt, and even now, with everything I know, I have to be honest: there was something in the way she moved that my body responded to as beautiful before my mind could object. A long, unhurried grace. Each step placed as though she had never, in her entire existence, needed to hurry for anything. 

The humming in the branches didn't change pitch as she got closer. If anything it settled, evened out, like the sound itself was steadying to match her pace.

She crouched down in front of me, and up close the wrongness of her was almost worse for how careful it was, symmetrical in a way real faces aren't. The milky blue eyes not tracking quite the way eyes should, fixed on my face with an attention that felt total. 

She reached out and adjusted the collar of my jacket, an oddly domestic gesture, the kind of small correction a mother makes to a child's clothing without thinking about it. I understood in that moment, with the smell of my own childhood still thick in my throat, exactly what role I had wandered into and exactly how long some part of her had likely been waiting for someone to fill it.

Then she knelt fully in front of me.

The long shapes at her back unfolded, slow and deliberate, spreading wider than I would have believed they could. She drew them forward around me until they closed behind my back.

A dry, papery warmth settled on either side of my face and shoulders, folding me against her the way you'd wrap a child in a blanket too big for them.

I couldn't have pulled away by then even if the fog in my head had let me want to. She leaned in and kissed me on the lips... slow, cool and dry, and that's the last clear thing I remember. The humming, the smell, her wings closed around me like a second, softer kind of dark, and then nothing.

I woke up at first light, on my back in the same spot on the trail, jacket still adjusted the way she'd left it, pack still on my shoulder like I'd never taken it off. The branches above me were empty. 

No eyes, no small shapes, nothing but ordinary trunks and ordinary gray morning light, and if it hadn't been for the smell still faintly caught in the fabric of my collar, my mother's hand soap, that closet, I could have told myself by lunchtime I'd fainted alone in the woods and dreamed the rest out of low blood sugar and bad instinct.

I packed up in about four minutes and hiked the six miles back to the trailhead without stopping once, and I have not gone back to that section of forest, and I don't intend to.

It's been about six months now. I want to tell you that's the end of it, that I got out and the story stops there, but I'd be lying, and I think lying to you at this point would be worse than whatever you're about to think of me.

Twice in the last month, I've woken up on the floor of my own front hallway, doors locked, alarm untouched, with no memory of getting out of bed, and both times the house smelled, faintly but unmistakably, like my mother's hand soap, a scent I haven't kept in this house a single day since I moved out of my childhood home twenty years ago.

Last week I found a single small, white, root-blunt tooth sitting in the tray by my front door where I keep my keys.

I haven't told my wife. I don't know how to say it in a way that doesn't sound like I need to be committed, and some small, warm, humming part of me, a part I don't fully trust anymore, keeps telling me there's nothing to be afraid of, that I'm only being called home.
