Never leave the heavy machinery yard unlocked after nine, and never stay behind alone to run the dry-kiln blowers. That was the standing rule on the south yard of the Blackwood timber cut. If you broke it, and a line froze or a kiln motor seized up without a full crew on site, the company fired you that night and kept your equipment deposit.  
By twenty past ten, I was still out there. The second-shift mechanic, a local guy named Dan, hadn’t signed off on the manifold pressure for Kiln Four. The yard was dead quiet. We were seven miles outside of town in an Appalachian cut where the ridge blocks radio reception. My work truck was idling near the chain-link gate, low beams pointed into the gravel.  
I walked over to the kiln bay to check the valves myself. Smelled like damp sawdust and motor oil. The maintenance floor was swept clean, but Dan’s hard hat, his leather work gloves, and his clipboard were sitting right in the middle of his bench. His truck was parked right outside the rollup door, doors locked and engine cold. Honestly, I figured he'd hitched a ride into town with the evening timber hauler and left the rest of the sheet for me to finish.  
I picked up the yard radio off the bench, keyed the mic, and called for him twice on channel three. Nothing came back through the speaker. Just dead carrier hum. No static. No click. I put the radio down, checked my cell, and saw the screen was sitting on zero service. That wasn't unusual out there. I grabbed a fourteen-inch pipe wrench off the wall to work the manual bypass on the kiln feed.  
Right then, the floodlights on the south shed buzzed once and went black. The yard went dark, except for my headlights and the little red pilot bulb over Kiln Four.  
I walked out onto the gravel. Kiln Four usually runs at eighteen hundred RPM. It shakes the ground enough that you feel it through the soles of your boots. The slab was completely still. I walked up to the analog gauge on the side of the housing. The brass needle was pinned hard past thirty pounds, way into the red. I took the wrench and cracked the manual bleeder valve. Nothing came out. No air, no water, no pressure release. The tank was totally depressurized, but the needle didn't drop. It stayed pinned against the stop.  
Then the phone inside the main office started ringing.  
It was thirty yards back across the yard. It was an old hardwired landline the county used for timber permits. It rang three times, stopped, and rang again. Nobody had a key to that trailer except me and Wayne, the site super. Wayne had left for Charleston around three that afternoon to handle a boundary dispute over an old homestead the company had cleared out months back.  
I walked over, stepped up the wood stairs, and turned the knob. The door opened right up. The deadbolt wasn't caught at all, even though I knew I'd locked it when the crew took off. The room smelled like cold coffee. The desk light was switched off. The receiver sat in the cradle, quiet, but the red message light on the base was lit solid.  
I picked it up and held it to my ear. No dial tone. No line noise. Just dead air for three seconds. Then my own voice came through the earpiece, perfectly clear. It repeated the exact call I'd put into the radio five minutes earlier. \*Dan, check in on three. Dan, check in on three.\* The pitch was flat. The volume didn't change. It sounded like an audio file played back through a clean speaker.  
I dropped the receiver onto the desk and stepped back outside. The air had turned freezing. My breath was fogging up in the red light off the kiln. I looked across the gravel toward the north log decks where the fresh pine was stacked up waiting for morning transport.  
There was a man standing right at the edge of the stacks, about sixty yards out.  
He was dead still. He had dark clothes on, but he didn't have a headlamp and he wasn't holding a flashlight. Nobody walks that yard in the dark without a light because the ground is covered in scrap iron and loose cables. He stood straight up, shoulders squared, facing the trailer. He didn't wave. He didn't move his boots. He just stood there against the pine logs.  
I kept my hand on the pipe wrench. I shouted across the lot, telling him the yard was closed and that the gate was being locked for the night.  
He didn't move. He didn't turn his head. Nothing made a sound except my truck idling behind me.  
I didn't walk out to the woodpile. I walked straight to my truck, unlocked the door, climbed inside, and hit the door locks.  
I threw the truck into reverse to swing around toward the main drive. When my headlights swept past the maintenance bay, I stopped.  
Dan’s truck wasn't there.  
The dirt spot where it had been sitting locked up ten minutes earlier was totally empty. No tire tracks in the dry dust. No exhaust in the air. Nothing. The sliding gate on the access lane was fifty yards away, and the heavy chain and padlock were still hanging tight from the inside.  
I dropped the truck into drive, pulled straight up to the fence, set the emergency brake, and got the bolt cutters out of the bed. I cut the master lock off the chain, pushed the gate open just wide enough to clear the mirrors, and drove out. I didn't let off the gas until I hit the twenty-four-hour diesel station twelve miles down the state route.  
The troopers and the super met me out at the yard at daybreak. The office door was locked up tight. The analog gauge on Kiln Four was sitting flat at zero like it was supposed to. Dan’s truck was parked inside the closed shop bay with the keys sitting on the dashboard, his hard hat right where he left it, and the shift log signed off all the way to midnight. Dan wasn't there, and his truck was still in the shop when the troopers checked his place in town. When the county investigator checked the company safe that afternoon for the property deed on the north tract, the file was missing, and the safe door was standing open two inches.
