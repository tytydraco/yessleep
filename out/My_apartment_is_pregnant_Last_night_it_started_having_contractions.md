At 2:13 this morning, my bedroom got smaller.

There used to be enough room between the foot of my bed and my desk for me to walk through. Not much, but enough. I crossed that gap every morning to turn off my alarm.

When I woke up last night, the desk was pressed against the bed frame.

I was still trying to figure out how I could have moved either of them in my sleep when both walls moved.

The bed frame shrieked across the floorboards. My nightstand tipped over. The radiator folded inward like an accordion and spat black water across the rug.

For about ten seconds, the room tightened around me.

Then the walls relaxed.

The radiator straightened with three metallic pops. The bed slid backward, but the gap between it and the desk did not return.

There was also a door beside my closet.

It was about three feet high and covered in the same yellow wallpaper as the bedroom. There was no knob. A shallow impression had been pressed into the center, shaped like a human palm.

That door had not been there when I went to sleep.

I stood in the hallway wearing boxers and one sock, staring into the room while black water dripped from the radiator.

Seven minutes later, the apartment contracted again.

This time every room tightened.

The hallway narrowed until both walls touched my shoulders. The ceiling sank low enough to brush my hair. From the kitchen came the sound of cabinets slamming and dishes breaking. Something wet moved through the pipes with a long squeezing noise.

Then it stopped.

I grabbed my phone and ran outside.

Five people were waiting in the fourth-floor hallway.

Mrs. Harlan from 4A stood directly across from my door in a clear plastic apron. She held a stack of folded towels against her chest. The man who lived below me carried a metal bucket full of steaming water. I recognized the others from the elevator and laundry room, though I had never spoken to them.

Nobody looked surprised.

Mrs. Harlan checked the timer on her phone.

“Seven minutes,” she said. “That’s quick.”

“What’s quick?”

She looked past me into the apartment.

“The contractions.”

I called Mara, my landlord.

She answered on the second ring.

“My apartment just got smaller.”

“How far apart?”

“What?”

“The contractions.”

I looked at Mrs. Harlan.

“Seven minutes.”

“Has the inside door opened?”

“No.”

“Good. Don’t touch it. Move anything blocking the bedroom.”

“What is happening?”

“Call me when they reach five minutes.”

She hung up.

Mrs. Harlan shifted the towels in her arms.

“You should put some clothes on,” she said. “It gets cold afterward.”

To explain why I did not run down the stairs and keep going, I need to explain what Apartment 4C had already done for me.

My mother and I moved constantly when I was growing up.

Apartments, motels, shelters, friends’ houses, men’s houses. Once we stayed in a farmhouse that belonged to a man serving time for armed robbery. My mother told me he was her cousin. Years later she admitted she had never met him.

She kept our important belongings in plastic tubs so we could leave quickly. I learned not to hang posters because tape peeled paint. If something could not fit inside one backpack, I tried not to care about it.

My mother kept old keys too.

Whenever we moved, she dropped the key to the old place into a coffee tin. Sometimes she had forgotten to return it. Sometimes we left before anyone asked. Sometimes she made a copy without telling the landlord.

The tin followed us everywhere.

Most of the keys probably stopped opening anything years ago. One was bent near the end from when my mother tried to pry open a bathroom window. Another belonged to a motel dresser drawer, though I never understood why the drawer had a lock.

Whenever I asked why she kept them, she said, “You never know when a place might let you back in.”

When I cried about leaving somewhere, she had a different answer.

“Walls don’t love you back.”

She usually said it while throwing our clothes into trash bags.

By twenty-seven, I had lived in more places than I could list in order. Most were rented rooms. One was a converted storage unit. I slept on a friend’s enclosed porch for almost a year and called it a studio because that sounded intentional.

Then I found the listing for Apartment 4C.

ONE BEDROOM. UTILITIES INCLUDED. QUIET BUILDING. LONG-TERM TENANT PREFERRED.

The rent was low enough that I assumed someone would steal my deposit and block my number.

The building stood at the end of a narrow street between a closed elementary school and a concrete drainage canal. It was six stories tall, built from dark red brick, with narrow windows that did not line up quite right.

From across the street, it looked as if the building were leaning forward to listen.

Mara met me in the lobby.

That was the only name she gave me.

She had colorless hair, a gray suit with brass buttons, and a ring of keys hanging from her belt. Instead of asking about my credit score, she asked which room I remembered best from childhood.

“The motel outside Wichita,” I said.

“What color were the walls?”

“Blue.”

“Did you feel safe there?”

“Sometimes.”

She wrote that down.

“Did anyone sing to you?”

“My mother.”

“What did she sing?”

“Is this part of the application?”

“It helps us place you.”

I thought she meant deciding which unit I would get.

The moment I stepped into 4C, I wanted it.

The apartment was not beautiful. The floorboards were scratched. The kitchen appliances were older than I was. Yellow wallpaper covered every room except the bathroom, where the tile was pale green.

But pieces of it felt familiar.

The cabinets were the same green as the ones in my aunt’s basement. The bedroom radiator clicked nine times before turning on, just like the heater in the Wichita motel. A water stain above the front door looked like Oklahoma. I had stared at one shaped exactly like it when we lived in the farmhouse.

It felt less like entering a new apartment and more like opening a box of things I had forgotten I owned.

Mara watched me walk through the rooms.

“It adapts,” she said.

“To what?”

“Occupancy.”

I thought she meant the heating system.

I signed the lease that afternoon.

For the first month, I kept most of my clothes in plastic tubs. Then one night I realized I had lived there for five weeks and had not checked rental listings once.

I unpacked everything.

I bought curtains. Shelves. A desk too heavy to move alone. I framed photographs and hammered real nails into the walls.

That mattered to me.

I was leaving damage I expected to be around long enough to pay for.

The apartment started doing small things for me.

A picture I hung crooked would be straight when I came home. The bathroom light switched on before I reached the door. A mug left beside my bed appeared clean in the kitchen cabinet the next morning.

When I forgot to close a window during a storm, I woke to find it latched. The rainwater had been wiped from the floor.

I called Mara and asked whether anyone had entered.

“No,” she said. “It likes you.”

She laughed after saying it, so I did too.

The first time I knew the apartment was listening was because of my mother.

She called one Thursday while I was eating noodles over the sink. We had not spoken in six weeks.

“Are you still in that place over the laundromat?” she asked.

“I haven’t lived there since college.”

“Oh.”

I heard her lighter click.

“Which place is this?”

I told her.

“Is that the one with the porch?”

“That was three places ago.”

“Right. Sorry. I’m trying to keep up.”

She said it as if my moving had been exhausting for her.

Then she asked for four hundred dollars.

I had two hundred and sixty dollars until payday. I told her that. She asked whether I could put it on a credit card.

“I thought you said the new job paid well.”

“It pays fine.”

“And this apartment is cheap?”

“Yes.”

“So what do you spend your money on?”

I looked at the green cabinets. At the curtains I had bought new instead of pulling somebody else’s from a trash bag.

“Things for the apartment.”

My mother made a sound that was not quite a laugh.

“Careful,” she said. “Walls don’t love you back.”

Then she hung up.

I stood over the sink until the noodles swelled into a cold lump.

The kitchen light went out.

I thought the bulb had blown, but the lamp in the living room switched on. Then the bedroom lamp.

A path of warm light led away from the kitchen.

I followed it.

The bed had moved against the wall. I had left enough space between them for my phone charger to hang down. Now the mattress rested inside a shallow curve in the plaster, as though the room had made a place for it.

I lay down.

The wall was warm through my shirt.

The radiator clicked nine times. Then the pipes tapped the first few notes of the song my mother used to sing in the motel.

Not a recording. Not a voice.

Just soft knocks moving through the wall.

I cried until my nose bled.

The wall stayed warm until I fell asleep.

That should have been the moment I left.

Instead, it was the moment 4C became home.

After that, I stopped thinking of the things it did as intrusions.

When my desk chair began hurting my back, the floor beneath it rose slightly. When I bought a lamp, an outlet appeared behind the table where I wanted to put it. Coffee stains faded from the rug.

I worked remotely. I stopped going to coffee shops. Then I stopped going anywhere except the grocery store.

Other people’s apartments felt careless. Their rooms did not know where they were going to walk. Their walls stayed cold when they were sad.

I knew that was not a normal thought.

I did not care.

It was easy to ignore the strange things because the strange things were kind.

Then my hair began falling out.

At first it was only a few strands on my pillow. Then enough gathered in the shower drain that I started checking my scalp.

One afternoon, I found three hairs growing from the outlet beside my desk.

I crouched to look closer.

They were dark, fine, and curled at the ends. I pulled one.

It resisted.

When I pulled harder, pain flashed across my scalp.

I let go.

The strand slid backward into the outlet.

I unplugged everything and covered the socket with tape.

That night, a faint buzzing kept me awake. I peeled the tape away.

The outlet was packed with thin copper wire.

The wires curled the way my hair curled when it got wet.

I called Mara.

“There’s hair coming out of my wall.”

“Copper?”

“I think so.”

“How long have you been in the unit?”

“Four months.”

“Any movement inside the walls?”

“What kind of movement?”

“Fluttering. Pressure. Something changing position.”

I looked toward the bedroom.

The wall beside the bed gave one soft thump.

“Maybe.”

“Has a door appeared?”

“No.”

She breathed out.

It sounded like disappointment.

“Call me if one does.”

The next morning, I woke with a gap between my front teeth.

There was no blood on the pillow. The gum had already closed. It looked as though the tooth had been gone for years.

I searched the bed and floor.

Then something rolled beneath the bathroom tile.

I heard it travel from the wall to the center of the room. It stopped under my bare foot.

The pale green tile had turned cloudy.

My tooth was inside it.

Not underneath it. Inside.

Fine white roots spread from the tooth into the grout.

I packed a bag.

The apartment made small attempts to stop me.

My closet had grown shallower around the plastic tub holding my clothes. The front door would not stay open. My keys disappeared from the hook and turned up two hours later frozen inside an ice cube.

I melted them out under hot water.

As soon as I touched the doorknob, the wall behind me thumped.

Once.

Twice.

Then a quick flutter, like heels kicking against a mattress.

I left anyway.

I made it across the street before I realized I could not remember my mother’s face.

I knew facts about her. Blond hair. Menthol cigarettes. A scar through one eyebrow. She bit the inside of her cheek when she drove.

But the face itself was gone.

I could remember the Wichita motel room in perfect detail. Blue walls. Brown flowered blankets. The crack in the sink.

I remembered that someone had sung to me there.

I did not remember who.

I sat on the curb and tried until my head hurt.

When I looked up, the bedroom window of 4C was glowing.

A person stood behind the curtain.

It was about my height.

I raised my hand.

It raised its hand too.

I went back inside.

My mother’s face returned the moment the door closed behind me.

It did not slowly come into focus. It hit me all at once, every line and pore, so complete that my knees gave out.

The apartment had not erased her.

It had moved her.

I lay on the floor and cried. The boards warmed beneath my cheek.

Something shifted behind the wallpaper, following the shape of my body.

I stayed for three more months.

During that time, I began noticing holes in other memories.

I remembered the apartment above the nail salon but not the owner’s name. I remembered the farmhouse kitchen but not the man who made pancakes there. I remembered a shelter with orange blankets and could not remember whether we stayed there in winter or summer.

The rooms remained. The people thinned out.

I began writing names on the backs of old photographs.

MOM.

AUNT LUCY.

MR. BENSON, MOTEL MANAGER.

Sometimes I found a photograph I had already labeled and still did not know who the person was.

Last week, the bulge appeared in the bedroom wall.

It began near the floor, no larger than a fist. The wallpaper stretched over it without tearing.

Each day it moved a little higher.

Sometimes it shifted when I entered the room. Sometimes it pressed outward and stayed there, as if listening.

I started sleeping on the couch.

The bedroom grew hot. The vents breathed out a sweet smell, like warm milk mixed with wet plaster.

On Sunday, Mrs. Harlan knocked on my door.

She looked at the gap in my teeth.

“You’ve started giving calcium,” she said.

“What?”

“Your tooth.”

I stepped into the hall and pulled the door shut behind me.

Up close, I noticed a thin strip of brass beneath the skin of her throat. I had seen it before and assumed it was jewelry. It moved when she swallowed.

“Do you know what’s happening in there?”

“You should speak to Mara.”

“She won’t tell me anything.”

“Mara manages the property.”

“So?”

“Managers keep things occupied.”

Her voice came through the intercom speaker above us half a second before it came from her mouth.

I looked at the speaker.

Mrs. Harlan touched the brass in her throat.

“Mine took my voice,” she said.

The intercom hissed.

“Not all of it.”

The door to 4C knocked three times from the inside.

Mrs. Harlan looked at it.

“You must have made it feel safe.”

That night, I tried to leave without packing.

The hallway stretched.

My front door stayed twenty feet away no matter how long I walked. The floor moved beneath me like a treadmill. I ran until my lungs burned.

The apartment breathed faster.

When I turned around, the bedroom door was directly behind me.

The bulge in the wall had grown into the shape of a seated person.

I called Mara and screamed until she came upstairs.

She arrived carrying a black leather bag.

Mrs. Harlan came with her.

Mara pressed both palms against the bulge. Something pushed back and briefly showed a face beneath the wallpaper.

She smiled.

“Healthy.”

“What is that?”

“Another unit.”

“Inside my wall?”

“For now.”

She opened her bag and removed a pair of heavy scissors and a brass key.

The key was warm enough that I could feel it when she placed it on the dresser.

“What is it making the unit from?”

Mara looked around the bedroom. At the radiator. The photographs. The green tile visible through the bathroom door.

“Whatever you gave it.”

“I didn’t give it anything.”

The shape behind the wallpaper moved.

Something in my chest moved with it.

“What happens to me?”

“That depends on what it still needs.”

Mrs. Harlan’s voice crackled through the intercom.

“It always needs somebody inside.”

Mara put the scissors and key back into her bag.

“When the door appears, don’t open it. Call me when the contractions reach five minutes.”

“What if I leave?”

She looked toward the hallway.

“You already tried.”

Then they left.

The door appeared last night.

Now, standing in the hallway with five tenants dressed like people preparing for a home birth, I understood that they had all been through some version of this.

The apartment contracted again.

Everyone leaned with the walls.

When it released, Mrs. Harlan checked her phone.

“Five minutes, fourteen seconds.”

The elevator opened.

Mara stepped out wearing a clear apron over her gray suit.

“Inside,” she said.

They entered my apartment and began moving the furniture away from the bedroom. Nobody asked permission. They rolled up the rug and laid towels across the floor.

Mara removed the scissors and key from her bag.

She gave the scissors to Mrs. Harlan.

“When the opening clears, cut it free.”

Mrs. Harlan nodded.

Mara kept the key.

The next contraction came after four minutes and forty-eight seconds.

The small door opened.

Warm, wet air rolled into the bedroom.

There was no hollow space behind it.

There was a room.

At first it looked no deeper than a cupboard. Then my eyes adjusted and the back wall seemed to pull away.

The interior shone beneath a clear membrane. Narrow beams curved overhead. Electrical wires floated in cloudy liquid, pulsing with weak blue light.

At the far end was an apartment folded inward on itself.

My apartment.

The kitchen opened into the bedroom, which opened into the bathroom, which opened back into the kitchen. Doors sat inside other doors. The rooms looked small, but the distances between them were enormous.

In the center, curled against the floor, was a man.

Me.

Not a baby. Not a younger version.

Me as I was now.

His knees were pressed to his chest. Copper wire drifted from his scalp. His teeth were pale green tile. His fingernails were narrow panes of glass.

A white cord ran from his stomach into the kitchen wall behind him.

His eyes opened.

I felt them open.

For one second, I was looking from both sides of the door.

I saw myself kneeling in the bedroom.

I saw Mara behind me.

I saw Mrs. Harlan holding the scissors.

I saw the coffee tin of old keys on the shelf in the living room, even though it was around two corners.

Then the second view disappeared.

I vomited across the towels.

Mrs. Harlan placed a hand on my back.

Her voice came through the kitchen intercom.

“Breathe.”

“What is that?”

“The first tenant,” Mara said.

“That’s me.”

“It learned from you.”

The next contraction came.

The space behind the door did not grow wider. It grew deeper.

A new hallway appeared. A closed door formed in the folded apartment’s kitchen.

The copy of me pressed his hands against the membrane.

Pain spread through my fingertips.

I looked down.

My fingerprints were disappearing.

Inside the new apartment, pale whorls rose across the yellow wallpaper.

“You knew it was taking parts of me.”

Mara said nothing.

“My hair. My tooth.”

The kitchen intercom switched on.

My own voice came through it.

“My memories?”

The words traveled into the walls. I heard myself ask from the bedroom vent, the bathroom drain, and the radiator, each repetition quieter and farther away.

The next contraction crushed the bedroom inward.

Clear fluid burst through the membrane and washed across the towels.

The copy took his first breath.

Every light in the building went out.

In the dark, doors began opening.

Not the doors in 4C. The doors inside the new apartment.

Each one opened onto a different empty room.

A kitchen with gray cabinets.

A bedroom without windows.

A motel room with blue walls.

A basement with green cupboards.

They were not miniature. Each doorway contained a full-sized place somewhere else. Cold air came through one. Rain blew through another.

The newborn apartment was not coming out of the wall.

It was spreading.

Mara pressed the brass key against the blank wall beside my bed.

The wallpaper softened around it.

A keyhole opened.

She turned the key.

A doorway appeared in the wall.

On the other side was an empty motel room with beige walls.

The copy of me stood.

He unfolded too smoothly, as if he had not needed joints until that moment. He stepped through the rooms behind the membrane.

Each one changed when he entered.

The gray cabinets turned green.

The windowless bedroom gained the motel radiator.

Brown flowers spread across a blanket.

Then my memories began to move.

The farmhouse pulled loose from behind my eyes. I smelled pancakes and hot grease, then nothing.

The apartment above the nail salon drained through my jaw. My mouth filled with the taste of acetone.

My aunt’s basement left through my hands. For a moment my fingers smelled like damp cardboard.

I grabbed the bedroom doorframe.

The wood warmed beneath my palms.

The Wichita motel came last.

Blue walls. Brown flowers. The cracked sink. My mother sitting beside me while people fought in the next room. Her fingers moving through my hair. Her voice low enough that only I could hear it.

The new apartment pulled.

I held on.

The blue motel room behind the membrane brightened. My copy sat on the bed. He looked at somebody beside him, somebody I could not see.

Then he smiled with my mother’s smile.

I forgot the song.

Not the melody.

I forgot there had ever been a song.

I crossed the room toward him.

Mara caught my arm.

“If you interfere now, it will keep reaching.”

“Give it back.”

“It can’t.”

“Then stop it.”

“If we finish, it leaves.”

I looked at Mrs. Harlan. At the brass shifting beneath her throat.

They had not come because they wanted the apartment to succeed.

They had come because they knew what happened when one did not leave.

The copy pressed his palm against the membrane.

I put mine against it.

His fingerprints were mine.

Mine were gone.

“Stay,” he said.

The word came from my mouth too.

The wall curved around my back.

It was warm.

For a few seconds, I wanted to let it happen.

No more leases. No more plastic tubs. No more waking up somewhere new. I could remain inside every detail. The pipes. The tile. The place where the bed met the wall.

Nobody could make me leave because there would be nowhere I ended and the home began.

Walls do not love you back.

My mother had been wrong.

The apartment loved me the way a mouth loves food.

I bit Mara’s hand.

She shouted and released me.

I grabbed Mrs. Harlan’s wrist. She raised the scissors, thinking I meant to attack her.

I forced the blades toward the white cord.

Her voice snapped through the intercom.

“Wait.”

The copy’s eyes widened.

I cut.

The cord did not bleed.

It split open like an overpacked suitcase.

Photographs spilled out. Hair. Keys. Baby teeth. Scraps of wallpaper. Receipts from stores that no longer existed.

The smell of every place I had lived hit me at once.

Thousands of voices whispered from inside the cord.

Some were mine.

Most were not.

The building convulsed.

The floor tilted. Furniture slid toward the bedroom. The tenants fell into one another. Glass shattered somewhere below us, floor after floor.

The doors inside the newborn apartment began slamming shut.

The gray kitchen disappeared.

The basement folded into darkness.

The motel room compressed around my copy. He threw himself against the membrane, but the clear surface had hardened.

His face aged as I watched.

Thirty. Forty. Sixty.

His skin dried into plaster. His eyes darkened into windows reflecting empty rooms.

I felt myself getting lighter.

Not in my body.

In the amount of past attached to it.

I forgot my first school.

I forgot my aunt’s last name.

I forgot how old I was.

Mara crawled toward the key in the wall.

“It needs an anchor.”

I knew she meant me.

She caught my ankle.

I kicked her away.

The brass key was still in the wall. I had watched her create the doorway with it.

I pulled it free and pushed it into the wall beside the bedroom window.

For a second, nothing happened.

Then the plaster softened around the metal.

I turned the key.

A door opened onto the concrete drainage canal behind the building.

Cold night air rushed into the bedroom.

I climbed through.

Mrs. Harlan caught the back of my shirt.

Her real voice came from her mouth, faint and rough.

“Take the scissors.”

She pushed them into my hand and let go.

I fell into the shallow water below.

The doorway closed above me.

The building contracted once more.

From outside, every window appeared to draw breath at the same time.

Then it went still.

I spent the rest of the night beneath an overpass.

At sunrise, the building looked normal.

No broken windows. No water running down the brick.

The curtains of 4C were open.

Somebody stood behind the glass.

He raised one smooth hand.

I did not wave back.

I have been staying in a motel for three nights.

I chose it because every room opens directly onto the parking lot. No shared hallway. No elevator. If I needed to leave in the middle of the night, I could reach my car in seconds.

The walls are beige. The comforter is gray.

My memories are still damaged.

I know I had a mother. I know she smoked. I know there was a song, although I cannot remember a single note.

I remember the rooms we lived in better than the people who lived in them.

Yesterday morning, the old coffee tin was sitting on the table beside the motel television.

I left it in 4C.

There was a new key inside.

A plastic motel key card with my room number printed on it.

I called Mara. Her number had been disconnected.

Then I searched for Apartment 4C online.

The old listing was gone.

A new one had appeared that morning.

ONE BEDROOM. UTILITIES INCLUDED. QUIET BUILDING. LONG-TERM TENANT PREFERRED.

The address was not Mara’s building.

It was this motel.

The photographs showed my room after I checked in. My suitcase sat open on the chair. My shoes were beside the bed.

One photograph had been taken while I was asleep.

The man from 4C stood beside me.

He had my face. His skin was the color of old plaster. Copper wires grew from his scalp and disappeared into the wall.

One hand rested on my head.

The other was pressed against the wallpaper.

Beneath his palm, something pushed outward from inside the wall.

The listing said the room would be available soon.

I left everything behind.

I have been sleeping in my car since then.

This morning, I found a strip of yellow wallpaper beneath the passenger-side floor mat.

I peeled it up.

There is plaster underneath.

The scissors Mrs. Harlan gave me are on the seat beside me. The blades are warm.

A few minutes ago, the passenger door locked by itself.

Then the seat moved closer.

There used to be enough room between it and the center console for the coffee tin.

There is not anymore.

The tin is crushed between them now.

I can hear the keys turning inside.

And where the glove compartment used to be, there is a small yellow door with a human hand pressed against it from the other side.
