On Monday — Day One, if you're counting, and I'm always counting — I closed my front door. My front door takes three. Bolt, chain, then my palm flat against the wood. I know the palm part doesn't do anything. I do it anyway, because three is the number that lets me walk away from the door. On bad mornings three isn't enough and it turns into nine, and nine is why I missed the 7:40 bus again this morning and clocked in eleven minutes late.

Eleven minutes was enough. My supervisor handed me a final written warning at lunch. Twenty-two years counting stock at the same warehouse — and here's the joke: the thing that made me good at that job is that I check everything three times. The thing that's getting me fired is that three keeps turning into nine.

I need to explain about my head, or nothing else in this is going to make sense. I get thoughts I don't want. Pictures. Ugly ones — the kind I'm not going to describe, because if you know, you know, and if you don't, be glad. I've had them for fifty years now, and doctors call them intrusive thoughts. The one thing I need you to understand — the thing that took me the first thirty of those years to believe — is that they are not wishes. They're the opposite. The picture shows up BECAUSE it's the worst thing my brain can find. It horrifies me every single time, and the horror is the proof. People who want things don't feel sick when they think about them. So: I count. Counting gives my head something else to hold until the picture passes. I call them the regulars — they keep schedules, like anything else that visits. That's the whole system. It's stupid and it costs me buses and jobs and it works. I'm telling you all this for one reason: I know my own thoughts. Fifty years of them. That's the only reason I noticed when something that wasn't mine showed up.

The worst one is about my sister. It first showed up when I was nine and Kate was six — I won't tell you what it shows me, just that it involves her, and that I cried for an hour the first time and didn't tell anyone for thirty years. Here's the thing I eventually figured out: the pictures always pick the person you love most. That's how they work. They go where it hurts. Kate is the person I love most. She always has been.

Kate called tonight at ten to seven. Her marriage is over — Steve keeps the house, it's all "very civilized," she's "fine." She said fine twice, which is how I knew she wasn't. Then she asked if she could come stay with me on Friday, just for a while, just until she figures things out. "You'll hardly know I'm there," she said, and laughed in a way I didn't like at all. I'd burn my own flat down for her. I said, "Course. Friday." Two words, because my voice wasn't going to hold for more than two words.

I made up the spare room tonight instead of eating. Fresh sheets. The good lamp from my own nightstand. Her old mug from college, washed three times, handle turned out the way she leaves handles. I counted the room through three times and stood in the doorway for a while with the light off, and it felt good. Ready. Friday. Four days. I did the door in three, the stove in three, the taps in three, and went to bed almost happy.

I want you to remember that. The almost happy. It's the last one I get for a while.

Nothing about Tuesday, Day Two, looked special. Shift, bus, home by six. The regulars did their usual rounds and I counted them down like always. If you'd asked me at dinner how the day was going, I'd have said fine, and I'd have meant it.

I was doing the stove — off, off, off, palm over the burners, three passes — when I had a thought.

*That's enough. It's off. You can sit down.*

I was halfway to the couch before I stopped. Because here's the thing: that is a completely normal thought. That's the thought a healthy person has. I have just never had it. Not once. My thoughts do not excuse me from the stove. My thoughts are why the stove takes three passes.

So I ran the drill, same as I would for any of the regulars. Notice it. Don't own it. Let it pass. And it passed — immediately. Politely. The regulars don't pass when I ask them to. They pass when they're done with me. But fine. Maybe I was just tired. People have quiet days, I told myself, even people like me.

Eleven o'clock. Door in three, stove in three, taps in three, lights out. And in the dark, friendly as anything, it came back.

*You've earned a rest. Take tomorrow off.*

I lay there a long time with that one. Because I know every thought in my head. I've lived with them my whole life. I know what they sound like, I know what they take, and they have never — not once, not ever — given me anything. And here was a brand-new one, telling me I'd *earned* something.

I didn't sleep much. Not because the new thought was ugly. Because it wasn't.

I want to be careful about Wednesday, Day Three, because this is the part that scared me more than anything ugly ever has, and I need you to understand why.

Evening. I was on the route — door done, stove done, starting the taps — when it spoke up, friendly as Tuesday. Except this time, it counted.

*Check the knife drawer. Once. Twice. Three times.*

The drawer is not on my route. It never has been. The route is the door, the stove, the taps — three stations, fifty years. The regulars scream at me from outside the routine; that's what they are, that's their whole game. Nothing in my head has ever asked to be PART of the routine. Nothing has ever used my numbers.

And here's what I can't get past: my hands wanted to do it. It sounded right. It sounded like me. Three times — that's mine. That's my number. I stood in front of the taps with my palm out and I genuinely could not tell you whose idea the drawer was.

That's the thing it took from me on Wednesday. Whatever else the thoughts ever did, the counting was mine. The counting was the answer. Now there's a question inside every count: is this me warding it off, or is this me being walked somewhere, three steps at a time?

I didn't check the drawer. I finished the taps, did the door again to be sure, and went to bed. It didn't say anything else all night. It didn't have to.

Kate comes Friday. Two days. I keep saying that like it means something. It's starting to.

They let me go at around eleven on Thursday. Day Four. Twenty-two years, and it took about four minutes — a conference room, an HR woman I'd never met, some sentences with the word "performance" arranged politely around me. I'd known it was coming. The nine-checks had been beating the three-checks for a while, and warehouses run on clocks, not on men who are sure. I signed where she pointed. Once. She only needed once.

I walked out with a cardboard box. I didn't cry, if you're wondering. I did count the box. Eleven things. Eleven is not a good number. I stood at the bus stop holding twenty-two years in eleven objects, and I was still doing that math when it arrived.

*It's done now. You did everything right. Rest.*

And I have to be honest with you about what happened next, because if I'm not honest this whole thing is pointless: for most of that afternoon, I liked it. It was the only voice in my life that day that was kind to me. Nobody from work called. Kate doesn't know yet. The regulars don't do comfort. And there it was, warm as a coat, telling me I'd done everything right. I sat in my flat with the box on the table and let it be nice to me, and I'm ashamed of that, and I'd probably do it again. That's how it gets you, I think. Not through the fear. Through the tired.

Then, around six, it overplayed.

*You'll want to be rested. Before Friday.*

Here's the thing. My thoughts know what I know — that's what thoughts are. Of course it knows Kate comes Friday. I know it. That's not what stopped me cold in my kitchen with a tea bag in my hand. What stopped me is that in fifty years, none of my thoughts has ever looked *forward* to anything. They don't plan. They don't prepare. They show up, they show me the worst thing, they leave. This one was keeping a calendar.

And I remembered something, standing there. Two nights ago it told me to take tomorrow off. I said no. Now every day is off. I'm not saying those two things are connected. I'm telling you I noticed.

Kate comes tomorrow.

Friday morning — Day Five — I got an emergency appointment. Dr. Okafor has been my therapist for six years, and I want to say this up front, because of where this is going: she is good at her job. Everything she told me was right. Remember that.

I tried to tell her sideways, because sideways is the only way. "I've got a new one," I said. "It's different. It doesn't scare me." Even that much felt like too much.

She asked the right questions. How often. What content. I lied a little about the content — I didn't mention the drawer — and I hated lying to her, and I did it anyway. And then she gave me the rule. The one she's given me a hundred times. The one that is true, and that has kept me alive: "The thoughts that horrify you are the disorder, Frank. The horror is the proof. The more a thought disgusts you, the safer you are."

I sat there nodding. But her rule works in both directions. If horror is how I know a thought is the disorder, then what is the one that doesn't horrify me? The one that's kind. The one that passed the only test I have, and uses my numbers, and keeps a calendar. What do you call that one?

Not mine. That's the answer. And not the disorder, either. A third thing. Not in my head the way the regulars are in my head. In the room with me. Her rule covers two kinds of thought, and I am now the only person on earth who knows there's a third kind, and I found out in a beige office, nodding, while the person I trust most in the world smiled at my progress.

I didn't say it. You know why I didn't say it. Say it out loud — "there is a thought in my head that is not mine and is not my OCD, and it has plans for my sister's visit" — and the next conversation happens in a hospital, with kind people assessing me, while Kate stands on my doorstep tonight ringing a bell nobody answers. So I nodded. I took the worksheet. I booked a follow-up for Tuesday. It was, she said, a good session.

On the walk home, it said one thing. Just once, very quietly.

*Good. Just us.*

Kate got in at six. I'll tell you about the night when my hands stop shaking enough to type it.

Friday night, then. Kate got in at six with two suitcases and a face that was doing its best. We ordered curry. She talked about Steve for three hours — angry, then funny, then quiet — and when she went up to brush her teeth I did the route, door in three, stove in three, taps in three, and she watched me from the stairs and didn't say anything. She has never once said anything. That's one of about a thousand reasons.

It was a good evening. I want that written down. It was the best evening I'd had in years.

I woke up at three in the morning standing in my kitchen, and there was a knife in my hand.

The drawer was open. The bread knife — that one, the one from the pictures, the one that's been showing up since I was nine — was in my right hand. And the thought in my head, I swear on my life, was *I just want a glass of water.* Look at that sentence. Look how it doesn't fit. Water was the story I was being told while my hand was doing something else. And it felt like mine. That's what I need you to understand. It felt exactly like mine.

*You're just getting water.*

The sick feeling hit me one second later, and I have never in my life been so glad to feel sick. That's her rule, remember — the horror is the proof. I was still me. I put the knife down on the counter, and then I asked the thing I've spent fifty years learning to ask: where did this want come from? Real wants have a history. Thirsty starts upstairs, in a dry mouth, in bed. This want had no history. This want started at the drawer.

Now the part I'll say once, and then move on. It was three in the morning and I hadn't slept properly in five days — remember that, because I have to. My kitchen has shadows. Fridge, table, door frame. I've counted that room for twenty years; I know its dark the way you know your own hands. There was one extra. Between me and the hallway door. And I want to be exact here, because "shadowy figure" is not what I saw. It wasn't shaped like a person. It wasn't shaped like anything. It was a block of dark with edges — a shadow is supposed to lie flat ON something, a wall, a floor, and this one stood on its own, with a front and a back and sides. It had corners. I counted them, because counting is what I do, and the corners would not come out the same number twice. And once — only once — it changed shape. The way a shadow changes when someone moves the lamp. Nothing moved. There was no lamp. I stopped looking at it then, and I'm not describing it any further, because there isn't any more. It stood there. Patient. Between me and the door.

So I did the only thing I have ever known how to do. I counted. Out loud. Into the dark. ONE. TWO. THREE. The drawer, shut, three times. Then I put on every light in the kitchen — every single one — and I started the route from the top, out loud, like a madman, like a man praying in numbers. Door in three. Stove in three. Taps in three. Again. And somewhere in the middle of it, very flat, very close, it said:

*Stop.*

First thing it ever asked of me that wasn't wrapped in a gift. I didn't stop. I took every piece of cutlery out of that drawer and counted it back in, one at a time, out loud, because it is MY drawer in MY kitchen and they are MY numbers, and if it wanted to sit in my dark and watch me, then it could watch me do the one thing I know how to do until the sun came up.

Somewhere around the twentieth fork, it touched me. Something cold took my right wrist mid-count — not squeezing, not pulling. Holding. The way you'd still a child's hand. I want to be exact again, because this is the part I can't take back: it was a grip, it had weight, and it had a temperature, and the temperature was wrong. I never saw it move. When I stopped looking at it, it was across the room — and now the cold was on my wrist, and I'm not going to do that math for you. I said ONE with my arm frozen over the drawer. I said TWO, and nothing moved — not my hand, not the cold. I said THREE, and on the number, like a rule it hated, it let go. I put the next fork away with a hand I could still feel the cold on. It hasn't touched me since. It didn't need to. Now we both know it can.

The bread knife went in last. Touched three times. Counted. Shut.

That's how Kate found me. Ten past four in the morning, every light on, her brother crying at the counter with forty pieces of cutlery laid out, counting forks in threes. Fifty years I have kept her from seeing it — the full thing, the real size of it. She stood in the doorway in her old college T-shirt and I looked at her and I let her see.

She didn't ask if I was okay. She didn't reach for her phone. She came in, picked up a fork, put it in the drawer, and said, "One." We did the whole drawer together. Then the door, then the stove, then the taps, and she never asked why, because she has known why her whole life. She was just waiting to be invited.

By the time we finished, the kitchen was a kitchen. Shadows all accounted for. And yes — the extra one was gone when the lights came on, which proves nothing, which is exactly what it would prove either way. But Kate stopped in the doorway for a long moment, looking at the space between me and the door. She's never told me what she was looking at. I've never asked. Kate made tea. We didn't talk about it. She squeezed my hand once, hard, and went up to bed, and I sat there with my raw hands around a mug I'd washed three times, and for the first time all week, my head was quiet.

Completely quiet. I want to be honest about how that felt. It felt like being alone in a room where somebody has just left, and taken something of yours with them, and you don't know yet what.

It's a Tuesday now, three weeks later. Kate found a flat ten minutes away — close enough to walk, far enough to be hers. She calls every night at eight. She knows everything now. Well. Almost everything. Some nights she does the route with me over the phone, and I can hear her locking her own door in threes, which is either the saddest thing in the world or the nicest thing anyone's ever done for me. I haven't decided.

There's a support group on Thursdays. I hate it. I go. There's an interview Monday at a smaller warehouse — Kate did my CV. Under "attention to detail" she wrote "unmatched," and laughed for about a minute.

The regulars are still here. All of them, same schedules, same ugly pictures. Still awful. Still mine.

And people will want to know about the other one. If it's gone. It's not gone. It doesn't offer things anymore. It doesn't ask for things. It doesn't touch. It doesn't say anything at all, most days. It just attends.

I don't know how this ends. I'm not immune — I don't believe anyone is, whatever their head is like. I know I can lose. Some night, some year, tired enough, alone enough. But I'm not alone, and I have the numbers, and Kate has them now too. So: not tonight. That's the whole plan, if you were wondering. Not tonight, every night.

It's eleven. Time for the route. Door in three. Stove in three. Taps in three.

*One. Two. Three.*
