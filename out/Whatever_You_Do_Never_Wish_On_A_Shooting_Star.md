”Isaac, can you bring Grandma her dinner? I think it's been long enough.” My dad asked, putting a hand on the couch as though to support himself. “I need to go make sure Anna washes her hair.”

I smiled, though the plaster had worn thin on my positive expression. “Sure, Dad.” I loved Grandma, and of course I was willing to help her and my dad, but it was so hard to see her this way.

Dad returned the forced smile. “Thanks, son. I'll finish feeding her when I've got Anna in her pajamas.”

He patted my shoulder, and made his way down the hall to snatch my little sister from her room. I watched him go, then stood and made my way to the kitchen, where Grandma's pot pie had cooled enough for her to eat. I put it on her tray, along with a bottle of water with a straw sticking out of the top.

The guilt was a pit in my stomach, slowly pulling the rest of me into it until my chest felt tight and my posture slouched. How was I to settle these two opposite feelings? The want to be with my Grandma during her final days, and the dread I felt every time I saw her. She was clearly suffering and dying. I wanted her to get better, but at her age, that simply wasn't in the cards. A part of me just wanted it to end; in whatever way it had to. Upon accepting that these dark feelings existed, I had been in a civil war between logic and emotion.

I took a moment to collect myself before entering her room. It had been a guest bedroom before she had taken up permanent residence; the classic liminal room in the house kept tidy and sterile, only being used once every few months, but set to exist as an afterthought otherwise, and only considered when counting the room and bath of the house.

Ever since Grandma had moved in all those months ago now, the room has undergone a metamorphosis. What had once been uncanny felt almost welcoming back when she was healthier. It felt good to see her and help her, especially with how grateful she had been. As she degraded, however, the space seemed to suffer as she did. The smell of her perfume was replaced by must. Her whirring and hissing machines battled harder than ever, as though they waited to shield us from the sound of their failure in every ragged breath Grandma took. Any welcoming feeling had vanished, and now this room was one of final chapters and last days, the ghosts of which would haunt it forever, perhaps as a waft of chemical scent years down the road, or a phantom beep from a machine long removed. Today however, those scents were strong, those machines raged, and Grandma was hungry. I knocked and after a moment, pushed in the door.

The scent hit me instantly. That familiar must of the decrepit; a smell so pungent it almost seems to have thickness to it. The smell of plastic also filled the room, emanating from the tubes and bags that filled her with air and removed waste. Her breath, like a waking snore, cracked through the tubes which clouded with compressed air from her pump. She didn't even look at me; her foggy eyes were half shut, like an old trunk so full of memories that you could no longer close the lid.

”Hey, Grandma.” I said as I slowly entered the room. “I have your dinner ready for you.” Her only reply was another deep wheeze which was absorbed into the whirring machine that refused her release.

I again felt a stab of guilt as the thought crossed my mind. I loved her dearly, and she was all I had left of my mom; her daughter. Of course I didn't want her to die. But this… was this living? Are we just prolonging her suffering out of some selfish desire to hold on to her, a slipping rope that burns our hands as we grasp ever tighter? Or is it a moral obligation, with us only feigning altruism? Refusing her release not because it is what we want, or even what she wants, but because it is what we believe we must do?

I realized I had been standing there for an awkward minute, allowing my thoughts to circle around and devour themselves - a mental ouroboros. Grandma, of course, didn't care. She couldn't care.

I approached the side of her bed where we had a chair and TV tray set up for dinners. She had been propped up by pillows in such a way that she could eat or sleep in roughly the same position. We tried to move her when we could, but she was so fragile that we worried that we would do more damage than any bed sore would, the way she whimpered when we did try to move her fueled this fear. Not to mention, at this point we doubted she had long enough to seriously develop any, so the practice had slowly ended.

I began mixing her pot pie, a shallow attempt to retain dignity and normalcy for my grandmother, into a slurry of food while I told her about my day. I didn't know if she could even hear me, much less understand, but it makes me feel better; more connected to her.  
Once I had mushed her food, I poured some water into the mess to slurry it even further. I looked over at her. The mask which covered her mouth and nose obscured much of her face beneath the fogged gray. I gently reached out and pulled the mask down. As I did, air hissed from the plastic and her wheezing became much more labored.

I took a moment to admire her features, as I occasionally did. I could see the faintest impressions of my late mother in her; Grandma's blue eyes and high cheekbones had survived the generational leap in my mother. I wondered if my mother had lived into these late years of life, would she have looked like the woman before me now? I wasn't sure if I wanted to know the answer to that.

I brought the spoon to her mouth. She retained enough awareness to recognize this, and opened. Her mouth closed shakily around the spoon. She began to chew, or at least make the motions of chewing. She didn't need to nor could she truly, as she had no teeth left. I tried to catch the food mixed with spittle that rolled out of the corners of her mouth. I caught it all, though a glossy finish stained her dull skin where it had dripped; tacky to the touch.

She gulped and began to cough. Luckily she had swallowed before her shallow breaths failed her, and she didn't spew the food right back out at me. I replaced her mask and let her take a few assisted breaths before delivering her next bite.

This process continued for about ten minutes. It was grindingly slow, between her bites, the time it took to “chew”, her breaks to breath, and trying to prevent her from getting too messy. As I did this I thought about how similar it must have been to feeding a baby. Ironic, that we begin and end our lives in such similar fashion.

Letterman, constantly playing for her simulation, was a mindless buzz in the background. I was so consumed with my own inner strife that I didn't hear my dad shouting down the hall. It was only when my little sister pushed her way into the room that I was snapped out of my lull.

”Anna? What are you-”

”Sorry, she slipped away from me.” My dad apologized. “I was looking for her hairbrush and she scurried off.”

Anna beamed at our grandma. “Hi Grandma! Can we do weasel?” She asked, raising her hands up to our grandma's bed.

I cringed slightly. We had been shielding Anna from this whole ordeal the best we could, so she didn't really grasp how far gone our grandma was. She knew Grandma was sick, but not that she was actively dying. Anna had been so excited when Grandma moved in, but had increasingly grown frustrated with the excuse that Grandma is too tired to play with her.  
”Sweetie…” my Dad said carefully “No weasel right now, okay?”

”Aww!” Anna pouted, folding her arms.

Memories flashed through my mind of Grandma singing to us “Round and round the mulberry bush, the monkey chased the weasel,” she would bounce her fingers and bob from side you side as she just have done beside her radio in her youth “the monkey thought is was all in fun, POP goes the weasel!” She would poke us in the sides, tickling us when she said pop. I had long since outgrown it, but it still made Anna giggle.

”Why don't you and Isaac go watch some cartoons? He can pop you guys some popcorn?” My dad glanced at me as he said this; an offer extended.

I nodded appreciatively. “Yeah, that sounds good. Come on Anna.” I ushered her out of the room. She was still pouting, but did not struggle against me. I glanced back at my dad as we left as he took up my post. He was playing with the silver crucifix that hung loosely around his neck; a nervous habit of his. I sighed and shut the door.

After popping the popcorn I sat down for some Fairly Odd Parents with Anna. I wasn’t paying much attention, instead scrolling on my phone. I didn’t realize I had been listening to her laugh until she stopped. She was sitting cross-legged on the ground, looking down.

“Hey,” I said after watching her for a minute. “You okay?”

“Isaac? When will Grandma get better?”

My heart sank. I was not equipped to deal with this. I was just a teenager, who was I to explain this to her? She knew our mother was dead, but that was a different concept from someone dying. She didn’t remember our mom, but she knew and loved our grandma.  
“I…” My brain buzzed trying to come up with an answer. “I don’t know, Anna. She’s pretty sick.” I mentally kicked myself for this response, knowing how blunt and crude it was.

She simply nodded. “Yeah, I know.” I grimaced as I met her eyes. Knowing beyond their young years. Our efforts to shelter her were in vain and honestly absurd. She heard us talk and plan. She knew what life was and knew that what Grandma was doing was not living. She returned her gaze to the television. “I wish she would get better.”

I followed her gaze, but was not watching the cartoon. My mind was occupied. “Yeah, me too.” If only. The golden fix to my struggle within. What if she just got better? Her suffering would end, and she wouldn’t have to die. We could have our cake and eat it too.

Soon after I heard a door shut down the hall. My dad entered the living room and sat with us. His expression was solemn. None of us said anything to each other, instead opting to let the television do the speaking for us. After about a half hour, he stood and announced that it was getting late. He took Anna to her room for bed, and I went to mine. I wasn’t tired, but it wasn’t like I was doing anything else, might as well set a good example.

As expected, my attempt to fall asleep was met with resistance and wide eyes into the night. Pushing off of my bed, I went and sat at my desk. I considered booting up my computer, but took a moment to look out the window to my side. The moon was hiding tonight, some stars were poking through the dark of night. I bounced my gaze from star to star. Was my mother up there, watching over us? I tried to empty my mind of thought and worry, pushing anything that began to creep into my mind up and away into deep space. I don’t know how long I was doing this for when I saw it.

Up in the sky, I saw a brilliant streak of white. A shooting star, bold and bright, cut its way through the heavens. In an instance just as quick as the one that spawned the light, it was gone, leaving a slight trail of smoke in the sky where it had descended before it burned away.

I realized my mouth was agape. I knew a meteor shower was predicted in the next few days, but didn’t expect to see a shooting star so vibrant this early.  
Time to make a wish.

Instantly I thought back to Anna, wishing that Grandma would get better. I thought about how perfectly it would solve everything I was struggling with right now. I closed my eyes, and as though in prayer, silently whispered “I wish that Grandma wasn’t sick anymore.”  
My eyes shot open. I realized that I had phrased the wish in such a way that I was faced with the same dilemma. There were a few ways that she might escape her sickness, not all of them pleasant. I groaned, but tried not to worry too much. It was just a dumb wish. Besides, it was up to the universe on how to interpret the wish. I knew I had good intentions, surely it did as well.

I turned away from the window and stared again at the blank monitor. I again contemplated turning on the computer, but opted instead to attempt sleep again. This time, I was successful.

I opened my eyes to find my room still dark. I looked over and checked the time. My bedside clock digitally projected the time 12:40 in bright crimson. I stared at the clock, trying to ground myself. When did I fall asleep? Something about that time seemed off. As I held my gaze with the clock, I watched the final number wink out of existence. The soft illumination of the 12:4 did not provide enough light to illuminate the room.

A flash of light like a lightning strike was cast from my window. Turning, I began to feel my way over to my curtains, but soon didn’t need to. The initial flash was followed by another, and then another. The flashes began to pick up speed, and I was able to quickly find my way to my window and pulled back the curtains to look outside.

The spectacle beyond was awe inspiring. It was a meteor shower the likes of which I had never seen. Countless streaks of light cut through the sky like fireworks. The display was such that the stars seemed to fall down towards the earth in their arc. My heart began to pound as the rate of falling stars steadily increased. It was almost as though the sky was falling. I took a step back from my window, and was met with another light.

My room began to glow red, dull at first but increasing in luminosity. I turned and had to shield my eyes from the clock on my nightstand. 12:4 blazed on the small screen, growing brighter and brighter until I fell to my knees and buried my face in my hands, shielding my eyes from the now painful light.

I woke up sweating. Normal daylight peaked through my curtains as I sat up. I looked around my room, focusing for an extra moment on my alarm clock. It read 6:45, about when I usually get up. I took a moment for myself, a brief reprieve of solitude before anyone knew I was awake and responsibility fell on me once again. Grunting I pulled myself out of bed and accepted the burden of a new day.

As I walked down the hall, I heard speaking in the dining room. My father was in a conversation with a familiar voice. It couldn’t be… I picked up the pace and pulled myself around the corner. My breath caught as I stood witness to my father sitting at the dining table with Grandma. She was holding a cup of coffee. She removed her oxygen mask which was connected to a hissing oxygen tank briefly so she could take a sip, and then quickly returned it.

“Isaac!” She gave a wide smile as she saw me approach. Her voice was muffled by the mask. “How did you sleep dear?”

“Grandma?” I asked slowly, unbelievingly. “I… How…”

She chuckled. “I know, surprising. I feel much more like myself today, dear. And I was terribly tired of that musty old room, so I decided to get some air. Damn near killed your dad when he saw me!”

My eyes widened. I had never heard my grandmother swear before. Of course, after what she had been through, I suppose she had earned a few swears.

“It’s a miracle.” My dad said, smiling up at me.

“Nothing like that.” Grandma replied, narrowing her eyes. “I just got sick of Letterman, and decided to finally stand up and turn the damn show off.”

My surprise was harder to hide this time, but luckily about this time Anna came bolting down the hall.

“Grandma!” She exclaimed. She ran up and hugged our Grandma. My dad shot out a hand to stop her, but my Grandma embraced her with a strength I hadn’t seen in months. “You’re better!” Anna said, tears welling in her eyes.

Grandma held a silent gaze with Anna, giving her a warm smile. At first it was sweet, but began to get unnerving as she refused to move. Anna glanced at my dad and I. Her mind is still failing her… I realized. Maybe she was physically better, but she clearly wasn’t all there. This could still be bad… My mind turned to stories of people suddenly seeming healed right before they passed. It was called terminal lucidity, and I feared my grandma had been struck with it.

“Anna, let’s give Grandma some space.” My dad said, walking over to her. “Come on, let’s brush our teeth and get ready for the day.” He bent down to pick her up, and his crucifix slipped from his shirt, dangling under his head.

As soon as she saw it, Grandma gave Dad a furious look. “You’re still wearing that damn thing?” She growled, her voice pushing its way through her mask. “What has it ever done for us?”

“Milly, what are you talking about?” Dad stood up and gave her a concerned look. “I always wear this.”

“Even after my daughter, your wife, died?” Grandma demanded. “You would praise the thief that stole her from you? The one that bent my back and stole my breath? The one who will take you and your children one day, and let your bodies rot under the earth?”  
“Jesus, Milly!” My dad stepped back, aghast.

Grandma spat as he said this, leaving a slimy film on the inside of her mask. “Fucking fool!” She stood, bones creaking, and pushed away from the table. She dragged her oxygen supply back into her room, not bothering to use the wheels and instead pulling it sideways and letting it bump violently behind her. She slammed the door once she crossed the threshold.  
I met my dad’s eyes. In them I saw the same concern I felt. Grandma was not well. If this was terminal lucidity, it had only affected her body. Her mind was clearly gone. Anna was wide-eyed and staring at us. My dad pulled her away and whispered encouraging words to her. I took a shaky breath, and continued my morning routine.

I was all packed and ready for school, I bid farewell to my dad and Anna, and as I made my way through the kitchen, a buried civility kept me hooked from my day. I sighed and made my way to Grandma’s room, knocking.

“Come in.” A muffled voice called. I shivered, unfamiliar with her weakened voice. I cracked the door to her room and glanced in. Inside it was almost completely dark, save for a few streams of light etched on the floor from the spaces between her blinds. The must had not cleared, and indeed seemed to have grown worse. “Yes?” I heard her croak. In the dark I could only make out her figure on the bed, unable to make out details. The oxygen continued to hiss as she fell silent.

“I… I’m heading out. I hope you have a good day. Love you Grandma.” I stammered.

There was silence for a beat, and then she responded “Have a good day, dear.”

I smiled and retreated, gently shutting the door behind me. I wasted no time making my way out the door. I wasn’t the biggest fan of school, but right now it seemed paradise compared to the unease I felt at home.

The day passed with no incident. I joked with friends, flunked a test, and drove through Chick-fil-a for lunch, making myself late for my next class. School was an escape, a time for me to be a normal teenager with responsibilities I could easily shove off with no guilt. It was almost freeing.

Pulling into the drive way after school, though, was like returning to my cell. I sat in the car and let my song finish out. After I turned off my car, I still waited for a few minutes. When I saw Anna peek through the blinds, I finally pulled myself from my car. I forced a smile and waved at her.

Making my way into the house, I felt the oppressive atmosphere as soon as I crossed the threshold of the front door. Anna raced to meet me and gave me a hug. I patted her on the head, but she did not let go as she usually did at the gesture.

“What’s up?” I asked.

“Grandma’s mean…” She whispered.

“What? What did she do?” I knelt down to meet her gaze. She refused to meet my eyes and looked away. She shook her head.

“Her and Dad got into a fight. She’s in her room now.” She turned and looked in the general direction of Grandma’s room. I grimaced thinking about her scowling, tight features in the dark of that room.

“Where’s Dad?” I asked her.

“Working on dinner.” She told me. “We’re having lasagna.”

I smiled at her. “At least there’s some good news. Come on, I’ll put on some cartoons for us while we do our homework. I led her into the living room and did exactly that. I put on some SpongeBob and helped her get set up on the coffee table. I excused myself to go say hi to my dad real quick.

“Hey Dad.” I said as I approached him. He had been turned away, facing the sauce he was preparing. He turned and gave me a weak smile.

“Hey, champ.”

“What the hell happened?” I asked, trying not to sound demanding. “Anna was about in tears when I got home!”

My dad sighed. “I don’t know.” He admitted. “I got home from picking up Anna, and your Grandma had the television on. She had pulled up a video… Christ… I don’t even know how she found something like that… I rushed in and turned it off before Anna could see, and she lost it on me. I have never heard her use that kind of language before, she said such awful things.” He pulled his crucifix out of his shirt and began to rub it. “I started doing this, you know how I am, and she became downright explosive. She moved faster than I thought possible and slammed the door to her room behind her. She might have broken something on the door, I need to check, I just… can’t right now.”

I put my hand to my forehead. “Jesus Dad, should we call someone?”

He sighed. “I don’t know. I think we need to get her into the hospital, but I doubt she’ll go willingly. We might just have to call the cops if something like that happens again and hope for the best.” He turned back to the meal. “Hopefully it doesn’t come to that.”

“Yeah…” I whispered. I gritted my teeth and made my way back to the living room. Before I was even there I could hear the hiss of her oxygen tank. I quickened my pace and shot into the living room.

Grandma was there, bent low and whispering in Anna’s ear. They were both turned away from me, so I couldn’t see their faces. “Hey.” I said, trying to project my voice.

Grandma stopped whispering and shot up straighter than I thought her back would allow her. She turned and looked at me. Despite seeming better, her appearance betrayed a different story. Her eyes had seemed to sink in, and the skin on her gaunt face was pulled so tight she seemed almost skeletal. I hadn’t realized she had removed her mask to whisper to Anna until she put it back on. She met my gaze, and I swear I saw her smile under the fog of her oxygen mask. She pulled away and made her way back to her room. I didn’t hear her shut the door.

I rushed over to Anna and knelt beside her. “Anna.” I said, concerned. “What did Grandma say to you?”

She looked up at me, tears in her eyes. “She told me that Mom is all mushy under the ground, and that she hurts a lot. She told me that she’s scared of the dark and won’t stop screaming. She said she can hear her. She… said I could hear her too, if I listened.”

I pulled Anna into an embrace, and then held out so that I could meet her eyes. “Anna, I need you to understand something.” I told her. “Grandma isn’t just sick, she’s dying. I think you knew that already. I don’t know what’s happening to her, but I think she’s getting worse. Her brain isn’t working right anymore. I don’t want you to listen to what she says, okay?” Anna wiped at her tears but said nothing. “Anna, I think Grandma is getting really close to passing away. It’s doing scary things to her, but it’s almost over.”

Anna looked at me. “So she’s going to die soon?” The tears returned. I bit back a cuss. Through Grandma’s actions and my careless words, I had instilled the same dilemma in my little sister as I had been struggling with for months. We didn’t want Grandma to die, but we wanted this to be over and done.

“She won’t hurt soon,” I told her instead. “And she won’t be mean. Her brain will work again, and she’ll be with mom in heaven. It will be better for her.”

“Will she scream too?” Anna asked, choking back a sob. I pulled Anna back in close and whispered more encouragement to her. After a time, Dad called us for dinner. I intended to talk to my dad after dinner about Grandma, but right now I just wanted to get Anna fed and in bed.

We sat around the table and began to say grace. The door down the hall slammed open and I heard the hiss of an oxygen tank approach. I braced and turned around to see Grandma approaching, eyes wide and unblinking like a serpent’s.

“Knock that shit off!” She screeched. She grabbed my plate of lasagna and turned back down the hallway. She took the food into her room and slammed the door again. We all sat there, stunned. I heard a chair squeak as my dad pushed away from the table to grab me a new plate. We finished dinner in silence.

Dread hung over us the rest of the night. We seemed to walk on eggshells, afraid to incur Grandma’s wrath. I never ended up having that talk with Dad; he spent the whole night trying to console Anna, and I think he ended up sleeping on the floor next to her bed.  
I took some Benadryl, a practice I tried to avoid, in an effort to get some sleep. I knew without it I would be up all night, almost like every other night, but certainly worse off. At first I felt the buzz of drowsiness without the release of sleep, but soon my wakefulness gave way to oblivion.

If I dreamed that night I can’t remember. What I do remember was waking up to a strange sound. I blinked weariness from my vision as I looked around the room. It was dark, but nothing seemed out of place.

Then the tapping began again. On the wall behind my bed, I heard twelve distinct taps. There was a pause, and then four more taps. My breath caught. My room bordered Grandma’s room. Again, twelve taps sounded, followed by a pause, and then four more. Grandma’s bed was on the opposite side of the room from the wall that was shared with my room. That meant she had gotten out of bed and was standing on the other side of the wall, tapping. The pattern repeated: twelve taps, pause, four taps.

I quietly got out of bed. I took a few steps to the side, and waited for the tapping to continue. It did, but it wasn’t in the same spot. It was directly to my side. I nearly jumped out of my skin. She had followed me on her side of the wall and continued tapping. Twelve, four.  
I grabbed my phone and crept towards my bedroom door, my heart beating against my chest like a madman desperate to escape his confinement. I quietly opened my door and tip-toed into the hallway. The tapping had ceased. I took the few steps necessary to make it to Grandma’s room and slowly opened the door. I heard the familiar hiss of Grandma’s oxygen tank in the corner of her room where her bed was. I breathed a small sigh of relief, a small piece of my world settling back into place.

I was suddenly met with a strange impulse. Out of instinct I pulled my phone out. I didn’t want to shine a flashlight in Grandma’s face, so I just turned on my lock screen. The room was filled with a gentle light. I turned up the brightness a little bit and held the phone out towards where I heard the hiss; in the corner where Grandma’s bed was. In the new light I could clearly make out the empty bed, and the discarded oxygen mask, hissing air into the open room.

I barely held in a scream. Instantly I knew if Grandma wasn’t in her bed, she was somewhere else in the room, likely watching me, and I didn’t know where. I almost fell backwards as I pulled the door shut and scrambled back into my room. I locked the door behind me and darted up against the wall furthest from the door.

The reality of the silence of the night was distorted by the blood rushing through my ears, and the heartbeat radiating up my chest. Nothing happened. I must have stood there for ten minutes before deciding to climb back into bed. As I pulled the covers up over me, I realized that since I never saw Grandma, I couldn’t even be sure that she was in her room. For all I knew, she could have crept out before I went in there. She could have been behind me the whole time. She could have even snuck into my room while I was investigating hers.  
I shot up and turned on my phone’s flashlight. I panned around the room, seeing nothing out of place. I reached for one of my baseball bats I had thrown into the corner after practice and held it defensively to my chest. I first checked under the bed, and then in my closet. She was nowhere to be found. Satisfied that she wasn’t in my room, I decided to lay back down.  
I had finally begun to calm down after what must have been half an hour when I heard a familiar beat. The tapping had resumed, this time at my bedroom door. Twelve, four. Twelve, four. Twelve, four. At first it was at the same rate as before, but it began to pick up speed. The tapping became so rapid that the pattern was impossible to make out; it was just a constant rapping at my door. The doorknob began to jiggle, and I gripped my bat as tight as I could. All at once, the tapping and the jiggling stopped. I sat awake the rest of the night waiting for a new noise, but one never came.

The next day I didn’t even bother with breakfast. I got ready, said goodbye to my Dad and Anna, and bolted out the door. All I wanted was to get out of that house. I was exhausted, but the euphoria I felt growing as my house shrank in my rear-view mirror was worth the fog in my mind.

That day at school I could barely focus. I fell asleep in three classes, and was chewed out by five of my teachers. When my friends asked what was wrong with me, I told them what my Grandma had been doing. I was met with “Bro fuck that” and “Man that’s crazy”, both of which were entirely useless inputs, so I dropped the topic.

I stayed out late that day, opting to do homework at a nearby park instead of going home. I stayed there until the sun had nearly completely set before I steeled myself enough to make the trek back to my living nightmare.

When I got home, I found some Chinese takeout sitting on the table. My dad was sitting alone at the dinner table, a nearly full plate of orange chicken set before him.

“Hey…” I said as I approached. “Sorry I was out I…” I sighed. “I didn’t…”

“Don’t worry.” My Dad mumbled. “I get it.”

I took a seat at the table, and we sat in silence. After a beat, I asked “Where’s Anna?”  
“Bed.” He told me. “She didn’t sleep well last night, kept having real bad nightmares. I told her she could sleep in my bed with me tonight. She has a show on right now to calm her down.”

I nodded, unsure how to approach the looming conversation. “Dad… what are we going to do?” I asked. “Something is seriously wrong, and I don’t know if it is just with her health.”

My Dad nodded slowly. “I’ve been doing some… research. Making some calls. I’m going to try to schedule something with Learing.”

I knew the name. Father Learing; our pastor. Dad likely left out the ‘Father’ in an effort to avoid Grandma’s seemingly supernatural ability to hear. Considering she didn’t come crashing out of the room, I think it must have worked.

“I think that’s a good idea.” I told him. I looked at the food left out. “You should go lay with Anna. I’ll clean this up.”

My dad looked at me. The bags under his eyes were haunting. “Don’t bother. We can get to it later. Do you want to sleep with us?”

Part of me did, but the other part of me felt my pride bruising at the thought of it. “I’ll be okay.” I told him.

He held a worried gaze with me, but finally nodded. He got up and made his way to his bedroom. I didn’t even bother getting ready for bed; I just went to my room.

I locked my door and took another Benadryl. Hopefully I could sleep through the tapping if it continued. I briefly checked my room again, and finding no old crones, I settled in for sleep. It soon took me; a combination of the pill and my exhaustion from last night.  
I woke up to something touching my hair. I was my wall; the one opposite of the door, which I currently had my back to. Again something touched me. It was fingers, running through my hair. It kept getting snagged and pulled on by broken finger nails.

My breath caught. Someone was petting my hair. “Grandma?” I whispered.

“Shhh…”  the sound came like wind rustling leaves in a tree. She continued to gently pet my head, running her nails along my neck when she reached the end of my hair. I began to turn but the hand pressed into my head. “Don’t look at me.” The words spilled out of her mouth like tumbling rocks; emotionless but forceful. I froze and allowed her to continue petting me.  
“Round… and round… the mulberry bush…” She crooned “The monkey… chased… the weasel…” With every few syllables, she brushed my hair harder and harder. “The monkey thought… it was all… in fun…” With these last few words it felt like she was pulling my hair with how hard she petted me. I tensed, waiting for the pop. It never came. After the last word, ‘fun’, she had ceased touching me. I must have laid there frozen for five minutes. Finally, slowly, I turned to face the door. It was wide open, and I could see her peeking from around the corner. In the dark of the room I could only make out her silhouette, but she was bent at an odd angle to make it look like she was peeking up from the side of the doorframe. Her eyes seemed unnaturally reflective in the dark, giving outline to their shape against the black and shadow. She silently pulled away.

I waited another five or ten minutes, then shut my door, locked it, and put my desk chair against the door. Another night passed with no sleep.

The next day I skipped school. I went to the same park I had been at the day prior, and slept. I hadn’t realized how tired I was, because I fell asleep for the rest of the day. Again, it was nearly nightfall by the time I was making my way home. When I made it into the house, I found my dad sitting on the living room couch.

“Isaac.” He said, standing to meet me. “Are you okay?”

“Yeah, Dad.” I told him. “How are things here?”

My Dad gave me a dark look. “Grandma locked herself in her room. She put something heavy against the door so we couldn't get in. I don’t know what she’s doing in there.”  
“Do we want to know?” I asked.

He shook his head. “I don’t know. Learing will be over tomorrow, though. We just have to make it through one more day. If anything crazy happens tonight or tomorrow, call the cops.”

“Okay, Dad.” I nodded. We were so close. Did I believe a religious intervention would help? At this point, even if we just triggered her enough to get the police to force her to the hospital, it was good enough for me. If Learing wanted to help do that, I was more than willing to accept his help. We skipped dinner that night and went to bed, anxious to end this nightmare.

Sleep did not find me that night, even with chemical help. I lay awake all night, bat in my hand, waiting for something to happen. There was a tension in the air, like when you know a balloon, overfilled with air, was about to burst. I waited in the dark for whatever was to come.  
After a few hours, I heard footsteps outside my room. There was whispering as well. I heard two voices. One ragged one belonged to my Grandma. The other one, soft and high, could only belong to… “Anna…” I bolted to the door and threw it open. I peeled down the hall and found Grandma and Anna standing at the front doorway. “What are you doing with her?” I demanded.

Grandma turned. Her face now more than ever seemed like a skull; one that had been pulled at the bottom and the top to stretch it out. That in conjunction with her bent frame, pulled tall, yet retaining crimps where her joints supposedly had failed her, she hardly seemed human.

“I’m taking the child to watch the stars fall.” Grandma growled. Anna stood beside her, appearing almost in a trance.

I didn’t even respond, I just came at her with the bat. She caught it in her hand effortlessly and tossed it to the side. She snarled and raked her hands at me. Her long, broken nails drew blood as they slashed my cheek. I stumbled backwards.

She began to approach, when she hissed at someone approaching from behind me. I looked up and saw my Dad, sporting a huge welt on his forehead. He was holding a book out before him, and reading off from it.

“Behold, I have given you authority to tread on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy, and nothing shall hurt you!” He recited from his Bible. “By the authority of Christ, you have no power here!”

Grandma’s head twisted at an awful angle. She began to spew black bile, her mouth hanging limp and open. Her hand shot out, her wrist bent completely wrong. My dad yelped as his Bible burst into flames. He fell to the ground, clutching his hands. I watched in horror as the Bible’s pages began to flip on their own, the raging fire growing brighter.  
As the pages turned, one came loose and fell towards me. It was singed and blackened almost completely, but by the light of the engulfed holy book I could make out part of one verse: Revelations 12:4.

*And his tail drew a third part of the stars of heaven, and cast them to the earth. And the dragon stood before the woman who was ready to be delivered to devour her child as soon as it was born.*

In that instant I realized the evil I had invited upon our home. I looked up at Grandma, or rather the thing that was puppeting Grandma’s corpse.

“Do you see, dear?” It asked. “Your wish… A contract… Sealed.” It lunged.

Its hands fell around my throat. It squeezed with a strength not natural to a human, let alone one as ancient as her. As my vision swam, I felt one of its hands let go of my throat. I watched it bring its bony fingers to my eyes.

“Round and round the mulberry bush, the monkey chased the weasel…” It began to drone, bringing its fingers to my eyes. I shut them as the fingers pressed into them, and the demon began applying pressure. “The monkey thought it was all in fun…” I began to scream as it pressed harder and harder into my eyes. I began to see stars, then flashing lights as the pain became overwhelming.

Suddenly, it howled and fell off of me. I tried to blink the pain away, stars filling my vision which was slowly being restored. I saw the shape of a flailing figure before the details fell into place. Grandma’s body writhed on the ground, clawing at its neck. Around it hung my dad’s silver crucifix; which he must have slipped on her while she was torturing me. My dad grabbed my shoulders and pulled me back, before doing the same for Anna.

Grandma’s body watched him pull her away, and shrieked, reaching for them, but quickly bringing the hand back to its neck. It stood, bones popping, and breathed shallow, pained breaths. It stared scornfully at my dad. It tried to speak, but its words failed it. Slowly, it brought its hand up to its neck, and gripped the skin. My dad barely had time to cover Anna’s eyes before it pulled out its own throat with the sound of tearing leather. Blood and gore shot from the wound as the body fell, twitched, and died.

That was an hour ago. My dad did his best to console Anna and called the police. They are here now with paramedics, trying to piece together what happened. I managed to slip away from them all so that I could write this account.

As I look up into the sky I can see the meteor shower in all its mockery of glory. I had to get this out there to warn as many people as I could as soon as possible: don’t make any wishes on falling stars. Any wish, just like my own, is an affront to the natural course of creation, and an invitation for something unnatural to grant it. Those falling stars are not to be awed; they are echoes of rebellion and betrayal. They are the essence of lies; beautiful for a flash, but will ultimately leave you in darkness.
