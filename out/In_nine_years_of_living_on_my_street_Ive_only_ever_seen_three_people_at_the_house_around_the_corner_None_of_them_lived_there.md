I don’t really know where else to put this, but it’s been bothering me again lately.

A few weeks ago, police arrested my next-door neighbor for murder.

Then they found out it wasn’t the first time.

The strange part is that I’d been noticing things for years. Small things. Weird things. Nothing that sounded serious enough to call the police over. Nothing that really made sense until afterward.

In nine years of living on this street, I’ve only ever seen three people at the ranch house around the corner.

None of them lived there.

They lived next door to me.

Ned. His wife Melinda. Their son Jeremy.

In nine years, I spoke to exactly none of them.

That’s not an exaggeration. Not once. Not even when I moved in.

The day the moving truck pulled away, I was standing in my driveway holding a box of kitchen junk when I waved at Ned while he was out in his yard.

He looked straight at me.

Then he turned his back and walked into the house.

I remember thinking that was rude as hell.

That was the first thing I noticed about them.

The second thing was the fence.

Their backyard fence was built about two feet inside their property line, which meant there was a strip of grass outside the fence that technically belonged to them but looked like it belonged to me.

That first summer, I realized I’d been mowing it.

On the other side, the fence stopped short of the next neighbor’s fence too, leaving this awkward narrow gap no mower could even fit through.

Little things like that stick in your head when you live somewhere long enough.

And I notice things.

For example, they had two small white dogs. I only ever saw them through the fence. Anytime those dogs barked near the property line, one of them—Ned or Jeremy—would rush outside and drag them back in like they didn’t want anyone hearing them.

That kind of thing stays with you.

But none of it really mattered until about three years ago.

That’s when I noticed the grass in the street.

I was mowing my own lawn when I first saw it. Two thin green lines of fresh clippings running straight down the middle of the road.

Not scattered.

Two clean lines.

Like someone had pushed a lawnmower right down the street.

I remember stopping and staring at it.

Why would anyone push a mower down the middle of the road?

It was weird, but not weird enough to do anything about.

Later that afternoon, my wife and daughter wanted pizza, so I got in the car and headed out.

That’s when I saw the lines again.

They ran straight down the road in front of me.

Without really meaning to, I followed them.

They went all the way down the street.

Then turned the corner.

Right to the ranch house.

I’d seen that house before, obviously. Everyone had. But it had never really stood out.

What stood out now was the yard.

Freshly cut grass.

And the two lines of clippings ended just beside the driveway.

I sat at the stop sign for a second because that same morning I had watched Ned mowing his own yard.

Now it looked like he’d mowed this one too.

That was the first time I wondered why my neighbor was maintaining a house that didn’t belong to him.

Up until then, that house had just been another house.

Now that I think about it, I never really saw movement there.

My daughter and I used to walk behind it through the open field to get to the woods sometimes. Nature walks, exploring, that kind of thing.

I remember one time our dog Coco got loose and sprinted straight toward that property.

A dog in the backyard went absolutely insane barking at her.

Coco thought it was a game.

Other than that, I never saw much.

No cookouts. No visitors. No trash by the curb that I can remember.

Nothing.

Our neighborhood Facebook page was usually the most exciting thing going on. Missing Amazon packages and dog poop complaints.

That’s actually how I learned my neighbors’ names.

Ned posted once about a missing package.

When I saw the address, I realized it was the same guy who had turned his back on me the day I moved in.

I can’t say I searched very hard for it.

Later, neighbors behind us told me Jeremy had gone to school with their daughter.

Apparently he was kind of a jerk.

Still, none of that mattered.

Then things got weirder.

One morning about two years ago, I was leaving later than normal for work.

As I walked outside, I noticed Melinda leaving at the same time.

No big deal.

She drove off.

I checked an email, got in my truck, and pulled out a few minutes later.

When I reached the intersection at the end of the street, I froze.

Melinda’s car was parked in the driveway of the ranch house.

She was at the mailbox.

I watched her pull the mail out, walk through the open garage, and go inside like she owned the place.

I sat there way too long.

Why was she collecting their mail?

Why was she going inside?

She didn’t come back out.

After about five minutes, I left for work with a weird sick feeling in my stomach.

By then, Ned and Jeremy had already been mowing that yard every week for at least a year.

I’d told myself they were just helping someone out.

Now I wasn’t so sure.

That was also when I realized something else.

There hadn’t been a dog in that backyard for a long time.

I walk my dog every morning and every evening.

I love dogs.

I notice dogs.

And I hadn’t heard one over there in months.

At night, the lights were never on.

It became the house that stayed dark.

Then last year I injured my knee badly and was home recovering for a while.

One afternoon, I came around the corner and saw roofers at the ranch house.

Full roof replacement.

Melinda was standing in the driveway talking to them like she was in charge of the whole job.

At that point we were way past “friendly neighbor.”

Why would she be replacing someone else’s roof?

By then I was seeing her check that mailbox at least once a week.

I’m not stalking anybody. I just kept seeing it.

She wasn’t hiding it.

A few months later, in the summer, I was outside watching my daughter mow the lawn while my knee healed.

She shut the mower off for a drink.

That’s when I heard giggling.

Jeremy was standing in the driveway with his girlfriend.

She’d been around for about a year.

I called her Bee because of a bumper sticker on her car that said SAVE THE BEES.

The other sticker said I HEART HOT DADS, but Bee felt easier.

They were smiling, flirting, acting like young people in love.

Then I looked toward the garage.

Melinda was standing in the shadows near the back wall.

If she hadn’t been wearing a white shirt, I might not have noticed her.

She was staring at them.

No smile.

No pride.

No amusement.

Just this hard, angry glare.

And something came back to me.

The only time I had ever seen Melinda smile.

Years earlier, before the pandemic, I heard loud music outside and looked through my second-floor window.

Jeremy and Melinda were standing in the driveway.

Almost the exact same place he and Bee were now.

Melinda was laughing.

Really laughing.

Then she put her hand on Jeremy’s chest.

She leaned into him while he told a joke.

And she didn’t move her hand.

Not right away.

I remember staring for a second, then forgetting about it.

Or trying to.

That day in the garage, I locked eyes with Melinda.

She saw me seeing her.

I’d only ever seen emotion on her face twice.

Once when she laughed.

And now.

She disappeared back into the garage almost instantly.

A few weeks later, I came home around six and turned onto my street to flashing blue lights.

Sheriff’s cruisers everywhere.

A deputy stopped me, checked my ID, and let me into my driveway.

There were more vehicles next door.

An ambulance.

And the medical examiner.

My daughter came outside before I even killed the engine.

“The cops have been here forever,” she said.

Then she told me someone had been led out in handcuffs with a blanket over them.

I looked next door.

Deputies moving everywhere.

Someone inside that house was dead.

That’s when a detective in plain clothes walked over and asked if I’d answer some questions about my neighbors.

I had plenty to tell him.

Three weeks later, the Charlotte Observer ran the story.

Ned had confessed to killing Melinda during an argument.

Then he confessed to killing Claude and Edna Bennett years earlier.

They were the elderly couple who owned the ranch house around the corner.

According to police, they had come by one night to thank the family for helping with yard work.

The front storm door had been left open.

They saw something inside the house they weren’t supposed to see.

Later that night, Ned went to their home and killed them.

For years after, the family kept maintaining the property so it looked occupied.

Mowed the grass.

Collected the mail.

Replaced the roof.

Everything.

Jeremy was not considered involved.

I sat there staring at that article for a long time.

The paper didn’t spell out what Claude and Edna saw.

It didn’t have to.

I already knew.

The driveway.

Her laughing.

Her hand on Jeremy’s chest.

My stomach turned so hard I had to step away from the table.

I kept thinking about the grass in the street.

The mailbox.

The roofers.

The house that stayed dark.

At night, that house feels even darker now that I know.

In nine years of living on this street, I’ve only ever seen three people at the ranch house around the corner.

None of them lived there.

Now no one lives there.
