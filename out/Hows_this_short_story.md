I once read, “The darkest heart fears the dark the most.” I don’t know why that came to mind when I sat down and found the toilet seat still warm.

I live alone.

I told myself it was the pipes—the radiator two floors down, a draft with nowhere better to go. That’s the kind of lie you tell yourself at two in the morning: quick, convenient, and never really listened to. I climbed back into bed like a woman who believed her own excuses.

I haven’t always lived alone.

I think you should know what that cost before we go any further.

So let me start at the beginning, the way a good book should.

Years ago, Emma, my only sister, had come to me, mascara running, and told me her heart was broken. His, hers—I never asked which she meant. I already knew, even before either of them had the chance to confront me.

That night, I opened her up and showed her that it wasn’t. Broken things don’t beat like that. Hers was working just fine, right up until I took it out.

The next evening, I made my husband a very good dinner.

He didn’t like it. To my surprise, he vomited. He had always liked Emma. In fact, he already knew what she tasted like, and now he could barely touch his plate.

I’ve found there’s only one way two people can keep a secret: one of them has to stop being able to tell it.

My husband lasted a good while longer than his sister-in-law did. Long enough to stop asking questions about Emma. Long enough to forget that he ever should have.

Until one evening, he finally asked the question he should have asked long ago.

I answered him.

After that, he never asked another.

And the house learned a deeper kind of silence—the kind that doesn’t come from peace, but from having nothing left that can speak.

This is what I told the police the night he died. It’s even mostly true.

His eyes rolled back in his head, and I wept beside his body for hours. When they finally arrived, they had the audacity to take the knife from my hands.

I did weep. I want that on the record.

I’ve simply learned that grief can be real and deliberate at the same time. Nobody has ever quite forgiven me for figuring that out.

They never charged me. The case went cold, the way cases do when nobody wants to look too closely at the widow.

It’s strange, when I think about it now. Nobody ever solved what happened in that house. I walked into my office every morning afterward, answered my emails, poured my coffee, smiled at the right people—and not one of them ever looked at me twice.

I had a son by then. I want to tell you about the night things really ended, because it wasn’t the night with the knife.

He was ten. I tucked him in the way I always did, and he grabbed my sleeve the way he always did.

“Mommy, check for monsters under my bed.”

I always checked. I think some part of me needed to prove—to him, to myself—that I could still be the one who kept the monsters out, instead of the thing I actually was.

I got down and looked underneath.

Nothing.

Just darkness.

I stayed there a moment longer than I should have. There was something about that darkness I didn’t like. It seemed deeper than it had any right to be, the way a held breath goes on a beat too long.

“Nothing there,” I told him, climbing back up.

I was almost at the door when he asked, very quietly, a question.

A question I had never wanted my son to ask.

I didn’t answer.

Somehow, my silence answered it for me.

Neither of us slept that night.

Within the month, he was gone—not dead, I want to be clear. Just gone, the way children go when the people meant to protect them stop being safe to be near.

I didn’t fight it very hard.

I have lived alone ever since. Which brings us back to tonight—the warm seat, the empty apartment, all the years since I last saw my son’s face.

I didn’t sleep, of course. I lay in the dark and listened to the apartment breathe, and somewhere past three in the morning, a sound came from the hallway.

I didn’t call the police—I have complicated feelings about what they do to people like me. I got up instead, and walked toward the voice, because I was tired after all these years.

He was standing in what used to be my son’s room. A man now, tall in a way that made me think the dark itself had grown him, his face lost in shadow. He spoke before I could.

“I spent ten years looking for whoever destroyed my family,” he said. “Ten years. And when I finally cornered them in the dark—”

I didn’t stay to hear what he meant by that.

I won’t describe what happened next. I’ve noticed that the people who ask for details rarely want them as much as they think they do.

I ended the monster.

I’ll only tell you it was fast, and that I have always been better at ending things than understanding them—and that it wasn’t until the streetlight slid through the window, slow as a confession, and finally reached his face, that I recognized the small scar above his eyebrow. The one from the porch steps, when he was four. The one I used to kiss better.

Only to realize, far too late for it to matter, that he had never been the monster at all. He was the angel that gets sent in, right at the end, to finally do something about the demon.

I just didn’t let him finish the job.

That’s the story, more or less. I’ve read enough books to notice that on the first page, the killer is always the one afraid of the dark. That’s meant to be the joke—the little turn you’re supposed to feel clever for catching.

Here’s the part nobody tells you. You’re not supposed to forget about me once you’ve finished reading it. Not yet. I’m still afraid of the dark, and you’re the only company I have left, and you’ve been very patient, sitting here in it with me.

You spent this whole story trying to work out how everyone in it died.

I wrote it to tell you exactly how I did it.

One more thing, before you put me down. Do you remember, right at the start, when I told you I climbed back into bed and went to sleep?

I told you I fell asleep.

I never told you I woke up.
