Look. Nothing scares me.



I’ve seen my fair share of shit, and I can confidently say that after almost two decades of being a Paramedic, I’ve seen it *all*—murders, suicides, overdoses, car wrecks, repeat. It turns out that in addition to inventing cool shit like shuttles to fly to the moon and ships to sail the seas, people are great at finding ways to fuck themselves up. You can only be exposed to all kinds of life-threatening emergencies for so long before it all begins to bleed together.



In the beginning of everyone’s career in EMS, we all promise ourselves that we’re going to remember everyone. Every code, every name, every patient who’s given us a heart-wrenching life story and spilled their last terrible secrets in the back of our rig.



We’re the professionals. The ones who can help grieving families by giving them the same schtick over and over again—*“I’m so sorry, ma’am. We did everything we could for him, but he’s dead.”* We deliver people to the Reaper after throwing the kitchen sink at them with treatments. When you and your loved ones go to sleep, snug in your beds, we’re fighting the meth head on the sidewalk who tried to break into a church and thinks there’s snakes in his ass. (If you’re reading this, Leroy, I’m talkin’ about you. Quit calling us for a free ride to the hospital.) We’re prying Meemaw from between the toilet and the bathtub after she fell and had diarrhea everywhere two days ago.



We always have a choice. In one shift, every call can be the one that makes us call it quits.



But not me. I broke my promise to remember everyone about 3 years in. I’ve been lathering, rinsing, and repeating ever since. Nothing scares me.



Except tonight.



Except Debbie.



\----



*“I’m just a Gigolo…and everywhere I go…people know the paaaaart I’m playin’….”*



2 AM. The oldies station was droning through the static on our truck, doing its best to play Louis Prima.



“*You’re listening to the Golden Oldies, folks! Stay tuned for the heartthrobs you remember when you wore bobby socks and did the Doo-wop—on 93.3, Theeeeee Dust!”*



It was me and my partner, Hank. Just two old fat dudes trying not to doze off with the heater running at our post. Our ambulance was parked in its usual spot—right next to the Piggly Wiggly, middle of the city, ready for anything. Our rig was hidden in the shadows near the building so some bum wouldn’t spot us and try to harass us for some blankets and our narcotics box where we kept all the good stuff.



“Boy…my ass is asleep.”



I looked over. Hank was leaned up against the window in the passenger seat, his belt straining against his belly and his radio strap giving him some pretty good side-boob. There were crumbs from the donut he had just eaten scattered all over his pant legs with powdered sugar still in his beard. The shadows gave him a voluptuous silhouette reminiscent of John Goodman. His hat was pulled low over his eyes, but I knew he wasn’t napping. Neither of us ever slept even when we went long hours between calls. Napping meant something bad was bound to come in.



Hank was a good guy. We had been partners for so long that I remembered him when he still had hair on his head. I probably felt more comfortable around him than I did my own wife at home. Liked him better, too. He was funny as hell and was famous for his enormous appetite. Gas station clerks trembled in their boots when we pulled up to 7-Eleven for our midnight snack runs of hot dogs and Big Gulps. Good medic, too, with a knack for getting difficult intubations on anaphylaxis patients. What I liked the most about Hank, though, was that he had a great spidey sense for sketchy shit. He was always the first one to notice the tweaker in our side-view mirrors coming up, the first one to see Meemaw’s DNR in the house when we were about to do a code.



Suddenly, a familiar automated female voice crackled over the EMS base station radio.



*“Medic 7…units are responding to a 32 Delta, unknown sick call. Repeat: Medic 7…units are responding—”*



The analogue clock on the truck’s dashboard read 02:08.



Ah, shit. Right on time.



Hank stirred and grumbled, but I already knew what he was going to say.



“Ugh. Fucking *Debbie*. I knew it.”

\----



Deborah Lawsonne lived about three blocks over. She was what we in EMS liked to call a “frequent flyer”—the kind of lady who called every Monday, Wednesday, and Friday night like clockwork at the same exact time. This had been going on for years. Always the same shit. *“I just don’t feel good, I think I’m dying, someone’s in my house, I need someone to come check my vitals.”*



Debbie was eighty-three years old and mean as hell. Everyone who had ever encountered her—which was pretty much all of us, almost like a rite of passage—always bitched about the same thing. *Man, her house smells like something died in it. Why is her skin so flaky? She’s such a bitch. Word on the street is that she just reuses the same Depends.* All valid points, with only insights that Debbie could provide. She had the usual laundry list of maladies that every geezer had—CHF, under-boob rash, kankles that would give Hank a run for his money.



Usually with us, she refused to go. Sometimes one of the other crews would humor her and take her to the hospital on the off chance that everyone got lucky enough and she wouldn’t make it back home.



My two cents? Debbie loved to torture people, and abusing the 911 system was how she did it. If she called, we always had to show up. There was no fighting it. We had complained repeatedly to our medical director, and the best thing they could come back with was, “well…you never know if this call is going to be legit. You gotta go, guys. I’m sorry.”



Hank and I had given up the good fight long ago. He kept grumbling as I heaved the truck in drive and flipped on the headlights, but we both knew that we cared more about getting written up than we did standing on our soap boxes.



“C’mon, man. Let’s just get it over with. We’ll probably get a refusal on her and get back here in no time.” I tried to keep the irritation out of my voice as the ambulance lumbered through the parking lot and onto the main road.



Half the time Debbie just tried to get the hot young firefighters to stick around as long as possible. As soon as she recognized our ugly mugs, she’d probably cut us loose within a few minutes and yell at us to get out of her house.



There were some perks to being an old, fat guy.



\----



We were only six minutes out but still had to go lights and sirens to her address per protocol. I turned off the oldies station so we could hear the EMS radio traffic in case they tried to hail us.



Debbie lived in a run-down part of town where every yard had broken fencing and barking dogs. Our emergency lights cast an eerie glow over smashed windows and boarded-up doors. In this neighborhood, you could never tell if the house you were going to was abandoned or occupied.



As the truck slowed, I saw the familiar crooked mailbox hanging on by a thread and I knew we were there.



Debbie’s house looked worse than usual, and that was saying something.



Huge black trash bags full of old adult diapers and food scraps had been ripped open by a hobo or raccoon recently and everything was scattered in her front yard. Spiky weeds poked up from the cracks lining the sidewalk leading up to her door, which looked especially sticky for such a hot and humid night. A long-ago kid’s plastic playset, sun-bleached after years of sitting outside, lay discarded next to a kiddie pool that was filled with what looked like either Mountain Dew or the mother of all lawsuits for a water park in the form of toxic green sludge.



As I opened the driver’s door to the ambulance and got out, I could see the rope-like tail of a rat roughly the size of Hank whipping as it skittered back to its nest under the sagging porch.



*Please, Debbie. Just say you don’t need anything this time and let us get the fuck out of here.*



My lower back started aching just looking at the house.



“Alright, let’s make this quick,” Hank grunted as he eased himself out of the passenger side of the truck. He grabbed the charting iPad. We pulled our go-bag, oxygen, and gurney from the ambulance. The street was deserted except for us. Hank took the front handle of the gurney, and I pushed from the back.



As we began the journey up the broken curb and towards her front stoop with all our crap, Hank suddenly stopped.



It was such a sudden move that I ran straight into him—“Dammit, Hank, what the *hell*?!”—and stubbed my toe pretty good.



Hank stood stock-still, like he didn’t even hear me. His eagle eye was trained on the living room window.



I leaned down and hissed as I massaged my big toe, following his gaze. I almost didn’t see it, but the pulsating blue-and-red lights caught something.



A bloody handprint on the inside of the dirty window.



Smeared, exaggerated, with finger marks that looked…not quite right. Longer than fingers should be. It looked old, rusted. Something shriveled and dark like a raisin clung to the glass.



“That’s…” I started.



“…not good,” Hank finished for me.



*Ah, shit.*



Our awareness suddenly zinged to life. Full alert. *Red flag.*



I felt the hair on the back of my neck start to creep up, even though I had been to this house a million times.



Hank finally moved again and we got a bit closer. As we did, an unmistakable foul stench came emanating from the screen door.



Hank knew that smell. I knew that smell.



Death. Decay. The smell of a dead body on a hot August afternoon.



A million images of skin sloughing off bone, of flies and maggots, flashed through my mind.



*Big red goddamn flag.*



We inched a bit closer to the front door.



Hank hesitated, then reached out to grab the doorknob. For a second I was worried the nauseating smell would make his powdered donut come back up and feed the rat on the sidewalk.



We braced ourselves.



And then—



*“KSHHHHH—Medic 7, WELFARE CHECK—”*



We damn near jumped out of our skins and saw Jesus.



Right as Hank was about to open the door, our dispatcher’s canned voice blared at us from the radios on our hips. Had it already been ten minutes since we had gotten on-scene?



Hank staggered back from the door, fumbling with his radio strap wedged between his tits hurriedly to respond. When he cued up his mic, he gave a shaky laugh. I could see sweat on his brow.



*“*Medic 7, all safe. You guys almost gave me a heart attack.”



*“Copy that, all safe. Sorry, big guy.”*



He wiped his clammy hand on his pants before turning down the volume on his radio. I did the same. The less noise, the better.



He reached for the knob and turned it.



The door was unlocked and opened with a rusty creak.



Hank took a deep breath and was about to belt out his usual greeting through the screen door, the universal “please-don’t-shoot-us-we’re-the-good-guys” shout into any house we went to—*“EMS, we got a 911 call—”*



“*Come in.*”



It was Debbie’s voice, unmistakable. It wafted towards us from somewhere deep in the house, riding on that smell.



Hank called out:



“Uh…Debbie?”



Silence.



Then—



*“I’ve been waiting.*”



We exchanged glances.



The smell was overpowering now, reaching out and enveloping us. My eyes watered as we stepped in. The red flags started doing cartwheels in my head.



\---



In the din, a lifetime of Debbie’s hoarder crap lay scattered everywhere.



Newspapers from the early 90s littered the hallway in haphazard piles. Soggy cardboard boxes teetered atop one another, teeming with junk. Dead potted plants from either the garden section of Home Depot or the prehistoric Jurassic era lay in their usual spots on the filthy carpet that hadn’t been vacuumed since Nixon was president. A broken ceramic gnome leered at nothing from a shelf, covered in cobwebs.



Yep. Everything was in order.



We left the gurney at the front door and went further into the mess with our go-bags.



A dim light emanated from the back room where Debbie usually parked herself—the room where she ate, slept, shit, and existed. There was a radio somewhere in the corner that was always on. We could hear it cut through the gloom as we advanced.



*“I’m just a Gigalooo…”*



93.3, “The Dust”.



Huh.



As we neared the room, I braced myself for a horrible sight.



The stench was at an entirely new level of awful that I could barely describe—heady and moist, almost sweet, like a million whales were getting it on while beached at low tide in the hot sun. I heard Hank gag and had to swallow hard. *Shit.* My mind started racing and I could feel my mouth salivating like I was about to heave. I mentally prepared myself for bugs everywhere, swarms of flies, for bodily fluids and pus and—



I blinked. We were there.



She peered back at us.



Debbie was leaning as usual in her La-Z Boy recliner. Her stupid little slippers were perched on her thick feet, potato-chip toenails poking out of the tops. Her stained floral moomoo covered her massive bosom and her hands rested in her lap. She had her oxygen concentrator humming in the background and her frizzy grey hair was bunched up with a scrunchie on top of her head as if she needed to even bother looking good. Her beady eyes tracked us as we walked in.



Hank froze. His gaze narrowed in confusion. There was something…off.



Debbie was smiling.



Debbie *never* smiled.



\----



Silence stretched between everyone for a beat. Hank finally cleared his throat.



“Oh shit, you’re ali—I mean, oh hey, Debbie.”



Debbie just kept smiling.



It looked tight, almost painful. All seven of her yellowed teeth were fully on display. Her eyes were wide open.



*“People know the paaart I’m playin’…”* Louis Prima crooned in the background.



Another stretch of silence, and then—



“You’re *late.*”



She sounded like herself, but…



Where was the screaming and drama? Where was the usual “oh-my-god-my-chest-hurts-and-I-can’t-breathe”? Where were the theatrics? Debbie always liked to put on a show when she called us. She loved to ham it up. It always gave her an excuse to try to cop a feel on one of the firefighters when she made them move her on the chair. She got off on forcing them to put those slippers back on her hideous claw feet.



This was different. This was…*wrong.*



Debbie was calm. It was eerie, unnerving.



“Uh…” Hank tripped over his words, totally thrown off.



I finally piped up.



“What’s the problem tonight, Debbie?”



Debbie trained her eyes on me.



“I *knew* you’d come.”



Still smiling that creepy-ass smile.



I felt a flicker of annoyance despite the situation, which was comforting. No matter how unsettled I was, I could still count on her being a pain in the ass.



“Yeah, Debbie. We’re here all the time. Don’t you remember us?”



Debbie nodded—slowly, only once. The rest of her body stayed perfectly still. I suddenly realized that she hadn’t even blinked yet. Her eyes were bloodshot and rheumy as she stared at me.



“It’s time to go. I need…to be around people.”



She sounded detached, almost dreamy as she spoke.



Debbie never wanted to go to the hospital with us. Because we weren’t exactly the young studs that made the cut for the annual Firefighter “Hot Hunks with Kittens” fundraiser calendar, she always refused.



*Something is definitely wrong.*



“You…I’m sorry, you mean you want us to take you to the ER?”



Another single nod, exactly like the last one. We were speechless.



Hank finally cleared his throat.



“Well, uh…let’s go, Debbie.”



He didn’t even ask what her medical complaint was. I could tell we were both desperate to get out of that house, which at this point felt absolutely suffocating.



*Fuck this. We can get the vitals on the truck.*



I didn’t want to spend another second there.



\----





Loading Debbie up on the gurney wasn’t as hard as I anticipated it would be.



It was strange—as soon as we agreed to take her she had practically sprung up from her recliner and began hurrying out of the room with weird jerky movements, startling us with her speed.



Had she always been that spry?



I couldn’t remember the last time we had even gotten her to stand up, but she had led the way out through the dark hallway. Hank and I had no choice but to follow her. When we got to the front door, Debbie abruptly whipped back towards us and stopped us in our tracks.



She was still smiling that too-wide smile.



Debbie reached out her right hand to open the screen door, and I suddenly caught sight of it.



It looked like her fingers were blackened and shriveled. The skin was waxy and sallow. Most of her thumb was missing.



*How the hell had I not noticed that before?*



She caught me looking at it and met my gaze. Debbie winked, but it looked false and exaggerated, like when you slowly tilt a baby doll’s head back.



I shuddered.



Debbie abruptly turned and ambled out of the house before plopping herself down on the gurney. She didn’t even need to be told to scoot up to seat herself properly. Her hands resumed resting in her lap, and she looked placidly at us.



We were mystified. Patients never did that.



“Uh...thanks, Debbie. Just some seatbelts for the ride…” I mumbled, stepping forward.



I grabbed the straps along the gurney railing. As I secured her lap belt, I leaned over her for the buckle.

I suddenly heard a sharp inhale right next to my ear.



*Whiffffff.*



Like someone was deeply smelling a bouquet of flowers. I turned and found myself level with those bloodshot eyes.



Debbie’s smile got even wider.



“You smell so…”



Another deep *whiffffff.*



*“Alive.*”



The last word dropped an octave.



Hank grabbed the front of the gurney to make a beeline to the ambulance. I heard him muttering to himself as we heaved the gurney along the bumpy path back out to the street.



"What in the wild world of sports is going the fuck on?”



\----



I internally groaned.



At the beginning of each shift, Hank and I liked to get a quick snack from the local gas station near base. We had this ritual where we grabbed a pack of Twizzlers, bit one of them in half, and put it along with a normal one in a clenched hand. We’d both look up at the sky and one of us would draw the “short straw.” That’s whoever would take the first patient.



I really, *really* regretted being the one that got the half-Twizzler right now.



“GPS says 6 minutes out from the hospital,” Hank said from the front seat.



I peered up at him from the bench in the back of the ambulance where I sat alongside the gurney that held Debbie. He was steering us out of the neighborhood and down the quiet street. I had dimmed down the lights in the truck.



I willed myself to turn back towards Debbie.



She sat peacefully, hands in her lap, staring straight ahead. I attached the oxygen saturation probe; she was already hooked up to the cardiac monitor. I put the blood pressure cuff on her left arm and reached for my stethoscope.



As I did so, I suddenly noticed that even through my gloves her flesh felt ice-cold.



Had I imagined it, or was her arm…*stiff*?



Her skin felt like rubber.



I tried to shake it off. I put my stethoscope against her inner bicep, like I always did.



*Pump, pump, pump.*



I squeezed the bulb attached to the cuff to auscultate a pressure. Watched the gauge slowly tick back to zero, and…



I frowned.



I wasn’t hearing anything.



That was weird. I knew I had placed the bell in the right spot.



I glanced over at the oxygen saturation probe screen out of habit, and the numbers didn’t make any sense.



*HR: 0. SpO2: 0%.*



What the fuck?



A lump rose in my throat along with a wave of nausea.



My eyes darted to the cardiac monitor, the most reliable piece of equipment we had on the ambulance. I looked at the rhythm on the EKG reading.



*Asystole.*



Debbie was flat-lined.



Unless I was hallucinating, Debbie was dead.





Hank was cruising now, making his way to the highway. He casually glanced at the GPS screen to check on traffic updates.



*ETA: 6 minutes*



His brow furrowed. He tapped on the screen once to check if it was frozen, then back at the road. We were cruising along just fine.



He looked up at the rear-view camera that pointed into the patient compartment; it was a safety feature in all of our ambulances that let the driver see what was going on back there if their partner needed any help in real-time. It didn’t record anything, but it was useful if safety was an issue or if he needed to pull over.



Debbie was staring directly at the screen.



Directly at the camera.



Her head was tilted at an impossible angle—almost completely upside down, over her right shoulder. Her neck flopped in time with the bumps in the road like it was broken.



She was still leering.



Worse, she appeared to be *convulsing.*



Her hands were moving at an incredibly fast speed in a repeating clawing motion against her thighs. Her legs strained and bucked repeatedly against the seat belts.



Hank looked back at the road.



Then at the GPS screen.



*ETA: 6 minutes*



Then back at the camera.



“Uh…hey—”



\----



I looked up, still trying to make sense of the vitals I had just taken.



“Yeah?”



Hank didn’t answer immediately.



“Is Debbie…looking at me?”



I glanced at Debbie. She was still staring straight ahead, hands in her lap.



“Uh…no.”



\----



Hank continued to stare at the rear-view camera.



Debbie was still staring straight back at it.



Still smiling.



Then her voice came from directly behind him.



Except it wasn't her voice.



It was deeper. Low and crooning, intimate like a lover pressing their lips against someone’s ear to murmur.



“*I can see you*.”



\----



I immediately went into autopilot.



My hand flew to Debbie’s neck and I sank my fingers into the carotid artery buried in her fat neck to feel for a pulse.



She remained motionless. I couldn’t feel anything.



Her eyes were fixed forward and I could see a tell-tale film that glazed them in the reflection of the passing streetlights. They were the eyes of every corpse I had ever seen on a code, the eyes of someone who hadn’t blinked in several minutes and never would again.



I tore my gaze away from her face long enough to look at her chest to try to count her respirations just in case I was going crazy.



Nothing. Not even gentle chest rise.



*Holy shit, Debbie.*



*Did you just fucking die in the back of my ambulance?*



A familiar and somehow thankful feeling welled up inside of me.



It was the unbridled rage of a Paramedic who just had a patient break one of the most sacred cardinal rules in EMS:



*Nobody dies in the back of an ambulance.*



The image of the pile of paperwork we would have to fill out, of having to pull over to be put out of service for the coroner to take his sweet-ass time to make his way to our truck to officially pronounce time of death…



It snapped me back into reality.



I steeled my nerves and felt clarity come over me. I knew what to do.



Instinctively I reached up and grabbed the head of the gurney, immediately pulling the lever to lie Debbie flat on her back. My hands stacked one over the other and centered on her chest to start administering CPR.



My mind started going down a familiar mental checklist, systematically going through my cardiac arrest algorithm that I had memorized by heart decades ago. *Alright, I’ll start compressions. I’ll pretend I’m listening to “Stayin’ Alive” to keep count. I need to get IV or maybe IO access after that. What time is it? I’ll have Hank pull over and he can get the Defib pads put on her, and then—*



My hands came down over Debbie’s sternum. I stood and leaned over her to put my weight into the first compression.



Something suddenly grabbed my wrist in the darkness.



Hard.



I froze.



For one stupid second, my brain refused to acknowledge what had happened.



I looked down.



Debbie’s right hand was wrapped around my wrist. Her withered fingers dug into my skin through my uniform sleeve.



I stared at them.



Then at her face.



Her eyes hadn’t moved.



Neither had her head.



The monitor continued its steady, unwavering line across the screen.



No pulse.



No respirations.



“Debbie?”



Her grip tightened.



Pain shot through my wrist.



I tried to pull away. I couldn’t.



“What the fuck—”



Her lips barely moved.



“*Don’t*.”



I froze.



From the cab, Hank shouted.



“Hey!”



I looked up.



His eyes were flicking between the road and the rear camera.



“What?” My voice sounded practically hysterical to my own ears.



“What the fuck are you doing back there?”



“She's *coding*!”



“No, she's fucking not!”



I stared toward the pass-through to the front of the cab. I could hear the blood in my ears thundering with my pounding heartbeat.



“*Ex-fucking-scuse me*?”



Hank looked at the camera again.



His face went pale.



“She’s—”



He stopped.



“What?”



Nothing.



“Hank, *what*?!”



His eyes stayed fixed on the screen.



Then he whispered:



“She’s looking at me.”



I turned toward Debbie.



She wasn’t.



Her head rested against the gurney where I had lowered it. Her cloudy eyes stared blankly at the ceiling.



Her hand was still crushing my wrist.



“Hank, she has no pulse.”



“I know.”



“What the fuck do you mean, you *know*?”



“I can see her.”



That shut me up.



\----



Hank looked down at the GPS.



*ETA: 5 minutes*



Sweat trickled down his forehead.



\----



Debbie’s fingers suddenly released me.



I jerked my arm against my chest and stumbled backward onto the bench.



For several seconds, nobody spoke.



Debbie lay completely motionless.



The cardiac monitor remained flat-lined.



Then, slowly, the corner of her mouth twitched upward.



“*Keep driving*.”



\----



I don’t know how I managed to keep it together for the rest of the drive.



I tried to stay grounded, but it felt like I was in the fucking Twilight Zone. Nothing made sense. Everything felt wrong. If I couldn’t trust the numbers on my equipment, what *could* I trust?



Hank kept glancing at the GPS screen to see if the ETA had changed.



*4 minutes.*



*3 minutes.*



The ambulance continued down the highway without incident.



Debbie didn’t say another word.



Neither did I.



I stayed planted on the bench as far away from her as the cramped patient compartment would allow, watching her out of the corner of my eye from my seat.



She didn’t move.



Didn’t blink.



Didn’t breathe.



The cardiac monitor continued drawing the same unwavering line across the screen.



I didn’t touch her again to get a repeat set of vitals. Fuck that.



*2 minutes.*



The familiar glow of the hospital appeared ahead through the windshield.



Hank hit the turn signal and swung into the ambulance entrance. I felt the truck careen around the corner as we passed beneath the big red “EMERGENCY” sign and pulled into the bay.



Bright fluorescent lights flooded through the windows.



Other ambulances were parked nearby. A security guard stood outside drinking coffee. Through the glass doors I could see nurses moving around the ER, people sitting in the waiting room, a janitor riding around on a little Zamboni.



People.



Lots and lots of normal, living people.



For the first time since walking into Debbie’s house, I felt myself breathe.



We were here.



Whatever the fuck was happening, we weren’t dealing with it alone anymore.



Hank threw the ambulance into park. For a moment, neither of us moved.



Then he looked at me through the pass-through.



“You good?”



I tried to come up with something witty and biting. In this moment, I felt like I was going to cry.



“No.”



“Me neither.”



“Let’s get her the *fuck* out of our truck.”



“Best idea you’ve had all night.”



Hank climbed out.



I stood cautiously and reached for the rear doors, deliberately avoiding looking at Debbie as I passed her. My body tensed up, bracing itself for her cold hands lashing out and catching part of my uniform as I did so.



My hand wrapped around the handle.



Behind me, I heard the faint creak of the gurney.



Jesus Christ, I *really* did not want to turn around.



Silence.



I looked over my shoulder.



Debbie was sitting fully upright again, stiff as a board.



Right then and there, I almost lost it.



I had the absurd urge to uncontrollably laugh. Despite the horror of the situation, I couldn’t help but picture those cheesy old movies from the ‘30s where a mummy starts coming out of its sarcophagus.



*Stop. Keep it together, old boy. Just get her the fuck out of your rig.*



Debbie’s hands were folded neatly in her lap. I saw them there.



I swallowed the rising bile in my throat. I forced myself to finally look at her face.



She was smiling.



Just like she had been when we left her house.



Her cloudy eyes drifted past me toward the brightly lit emergency department. They widened with glee.



For the first time all night, her smile looked almost genuine.



She was happy to be here.


\---



I booked it to the rear doors and threw them open.



The cool night air hit me.



Hank was already there. He grabbed the foot of the gurney and I released the lock, helping him pull Debbie out of the ambulance as fast as we could. The wheels dropped and caught against the pavement with a familiar *clack*.



For the first time all night, everything felt normal. I almost teared up.



We pushed through the automatic doors into the ER.



The place was packed.



Monitors chirped behind curtains. Phones rang. Somewhere a baby was screaming bloody murder. A nurse hurried past us carrying an armful of IV supplies while another argued with somebody on the phone. The waiting room was overflowing with the usual Saturday-night assortment of coughs, drunks, abdominal pains and people who had apparently decided that 3 AM was the perfect time to have a medical emergency they'd been ignoring since Tuesday.



It was the most fucking beautiful thing I had ever seen.



I wanted to kiss the floor.



Hank and I pushed Debbie toward the triage desk.



A nurse I recognized sat behind the computer, clicking through a chart while one-handedly demolishing what remained of a vending-machine burrito.



She glanced up.



“Hey, guys. What've you got?”



I opened my mouth. I tried to somehow put the enormity of the situation into words—the smell, the equipment going haywire, the GPS refusing to count down our ETA. I wanted to tell her everything like a whore in a confessional. I wanted to warn her and beg her to put Debbie somewhere far, far away, to burn her like a witch, because *something definitely not normal and super fucking bad was happening—*



Nothing came out.



For probably the first time in my entire career, I had absolutely no fucking idea how to give a patient report.



Hank looked at me.



The nurse raised an eyebrow.



“You two okay?”



“Yeah,” I croaked out.



“No,” Hank said simultaneously.



I shot him a look.



The nurse slowly put down her burrito.



“Okay…”



I finally managed to clear my throat.



“Debbie Lawsonne. You know her.”



That got an immediate groan.



“Oh, Jesus. What is it this time?”



“That's…” I hesitated. “Kind of the problem.”



The nurse frowned. She stood and walked around the desk to where the gurney was in the triage area where all the ambulances brought in the patients.



“She called for the usual shit. She was conscious when we got there. Talking. Walked herself out to the stretcher, actually.”



Hank made a strange noise beside me.



I ignored him. I knew I was babbling but I couldn’t help it.



“She was alert during transport, see, but then I couldn't get a pressure. Pulse ox wouldn't read. Monitor showed asystole. I checked manually and couldn't find a carotid. No respirations either, but she was still—”



I stopped.



The nurse wasn't listening anymore.



She was staring at Debbie.



The annoyance had disappeared from her face.



“Guys.”



Neither of us answered.



She stepped closer to the gurney.



“What?”



The nurse didn't respond.



She reached down and touched Debbie's wrist—and immediately whipped her hand back as if she’d been burned.



Her eyes snapped to mine.



“What the *fuck?*”



My stomach dropped, mouth going dry.



“What?”



She leaned closer to Debbie's face.



“Debbie?”



Nothing.



The nurse put two fingers against her neck.



I watched her expression change.



Confusion first, then alarm.



Then something much worse.



She reached for Debbie's eyelid and pried it open.



“Get a doctor.”



“Huh?”



She looked over her shoulder and shouted it this time.



“Get a fucking doctor over here!”



People started staring.



Another nurse hurried over. Then one of the ER physicians emerged from behind a curtain, visibly irritated.



“What happened?”



“I don't know. EMS just brought her in.”



The physician stepped up to the gurney.



I started again.



“She was talking to us. I know how this looks, but she was literally talking to us less than—”



He wasn't listening either.



He touched Debbie's neck. Then her wrist. Lifted an eyelid. His stethoscope came from around his neck, right over her heart. Listening.



His expression hardened.



He reached for Debbie's jaw, then her arm.



It didn't move normally.



I watched him try again.



That awful stiffness was still there.



The physician slowly straightened.



He looked at Debbie.



Then at me.



Then at Hank.



“What exactly did you two bring me?”



My mouth went dry.



“What do you mean?”



He stared at me for another second as if he was waiting for the punch line.



“This woman is dead.”



“*I know!”* I was beyond exasperated by this point. “She coded on the way here. That's what I'm trying to—”



“No.”



Something in his voice stopped me.



He looked back down at Debbie.



“She didn't code on the way here.”



The ER suddenly didn't seem quite as loud anymore. I could feel the blood rushing through my ears and I began to get lightheaded.



“What?”



He lifted one of Debbie's stiffened arms slightly and let it go. It stayed slightly elevated mid-air.



“This woman is in full rigor.”



I stared at him.



“That’s…not possible.”



“I'm telling you, she has rigor.”



Hank stepped forward.



“No. Fuck that. She walked out of her house.”



The doctor looked at him.



Hank pointed at Debbie.



“She walked herself to the goddamn stretcher.”



A few nearby nurses had stopped what they were doing.



The doctor looked between us.



“You're telling me this woman was walking?”



“And talking,” I said.



“When?”



“Ten minutes ago.”



Silence.



The doctor stared at us like we'd both lost our minds. I could see him searching for any rational explanation. He looked at Debbie again.



Except something was different.



Her smile was gone.



I hadn't noticed when it disappeared.



Her mouth hung slightly open now. Her sallow cheeks sagged. Debbie’s cloudy eyes stared blankly past the fluorescent lights overhead.



There was nothing mischievous about her anymore.



Nothing watching me.



For the first time that night, Debbie Lawsonne looked exactly like what every piece of equipment on my ambulance had been trying to tell me she was.



A dead, decomposing old woman.



And somewhere between her house and this room—



whatever had been smiling at me through her face was gone.



The doctor noticed it, too. I heard his breath hitch as he became a part of whatever collective hell we were experiencing and stared, flabbergasted.

For a moment, nobody spoke. And then—



“CODE BLUE, FOUR!”



A shrill alarm erupted somewhere behind me.



The ER exploded into motion.



The physician beside us immediately turned and ran. Two nurses followed, one dragging a red crash cart behind her.



I barely noticed.



I was still staring at Debbie.



Her mouth hung open.



Her eyes were dull.



Empty.



Actually empty.



Hank stepped closer to me.



“Hey.”



I didn't answer.



“*Hey*.”



“What?”



His voice dropped.



“Where'd it go?”



I looked at him.



“What?”



“The thing I saw.”



A cold sensation crawled up my spine.



Hank stared down at Debbie.



“It isn't…*in…*her anymore.”



From Room Four came shouting.



“Start compressions!”

“Get me pads!”

“Bag her!”



I slowly looked around the emergency department. At the packed waiting room, the stretchers lining the hallways, the elderly man coughing into his hands, the woman curled into a ball beneath three layers of hospital blankets, the sleeping drunk in the hall bed.



Dozens of people.



Sick people.



Dying people.



*It’s time to go.*



My stomach dropped.



*I need…to be around people.*



“Oh, fuck.”



Hank looked at me.



“What?”



“I know why she wanted to be here.”



A scream came from Room Four.



Not a frightened scream—a fucking terrified one.



Something crashed.



“SECURITY!”



Everyone in the hallway turned.



Then another monitor began shrieking from somewhere behind us.



A nurse ran past.



“What the hell is happening?”



Another alarm joined it.



Then another. And another.



The ER erupted in chaos. More screams joined Room Four from all around us.



Hank grabbed my arm.



“We need to go.”



For once, I didn't argue.



We started backing toward the ambulance entrance.



Behind us, somebody shouted.



“We've got a pulse!”



The words should have been reassuring.



They weren't.



Just before we got out of the ambulance entrance at the double doors, something crackled to life from my hip.



I stopped.



Hank nearly ran into me.



“What?”



*KSHHHHHH.*



I looked down.



My portable radio.



“No.”



“What?”



I pulled it from my belt.



The power switch was in the off position.



Static hissed through the speaker.



Then…music. Faint. Tinny.



Almost drowned out by the alarms.



“*I’m just a Gigolooo*…”



Hank went white.



*“And everywhere I gooo…”*



Across the ER, the commotion in Room Four suddenly quieted.



I looked up.



Through the open doorway, I could see the patient. The monitor beeped steadily above her.



An elderly woman.



Alive again.



The nurses were congratulating each other, relief evident on their faces. Somebody laughed nervously. A respiratory therapist squeezed past the crash cart with their rolling equipment to set up a ventilator.



*“And there will come a daaay…when you will pass awaaay—”*



Nobody noticed when the woman's eyes opened.



Nobody noticed them slowly move around the room.



Until they found me.



From thirty feet away, she stared directly into my eyes. Recognition flashed across her face.



*“When the end comes I knooow…”*



The corner of her mouth curled upward. Just a little.



Then her lips moved.



I couldn't possibly have heard her from that distance.



But I did.



“*You smell so*…”



Click.



My radio went silent.

My hands have been hovering over the keyboard for the last fifteen minutes. I haven’t typed a word.



What am I supposed to put?



*Patient walked to stretcher. Patient conversed appropriately. Patient had no pulse, no respirations, and was in full rigor mortis. Patient possibly possessed by unknown demonic entity. Transported without further incident.*



*Yeah.*



That’ll go over great with our QA department.



Hank is still white as a sheet. He’s been slumped over the steering wheel since we left the ambulance bay, his head buried in his hands.



“You know, maybe this is a sign,” he mutters. “Maybe I need to retire. I always wanted to go to Corpus Christi. Get me a boat or somethin’. Go fishin’. The old lady keeps tellin’ me I need to eat better. Find some hobbies.”



He’s rambling.



I know what he’s doing because I’m doing the same thing—trying to put as much distance as possible between ourselves and what just happened.



Trying to find something normal to hold onto.



I’m too numb to even make fun of him.



The oldies station is humming quietly through the truck speakers.



Neither of us turned it on.



I don’t mention that.



Neither does Hank.

The cursor blinks.



*Patient assessment:*

Blink.

I stop.



Hank stops talking.



Neither of us moves.

*“…alive.”*

He looks at me.

That’s it. I’m calling out sick tomorrow.
