[\[part three\]](https://www.reddit.com/r/nosleep/s/9926JhFXLk)

I stayed in Cleveland for two days, sleeping on my brother in law’s pull out while Sarah and Wyatt stayed in the guest room. Every time a car door slammed outside or a neighbor’s dog barked down the street, my hand twitched for a shotgun that was locked in the cab of my truck.

By Thursday morning, I couldn't put it off any longer. The Huron County Sheriff’s deputy left a voicemail saying he’d checked the property line, photographed the claw marks on the siding, and needed me to sign off on the incident report for my insurance claim.

And with November cold setting in, I needed to shut off the main water valve so the pipes wouldn't burst while the house sat empty, and grab our winter coats, birth certificates, and Wyatt's school things.

Before heading south that morning, I stopped by an auto parts store on the south side of Cleveland, bought a proper new battery and a remanufactured alternator, and bolted them into my Chevy in the parking lot so I wouldn't have to rely on the spare tractor battery I'd slapped in just to limp up the turnpike.

When I pulled back onto the road around ten, the sun was bright and cold in a clear blue sky. Seeing the farmhouse in broad daylight almost made the whole thing feel like a bad dream. The deputy’s cruiser was already parked out front by the mailbox.

We walked the yard together. He looked at the shredded vinyl siding under the second story window, looked at the bungee cords holding down the crumpled hood of my Chevy, and shook his head, writing down notes on his clipboard.

"Biggest damn black bear I've ever seen pass through Huron County," he muttered, squinting toward the north woodline. "Must've come up from the river bottoms looking for late season corn. Good thing you had slugs in that Remington, Mark. Looks like you clipped him pretty good."

I signed his form, thanked him, and watched his cruiser roll back down the gravel road toward town.

Then the silence settled back over the eighty acres.  
I didn't waste time. I walked over to the pole barn to drop off my old John Deere battery, keeping my shotgun slung over my shoulder the entire time. Every thirty seconds, I stopped and looked up toward the cornfield and the oak timber a hundred yards away. Nothing moved. Not even a crow landed in the dead stalks.

When I walked back toward the Chevy to grab my house keys off the dash, I noticed something wedged behind the truck's busted front grille.

I crouched down and shined my flashlight into the narrow gap behind the bent plastic slats.

Caught on the radiator bracket was a thick clump of coarse black hair, stiff with dried, dark blood. Tangled right in the center of it was a flattened, mushroomed chunk of lead, one of my 12 gauge slugs. It hadn't even penetrated deep enough to hit bone. The impact against its thick muscle had just knocked it loose when it spun sideways off the porch railing.

My stomach went cold.

I stood up fast, unlocked the front door, and ran straight to the basement to crank the main water shut off valve. After that, I took the stairs two at a time to grab our lockbox of important papers and a couple of suitcases full of Sarah and Wyatt’s winter clothes. I didn't care about the furniture, the tractors, or the crops still sitting in the field. I just wanted to lock the deadbolt and leave Huron County for good.

I hauled the bags out onto the back porch and turned around to lock the heavy oak door. Just as I slid the key into the deadbolt, I caught movement out of the corner of my eye.

I spun around, bringing the Remington up to my shoulder.

Standing at the edge of the north cornfield, completely out in the open in broad daylight, were all three of them.

They weren't hiding behind the oak trees. They weren't crouching in the tall grass. They were standing upright in a neat horizontal line about twenty yards apart, their massive frames towering over the broken cornstalks.

The one in the middle had a thick smear of grey dried mud packed over its left shoulder where my slug had hit it. The two flanking it were even broader, their coarse black coats gleaming in the morning sun, long arms hanging down past their knees with those thick, human like fingers flexing slowly at their sides.

As I stood frozen on the porch steps, all three of them tilted their wolf heads at the exact same angle, watching me across the yard.

Then, slowly, the one in the middle raised one long arm and pointed a single clawed finger straight at the farmhouse, specifically, up at the plywood sheets covering Wyatt’s bedroom window.

They weren't scared of the daylight. They weren't scared of my shotgun. They knew my boy wasn't here, and they were telling me they knew exactly where he slept.

I didn't even bother locking the door. I grabbed the two suitcases off the porch, threw them into the bed of my Chevy, and jumped behind the wheel. I spun gravel all the way down the lane and didn't take my foot off the gas until I hit the turnpike heading north.

I’m back in Cleveland now, sitting at my brother in law's kitchen table while Wyatt watches cartoons in the living room. I’ve got a map of Ohio spread out in front of me, and I’m loading the rest of our bags into the truck.

We aren't going back to Huron County. Tonight, we’re driving straight through to Sarah’s parents' place down in Kentucky, five hundred miles south.

I just hope to God that’s far enough. Because when I pulled out onto the township road this afternoon and checked my side mirror one last time, those three sets of amber eyes were still locked onto my bumper watching which direction I turned.
