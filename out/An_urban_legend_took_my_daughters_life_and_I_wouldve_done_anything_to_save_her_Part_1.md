Looking around at all the people in my house, I wanted them to take their sympathy and leave me the fuck alone.  A funeral for Nina shouldn’t have been any kind of comfort to me.

My daughter was dead.

And I felt responsible.

Max had found his sister.  I should’ve been the one, but I was too distracted.  I should’ve done better.

Nina had always thought I could.  She believed in me more than I believed in myself.  Somewhere along the way, my daughter had gotten the idea that her father could fix anything.  And I let her believe it.

When I saw Chief Schnabel slip in the front door, I was furious.  I didn’t want this guy in my house or anywhere near me.  Schnabel was the one who told me the gunshot that killed Nina was consistent with suicide.

But they never found a gun.

I’m no Sherlock Holmes, but suicide victims don’t dispose of their weapons.

Still, this small detail didn’t stop Schnabel from shutting down the investigation three hours after they found Nina.

I was about to make a rage-filled beeline for the Chief when I spotted Willow, Nina’s best friend since middle school.  I hadn’t talked to her since Nina died, and I needed to.  Maybe she had answers.

Willow was emotional, but that didn’t stop me from bombarding her with lawyerly questions, making her cry.

I felt awful.  But before I could apologize, the rest of Nina’s friends swooped in.  There were Zach, Haley, Chase, and Hugo.  I’ve known these four for years, and think they’re all decent kids, apart from Zach and his frat-boy smugness.

I couldn’t tell if they were trying to protect Willow or hide something, but I was determined to find out.

But they kept giving me the runaround, especially smartass Zach.  I suddenly had the urge to punch him in the face, but my sister rescued me, dragging me into the kitchen.

Lauren and her wife, Kim, reminded me I was at my daughter’s funeral reception and probably shouldn’t be picking fights with her friends.

Lauren said I should be focused on Max.

This riled me up, but I knew she was right.

The reception was finally winding down, and I’d spent the last hour in the kitchen trying to talk to Max.  He played his Game Boy and refused to look at me, his fingers tapping a million miles an hour.

Max is the sweetest kid – when he’s not having one of his epic meltdowns.

His mother was good at consoling him, but she died last year of ovarian cancer.  Nina had gracefully stepped in as the family Max-whisperer in her mom’s absence, the best big sister a kid with emotional issues could wish for.

But now it was just Max and me.

“I promise I’ll do better, buddy,” I told him for the umpteenth time.  “We’re all we have now, and I promise I’ll do better.”

Max didn’t respond, but I knew he’d heard me because his fingers stopped moving.

Willow suddenly appeared at the door.  I was glad to see her, and I finally had my chance to apologize.

“It’s okay,” she said, her voice still shaky.  “I know you’re upset.”

I nodded and managed a small smile.

We were both upset.

We were both devastated.

“I just wanted to say goodbye,” Willow said after a long silence.

“I really am sorry, Willow.  I was out of line.  I know you were a wonderful friend.  Nina was lucky to have you.”

Willow started to cry again.  It was a little awkward, but I hugged her.

“Max, can I talk to your dad for a moment?”

Max dropped off his stool and walked out, never looking up from his Game Boy.

Willow wanted to tell me something important, so I let her take her time.  I didn’t want to spook her.

“I was there when it happened,” she finally said.

I wasn’t sure what she meant.

“I was with Nina when she did it.”

“You were with Nina when she died?” I asked, needing this to be as clear as possible in my head.

Willow couldn’t speak at first, but once she started, she was desperate to get it all out.

She told me they got hold of the gun and played the game at Zach’s house.

She told me Nina didn’t want to play at first, but the gun followed her home and seduced her.  And when she did finally play, she experienced a suicide, and the gun forced her to do what she did, even though Willow knew that was the last thing Nina wanted.

I was trying to make sense of what she was saying.

Willow kept talking, forcing the rest out.

She told me she got rid of the gun.  She told me that after a suicide, you have to protect the legend, or the gun will come after you and everyone you love.  So that’s what she did.  She drove out to Walton Park, dug a hole, and buried it.

She finally took a breath.

I could tell she was relieved to have told me, but at the same time, she looked as if she might break into a million pieces. 

“I’m really sorry, Mr. H.”

“I’m trying to understand what you’re saying, Willow,” I said as gently as I could.  “I *need* to understand.  This gun – whose was it?”

“It doesn’t belong to anyone.”

“And you say you played a game.  What do you mean?  What game?”

Before Willow could answer, her mother stumbled in, clearly drunk, mumbled her condolences again, and dragged her daughter out.

I would’ve stopped them if I’d been thinking straight.

I found my sister cleaning things up at the buffet table.  Lauren knew something was up by the look on my face.

I asked her if she knew of some urban legend about a gun and a game.

“Cock the Hammer,” Lauren said without even thinking about it.  “That story was big when we were kids.  Why?”

“I don’t remember that.”

“That’s because you were too obsessed with basketball and Francie Boysen.”

“So, it’s not real?”

“What?  A haunted gun?  No, of course not.  Why are you asking about this?”

I brushed the question off.  I didn’t want to try to explain what Willow had told me.  I just wanted to think for a moment.  I just needed time to think.

I jumped online as soon as my house was my own again.

Lauren hadn’t been kidding.  Cock the Hammer was real – or at least the urban legend was.  It had been floating around for decades.

I still couldn’t believe I’d never heard of it.

The story involves an old Civil War-era Lefaucheux revolver.  Cock the gun’s hammer and you’re pulled into one of the killings committed with it.  And the thing is, you aren’t just watching from a safe distance.  You’re inside the shooter, seeing things through their eyes.

But the game has a Russian roulette catch.

Every so often, the death you experience isn’t a killing.  It’s a suicide.  And if that happens, the game doesn’t let you walk away.  A player has to fire the gun into their head, or mouth, or wherever the person in the past shot themselves to end the session.

Or at least that’s the legend.

My first thought was this is some crazy, stupid shit.

But the more I read, the angrier I got.  This crazy, stupid shit was dangerous as hell.

How did Nina fall for it?  I couldn’t understand.  She was such a smart kid.  How could she have believed in such nonsense?  That some fantasy gun had magical powers.

I wanted answers.

But then again, maybe I didn’t deserve them.  I should’ve been protecting my daughter, but instead, this game and this so-called haunted gun had seduced her.

It was a hard truth for me to swallow.

I couldn’t sleep that night.  The gun haunted me.  Not that I believed any of it.  I didn’t.  But I worried another Nina would.

Willow said she had buried the gun, but that wasn’t good enough for me.  I needed to dig it up and destroy it.

I picked her up first thing in the morning and we drove out to Walton Park.

I knew what I was asking her to do made her nervous – she didn’t want to be anywhere near the gun again – but she could see how desperate I was.  Destroying it wouldn’t bring Nina back, but maybe it would save what was left of me.

The hike into the woods was longer than I anticipated.  Willow had wanted to get the gun as far away from everyone as possible.

She remembered exactly where she had buried it.  She didn’t want to get lost hiking out, so she chose Eagle’s Rock, a place she used to visit with her dad before he remarried and started a new family.

We finally reached the area and the mound of dirt where the gun was buried.

I had brought a small shovel and immediately started digging, tossing off dirt.  I kept digging.  I was surprised at how deep Willow had buried it.

Finally, I hit something solid.  But it wasn’t the gun.  It was a thick root from a nearby tree.  I couldn’t dig any deeper.

“Are you sure this is the right spot?” I asked.

Willow was positive.

She was suddenly on her knees.  She put her hands into the hole and they knocked hard against the root.

“Where is it?”

She started searching through the loose dirt.  She kept clawing at things with her hands, hoping the gun was somehow hidden in the tiny clumps.  “Where is it?!”

She was having a panic attack.

I put a hand on her shoulder.  “Somebody probably just found it.”

“No.  I buried it here so no one could find it,” she said, still staring into the hole.  “But I shouldn’t have done that.  I made it angry.”

I helped her to her feet.

“Someone came along and dug it up, that’s all,” I said in my most reassuring voice.

Willow shook her head.  Her whole body was trembling now.

“It’s going to be okay,” I told her.

“It’s evil, Mr. H.” 

“It’s just a gun, Willow.  It’s only evil if we make it evil.”

Willow told me I didn’t understand.  She said she would do anything to make me understand before it was too late.

All I wanted was to talk to Nina’s friends, and Willow agreed to text them and ask them to meet.

They felt ambushed when my car pulled into the 7-Eleven parking lot an hour later.

Willow told them she’d buried the gun, but now it was gone.

I wanted to know if any of them had dug it up.  By then, I was convinced one of them had.

Even though I now knew they’d all been playing the game the night Nina died, they still wouldn't talk.  But Willow vouched for me, and they softened.

None of them had the gun.  After what happened to Nina, none of them *wanted* it.

“You can’t bury it,” Hugo said.

“You can’t destroy it, either,” Chase added.

Now that I was hearing the same story from all of them – seeing the same ardent belief in something so preposterous – I wanted more than ever to get my hands on this gun.

Everything can be destroyed.  Even something evil. 

I asked Zach where he had gotten it, and he said he’d just found it.  He was walking home from school, cutting through Abbot’s field, when the gun was just there in his path like it’d been waiting for him.

“It probably sensed his fear,” Chase told me.

Zach shoved him.  “Bullshit, what fear?”

“Fine, dude, then it sensed your anger or stupidity or whatever.”

Zach came at Chase again, and the two boys scuffled.  I had to step in and break it up.

Zach stormed off in a huff, and Chase disappeared into the 7-Eleven.

Hugo tried to escape with Haley, but she pulled her hand away.  She wanted Hugo to tell me about his Uncle Ray.

Hugo was pissed that she’d brought this up.  He’d asked her not to.  They argued for a moment before Hugo came clean.  His Uncle Ray had played the game a few years ago.

I pressed Hugo for details, but he refused to say anything more, so Haley spoke for him.

She told me that his Uncle Ray played the game and experienced a suicide, but the person in the past trying to kill themselves had screwed things up.

“I mean, it was an attempted suicide, I guess,” Haley said.  “They put the gun to their head and pulled the trigger, but they didn’t die.  The bullet just got lodged in their brain.  And that’s what happened to Uncle Ray.  That’s how the game works.”

“He’s a vegetable,” Hugo offered.  “Or at least that’s what my dad thinks.  But my Aunt Kathy still visits him every day.  She talks to him, and swears he talks back – or at least on some telepathic level or something.”

“Where’s he living?” I asked.

“If you can call it living,” Hugo said with a pained chuckle.

“Cedarwood Manor,” Haley offered.  “It’s out by the interstate.”

I wanted to take Willow home – she had helped me enough – but she insisted on coming along.  I think being with me made her feel connected to Nina.

The feeling was mutual.

The receptionist pointed us toward Uncle Ray’s room.  The droning beep of the machines pulled us in.  The blinds were drawn.

To be honest, the guy looked dead more than alive.  I guess he hadn’t been out of bed since it happened – since he supposedly played the game and put a bullet in his brain, whether he wanted to or not.  His body had deflated like a balloon.

It wasn’t until Willow and I moved closer to the bed that we realized Uncle Ray’s eyes were open.  His blank stare was eerie.

It was also fixed squarely on Willow.

I wasn’t there for small talk, so I started asking the man questions about the gun and the game.  I was pretty sure Uncle Ray could hear me because his eyes kept glancing over at me, clearly triggered by what I was asking.

But for the most part, his focus stayed drilled on Willow.

I wanted to know if it was true that he played some game called Cock the Hammer.

I wanted to know how I could find the gun.

I wanted to understand what he was so afraid of.

This last question surprised me.  I hadn’t planned on asking it.  I wouldn’t have imagined why I would.  But the look of terror on Uncle Ray’s face made me realize it was the most important question of all.

Willow still hadn’t looked away.  I think she was waiting for Uncle Ray to blink.

I was waiting for the same thing.  The man hadn’t since we’d walked in.

*Blink.*

Uncle Ray’s hand suddenly shot out and snatched Willow’s arm, his bony fingers clamping down hard around her flesh.

Willow freaked.  She tried frantically to squirm free, but his hand stayed clutched around her wrist, draining it of blood.

She wanted to run, and she tried to, yanking the wisp of the man out of bed.

They were on the floor before I could even react.  I jumped in and tried to pry Uncle Ray’s fingers open, but it was like rigor mortis had set in.

The commotion brought a crowd into the room like a code blue.  The sudden crush of bodies somehow freed Willow from what I’m sure felt like the grip of death.

She scurried across the room on all fours.

I moved with her, wanting to comfort her, but she jumped at my touch.

Willow was still shaking when I drove her home.

She wouldn’t talk at first, but then she started mumbling something under her breath.

I told her I really wanted to hear what she was saying, but she would have to speak up.

She took a breath and started over.

She told me that when Uncle Ray grabbed her arm, there was a bright flash of light in the room, and Uncle Ray was suddenly sitting at the edge of the bed, the Lefaucheux revolver from the game pointed at his head.

He was trying to tell her something.

She was terrified, but she stopped fighting and just stared, transfixed, as the words formed on his lips.

*I... dare... you.*

“I dare you,” I repeated.  “I dare you to do what?”

“Play the game,” Willow said.

It was suddenly clear to me that she’d hit her head when she fell.  Whatever she’d experienced in Uncle Ray’s room was just some kind of delusion.

I told her that, hoping to reassure her.  But she insisted it had really happened.

“Why would he want you to play the game?” I asked, gently trying to poke a hole in her story.

“He doesn’t,” she answered.  “The *gun* does.”

I looked at Willow for a long moment.

She believed every word she’d just said.

I believed she was wrong.

But suddenly I wasn’t as sure as I wanted to be.
