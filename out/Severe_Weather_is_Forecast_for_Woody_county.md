Have you ever seen a weatherman fuck up his life?

And let's be clear, I’m not talking about small screwups with the news station. This isn’t kissing a coworker at the Christmas party. I’m talking about full-scale bridge burning, the kind of thing that makes it pretty certain that you’ll never stand in front of the camera again.

Let's take it a step further, though. What happens if that same weatherman goes on to ruin things with their friends, with their family, with anyone who would ever have given them a couch to sleep on?

Well, that particularly fucked-over weatherman probably ends up in a place just like the Woody County Weather Station. They end up in the same place as me.

I got the Woody County job last-minute after a few weeks of sleeping in my car and racking up debt on my credit cards. They paid like shit, but food and housing were included, and they didn’t seem too concerned about my work history or state in life. 

I sent a few messages to a faceless online job posting, and once they were able to verify that I actually had a degree in meteorology, they hired me on the spot. Apparently, the position needed to be filled ASAP. The previous occupant had quit unexpectedly due to stress, they said. A bit of a red flag, but I figured nothing could be worse than how I was currently living

Now that I’m on site, I kinda understand what happened to the last guy. The place is *remote.* Woody County is in the upper end of the Texas panhandle, right in tornado alley. It has a total population of less than three thousand. The name has to be ironic, because there’s not a tree for miles. Their main economic export is probably prairie shrubs or dirt.

You can see for miles from the job site. Far in the distance is a highway, with a long dirt access road that connects to the station. It’s on ranching land, so I’ll sometimes see cattle, but they’re never more than dark blobs on the horizon. Other than that, there are no signs of life. It’s just me and the sky.

The weather station itself is a metal shack approximately the size of my childhood bedroom. It has a cot, running water, and power, which is better than I was expecting as I drove in. Three of the walls are large glass windows, giving it a good view of the surrounding landscape. The fourth is covered in weather diagrams and charts, along with a single, old-timey computer monitor. This is where my job comes in.

There are hundreds of weather probes dotted across the landscape outside the station, monitoring everything from wind speed to precipitation to crazier stats like laser-measured cloud height. 

Every two hours, the monitor will display a readout from all of these instruments, including its predicted weather forecast for the day. It’s some kind of software that I’m not familiar with. 

My job is to double-check the numbers, flag any errors, then send the report to my superiors. There’s only so much I can do by hand, but for the first few weeks of the job, the computer was never wrong. I was doing the bare minimum calculations before sending things off.

Just recently, though, something has changed.

It started when I noticed the wind cone was blowing back and forth.

The thing had always felt a bit offensive to me. Made of bright orange fabric and ringed with white stripes, the cone was suspended off the ground less than fifty meters outside of my south window. Anywhere else, it would serve just fine as a rough estimate for the speed and direction of the wind.

In a station full of high-tech equipment, though, it felt like a slap in the face, an insult to my abilities as a meteorologist. But nonetheless, the cone had been installed, and on this particular day it was flapping about in the wind.

This presented a problem for me. None of my other instruments were showing any wind at all. I saw no dust whipping about, no movement in the shrubs.

I checked, then double-checked the anemometers, all twelve of them. They had installed an excessive number of instruments on the property, which I assumed was for redundancy. If one failed, the other eleven would give me the right reading. All of them, every single one, read zero for wind speed. Still, the cone continued to flap back and forth. 

I figured something was wrong with the entire system. It didn’t look windy outside, but maybe it was somehow, and maybe the computer had just bugged out. I did what I should have done in the first place and got up from my chair to check for myself.

There was nothing. The air was still, and the sun beat down on my skin. The sky was cloudless, and so, so big. In the distance, I could see the bright dots of cars on the interstate. There was no wind at all.

I returned to my desk, slumping down in my chair. I checked the instruments again, starting to feel slightly crazy. This had to be some kind of prank, right? But by who? Nobody lived for miles and miles. I guessed few people even knew this station existed at all.

In the corner of my eye, I could still see the thing flapping violently, an ugly, orange finger beckoning me to come outside and look at it. 

I have never been particularly good at resisting temptation. I got up to look.

I figured that if it was a prank, people would jump out with cameras and point and laugh. I’d feel like an idiot, but at least the damn thing would stop moving. 

I threw on my boots and began to trudge across the rocky, barren land. I was struck by an incredibly strange thought. When I was a kid, I had always wanted to be a cowboy, alone on the range. This was probably as close as I’d ever get to that dream. 

As I approached the wind cone, it wasn’t immediately apparent what was happening. The thing was a no-nonsense setup, a cloth cylinder around four feet long that tapered off at one end. It hung from a pole two meters off the ground. The end of it still flapped gently, but most of the cone now blew straight out, held parallel to the ground by wind that wasn’t there.

I looked around me. There were no obvious wires, no hints of a setup.

“Guys,” I shouted, to nobody in particular, “you got me. Good one.”

The wind cone continued to hold steady, as if blown by a consistent gust. I began to doubt it was any kind of prank, but I couldn’t explain it, either. Not really knowing what else to do, I took a step forward and reached out to touch the thing.

Two things then happened at once.

The cone swung wildly, whipping towards me with enough force that the fabric stung my hand. The steel pole that held it began to bend under the force.

I’d seen those kinds of setups snap before, but only on video. It would take winds of at least a hundred miles per hour. It was the kind of thing that only occurred during hurricanes. Or tornadoes. 

However, my attention was pulled away by a noise echoing over the plains. It was something I recognized immediately: the three short beeps followed by a long wail. It was an EAS weather alert, and it was coming from my shack. 

I sprinted back, throwing the door open. The noise was so loud that I had to throw my hands over my ears. The monitor was on, showing a flashing warning for severe weather. 

*Supercell conditions detected. Tornado warning in effect, with expected wind speeds of up to one hundred and thirty miles per hour. Seek shelter indoors. Stay low to the floor, and avoid windows.*

What the hell? I pulled up the individual readings off all of the probes. Still, even now, they all read zero. There was no wind, no humidity, nothing on the radar. The monitor continued to replay the alarm, over and over and over again.

Suddenly worried, I began to write a report. I included the data from the instruments as I typed:

*Something is wrong. Monitor continues to display tornado warning in clear skies. Computer reports forecast storms, wind that is not present. Very loud. Please advise.*

As I prepared to hit send, I glanced out the window at the cone. Damn, the thing looked like it was about to tear itself apart.

The monitor beeped. I had a message displaying itself in the bottom corner. I had never gotten a message before. 

*This is not a drill. Please deploy storm shutters, then shelter in place until given further instruction. To repeat, this is not a drill.*

What. The. Fuck. 

I felt dizzy. I had never, ever gotten a response from my higher-ups in the whole time I’d been here. Something told me they weren’t the type to mess around. I felt like I was dreaming. The alarm continued to blare.

Just to make sure, I checked the horizon one more time.

Have you ever watched a bug crawl on a window? You can focus on it all you like, but every now and again your eyes will catch on something outside the glass, snapping into focus on a tree or a bush. The bug becomes just a blur in your vision while your mind takes in what lies behind it. 

That's what happened to me. I stared out the window, and the focus of my eyes seemed to shift. I don’t know how to explain it, but I saw *through* the land in front of me. My eyes focused on something behind it. They focused on the storm. 

It was miles high, dark clouds blotting out the sun. I could see the paths of wind gusts, marked in the grass as they tore across the landscape. The walls of the station began to rattle. Curtains of rain fell, and lightning blazed across the sky. It looked like the end of the world. 

I blinked, I shook my head. It was still there, rushing towards me across the empty desert.

I typed a quick message on the monitor. *Storm. Sheltering in place.* I struggled to think what else I could do.

The weather station was built to be durable. Each window had a pair of massive shutters that could be dropped down under extreme weather conditions. When fully latched, the station became little more than a giant metal box. I’d been told during my briefing that it would hold up against almost anything. I guessed that I was about to find out.

The first two shutters went easy. I didn’t like how much they rattled even when shut, but I was short on options.

The third one, however, refused to close. One of the hinges jammed, and no matter how I pulled, the damn thing wouldn’t shut. 

That third window, left open by the shutter, let me see everything.

The clouds had begun to spin, a dark tendril reaching down like a finger to graze across the landscape. The tornado ripped up chunks of red earth and stone. I could see flashes of metal, too, as it reached the edge of the property and began to lift the weather sensors. I knew that some of them weighed thousands of pounds.

The noise was indescribable. I couldn’t hear the EAS alarm anymore. I couldn’t hear anything but the roaring of the wind. It was headed straight for me.

I knew that if I did not get this shutter latched, I was going to die here. I threw my entire weight on top of it, and the stupid thing finally started to inch closed.

As it did, I caught one last glimpse of the storm, of the tornado bearing down on me. I swear to you, what I saw wasn’t a hallucination. It wasn’t some misremembering of details caused by a surge of adrenaline.

In the center of the tornado, illuminated by the arcing lightning, was a massive arm, hundreds of feet tall. It trailed down from the sky, fingertips. In the final moments before the cone of the tornado hit, before the wrath of the storm fully embraced my tiny weather station, it began to reach towards me.

The shutter closed. I slammed the bolt and pressed my whole weight up against it. The station went dark as night, all windows blocked off from the sun. The entire building shook, rocking back and forth on its foundation. I couldn’t hear myself think over the wind. It felt like the only thing in all of existence was this tiny room, and the storm.

Even on my worst days before this job, I had never felt so small.

But eventually, after what seemed like hours, the storm did fade. The wind died down, and the walls of the station stopped shaking like they were about to collapse. The rain continued for a while after, but even that eventually quit.

I haven't left the station since then. I haven't even opened the storm shutters. I’ll be forced to eventually, I’m sure, but the electricity, and therefore the internet, has remained active. I have food and water. There are even a handful of books lying around.

A lot of the weather monitors were destroyed, but a handful of them are still running. They tell me that it’s clear skies with mild humidity. I’m in no rush to get back out there, though. I’m not sure if I can trust the sensors anymore.

From here on out, I’ll be relying on that damn wind cone.
