The last train pulled away without James, and he watched its red lights shrink into the dark like something swimming away from him.

He felt his shoulders deflate. He'd be walking home. Again. He sighed and silently berated himself, as he kicked a loose pebble near his foot.

The bar had seemed like a mercy at the time. Two drinks, maybe three — enough to dull the bitterness. The sting he felt about being passed over for the promotion.

He hadn't thought he was as drunk as he was. And perhaps he wasn't.

But goddamn, didn't the place have a clock? That was the excuse he settled on — that was why he'd missed the train, not the extra round he hadn't meant to order. It was the bar's fault. Not his.

As he began to walk from the platform, the streets had that underwater quality, every sound arriving a half-second late. His footsteps. The buzz of a streetlight that flickered as he passed beneath it, then steadied once he'd gone by.

He didn't notice the quiet at first. That was the thing about being watched — you felt it in your body long before your mind caught up. A prickle at the back of the neck. The sense that the empty street behind him was not, quite, empty.

James told himself not to turn around. He kept walking, a little faster now, his own steps loud against the pavement.

Click. Click. Click.

He stopped. He felt his face flush — hot, sudden, the way it does when the body knows something before the mind lets itself catch up. James didn't notice it. But he reflexively balled his hands into fists.

The sound went on for half a beat longer than it should have — then stopped too.

Were those his footsteps? They had to be, he told himself.

He stood very still and listened to his own heartbeat — fast now, beginning to burn through the alcohol — and told himself that echoes don't wait. Echoes don't choose when to stop.

He shook his head. No. What am I thinking? That's crazy.

But he couldn't help his eyes — as they slid from side to side, as if he could see behind him if he tried hard enough.

No. No, he didn't believe in this. It was in his head. He'd had a couple drinks — that was all. Sounds got strange when you were tired and a little drunk and alone. That was a fact. That's practically science. Everybody knew that.

James exhaled. But the knot in his chest didn't completely loosen. Still — the feeling on the back of his neck had diminished. The prickle was fading. James thought to himself — maybe he had really imagined it. Hadn't he?

There's no way.

But he didn't turn around.

He started walking again. Faster this time. One foot, then the other, quicker and quicker, his eyes fixed on the road ahead. He did not look back. He told himself he simply didn't need to.

And it only took a beat.

Before the footsteps started again. Click. Click. Click. And that presence at his back returned — that weight, heavier this time, that felt as though it didn't belong to the cold street. Closer.

He shivered. He hadn't meant to. It had just happened — a sudden, violent, full-body shudder working from the base of his spine to his shoulder blades, as if he could throw the presence off his back by sheer reflex. As if the thing could simply be shrugged away.

But somehow — and he didn't know why — he just knew that there was this certainty that he couldn't.

James felt himself clench his teeth. Reflexively, he stuck his hands into his pockets and drew his shoulder blades in, trying to steady himself. His eyes drifted down to the pavement as he walked, fixed there, lost in his own head.

He felt it press closer — settling against him the way cold settles into a house once the fire's gone out. Patient. Like it had all the time in the world, and knew that he did not.

So he walked faster. It didn't seem to matter.

Is this a nightmare? The thought surfaced, wild and irrational.

"What am I talking about," he muttered to himself. "I'm just drunk. That's all this is."

He glanced up. There — the deli he usually stopped at in the mornings. He knew this corner. Only four or five blocks left.

That short-lived confidence began to drain as the thought continued — four or five blocks of empty street, four or five blocks of that thing at his back, matching him step for step.

That thing. Where had that word come from? James shook his head, as if he could physically shake the word from his thoughts.

He told himself he wouldn't give in to the fear. He wouldn't run. Running would mean admitting it was real.

There was that thought again. "It." Why hadn't he thought "person"?

With a click in his throat, James swallowed, pushing the unease down, where it settled into his stomach and began to sour.

He caught himself mid-repetition — "It's not real. It's just in my head." — and pressed his lips together, as if he could keep the words from escaping.

He would not run.

And that's when the presence seemingly peeled away from him. All at once, there was a sudden release. The weight lifted, and the cold withdrew from his back. The tension in his shoulders began to ease, his attention drifting as it melted away.

James realized he'd been holding his breath — and it left him now, all at once, in sudden relief.

His gaze was fixed ahead, his mind already elsewhere — but some part of him had registered it anyway, at the very corner of his eye: a void, a darker patch against the dark. Had that been an alley he'd just passed?

He scoffed — at himself, at the whole thing. Perhaps the presence — no. Not a presence. Not something make-believe. It had to have been a person. A person, walking behind him, who'd simply turned off into the alley.

That alley...

He stopped. And he turned around. He couldn't help it. It was almost reflexive.

There was no one there. But that alley — there was that alley he'd seen out of the corner of his eye, maybe ten paces behind him.

One of those skinny ones — not truly wide enough for an adult to stand widthwise, just a space between two buildings. Black and silent.

But was that movement he saw? Was the darkness itself moving?

His eyes felt raw. He shook his head several times to clear the blurriness from his vision. He couldn't remember the last time he had blinked.

He strained his eyes to really stare into that open maw.

It wasn't the darkness. It was something just inside it. It had to be something. No — someone. And the longer he looked, the more it began to resolve — into a small figure. Small enough to be a child. Small enough to be about the size of his niece, seven, maybe eight.

He peered harder, trying to make out more of her — her face, her features, anything. But no matter what he did, he couldn't make out any real details.

Should he take a step closer?

No. This was wrong — the last of his inhibition finally beginning to leave him. He needed to get out of here. He needed to get home.

He began to walk away — but before he'd gone a step, he heard a noise. A sound. A voice?

He didn't know yet what he'd heard.

He felt the hair on the back of his neck rise, standing straight out — electric, every strand of it.

He gave in and fully faced the alley.

That's when he heard the sound again. It was a voice — still soft, still faint, but resolving, clearer this time.

"Hello? Is anyone there?"

And then he was sure. It was a little girl — someone his niece might know, might go to school with. And he found himself taking a step closer — the same reflex that makes you lean toward a whisper, or squint at something half-seen. He didn't decide to move. His body just... did.

Now that he was closer — even if it was just the one step — he stared harder at the little girl, half swallowed by darkness.

"Can you help me?" It still sounded pleading — but now there was an undertone to it. Something beneath the plea that hadn't been there before. Something he couldn't quite put a name to.

As he stared at her, he felt something off. At first he couldn't place it.

But then, as she repeated the plea — "Can you help me?" — a crazy thought came to him. Were her lips actually moving? Or was that the shadows?

No, that's crazy, he told himself. But it ate at him. He had to know.

So he took another step forward. Eight paces.

As he stared, he could see subtle movements of what he thought were her arms, reaching out toward him. But no matter how long he looked, he couldn't make out her face.

He took another step forward. Seven paces.

He could see it now. The girl's face began to resolve — and yet, the longer he stared, the less it settled. Something kept shifting, just at the edges, something wrong that his eyes refused to let hold still.

He felt that electric feeling return to the hair on the back of his neck. Goosebumps ran along his arms.

He glanced down. For a moment, it didn't make sense — his phone, already in his hand. When had he taken it out of his pocket?

His frustration flared. He squeezed it once, then shoved it back into his pocket — but he didn't let it go.

His attention had only been off the girl — this thing he'd only half seen — for a second. When he looked back up, he physically jolted — adrenaline flooding through him, cold and sharp.

She was closer now. Too close. There had been no sound — not a single footstep.

He took an involuntary step back, his breath catching in his throat.

Had he really not heard her move?

Even this close, she was still only half visible, the dark clinging to her like it refused to let go. The longer he looked at her face, the more it seemed to come apart into pieces.

Revulsion rose in his throat, thick and sudden. Naked fear right behind it.

Too many teeth. The thought surfaced out of nowhere, senseless and intrusive — just that, at first.

Then her lips pulled back into a wide, wild grin, and James understood. She didn't just have too many teeth. She had too many sharp teeth.

That's when he began to take in the other parts of it — this thing he'd only half seen. Those arms, no longer arms, were still reaching for him.

His ears were filled with a wet, tearing sound — and that's when the arms that weren't arms began to stretch toward him. It was almost as if they'd been waiting for him to notice — and now that he had, there was no reason to hide anymore.

James stood frozen, his mouth hanging open. A low, animalistic moan escaped him — as if whatever was left of his drunkenness had burned away in an instant.

He took an involuntary step back and slipped. As he hit the ground, he saw it — the things that had almost caught him weren't hands anymore, if they had ever been hands to begin with. At the ends of what had been fingers, each one ended in a small, lamprey-like mouth, rimmed with rows of conical teeth.

He scrambled away on his hands, not noticing them scraping raw against the asphalt — his eyes locked on those things. "What the f— what — what the f— what the fuck?"

A low, gurgling, guttural noise escaped the thing's mouth — no, not just its mouth. The sound was coming from everywhere, every one of those small, lamprey-like mouths at the ends of its fingers joining in, until it was less a voice than a chorus.

"Mister, aren't you going to help me?"

It said it again — the girl's voice, same as before. And again. But with each repetition, the warmth in it curdled. The gurgling grew, a heavy wetness seeping through the chorus of words, until the plea became something altogether different. It was hungry. And mocking.

Headlights swept around the corner, and for one bright, blinding second, light washed over the creature. It recoiled, shrinking back into the alley's dark, as if the light itself burned.

Instinctually, James hurled the phone, still gripped in his hand, straight at the thing — the monster. That was the only word that fit anymore. He scrambled onto all fours — gravel biting into his palms — and bolted, a choked scream leaving his mouth as he ran, before he was even upright.

Behind him, he felt it — one of those lamprey-mouthed things brushing against the back of his neck, always just short of catching hold.

Three blocks. His lungs burned, his legs screaming in protest, but he didn't dare slow down. Not with that thing still right behind him. And he knew it was right behind him. With the noise came a smell, thick and rank, rotting carrion — so strong he could taste it, thick on the back of his tongue. A smell that hadn't been there before. How could he have missed it?

Two blocks. Closer now — he could feel it, that same cold press against his back, unrelenting, patient even as it chased him. Something picked at the fabric of his suit coat, small and insistent, tugging at the weave like it was searching for a way in.

One block. His building. He could see it, finally, and something in him broke loose — one last surge of speed, pure animal desperation driving him forward.

He nearly slipped on the front steps, taking them two at a time, and burst through the door, slamming it shut behind him — then fell to all fours, chest heaving.

Not five seconds later, he heard the tapping.

Then: Click. Click. Click.

Through the frosted glass, he could see the silhouette — larger than before, right outside the door.

Only this time, it wasn't footsteps. It was the lamprey mouths — those conical teeth clicking against the glass, almost playful, the way fingers might idly tap a windowpane.

"Mister... I'm hungry. Open the door."

James looked up. The doorknob was already turning.

He lunged for it, reaching up and grabbing on with both hands, and held on — knuckles white, palms slick with sweat and blood.

"Open the door." Low, guttural. "Open the door." The door shook — something slamming against it, hard — and the silhouette swelled, filling the frosted glass, and then the voice broke, loud and raw: "OPEN THE DOOR, MISTER!"

James pressed his back harder against the door, eyes squeezed shut, hands locked white-knuckled around the knob.

Slowly, the voice softened again, curdling back into the girl's. "Open the door, please, Mister?" Her voice tinkled, bright and small — a playful malice coiled just beneath it.

James didn't dare look behind him, at the silhouette still visible through the frosted glass.

Then, silence.

That's when James heard it — a voice outside. He knew that voice. Walter, the man who slept in the doorway two buildings down, always quick with a kind word even on his worst days — the one James sometimes passed a few dollars to on his way to the station. "Hey, little girl? Are you lost? Do you need help?"

James's eyes shot open. Through the frosted glass, the silhouette had shrunk back down — small again, the shape of a little girl, just as she'd first appeared in the alley.

James went to say something — to warn Walter.

But then her voice slid through the glass, barely a whisper. "Stay put, Mister. I'll be back for you."

The words died in his throat.

Outside, Walter's voice came again, closer now. "Hey there — you okay, sweetheart?"

"I'm hungry, Mister. Can you help me?" Her voice, small and sweet, drifted through the glass.

Through the frosted glass, James watched the small silhouette pull away from the door.

He wanted to scream, to warn Walter, to do anything — but all he could do was sit there, his back against the door, his teeth clenched shut.

Outside, Walter's voice came again, confused now. "Hey — what are you doing? What—"

James squeezed his eyes shut, gripping the doorknob even harder, knuckles aching.

"Stop — let go. That hurts—"

James squeezed his eyes shut tighter, the gurgling noise outside rising, thickening — and beneath it, the sounds of a scuffle, scraping and struggling against the pavement.

Then it started to fade. Footsteps, or something like them, dragging farther away — fainter, and fainter, until there was nothing at all.

James stayed frozen there, his back against the door, his hands locked around the knob — until, at some point, exhaustion and shock dragged him under.

He jolted awake, heart slamming — she was supposed to come back.

But sunlight was already creeping through the frosted glass, pale and gray, and the street outside was silent.

James achingly pushed himself up off the floor, every muscle protesting, stiff from the hours spent huddled against the door. He stood there a moment, bracing himself against one hand still clutched claw-like around the doorknob, steadying his breathing.

Then he steeled himself, gathered his courage, and opened the door.

He looked out, searching — no sign of Walter, no sign of that thing. Just a jogger passing by, a delivery truck idling at the curb, the street waking up same as any other morning, no one sparing him a second glance.

Then he looked down. There, on the step, sat his phone — resting in a small pool of what looked like blood, mixed with something thick and sticky, like mucus. That smell was there again, fainter now — rot, and carrion, clinging to the morning air.

He stepped back inside and locked the door behind him. From his window, he found himself looking out toward the mouth of that alley, three blocks down — and he couldn't shake the feeling that whatever had been out there was still out there now, somewhere, waiting.
