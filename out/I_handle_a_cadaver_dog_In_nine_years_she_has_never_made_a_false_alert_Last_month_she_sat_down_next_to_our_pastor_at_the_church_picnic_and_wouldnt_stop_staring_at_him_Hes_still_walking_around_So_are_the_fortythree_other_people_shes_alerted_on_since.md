I need to start with what my dog does, or none of the rest of it is going to make sense.

If you've never seen a cadaver dog work you probably picture a bloodhound in a cartoon, baying and dragging some guy through the woods. It's not like that. A good HRD dog (human remains detection) is quiet. She works a grid with her nose low, back and forth, tail up, and you can actually see her thinking, like a kid doing long division in their head. And when she finds what she's trained to find she doesn't bark and she doesn't dig. She sits.

That's a passive alert. She sits as close to the source as she can get, points her nose at it, and stares. She won't look at you. She won't look at anything else. Rain, snow, swamp up to her chest, she'll stare at a patch of ground or a car trunk or the bottom of a lake until you give the release word and hand over the toy. Some dogs hold an alert twenty minutes. Mine holds it as long as it takes.

Her name's Juno. Belgian Malinois, nine years old, sixty-one pounds, tan with the black mask, and honestly the best thing I've ever done with my life except my son. I've had her since eight weeks. She's certified every year by two separate national organizations, which means every year she gets tested blind: somebody hides real human remains in one of several spots and decoys in the others, and neither me nor the evaluator knows which is which, and she has to find the right one. She's never failed. 226 deployments, for PDs and sheriff's offices and families, four states. She found a drowned boy at the bottom of a reservoir by leaning out over the side of a johnboat and staring at the water. She found a woman in a septic tank. She found ninety-year-old bone fragments in a farmer's field under two feet of plowed dirt.

She has never made a false alert. Not once. Not in training, not in certs, not on a search.

I need yinz to get that before I tell you what she's been doing. Like actually get it.

The one I think about most was four years ago at a reservoir down in West Virginia. Nine-year-old boy went off a dock at a family reunion and didn't come up. Divers had been on it three days in water so silted they couldn't see their own hands. And the family was standing on the bank in a line. Mom, dad, two grandmas, an uncle holding a toddler. Three days they'd been standing there.

We went out in a johnboat, Juno in her orange harness up in the bow, me on the tiller, a deputy with a GPS. Ran slow lines across the cove. She leaned out over the side with her nose maybe an inch off the surface, working the scent that comes up off the water in a column from whatever's below, drifting, breaking up, coming up again. Bottom of that boat was slippy as anything and she never once lost her footing.

About forty minutes in she sat down in the bow.

Boat's still moving, and she sits, and turns her head to keep her nose on one patch of water as we slide past, swiveling around until she's facing straight back over the stern, staring at a spot of brown water that looked exactly like every other spot of brown water in that reservoir. I cut the motor. Deputy dropped a marker buoy. She stared at it for twenty-five minutes while the dive team came out, didn't blink, didn't move, and the second diver went down the buoy line and came back up after four minutes and pulled his mask off and just nodded.

On the bank the mother sat down in the mud.

That's what Juno does. She doesn't guess. She doesn't get excited. She tells you with her whole body, *here*, and she keeps telling you until somebody listens.

OK. Me. I'm Delia, 46. I live with my son Caleb, he's 16, in a little town in the hills of western PA that I'm not naming, because anybody from there would know it in two seconds. All closed now. Last one shut in 1994. But they're still down there, miles of tunnels, rooms cut out of the seam and propped up with timber and left to fall in in the dark. Every so often somebody's backyard drops a foot overnight. Every so often a basement fills up with water that smells like rotten eggs. The old-timers call it the town under the town.

I'm a dispatcher at the county 911 center. Nights, three on three off. The dog stuff is volunteer, most HRD handlers are. I felt it through the leash before I saw it. Every muscle locked. She got up off my leg, took two steps toward Gene, and sat down right in front of him, maybe eighteen inches from his chest. Nose pointed at the middle of his body. And she stared.

Not at his face. At his sternum. At the space under his shirt between the buttons. That glassy, total, locked-on look I've seen 226 times over lake water and car trunks and plowed dirt.

So I told myself there was an explanation. There's always an explanation. Cadaver dogs alert on decomp odor, which is a cocktail of chemicals with ugly names, cadaverine, putrescine, a few hundred others, that a body starts putting out within hours of death. It's stubborn stuff. Gets into soil, fabric, wood, carpet, car seats, n'at. A dog can pick it up off a rug where a body lay twenty minutes, years later.

And Gene's a pastor. Pastors are around the dead all the time. They sit with dying people, they put hands on bodies in hospital beds and at the funeral home. So I'm thinking OK, he did a viewing this morning. Or last night. He was fine. That's the thing. He was totally fine. Cheerful. Preached. Visited the sick. Big laugh across the parking lot. But there were little things, the kind you only catch if you're watching, and by then I was watching.

So then I went to see Walt Bevan.

Walt's 93. Little house at the bottom of Furnace Street, porch leans like a drunk, yard full of rusted mine equipment he won't let anybody haul off. He listened with his eyes shut, rocking. When I was done he didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he said, "You know about 'twenty-seven?"

Everybody in town knows about 1927. There's a plaque outside the town hall. November 8, 1927, an explosion in the Number 4, about two miles in and half a mile down. Seventy-one men died. Thirty-eight of them were never recovered, because the fire burned for weeks and the company sealed that section with them still inside.

"Most of the boys that died," Walt said, "didn't die in the blast. They died of the afterdamp."

I didn't know the word. I'm putting it here exactly how he said it.

"After an explosion," Walt said, "the air goes bad. Blast eats up all the good air and leaves the bad behind. Carbon monoxide, mostly. Miners called it afterdamp. Whitedamp. You can't smell it, can't see it. It don't hurt. It just takes you, nice and quiet. You get tired. You get calm. You sit down for a rest and you don't get up."

He rocked a while.

"My grandad was in that mine in 'twenty-seven. One of the ones that walked out. And he told me something I never told nobody, because they'd have thought he was cracked." Walt opened his eyes. "And they was dead," Walt said. "Been dead since the afterdamp hit 'em, down there in the dark. They just hadn't noticed yet. Some of 'em kept going a week. Some longer. Walking around. Real calm. Real polite. Didn't eat. Didn't sweat. Then one morning they'd just be sitting in their chair, and they'd stay there."

He looked at me.

"My grandad said you could always tell which ones," he said. "By the dogs."

Linda Sorensen. Dale's wife. The coach. Juno had alerted on Dale outside the diner six weeks before. He was name number three.

It was spreading. Like a ring from a rock you drop in a pond. Like gas coming up through the ground.

There's been stuff over the years about the mine, for the record. CO in basements. A family got evacuated in 2009 when their alarm went off in the middle of the night. The fire in the Number 4 was supposed to be out by 1928. But there's mine fires in Pennsylvania that have been burning sixty years, look it up. And the company that sealed it went bankrupt in 1952 and nobody's checked that seal since.

So maybe that's all it is. Maybe the old fire never went out and the afterdamp is leaking up through the cracks in the town under the town, into basements and classrooms and the church. Can't see it, can't smell it, quiet. Making people tired. Making people calm.

Or maybe it doesn't matter what it is.

That night after Caleb was asleep I did something I'd been putting off. I put Juno's orange harness on in the kitchen and told her to search.

Gene came up on the porch and knocked. Real gentle.

I didn't open it. I talked to him through the door.

"Delia, honey, we're just worried about you." Warm. The big pink voice I've known my whole life. "Folks are saying the dog's been upsetting people. Sitting down in front of 'em. Scaring the kids. Helen says she can't hardly go to work without wondering if Juno's going to come at her."

"She doesn't come at anybody," I said. "She sits."

"I know, honey." Pause. "But it upsets folks. Makes 'em feel funny. Like she knows something about 'em they don't." Longer pause. "And Caleb ought to be at practice. He's missing the Blackwood game Friday. The boys say he's never been in better shape."

Juno was in the hall behind me. Not staring at the door. Turning in a slow circle in the middle of the hallway, nose up, sweeping, the way she does at the start of a search when there's so much scent in the air she can't decide where to start.

"Some of the folks think," Gene said, "maybe it's time she retired. She's nine. That's old for a working dog. Maybe let her rest." Quiet a second. "We'd all feel a lot more peaceful, Delia. You would too. I promise. It doesn't hurt. You just get tired. You've been tired a long time, haven't you, honey? We all have."

Upstairs I heard Caleb's door. Feet on the landing. He came halfway down the stairs and stopped.

"Mom?" he said. "Is that Pastor Gene? Can I go out and say hi?"

Juno ran up the steps past me and sat on the step below him, between him and the front door, and stared at his heart.

Anyway. I'm writing this from a motel off the interstate. Not saying which one. A hundred and ninety miles from home. It's four in the morning.

I put Caleb in the truck around midnight after they'd all gone. Told him we were going to his aunt's in Ohio. He didn't argue, which isn't him. Got in the passenger seat, buckled up, looked out the window at town going by, the church, the post office, the high school, and said real quiet, "I like it here, Mom. Everyone's so nice."

Then nothing. Not a word the rest of the drive.

Juno rode in the back seat behind him, not in the crate. Sat up the whole way with her nose a few inches off his headrest. Staring through it at my son.

At thirty miles I was watching her in the mirror. Praying. I'm not religious and I was praying out loud in the truck at quarter after one in the morning, please lie down, please lie down, please.

She didn't lie down.

Not at thirty. Not at fifty-three where the hospital is. Not at a hundred. A hundred and ninety miles she sat behind him and stared. And she's at the end of the motel bed now, in the dark, staring at him. He's on his back on top of the covers. Eyes a little open. Hands folded on his chest.

The way they fold them at the funeral home.

His phone's been buzzing on the nightstand all night. I picked it up an hour ago and read the screen. His friends, the team, numbers I didn't know. Dozens. All the same kind of thing, same gentle, no-hurry way. *come home man.* *everyone misses you.* *coach says no rush.* *its so quiet here now its nice.* *your mom can come too.* *tell her we're not mad about the dog.*

Last one came at 3:51. 3:51 in the morning. From Gene. I didn't know Gene had my son's number.

*Whenever you're both ready. We'll leave the porch light on.*

I turned the phone off. Caleb didn't stir.

It's not wearing off this time. The city was a reprieve. Whatever came up out of that ground is in him now. All the way in.

OK. Here's the actual reason I'm typing this at four in the morning instead of sitting with my kid.

We stopped for gas around two, truck stop off the interstate, different county, different state, a hundred and sixty miles from home. I let Juno out to pee on the strip of grass by the air pump. Guy filling up a pickup at the next pump. Fifties, hunting jacket. Nobody.

Juno walked over and sat at his feet and stared at his chest.

He laughed and said "Well hey there" and scratched her ears.

I got her back in the truck. And pulling out I saw a woman come out of the store with a coffee, and Juno in the back seat turned her head and tracked her across the lot with her nose. The way she does when she's on a scent she's trained to report. Then the cashier, through the window. Young kid, couldn't have been twenty. Then a guy asleep in the cab of a semi.

All the way down the interstate. Every car that got close. Every light in every little town. Her head kept turning. A man walking a dog. A woman at a bus stop. A family in the next lane at a red light, kids in the back waving at her through the glass.

It's not our town.

It was never just our town. We were just the town with a dog that can't be wrong.

I don't know how far it goes. I don't know if our old fire is the only one or if there's seams burning under every town, every city, the whole country, leaking afterdamp up through the cracks for years, decades, making everybody a little more tired. A little more calm. A little more peaceful. Till they don't notice the moment they stop. I don't know if this is new. Maybe it's always been like this and the dogs always knew and nobody listened.

But I'm asking yinz something. Seriously.

If you've got a dog, and your dog has started sitting down next to somebody and staring, not at their face, at their chest, and won't quit, won't take a treat, won't come when you call: don't laugh it off. Don't drag her away. Don't tell your buddy she's just being a goofball.

Look at the person. Look for the sweat that isn't there. Look at their plate. Look for the flies.

And then ask yourself how long it's been since you felt anything but tired.

It's five now. Caleb hasn't moved. Eyes still a little open. Little smile. Room's cold and getting colder.

Juno's been restless since three.

I heard her nails on the carpet. Heard her walk away from Caleb, along the side of the bed, slow, nose down, sweeping. Like a grid. Heard her stop.

She's standing next to my chair.

Not sitting. Standing. Nose pressed into my side, right under my ribs, breathing in, long and slow. She does that on a search when the source is deep. When the scent's only just starting to come up through the ground and she isn't sure yet. She checks, moves off, comes back, checks again.

This is the fourth time tonight she's come back to me.

She hasn't sat. Not yet.

I'm so tired. I've been tired so long. Since September. Before September. I don't know since when.

I'm going to post this now. Then I'm going to sit here with my hand on her back and watch her, all night if I have to, and see what she does.

She's never been wrong.
