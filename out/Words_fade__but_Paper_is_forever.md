**SESSION 23**

 

My mind isn’t reliable anymore… I know that now. But I can tell you with absolute certainty I know exactly 2 things. Cults are real. They’re just people looking for connection or guidance, a community. Most are harmless, others… well we’ve all seen the Netflix documentaries. The other thing I know for sure and you’ll come to know all too well is that horror stories are more real than people would care to believe. It’s people that make us afraid of the dark. The ones that make us shiver in the dark when we take the trash out alone and feel someone watching. The endless shadow in that room you just shut the light off in that you swear wasn’t that dark before but is somehow getting darker. It’s people that give us that fear… at least that’s what I used to think. I’m not so sure anymore. I think that’s why I’m here. Honestly, I don’t know what to think anymore. What’s real and what’s just the fear in my head telling me lies? The shiver down my spine isn’t going away… it never goes away. I think… I know I’m being stalked… cursed… haunted by someone… something. I’m not crazy, I know I’m not crazy.

  I’ve been in this office 23 times now. It was small and cluttered, paperwork stacked in leaning towers on every flat surface — organized chaos, the kind that only makes sense to the person who built it. On the far side of the room sat a cramped little reading nook, two oversized chairs facing each other: one pushed up against the window, the other against a bare wall.

Dr. Bell sat in the chair by the window. Late forties, a worn tweed jacket, glasses slipping down her nose as she paged through a folder of handwritten notes.

I sat across from her, restrained at the left wrist. I was in white scrubs. My skin had gone dry and coarse, and I knew without looking that the circles under my eyes had darkened into something like bruises. I kept my gaze on my own feet, working my thumbs against each other, not blinking.

I had not slept, not really, in longer than I could remember.

Dr. Bell clicked her pen several times and turned to a clean page.

"This is evaluation session twenty-three with patient R. Bonder," she said. "August twenty-ninth, five-forty p.m." She looked up. "Now, Officer Bonder."

"I've asked you repeatedly not to call me that," I said. "I'm not a cop anymore."

"But you were a law enforcement officer, Ms. Bonder. That's why you're here."

I smiled, and there was no warmth in it at all.

"I know why I'm here," I said. "Now please, yet again, for the last time — just call me Riley."

"How about we stick with Ms. Bonder, as usual. That work for you?"

"Fine." My jaw tightened. "So what's on the docket for today? More word association games?"

"Would you like to talk about what happened with your mother?"

I went rigid. I shifted in the chair, the restraint at my wrist clinking softly.

"No," I said. "No, I wouldn't."

"I'm not trying to trick you, Ms. Bonder. I know your mother was very ill when all this started. I know it must have added an unfair amount of pressure to an already unbearable situation."

I said nothing. I kept my eyes on the floor.

"How about you tell me a story," Dr. Bell said, "about why you're here. Let's start from the beginning this time."

"Why? You know what happened, I know what happened. We've done this twenty times already!"

"Do you know why I ask you to recount what happened, starting at various points?"

I exhaled through my nose. "To compile a baseline. To figure out whether I'm making shit up."

Dr. Bell allowed herself a small, amused look. "That's right."

"I've been here nearly three months," I said. "You can't tell if I'm insane yet?"

"Insanity is a legal defense, Ms. Bonder, not a clinical explanation for your mental state. And to answer your question — no. Considering the circumstances of your time here, and given all the details of your case, I'm still trying to figure out the best way to help you."

"You can't help me."

"I can try," she said, "if you'll let me."

I heard the sincerity in it, and it seemed to cost me something to sit with. I said nothing.

"Different states of mental duress result in—"

"I don't have a mental condition," I cut in. "I'm cursed. Or haunted. Take your pick."

"I know you believe that."

"You should too. After everything I've told you." I leaned forward as far as the restraint allowed. "If you eliminate every possible answer, the only thing left is the impossible."

"You're right," she said. "That's very true. But we haven't eliminated anything yet, Ms. Bonder. Only you have."

I leaned back and tried to rub my face out of habit, forgetting the restraint on my wrist. It caught, and the small failure made me angrier than anything else that had been said.

"I was out on patrol," I said finally. "It was going slow. Mid-day shifts usually are. So me and my partner—"

Dr. Bell flipped back through her notes. "Your partner, Officer Alex Wyatt?"

"Yeah."

She made a note, gestured for me to continue. Then came a knock at the door — loud, out of place.

"Bring it in," Dr. Bell called.

The door opened. Two orderlies wheeled in a television on a stand.

"Thank you, gentlemen," Dr. Bell said, and then, to me: "I apologize for the interruption, Ms. Bonder, but with your permission I'd like to try something different today."

"What," I said, "are you gonna try and clockwork orange me into being a good little zombie?" I let out a short, nervous laugh.

She didn't take the bait.

"This is edited footage from both your chest and cruiser dash cam," she said. "I would like to review it with you, and have you walk me through what we're both seeing."

I swallowed hard against a lump rising in my throat. "That stuff's only supposed to be used for legal matters."

"Well, technically, this is a legal matter, Ms. Bonder. But besides that — I really think it will help. Visual aids help not only to remember events in real time, but can also help put the subject back into the emotional state they were in at the given time."

I could feel my own hesitation, plain on my face.

"I'm not going to beg, Ms. Bonder," she said. "But I will insist that it'll help."

"Well, if you insist."

She picked up a remote from the coffee table and clicked on the television. And I watched myself on screen. Cam footage from my cruiser and chest cam. It was like watching the opening of a horror movie except I know how it ends and I could have changed it. But I didn’t. It was just a stupid game, a story. Kids playing make believe. When you aren’t afraid of the boogie man anymore you make up something worse. Only problem is when you crowd source a boogeyman to the fucked up kids online at 3 am you get something unimaginable. Enough people put their fears into something it becomes real to them… to everyone. I think that’s what it wanted. Or what someone told it it wanted.

Words fade… but paper is forever.

The first time I saw that phrase was at the crime scene of a 12 year old boy who’d disappeared out of his locked room. No struggle, no forced entry, nothing missing from the house. Only thing the detectives had to go on was a series of online stories he had open about something called the Paper Cult or the Paper Man Cult. Scary stories told by the campfire light of laptop and phone screens by kids trying to scare each other awake. The more we looked into it the more we found that it wasn’t an isolated incident. Dozens confirmed missing all connected by a single phrase. Words fade, but paper is forever. It doesn’t take much to stroke the imagination, just a single question. What does it mean? It’s drives sane people mad and brilliant people catatonic. Mysteries are a playground and everyone wants a turn on the monkey bars, especially children. I think it knew that too. Counted on it… fed on it.

I’m not crazy. I know that… I know, I’m not crazy. If I know anything it’s those 3 things... and I know what IT is. It hides it's face behind that paper mache mask, but I know what it is and everyone else will too... one way or another.
