It started how these things always start: with my kid coming home from school on a Friday and acting like her entire future depended on me buying some plastic nonsense I'd never heard of.

"Mum, I NEEEED a Labubu."

I was in the kitchen at the time, grating cheese over leftover spaghetti, so I sort of half-heard her at first. I thought she'd said "baboon" or "balloon" or some new YouTube word I was already too old to understand.

"A what?!"

"A Labubu," she said, with the exhausted patience of an eight-year-old who has just realised her mother is tragically uncultured. "Everyone has one. LITERALLY everyone."

I looked at her over the cheese grater. "And what's a Labubu exactly?"

"It's like this little monster thing. It's cute, but creepy cute. It has big ears and fur, and these...teeth." She pulled her lips back with her fingers to show me. "Nine teeth. Sharp ones."

"Lovely! Because that's exactly what every child needs. A furry creature with a dental problem."

She ignored that completely and started telling me about blind boxes, secret rares, different collections, how some of them were worth loads of money, and how Sam from school said if you got the ghost one, it could grant wishes. Or curse you. Apparently, Sam wasn't clear on the finer details.

This was the same Sam who once convinced half the class that swallowing watermelon seeds made vines grow in your stomach, so I wasn't exactly rushing to add his research to the family budget.

But my daughter had that look. The one kids get when a trend has fully taken over the playground and your refusal to participate will apparently destroy their social standing forever. So the next day, because I am weak and easily emotionally blackmailed by small humans, we went to Pop Mart.

The shop was packed! Wall-to-wall little boxes, all bright colors and glossy eyes, with strange creatures staring out from every display like they knew something I didn't. My daughter made a beeline straight for one particular shelf and picked up a box from the Exciting Macaron series, turning it over in her hands like she could somehow feel which one was inside.

"This one," she said. "This one's lucky."

We paid for it, and she didn't even make it back to the car before she started ripping at the packaging. By the time I'd unlocked the doors, she was already holding the little thing in both hands.

Her face fell.

"Oh," she said. "It's just a normal one."

I leaned over to look, and honestly… normal wasn't the word I'd have chosen.

It had greyish-brown fur, big pointed ears, and round, dark eyes that looked slightly too wet. Its little grin showed those famous nine teeth, and there was something about the mouth that bothered me straight away. It wasn't scary, exactly. Plenty of toys look a bit weird. But this thing looked like it was enjoying a joke I wasn't part of.

There was also a smudge under its chin, like a manufacturing mark, or dirt caught in the fur. I remember thinking I should wipe it off when we got home.

She named him, Boo.

From that moment on, Boo went everywhere.

He came to the table at breakfast, sat on the arm of the sofa during films, watched her do homework, watched me cook tea, watched us watch him. My daughter talked to him constantly, and at first I thought it was just normal kid stuff. Children give their toys personalities all the time. One of her teddies once had a three-week career as a dentist.

But Boo wasn't treated like a toy. He was treated like a friend who'd moved in.

"Boo doesn't like carrots," she told me one night, pushing them around her plate.

"Well, Boo can pay rent before he gets opinions on dinner."

She gave me this look, then glanced down at him in her lap. "He says you're rude."

I laughed, because what else do you do? Kids are odd. They say creepy things by accident all the time. They have no filter and a terrifying sense of timing.

Then, a few nights later, I woke up at around two in the morning with that horrible feeling that something in the house had shifted. You know when you wake up and you don't know why, but your body has already decided there's a problem? The house was quiet, rain tapping against the windows, heating pipes clicking like they always do. Nothing dramatic.

I got up anyway.

Her bedroom door was slightly open, and when I passed it, I heard whispering.

I pushed the door with two fingers, just enough to see inside.

She was sitting up in bed with her legs crossed, Boo balanced on the duvet in front of her. Her back was straight, her head tilted slightly down, and she was whispering to him so softly I couldn't make out the words. Then she stopped. She waited. After a few seconds, she nodded, like someone had answered.

Then she whispered again.

I stood there in the hallway, barefoot on the cold carpet, feeling like I'd walked in on something private.

I nearly went in. I nearly said her name. But then she smiled at the doll in this small, secretive way, and I backed away, instead.

The next morning, I told myself she'd been half-asleep. Maybe dreaming. Maybe playing. I told myself a lot of sensible things, because sensible things are easier to live with.

That afternoon, I found her in the garden.

We've got a big tree at the far end, too close to the fence, always dropping leaves and twigs and bits of bird mess all over the grass. She was sitting underneath it in her school tights, with Boo beside her on the dirt.

At her feet was a nest.

It was completely wrecked. Twigs snapped, feathers scattered, little bits of shell and down stuck in the mud. Three baby birds lay in the middle of it, their tiny bodies curled in on themselves, their beaks open like they were still waiting to be fed.

My stomach turned.

"Oh, sweetheart," I said, walking over carefully. "The nest must have fallen."

She didn't look up. She was running one finger over Boo's ear, smoothing the fur down.

"Yes," she said.

Something about her voice made me stop.

Then she added, "Boo didn't like the noise they made in the morning, anyway."

She said it casually. Flatly. Like she was telling me the post had arrived.

I stared at her for a second, waiting for her face to change. Waiting for upset, confusion, guilt, anything that looked like my daughter. But she just sat there, calm as anything, with mud under her nails and that awful little doll beside her.

I wanted to pick Boo up right then and throw him straight in the bin.

I didn't. I wish I had.

Over the next few days, I started finding feathers in the house. One stuck to the windowsill in her room. Two on the landing. A little grey one on the bathroom floor, damp from someone's wet foot. I kept telling myself they must have come in on shoes or clothes from the garden.

Then I found a small pile under her bed.

At first, I thought it was stuffing from an old toy, something she'd ripped apart and hidden because she knew I'd moan about the mess. I crouched down, reached under, and dragged it into the light.

The feathers were clumped together, grey and brown, and stuck with something dark.

Then, I saw the beak.

Tiny. Crushed. Almost hidden under the matted down.

I dropped the whole thing like it had burned me.

That evening, I sat her down in the living room. I made sure my voice stayed gentle, because whatever was going on, she was still my little girl. She sat on the sofa with Boo tucked under one arm, his face peering out over her elbow.

"I need to ask you about the birds," I said.

She blinked at me.

"The ones in the garden. And the feathers under your bed."

Her eyes moved to Boo, then back to me.

"I'm not angry," I said, even though I was scared enough to feel sick. "I just need to understand."

She stroked the top of Boo's head with her thumb. "He didn't like them."

"Boo?"

She nodded.

"And did you hurt them because Boo asked you to?"

She looked almost offended then, as if I'd misunderstood something obvious.

"I helped him," she said. "He said I could be his best friend."

That was the moment I stopped pretending this was just a phase.

That night, I waited until she was asleep. I stood in her doorway for a while, listening to her breathing, watching the shape of Boo beside her pillow. Then I went in, picked him up as quietly as I could, and took him out of the room.

I wrapped him in two pillowcases, taped them shut, put the whole bundle into a shoebox, put the shoebox in a bin bag, then another bin bag, and carried it up into the loft. I shoved it behind boxes of old baby clothes, Christmas decorations, and a broken fan we'd been meaning to throw away for six years.

There was no way she could get to it. She hated the loft ladder, and even if she didn't, she'd never find it under all that crap.

The next morning, she didn't ask where he was.

No crying. No tantrum. No searching the house.

She just watched me.

At breakfast, from behind her cereal bowl. In the hallway, while I found her shoes. Through the rear-view mirror, on the school run. Quiet, steady, patient watching, like she was waiting for me to admit something.

By lunchtime, I couldn't take it anymore. I went up into the loft.

The box was exactly where I'd left it. The tape was still sealed. The pillowcases were still wrapped the same way. Nothing looked moved, nothing looked touched.

But when I opened it, Boo was gone.

The pillowcases were empty.

Just dust, and a faint smell, like damp fur.

That night, I found him sitting on her desk.

I didn't think. I just grabbed him, took him outside, and carried him down the garden like he was something diseased. I put him on an old metal shovel, soaked him in lighter fluid, and struck a match.

The fire caught fast.

For a few seconds, I thought that was it. Fur curled. Plastic blackened. The little ears began to warp.

Then the screaming started.

It wasn't a sound in the garden. No neighbour came to the window. No dog barked. The scream was inside my head, high and sharp and constant, like static forced through teeth. I clapped my hands over my ears, but it made no difference. The flames didn't dance the way flames should. They twitched, jerking in short little movements, and the air around me tasted metallic, like I'd put a battery on my tongue.

I stayed there until there was nothing left but a blackened lump on the shovel.

Then I buried the ash at the bottom of the garden, under the tree.

When I came back inside, the house felt too quiet.

My daughter didn't come out of her room that night. No drink of water. No toilet trip. No asking for one more cuddle. Around midnight, I went to check on her.

She was lying flat on her back with her eyes open.

I stepped closer and said her name.

She turned her head towards me slowly, as if she'd been waiting.

"You made Boo angry," she said.

Then, her nose started to bleed.

It came all at once, dark and steady, running down over her lips and chin. I grabbed tissues, shouted her name, tried to sit her up, but her whole body went slack before I could get hold of her properly.

She's been in hospital for four days.

The doctors say they can't find a reason. No injury, no infection, no seizure activity that explains it. Her vitals are normal. Her scans are normal. She looks like she's sleeping, except she doesn't wake up.

I went home yesterday to get her clean clothes, and some of her things.

Boo was on her windowsill.

Clean. Dry. Smiling.

I didn't scream. I was past screaming by then. I picked him up with both hands, carried him to the back door, and set him on the step.

I didn't threaten him. I didn't burn him again. I didn't call him a stupid, little, cursed bastard, even though I wanted to.

I just whispered, "Please. Bring her back. Take me instead."

He was gone by morning.

The doll. The pressure in the house. The strange heaviness in the air. All of it.

And the good news is, she woke up yesterday!

She didn't remember anything. She was tired and confused, and starving. The doctors called it a miracle. For a few hours, I nearly let myself believe them.

I brought her home this morning. She laughed at something on telly, asked for toast, and hugged me so tightly I cried into her hair.

I'm writing this down now because about an hour ago, just before bed, my daughter said something.

She was standing in the hallway in her pyjamas, rubbing her eyes, completely half-asleep.

Then she looked past me, towards my bedroom door, and whispered, "I don't think you'll like the dark place."

I froze.

"What?"

She yawned and hugged her teddy tighter.

"Where Boo lives when he's not talking," she said. "He said you promised to go."

Then she padded back to bed like she hadn't just turned my blood to ice.
