The air in the district always tasted like torrid copper and wet asphalt, especially around 3:00 AM when the neon sign for Lester’s Transmission flickered its dying magenta guts against the peeling brickwork.

I thought the heavy thudding against the side door was just a stray dog or a junkie looking for scrap—or a quick fix.

My hands were still shaking from the three cups of black coffee I’d pounded to fight off the graveyard-shift drag. My nervous system was already a live wire. Being four months clean means every shadow in your peripheral vision looks like a ghost, and every sudden noise sounds like the floor dropping out from under you.

I was elbow-deep in a blown-out transmission pan, grease stinging my forearms. A muffled synth track pulsed off an old cassette deck on the workbench—warped analog tape-hiss looping over a steady mechanical bassline.

When I stood up and clicked the shop flashlight toward the chain-link partition, the light didn’t bounce off anything.

It just… died.

The thing standing on the other side of the wire mesh wasn’t a man—not really, though it wore the soaked canvas jacket of a shift worker. It was carved out of absolute, light-swallowing darkness. A matte-black silhouette that seemed to pull the neon glow right out of the air around it.

I didn’t hear it breathe. That was the first clear sign something was wrong.

A normal man running in the freezing rain sounds like a freight train—gasping, wheezing, heart hammering. There was only the wet, rhythmic crunch of gravel settling under an impossible weight.

And then, deep in the center of the shadow where a face should have been, a single point of solid, needle-sharp crimson flared open. Like a dying tube television turning on in an empty room. One that only I could see.

I didn’t call the cops. I dropped the wrench, hit the back deadbolt with all of my weight, and bolted straight out into the industrial park.

That was two nights ago. Or maybe three. Time stops working right when your blood is mostly caffeine and cortisol.

I’ve stolen three different cars, hot-wired a delivery van, and run until my lungs felt like broken glass. Somewhere in the middle of it I almost called my sister. Almost. The thought of her voice on the other end was the only thing that felt more terrifying than what was behind me.

In the back of my mind that gnawing old voice—the cynical, defensive part of my brain I fought so hard to silence—started whispering: You’re hallucinating. The relapse is just catching up. The withdrawal is finally painting walls for you.

Every time I kill the engine, crawl into a dark corner, and look back through the rain-streaked rear window—past the neon blur of the all-night diners and the skeletal frames of the shipping docks—there it is.

Walking.

Never running. Never resting. Its heavy boots hitting the pavement with a metronomic inevitability. And through the black pitch of its coat, that single red eye stays fixed on the back of my skull.

I tried desperately to prove to myself it wasn’t real.

Yesterday afternoon I ditched the van on a dead-end logging road behind the old abandoned slaughterhouse. Climbed fifty feet up an ancient white pine. Sat in the rotting canopy for six hours without moving an inch. My muscles cramped. My vision blurred with static.

I told myself: *When the psychosis passes, the clearing will be empty.*

At 4:15 PM the distinct clack-crunch started up the incline.

It didn’t look up at the tree. It didn’t scan the branches. It walked straight to the base of the pine, stopped, and the red iris burned upward through the gray needles, perfectly centered on my chest.

I didn’t wait to see if it could climb. I dropped out of the tree, tore my shoulder open on a jagged branch, and ran until my boots filled with blood.

Since then the hours have dissolved into a brutal, continuous blur of motion. Stumbling across forgotten service roads. Ducking behind rotting hay bales. Keeping just one step ahead of that steady, relentless rhythm in the dark.

Now my phone battery is at two percent.

The screen flickers with green horizontal scanlines, just like an old VHS tape at the end of its ribbon. The highway ahead of me is blocked by construction barricades. My legs are completely giving out, heavy as lead pipes.

I can hear the measured, rhythmic crunch of gravel coming up the shoulder now.

Close. Too close.

I ducked into an abandoned loading bay behind the old steel mill, wedging myself between a stack of rusted oil drums and a rotting pallet. My chest heaves.

I press the heels of my hands hard into my eyes, squeezing them shut, trying to force the static away.

It’s just my brain, I tell myself, pressing my nails into my palms until the pain is sharp and real. It’s just the lack of sleep catching up. It’s not out there.

Slowly, I open my eyes.

The loading bay is pitch black, illuminated only by the faint, dying pulse of my phone screen on the concrete floor.

And standing right in the center of the doorway, blotting out the gray drizzle and the distant neon hum, is the silhouette.

It doesn’t move. It doesn’t rush me.

But as I sit paralyzed against the oil drums, a strange, sickening clarity washes over me—not a hallucination, but a physical weight pressing directly into my cerebral cortex. A frequency. A low, sub-audible hum vibrating through my teeth, translating itself into something that isn’t quite language:

…found… residual damage… still usable…

My heart stutters in my chest. My breath catches.

If it’s a hallucination, why is the air around it dropping below freezing? Why is my breath frosting in the dark? Why are the rusted edges of the oil drums nearest to it melting away into fine-grained ash, as if exposed to absolute zero?

The red eye doesn’t need light to see me. It burns brighter in the dark, cutting straight through my retinas, straight through the paranoia, straight to the truth.
