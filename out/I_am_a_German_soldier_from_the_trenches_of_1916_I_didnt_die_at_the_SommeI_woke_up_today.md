My name is Johann. Until what feels like yesterday, I was an Unteroffizier in the Imperial German Army, stationed near the Somme. If you know your history, you know what happened there. The British artillery had been pounding our trenches for seven straight days. It wasn’t just noise; it was a physical weight. The air tasted like sulfur, blood, and pulverized soil. We were crammed into a deep dugout, listening to the earth collapse around us, waiting for the shell that had our names on it.

When it finally hit, it didn’t sound like an explosion.

There was a blinding, violet flash that burned straight through my closed eyelids. The deafening roar of the *Trommelfeuer* suddenly cut to absolute, terrifying silence. The heat was instantaneous, but it didn’t burn my skin—it felt like it was pulling my skin inside out. I felt myself falling through a freezing, bottomless void, suffocating on air that felt entirely empty.

Then, I slammed into the ground.

I choked, inhaling a lungful of cold, damp air. I expected the taste of mud and chemical gas, but instead, it tasted sweet. Clean. I opened my eyes, bracing for the ruined landscape of No Man’s Land, but all I saw was green.

I was lying in a dense, manicured park. The cratered wasteland was gone. The sky wasn't choked with black smoke, but bright and clear. I panicked, dragging myself backward, looking for my Mauser rifle, my gas mask—anything. They were gone. All I had left was my torn, mud-caked field gray uniform, stiff with dried blood that wasn't mine.

I stumbled out of the trees and onto a smooth, black road. It had no stones, no mud, and no horse tracks. That’s when the first beast screamed past me.

It was a sleek, metal carriage moving at an impossible speed, completely silent until it whined past, throwing a gust of wind in my face. There was no horse. No steam engine. Inside sat a man, casually turning a wheel, completely ignoring me. More of them followed.

I hid in the bushes, weeping. I thought I had died and gone to a very strange, clean purgatory.

Eventually, hunger drove me to walk toward a cluster of towering, glittering glass buildings in the distance. I now know I am in modern-day Germany. I managed to steal a strange, oversized garment from a clothesline to hide my uniform. I have been hiding in a public library, using these glowing glass slates they leave out for the public. It took me hours of weeping and staring to understand how to read this language—it is German, but chopped up, softened, and filled with strange English words.

I looked up the history. I looked up the war.

We lost. And then, twenty years later, we started another one under a madman, and we lost even more horribly. The Germany I fought for, the Kaiser I swore an oath to, the world I knew—it is all dead. A century has passed. My mother, my sister, my comrades—they all rotted into the soil while I slept for a microsecond.

But that isn't why I am writing this on this forum. I am writing this because I don't think I traveled alone.

Something went wrong when that shell tore the fabric of 1916.

Yesterday, the ringing in my ears started again. At first, I thought it was just the trauma of the shelling. But it’s getting louder. It’s the distinct, rhythmic thump of heavy artillery, miles away, echoing in my skull. No one else can hear it. When I stand in the middle of these crowded modern streets, surrounded by people staring at their tiny glowing rectangles, I can hear the distant screams of dying men beneath the hum of traffic.

And then there is the smell. The clean air is fading. Every hour, the stench of mustard gas and decomposing flesh grows thicker around me.

Last night, I was sleeping under a bridge when the phantom artillery dropped closer. A puddle of water nearby didn't reflect the modern streetlights. For a split second, the reflection showed a dark, muddy trench filled with wire. And standing in that reflection was a shape. It was wearing a gas mask, but the rubber was melted to its skull, and its eyes were hollow, glowing pits of that same violet light that brought me here.

It crawled out of the puddle. I ran until my lungs burned.

I am changing, too. This morning, I looked at my hands. The skin around my fingernails is turning a sickening, bruised black. It looks exactly like the trench foot my men used to get before their flesh fell off. It’s spreading up my wrists.

I don't think time allows for anomalies. I think the universe is pulling me back, or rather, the trench is coming to claim what it missed. The war isn't over for me. It’s catching up.

If you live in Berlin and you smell chlorine in the air, or if you hear the faint, terrifying whistle of a falling shell when the sky is completely clear... run. It means I am near, and whatever is hunting me is right behind me.
