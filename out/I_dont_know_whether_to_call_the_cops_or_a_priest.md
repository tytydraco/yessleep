The town of Brunsworth was a quaint and quiet place nestled in the mountains of Tennessee. Honestly, there ain’t much here. We got a couple of mom-and-pop restaurants and one of them burger chains, but we have to drive to Gatlinburg or even Asheville to get somewhere real nice. Brunsworth was the quintessential small town south of the Mason-Dixon. Our people were as sweet as the tea they served. The kudzu proudly reached towards the heavens. We had a routine of sorts that, up until this point, no one dared to break. The few office buildings and school houses were filled to the brim on the weekdays. Friday nights were spent cheering on our local high school, the Brunsworth Bears, who were doomed to never see State Champs. Saturdays were dedicated solely to college football with most people packed like sardines in Tammy’s. Sundays, however, were reserved for the preacher who praised the good word and asked us all to repent our sins–most of which were committed the night before.

I remember when our little city opened its first bar. They’d just turned a dry town wet, and I was damn excited to sneak a couple of beers. I’d spent my college summers working behind the bar at Tammy’s Tavern. When Bill, Tammy’s husband, finally retired, he gave me the keys and a keg. Other than a bad flood and the occasional snowstorm, our town hadn’t had too many problems.   
Everyone knew everyone. That was the motto that drove our tight-knit community to break at the seams after tragedy struck. Yesterday, Micheal Jones, an old friend of mine, came running into the bar in the early hours of the morning. It wasn’t even 11:00am and he was sweatin’ like a sinner in church. Horrid heat was already gettin’ to him and it was only June. 

What struck me weird was how he acted. My friend wasn’t a very dainty nor delicate person, like most of the men here in Brunsworth. He’d been drinkin’ since we were 16, and his gut started hanging at around 35.; It would have probably shown earlier if not for his 6’5” stature. He never walked into a room; he stomped. Today, however, wasn’t Micheal’s regular ruckus. Instead, he stumbled in lookin’ like he'd seen a damn ghost—tripping over his feet worse than a newborn fawn. His boots scraped against the floor, hands shakin' so bad I could hear his keys janglin' in his pocket.

“Ain’t it a little early to be drinking buddy?” I joked, but Michael barely mumbled a response. 

His face was locked into one emotion: fear. Quivering lips, shaky hands, and restless eyes dartin' around the room like something might jump out at him. Sometimes people walk into the bar, and I knew they had a story for me. All their emotions read like a book on their face, and with one drop of liquor, I’d open their covers and know why. Michael’s face was something out of a Stephen King novel. If I’d taken a photo of his face right then, you’d think I was showin’ you some movie poster for the hot summer thriller. 

Though it was too early to serve, I grabbed some tequila and poured a shot. From the get-go, I knew this was gonna be a while. The silence stretched between us, broken only by his ragged breathin'.

“A body!” Michael mustered from his jaw-dropped lips. “There’s a body by the water at Thistle Lake Docks.”

“Whose body?” I urgently asked, my pulse quickenin'. 

Usually, if someone drowns, we gotta wait for the police to dive down to the bottom of the lake and retrieve the body, but I hadn’t heard of anyone drownin’ recently. 

“I—I don’t,” Michael could barely get out the next words. “I don’t know. I—it looks like-like Frances Jr.” 

*Frances Jr.?* I thought. The man was in my bar until close the night before with some out-of-town clients. I'd even checked his tab—wasn't even that much compared to usual. Did he wander the wrong way home? Did he decide to go for a late-night swim? Well, that didn’t make sense either. None of it made any sense.

“Did a coyote get to him? Damn shame if so.” Maybe it was a little insensitive of me, but people die; it’s just a fact of life. 

While tragic, Frances Jr. was nearin’ 60 and a rack of ribs away from a heart attack. Honestly though, something just didn’t sit right with me. Other than the weird circumstances of him bein’ out in the woods so late, people don’t come runnin’ in for deaths like this unless there is something to talk about. When Mary Lous, the local elementary school principal, was found dead, some people came in with their theories, but they weren’t in a sheer panic like Michael is. After asking about the coyotes, I knew something was different. Michael’s face went from shock to dead serious. The color drained right out of him.

“No,” Michael grabbed my wrist after throwin’ back the shot I’d poured.  “You’re not getting’ me. There ain’t much of him left.” I was too scared to ask, but in this moment, my mouth moved faster than my brain.

“What do you mean?” I said, soon regretting why I even asked him.

“Something…well someone ate part of his face.” He muttered. The words hung in the air like smoke. 

“Someone? How do you suppose?” I asked, like a stupid person. 

Michael couldn’t really explain it. He tried to talk about the size and shape of the bites, but it’s not like the man ever went to medical school or nothin’. I tried to attribute the loss to a number of things: wild animals, one too many drinks, and damn male stupidity, but none of it was makin’ sense. Michael offered to lead me to the body of Frances Jr. I wish I’d said no. I wish I waited for the police. I wish I was an ounce smarter back then. Hindsight really is 20/20.

Michael led me down the same path I’d taken to go swimmin’ as a kid. Him and I had grown up at the same time and were used to runnin’ down to the docks to cool ourselves off. With heat like this, no one survives without a dip or dive into the lake’s water. This hike, however, didn’t feel like one of those old times. The nostalgia had been masked by the adrenaline coursin’ through our veins. Our footsteps crunched on dry pine needles and broken twigs. Michael's breathin' was still rough, and I could hear my own heartbeat in my ears. As we got closer to the body, I could feel the air thicken and woods grow darker, despite the morning sun. Even the birds went dead silent as we approached the body of Frances Jr.—no chirpin', no rustlin' in the branches. Just our heavy steps on the forest floor and the distant sound of water lappin' against the dock.

Right before the dock, the first thing I noticed was the blood. It wasn’t a small amount or nothin’. Frances Jr. had completely bled out onto the forest floor. Dark stains soaked into the pine needles and moss, spreadin' out from where the body lay like spilled paint. The metallic scent mixed with somethin' else—decay, thick and cloyin'.  
When we finally approached Thistle Lake Docks, I got my first whiff of death. It hit me like a wall. No, worse. It hit me like a bullet to the skull. The stench of a dead body as it begins to decompose is grotesque—unlike anything I’d smelled before—some sort of mix between vomit, rotten eggs, and moldy fruit. Like you’d left meat in your fridge, lost power, and didn’t come back until days later. My eyes teared up, stomach churning, skin crawling with goosebumps. One hand was gripping my abdomen, the other forcing my mouth close as bile snuck up my throat and filled my mouth. Michael slowed, pointin' ahead through the trees.

"Right there," He whispered.

Then, I got a good look at the body, and my knees fell weak. Michael grabbed me by the waist so I wouldn’t fall onto the blood-soaked foliage. Everything I thought I knew about human nature was wiped clean from my memory with the image of Frances’ body burned into my psyche. That day, I learned that sometimes humans lack humanity.   
What frightened me the most was how human his kill was. The slices in his stomach and back were cut clean like a knife, and his body was covered in bite marks from a person’s mouth, teeth marks pressed deep into flesh as clear as a dental work. Frances’ neck was being held together by a thread. 

The most haunting part of the body was not the spilling guts of ripped out throat; it was his face. Red blood, most likely his own, was splattered across the cheeks and nose. Bruises were just beginning to form under his eyes, and his jaw was broken sideways. While the bugs had gotten to one eye, his glass one was fixed open. Frances’ face was paralyzed in the last emotion he would ever experience, absolute terror. No animal could do this, and I hoped to God no human could either. But standin' there, lookin' at what was left of my customer, my neighbor, I knew the truth. Nothing could make sense of it. But you just had to see it to believe it. I ain’t tellin’ you no ghost story either, this is the real damn thing. Somethin' evil had come to Brunsworth, and I fear this is just the beginning. 

After everything that happened, I decided to head back to the bar and close up while the police began their investigation. I was supposed to report to the station the minute I locked Tammy’s doors. Right as I was leaving, I got a call from my good friend Holly. 

“I heard you were the one who found Frances Jr.” Her voice shook. 

“Hon, I can’t say anything. The police said—“

“I know who did it.” Her voice was certain. “Look, I’m not supposed to say anything either, but it was Warner Jones.”

“Shrew? How do you—“

“He came and confessed.” She sniffled. “And I don’t know who else to go to. But you found Frances. You’re the only one who’ll get it.” She was tripping over her words, barely finishing the first sentence before the second one began. 

Holly then recounted the entire story over the phone, and now I’ll relay that to you. The secretary told me that he didn’t look like the same Shrew we knew. Like something changed.

Holly Bell has been working at the police station since she was nineteen years old. She’s seen her fair share of nightmares walk through the front doors with handcuffs tightly locked and police screamin’ at the newly charged defendants. Sometimes teenagers came through with their tails tucked between their legs to turn themselves in for vandalism or petty theft under their mama’s watchful eye. Other times it was drunks hollerin’ at deputies. Even the most violent domestic disputes didn’t shock the secretary, but Holly’s never seen anything like this; an adult calmly walkin’ the way Shrew did. 

The bell above the door chimed like it always did, that same cheerful ding that announced visitors every day. Holly looked up from her usual monotonous paperwork, ready with her usual “how can I help you,” but the words died in her throat. Shrew stood in the doorway, his clothes were tattered and torn like he’d been in a fight. Blood, thick and dark, stained his shirt front and sleeves. The metallic blood mixed with sweat created a putrid yet wholly human smell that made Holly's stomach turn upside down. A hint of sulfur emanated from his hair, like matchsticks, and a rich man’s cologne lingered like a ghost. 

However, it wasn’t the blood or the smell that made Holly’s hands shake as she reached for the phone. It was his eyes. Shrew has always been a beaten-down little man, shoulders hunched from years of takin’ whatever life threw at him. His eyes normally dart around like a trapped animal, never quite meetin’ anyone’s gaze straight on. Today, though, he stared right at her with eyes that didn’t seem to belong to the same person. Cold. Empty. Like lookin’ into a well that had gone dry. 

“Oh Warren,” She gasped. “What did you do? Please don’t tell me you went after the person who killed Frances.” 

Mere seconds after saying that, she realized that Shrew wasn’t a vigilante. From her description, it sounded like his very soul was sucked from his body and replaced with a darker force that didn’t give a damn about anyone but themselves. His gaze never faltered, stuck staring at the secretary to make sure she saw everything he was doing. A smile tugged at his cheeks, one as sinister as they come. Even as the words left her mouth, she knew that wasn’t right. This wasn’t some vigilante justice. The man standin’ in front of her wasn’t the same Shrew she’d known since he was knee high. A husk of a shell of a man. 

“Six.” A scratchy voice escaped his lips. “It’s ssssiiiii” He tried to conjure again.

“Six what? Six lane? Drive? Circle?” Holly tried “Use your words! Shrew, what is it?” Holly leaned forward, her hand still hoverin’ over the panic button hidden under her desk. 

It was just late enough in the evening that cops weren’t comin’ in and out like they normally do. Even though there was a room full of fully armed men right behind the doors, Holly felt as if it was just the two of them—the last people on the planet. No point in screamin’, no point in runnin’. *At least the crime scene team wouldn’t have to travel far*, she pondered.

“P-p-please i-i-it’s Warrrr—” Shrew’s hands shook violently. His mouth opened and closed like a fish gaspin' for air. 

"Warren, you need to—" she started, but before she could finish, his shaking hand shot out faster than she thought possible. Holly hadn’t even noticed that Shrew grabbed the knife from the desk until he held it to his neck and sliced it clean to the bone.

Shrew was always beaten down by the bigger kids growing up, and Frances was no exception. So, when we heard that Shrew had finally found the strength to confront his bullies, we knew it wasn’t going to be pretty. I guess we shouldn’t have been too shocked at his brutishness either. He was merely living up to his nickname – small yet deadly. After all, a shrew was meant to survive in the darkest of places, and Shrew’s upbringing was no exception. Ultimately, Shrew succumbed to the pressures of society, leaving him with no choice but to end his life as violently as he entered it. 

The only thing that kept our minds wrapped around the mystery of Frances Jr.’s death was Shrew’s final testament, “Six.” 

I still don’t know what this means. Neither Holly nor I have a clue, and the police won’t tell us anything. All I can say is that I don’t think this will be the last murder in Brunsworth.
