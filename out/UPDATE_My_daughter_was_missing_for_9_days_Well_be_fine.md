I’ve read the comments on [my previous post](https://www.reddit.com/r/nosleep/comments/1xjj0wv/my_daughter_was_missing_for_9_days_im_not_sure/) and I’m so glad to see people can still have a laugh at a mother’s pain and prey on her insecurities. A changeling, really? Is internet anonymity really such a power trip that you feel the need play with a stranger’s mind by convincing them their daughter was swapped at birth with a demon? My daughter is in distress, but she’s not a monster! She’s a scared child who just spent more than a week sleeping God knows where. She’s traumatized. She probably read the same stories and watched the same movies as you all did, and all of that is playing tricks on her developing brain.

Anyway, the post took off and some of you were nice and tried to comfort me, which is greatly appreciated. For those of you who reached out and actually cared, I’m making this post to reassure you all that my daughter is doing a bit better already.

 

I was jolted awake by the door opening behind me, my body suddenly firing on all cylinders to avoid the fall. I shot up to my feet and turned around. My daughter was standing on the other side of the frame, wearing her favorite pajamas. Her eyes were still red and puffy, and she looked exhausted, but I couldn’t have looked much better.

While my brain was still getting in gear and searching for the right words, she exhaled loudly.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

“It’s OK, sweetie,” I immediately answered.

She scoffed. “No, it’s not. I abandoned you for nine days. I come back and I’m acting crazy and I call you a liar for saying you love me and...”

I hugged her. “It’s OK. You came back. We’ll figure the rest out together, OK?”

“What if I hurt you?” she asked, wiping fresh tears against my shoulder. “What if I hurt you or I hurt my friends? What if I plow under this whole goddamn town?”

It always came back to this town. It was no surprise, really. There will never be a single teenager that will like living here. We live in one of the many holes in the Rust Belt. There just isn’t much to do or see, really. Adults cope with it fine, but it’s always been tough on kids. I know that: I grew up here. We always said we would move before she started school, but becoming a single parent made things a bit more difficult.

“Don’t worry,” I said, running a hand through her surprisingly silky hair. “Before you know it, you’ll be off to college and then you’ll get your own place. You never have to come back here if you don’t want to.”

“I don’t think I’m allowed to,” she cried.

“Don’t you dare fret over me or your friends or anybody else. If you ever feel homesick, just know that I’ll always answer the phone for you, OK? Knowing you’re safe and doing well is all I’ll ever need in this life. I’ll always love you, no matter where you are.”

I don’t know how long we stood there, but I could have spent the whole day with her in my arms.

“Mom,” she said pushing herself away, “how about I make breakfast while you call the cops and tell them I’m home? Let’s put this behind us.”

And we did. We shared eggs and bacon in silence while waiting for the police after they told me they would send an officer for a final interview, necessary to close the missing person case. I could see she was still stressed about something: she was playing with her food, focusing intently on that meaningless task.

A short while later, a knock came at the door, and I sprang up to open. As expected, the officer that came was a guy I grew up with. He had always been a bit of a dork, but his heart has always been in the right place. He also filled out nicely, but that’s neither here nor there.

“Hello, miss. May I please come in?” he inquired.

I rolled my eyes. “Of course you can come in.”

He shook the dust off his boots and took off his peaked cap, which he always wore, for some reason, before stepping in.

“Hello, miss,” he repeated, turning his attention toward my daughter, still sitting at the table.

She raised a palm in the air and let it fall back on the table.

“I won’t bother you for very long, I promise,” he said as he walked up and sat across from her. “But it is standard procedure. Before we begin, do you want your mother to leave us alone? I have a few questions to ask, and it is your right to answer in private.”

“No,” she said. “I’d prefer she stays.”

I’ll admit I felt immense relief in that moment.

“Very well,” he said, nodding emphatically.

He pulled out a small notebook and a pen from his breast pocket.

“May I ask where you’ve been in the last nine days, miss?”

“The night I went missing,” she started.

She hesitated.

“The night I went missing, I went to the old house.”

There is, on the outskirts of town, an old overgrown mansion. It was abandoned by its previous owner. The city maintained it for a while hoping to attract a buyer, but nobody with enough money wants to live here anymore. As far as I remember, it’s always been a hangout spot for rebellious youth and runaways.

“We searched that place. Several times, in fact.”

“You must have done a pretty shit job, then,” she answered. “There’s nowhere else I could have been! I’m sure it rained during those nine days; I would have needed shelter.”

“Miss,” he said while scribbling. “There’s no need to be hostile. This is a formality. You’re not in trouble. Are you sure that’s where you were?”

She nodded.

“Very well,” he said as he stabbed his paper to produce an overstated dot.

“Now, Miss, did you meet with anyone else during those nine days?”

“No,” she spat. “I didn’t see a single person while I was out.”

I was relieved once more when she abandoned that stupid notion of another mother.

“May I ask why you decided to leave the house?”

She scoffed. “I wanted answers, but I realized there’s only more questions out there.”

He looked up from his notes and stared at my daughter. I had a similar reaction.

“I know it’s me and everyone else,” she admitted, “but I feel like I don’t belong here. I thought maybe if I spent some time out there, maybe I’d figure out something. It was dumb, OK? I’m dumb,” she said as tears welled up in her eyes. She swept them away with the back of her hand.

“You’re not dumb,” he replied. “Everyone is a bit reckless from time to time. You made the right decision in the end. That’s what really matters.”

My daughter smiled.

“Oh, and one more thing,” he added. “It’s not important, but it might help us in the future. The mansion is quite a walk away from town. Where did you find food and water?”

Her eyes widened.

“What?”

“Food and water. Is there a known foraging spot or something like that? What did you eat during those nine days?”

She slapped herself on the forehead.

“Right. Sorry, I was in my head. I… I… just planned ahead and bought some non-perishables from the grocery store a couple of days before I left.”

“Very well and fair enough,” he agreed, closing his notebook. “As I promised, I won’t bother you any longer. You two have a great rest of the day and if you ever need anything else, just call, OK? We’re here to serve.”

He bowed before my daughter, shook my hand, and he was on his way.

“So that’s it,” my daughter concluded. “It’s all back to normal now? Like nothing happened?”

I shook my head.

“Things can’t go back to normal, sweetie,” I argued. “We both know you’re not doing well. We’ll figure something out. Maybe you could talk to your school counselor if you don’t want to talk to me.”

“Yeah, maybe…” she conceded.

“Tonight,” she added hesitantly, “could we cuddle and watch a movie, just like when I was a kid?”

“Sure! What do you want to watch?”

“Maybe Peter Pan?”

 

My daughter is currently in her room, probably chatting with her friends and catching up with them. We’ll watch a movie or two tonight, and we’ll carry on living. I’m sure there are going to be bumps in the road, but that’s just life. I’m not going to pretend my daughter is fine, but things are close enough to normal that we can work through the rest together.

As I said, this was a quick update for those who were worried about us. As far as I’m concerned, those who claimed my daughter is a Bigfoot or whatever can go eff themselves.
