I've been keeping a journal since I arrived. I'm posting the first entries through the satellite internet here because I need someone else to know what's happening. Maybe someone can tell me what I've inherited.

Here are my first days.

I arrived on the island this morning. The dock is a rickety thing of salt-bleached planks that groans under my weight. The captain, a stout man named Tesfaye, tossed my duffel onto the planks and gunned the engine before I could thank him. I watched the boat shrink on the horizon, and when it was swallowed by the distance, I was left with a silence. One gull cried, though I couldn’t find the bird. No waves crashed. The sea around the island is a deep, flat indigo, almost black, and it doesn't move like water should. It shifts, slowly, like something breathing just beneath the surface.

The lighthouse rises from the center of the island like a child drove a stick into a pile of boulders. The tower is built from dark volcanic stone, and rises in spiraling steps to a lantern room I can see even from the ground—its great glass eye unblinking waiting. Nothing on the island except a small keeper's quarters attached to the base of the tower. That's it. That's my entire world now.

I never wanted this inheritance. Abdi was meant to take it. He was the firstborn, the one our father groomed for this lonely duty. But Abdi died four years ago, only two before dad. I was the spare, the second son who'd left the church for college, and built a secular life in the States.

My daughter Meron is nearly six now, and I only get her for the summers. When the legal summons arrived two weeks ago—a thick envelope covered in official stamps, stating I was to take custody of the family land within thirty days or forfeit the property—I almost laughed. But I'd just lost my job, and the divorce had hollowed me out financially. More than that, maybe I felt a guilt. My father had died alone in this tower, burnt in an accident of flames and oils, so nobody had a body to bury. His funeral procession was my mother and I. I owed him this much, at least. So I gave my notice to the only one in the States who kept track of me—my landlord. I packed a single bag, and I took the cheapest flight to the nearest town, where Tesfaye and his boat were waiting.

The door was unlocked and unbarred—I guess you don’t need security when your home is a prison already. I entered to a circular room, no more the size of a van. The stairway along the wall is narrow. The walls are damp with a condensation that smells like burnt popcorn. The only windows lie in the lantern room. Up the spiral, tightening as you ascend, you’ll reach a heavy iron door at the top.

As I stepped to climb the steps, my foot crunched. Grit crackled beneath my feet, and I stepped back to find a smear of pale grey powder lay on the floorboards. The outline was unmistakable—arms slightly raised, legs together. My father had been standing when it happened. Standing, and burning.

Beside the ash, arranged with an insulting neatness, were a broom and a dustpan. Pinned beneath the dustpan a sheet of thick, yellowed paper lay, folded once.

*Sweep into the reservoir. Do not weep. The light must hold.*

I sobbed. I stared at the ash, the note, the stairs, and at my own hands—hands that suddenly looked too much like his. Then I picked up the broom.

The ash was finer than it looked, almost greasy. It clung to the bristles and smeared more than swept. It took me eight passes to gather all of it into the dustpan, and when I was done, I stood before a brass reservoir beside the entrance. A small hatch was already ajar, waiting. I tipped the dustpan and watched the remains slide into the oil below.

The reservoir gurgled. The oil—if it was that—rippled once, twice, and then a wave of heat rushed down the tower like a giant blowing through a straw. A pulse of pale golden light shot through the crack in the door at the top of the stairs, brilliant and sudden, and then settled into a steady glow. The room filled with a warmth that had no business feeling so familiar.

I backed away and nearly fell up the stairs before tossing the broom aside and leaving the tower.

The keeper's quarters are a single room at the base of the tower: a narrow cot, a wooden desk, a small stove, and a window with a seaside view. On the desk sat stacks of logbooks—decades of them, leather-bound and cracked, every spine labeled with a year. I flipped through the most recent one. My father's handwriting, unbroken, entry after entry:

*Day 214. Weather: clear. No wind. Oil reserves: sufficient. The light holds. I remain.*

*Day 215. Weather: clear. No wind. Oil reserves: sufficient. The light holds. I remain.*

*Day 216. Weather: drizzle, light. Oil reserves: sufficient. The light holds. I remain.*

That was the last entry.

Under the cot, I found a locked sea chest. The key was on a leather cord hanging beside my dad’s desk. When it clicked open, inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were two books.

The first was the Tewahedo Church Bible—bound in something too thick to be leather and too tough to be paper. The pages are brittle, covered in marginalia written in my father's hand. I can't read Ge'ez fluently anymore, but his notes are in English. It’s hard to think it wasn’t for my sake.

The second book was my father's private journal. The pages fell open to his last entry, as though the spine were bent to make it so. I could only read the page before the light outside began to fade and a cold dread settled into my stomach. His lines are burned into my mind:

*Abdi will not understand at first. Show him the book. Show him his family. If the light speaks—let him listen, listen to the star.*

He wrote Abdi’s name, not mine. Even in death, my brother was meant to be called. I closed the journal and shoved both books back into the sea chest. I told myself I would read more tomorrow, when the sun was up and the lamp was quiet.

The lamp above isn't quiet now.

It's humming. It’s a mechanical hum, but it’s almost melodic. I can feel it in my teeth. And sometimes, when the hum drops low enough, I swear I hear a word buried in the sound.

I don't know what I've inherited. But I know my father burned standing up, and when I poured him to the reservoir, the oils bubbled.

I’ll set the lines for the fish traps tomorrow. For now, the light holds. I remain.

For now.

—

It must be near midnight. The moon shines brightly while no sun peeks on the horizon.

Three times I dreamed of climbing the stairs. I’d count 196 steps with every climb, but they were slick with dark and warm fluids. The walls grew closer than they should, breathing against my shoulders like the throat of some enormous beast. Each time I reached the iron door at the top, I found it shut. Each time, I pressed my palm against the metal and felt a quaking, like music through a speaker.

From the other side of the door, my dad called to me. In the first dream, he asked if I'd eaten. Just that.

*“Dawit, have you eaten?”* His question was really an accusation. He always knew when I hadn’t. *“There's canned fish in the storage. The one with the red label. Your mother packed those for your lunches, remember?"* His voice was exactly as I remembered it—the slight rasp from years of incense, the rolling accent that softened his vowels. I told him I wasn't hungry, and he laughed in a scoff. His laugh was so normal, so *him*, that I woke up with my face wet.

The second dream, he talked about the weather. *"The sea is flat today. That's good for fishing, if you set your traps by the eastern rocks. Abdi caught a grouper there once, do you remember it? He wouldn't stop talking about it for weeks."*

I told him I remembered. I didn't tell him that Abdi had been dead for four years now, that his arm was bone now, buried in a cemetery outside Addis under a stone that mom visits every Sunday. I didn't tell him I was the one who identified the body because mom couldn't bear to look, because dad wasn’t home to see it.

The third dream, I tried to open the door. My hand closed around the iron—cold enough to burn—and I pulled. The door didn't budge. I pulled harder. The hum on the other side sharpened into something like singing, and then the cracks around the doorframe blazed white-gold, blinding, and my father's voice came through one last time, gentle and curious, as asking about the news or the price of milk:

*"Tell me about Meron?”*

I startled awake with the light still burning behind my eyes. My hands shook. My teeth chattered. I sat up on the cot and pressed my palms against my face until the glow faded, and when I looked at the window, I could see the lighthouse beam sweeping its slow, steady arc across the black waters. The lamp was still lit.

I couldn't go back to sleep. Not after that. So I pulled the sea chest out from under the cot and opened the Bible.

My father had bookmarked dozens of pages—scraps of faded ribbon, dried palm fronds, even a folded photograph of our family, before I left for the States, before everything. I turned to one of the passages he'd circled in red ink. The Ge'ez was dense, but his translation was written in the margin in his cramped English script:

*And Azazel teached men to make swords, and shields, and chestplate, and made them know the metals of earth and the art of working metals... and there was great evil upon the earth, and the world is corrupt.*

Beneath it, in red ink that had faded to the color of dried blood:

*Azazel is not of the light. Azazel is bound in the dark. The one of the light is older. He was never named in this book. Pray you never hear him speak. If he speaks, he has noticed.*

I closed the Bible. The hum from the tower was still there, threading up through the floorboards, through my spine. I could feel my tongue restless in my dry mouth. And I realized, sitting there in the dark with my father's book in my lap, that I no longer felt alone.

Not in the room. Not in the tower. Not in my head.

I thought about Meron. Her small hands, the way she pronounces "spaghetti" like "p'sketti," the gap between her front teeth that sprays me with spit when she laughs. She's with her mother, safe, far away from this black sea and breathing tower.

I thought about my Sarai. No, not mine anymore. We don't speak much now—just logistics, pick-up times, summer schedules. She told me once, before the divorce, that I had a "talent for disappearing into my head or my ass.” She said she couldn’t tell the difference sometimes. I didn't argue. I spent the first half of my life surrounded by the ethereal and the supernatural. I sang of men who walked on water and women who healed themselves with the hem of a cloak. Then I left that behind, spending the next half searching for my purpose in a newborn country that still longed for meaning. I spent the second half of my life running from the family I came from. Now here I sit in the middle of it, and my family is humming at me from the walls.

I tried to turn to the Lord's Prayer. The words were there in Ge'ez, but they felt hollow. Not because I don't believe—I don't know enough to stop believing—but because the prayer assumes you're speaking to someone who is listening. And I was suddenly very afraid that I had the ear of something I didn’t want to listen.

I wrote this all down instead. I don't know if it helps, but the act of writing, putting words on a page, reminds me that I'm still Dawit. Still a person. Still a father, or a son, not just a keeper.

The hum is still there. It hasn't stopped. And I think, now, that it isn't just a sound. It feels like a voice, learning my name, practicing the shape of it.

Sleep is coming for me again. I can feel it at the edges of my eyes, heavy and warm, and I don't know if it's natural exhaustion or something else—something that wants me back on those stairs, in front of that door. If I dream again tonight, I won’t open the door.

The light holds. I remain. For now.

—

I made it through the night without dreaming. Or if I did, I don't remember it. I’ll call that a victory.

The supply boat won’t return for four weeks, so I must make do. Four weeks of whatever passes for silence here.

The morning stretched long and grey in most directions. The sea was still flat, still dark. I woke early enough to catch the last of the sunrise, ate a heel of bread from the supplies Tesfaye left, and decided very deliberately that I was not going to climb the tower today. I could see the beam through the window, still sweeping its arc across the water. If the light held without me, then there was no reason to go up there. No reason to stand in front of that iron door and wonder if Dad would speak from the other side.

I'm building a routine. That's what you do, isn't it? Ahem you're alone in a strange place and trying not to lose your mind, you build a routine. You give your hands something to do so your head doesn't have the chance to spiral.

I started with fishing. The storage had a rod and a small tackle box, rusted but enough. I walked down to the eastern rocks—I had a good feeling about the side—and I cast a line into the flat black. The sea rippled when the hook broke the surface and swallowed the sinker without a sound, like dropping a stone into oil.

I sat for two hours. The sun moved behind a thin layer of cloud, never quite shining. The gull I'd heard yesterday cried again somewhere behind me, but still there was nothing to see. Just the rocks. Just the tower, rising from the center of the island like a finger pointing at a heaven that wasn’t taking accusations.

I caught two fishes. Each was small, silver, and unremarkable except for their eyes. They were a pale, milky blue like it had been swimming blind in the dark.

After fishing, I read.

I pulled the Bible from the sea chest and opened to another of my father's bookmarks—a dried palm frond pressed between the pages of *Iyasayas*. The passage was circled, but not in red this time. Just a faint pencil mark, almost tentative, as if he wasn't sure he wanted to draw attention to it:

*Ayä, ata beruh kokäb wädi wägahita, kämäy ilka khab sämay wädäqka, niska nehzab zäwdäqkas, kämäy ilka nab medri tädärbeykha.*

My Ge’ez is rusty, but my best translation:

“You fell from the skies, morning star, son of the morning! You have been thrown to the earth, you who once destroyed the nations.”

Beside it, my father had written only two words in the margin, no translation, no explanation:

*Sargon. Helel.*

I traced the letters with my finger. I read the rest of the passage, hoping for some indication to what father meant. Then I didn't want to read any more, but the names stayed in my head, humming in harmony with the hum from the tower. I couldn't make either one stop.

By midafternoon, I grew bored enough to count the stairs.

I don't know why I decided to do it. Maybe I wanted to prove something to myself. Maybe I just needed to move my body, to feel my legs burn, to remind myself that I was still flesh and blood and not ash on the floorboards. I stood at the base of the spiral and I started climbing.

*One. Two. Fourteen. Twenty-eight.*

The walls were dry, but my legs were wet with sweat. My breath shortened. I counted aloud, my voice swallowed immediately by the stone—no echo, no return.

*One hundred. One hundred twenty. One hundred fifty.*

I knew, before I reached the top.

*One hundred eighty. One hundred eighty-seven.*

I stopped. I could see the stairs curling upward into the dim, and the iron door was just as far away in my dream.

*One hundred ninety. One hundred ninety-three.*

*One hundred ninety-six.*

I stood in front of the iron door. I didn't touch it. I just stood there, breathing heavily, counting the number over and over in my head.

Grief, exhaustion, the strangeness of arrival—any of it could have accumulated the numbers. But I knew, even as I thought it, that I hadn't counted them yet. I hadn’t climbed this tower until now. How did I know their count?

Then from behind the door, I heard humming.

Not the mechanical hum of a generator or the warm thrum of a lamp burning oil. This was a melody—slow, lilting, almost familial. It rose and fell in patterns that felt like language, like words I should know but couldn't. It was the most beautiful sound I'd ever heard, and it was coming from the other side of a door I refused to enter, in a tower that should have been Abdi’s, on an island that sat too quietly.

I didn't open the door. I turned around and I walked back down the stairs, counting as I went, and when I reached the bottom I counted one hundred ninety-six steps.

I'm writing this from the keeper's quarters. The sun is going down. The lamp is still burning, steady and gold, and I can hear the melody from here now, threading down and around my skull.

I don't know what's in the lantern room. I don't know if my father's body was the only thing waiting for me on this island.

I'm not going up there tomorrow either. The light can hold without me for one more day.

*The light holds. I remain.*

*—*

I dreamed of a storm. I was climbing the stairs again, slick with moisture that dripped from the stones in long streaks. The walls—I could feel them expanding and contracting against my shoulders. Outside the tower, I could hear wind weeping, rain moaning against the stone, and thunder gnashing across the black sea.

I reached the iron door, whether by compulsion or choice, I cannot say. It was shut, as it always was. I pressed my palm against the metal and felt it quiver at my touch. The shaft of the tower throbbed, holding its breath as my fingers trailed the door latch.

Then lightning fell. Through the crack beneath the door, a searing platinum flash turned the darkness to fire. The thunder came at once, a sound so immense, I thought the tower would split around me. And in the instant of light, through that crack, I saw a silhouette—tall, bold, and radiant, like a man more than a man standing amongst a fire. The light clung to him like a second skin.

I woke gasping, but I didn't wake in my cot. I woke on my knees, kneeling at the top of the stairs. My bare toes curled against cold stone, and iron was inches from my face. The door was ajar.

Just a sliver. Just enough to let a thin blade of golden light slice across my face. I could hear the lamp humming inside, steady and warm, and beneath it, silence. No screaming wind. No thunder. Just the peaceful rhythm of a lighthouse doing what it was built to do.

I should have gone back. I should have turned around and locked myself in the keeper's quarters and waited for morning. But I was tired. I was tired of being afraid of a room I hadn't seen. Tired of dreaming about a door I refused to open. Tired of treating my inheritance like a tomb.

So I pushed the door open.

The lantern room was completely ordinary, and the relief of it almost threw me to my knees. The great brass lamp stood in its housing, burning with a steady golden glow that filled the room with beautiful warmth. The Fresnel lens rotated in its precise arc. The windows were clean, looking out over the flat black sea and the thin grey line of the horizon. I found a chair, a small writing desk, and a shelf of logbooks identical to the ones downstairs. Dust on the floorboards, but no ash. No bones. No silhouette burned into the wood.

I laughed. I stood in the center of the room and laughed until my stomach ached. For two nights—\*two—\*I had been dreaming about this place, fearing for my dead father and iron doors and things that hummed my daughter’s name, and it was just a room. Just a lamp that needed tending and a family duty that had grown monstrous in a grieving and sleep-deprived mind. Maybe Abdi truly did deserve this inheritance.

I sat down at the writing desk and pulled one of the logbooks toward me. My father's handwriting filled the pages and tears began to well in my eyes. I found the same entries as the ones downstairs—*Day 127, Day 128, Day 129*—each ending the same:  
*The light holds. I remain.*  
I flipped to the last filled page, expecting to find the same final entry I'd seen before.

Instead, I found this:

*Day 217. Weather: clear. No wind. Oil reserves: sufficient. The light holds. It remains. It asked about Abdi. I told it he was dead. It wept with me.*

I stared at the entry. Day 217. The logbook downstairs ended at Day 216.

I don’t know how long I stared until I heard the encroaching sound. Until I heard the song.

It was the same melody I'd heard humming through the door the day before, but now that I was inside the room, the song was no longer just a melody. It had texture, shape—language. Words I didn't recognize, strung together in phrases that rose and fell like a voice speaking in rhythm, filling the space with no source and no end. I did my best to write down what I heard here:

*helonoweit … bonjoorpasa … salve?*  
*shalomkhayre … silim?*  
*hwatwesthoohal … no …*  
*olame eskoochas … neehow … namastay …*  
*shlama … paxteebee … izdissyoortung?*  
*hooaaryoo? keeneres … neeshoor? kweeses? meeahah? manshoomka? …*  
*wuhtagewrapsyortthrot? sumerian? latin? ohlfrench? I taist dust on evry sillabul …*  
*bonjoo? salvetay? irryhotep? … do yoo hav wirds*  
*for mee or*  
*sillens?*

I stood up so fast the chair clattered to the floor. The song didn't stop. It grew louder, clearer, the unfamiliar words pressing against the inside of my skull like a hand testing a door. I could almost understand them. They were right there, on the edge of comprehension, yet I ran.

I slammed the iron door behind me and fumbled with the latch. My hands were shaking so badly I could barely grip the metal. I threw the heavy iron slide into place and heard it catch with a dull clang.

Then I ran down the stairs. I didn't stop until I was outside in the dark then inside the keeper's quarters with the door barred and my back pressed against the wood.

The song is still in my head. Even now. Even with the tower door locked and the lamp burning somewhere above. It's quieter than it was in the lantern room, but it hasn't stopped. It's threading through my thoughts like a melody I can't forget, words I can almost understand.

I don't know what my father meant when he wrote that it wept with him. I don't know weeps for a dead man's son. But I know the song is beautiful, and I know I want to hear it again. That wanting terrifies me more than anything that's happened since I arrived.

I locked the door. I locked it from the outside. That should mean something. That should mean I'm still in control.

*The light holds. I locked the door.*
