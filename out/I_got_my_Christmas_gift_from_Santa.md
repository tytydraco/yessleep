I like Jack. I mean, I didn’t love him deeply enough to die for him or spend the rest of my life with him. But I liked him well enough, the way a little girl loves a favorite toy. He made me happy, and I always felt relaxed around him.

Please don’t judge me for this—I was only sixteen at the time. Every Christmas, I would still stuff a note with my wishes into a stocking and eagerly look forward to the gifts I'd find the next morning.

Outside, a heavy snow was falling, and fierce winds rattled the window frames with a low whine. I stood in the living room, staring at the red-and-green Christmas stocking hanging above the fireplace. I had snagged it from the clearance section at Walmart a week earlier for about four dollars and fifty cents. It was printed with crooked snowflakes and a reindeer that looked like it had rabies. It was ridiculously large, almost two feet long.

I pushed a neatly folded slip of paper deep into the stocking. I liked Jack, so I wrote: “Please give him to me.” I was too shy to even dare write his name. Once I finished, I couldn't help laughing out loud at my own childishness.

Of course, I didn't believe in Santa Claus. Even if there really were some white-bearded old man in a red suit somewhere in the world, he would only pass out cheap plastic toys, wrapped candies, or a lump of coal. How could he possibly interfere with human free will? What power could he have to make Jack like me back?

Either way, it was nothing more than a silly psychological comfort. Besides, I didn't actually need magic—because tonight, at midnight, Jack and I had agreed to meet in the backyard.

Jack lived just two houses down from mine. Half an hour ago, he had sent me a text, mysteriously claiming he had a Christmas surprise for me.

And I intended to confess my feelings to him tonight. Whatever happened, I was ready.

I shook my head, clearing away the aimless thoughts wandering through my mind. The fireplace in the living room crackled softly, offering a faint warmth. Following my usual tradition, I turned off the lights early, climbed up to my small bed by the second-floor window, and wrapped myself snugly into the warm blankets.

−

I was awakened by a sharp, strange noise.

Thump, thump, thump.

I squinted at my phone—no new messages. He probably figured the notification chime wouldn't wake me and decided to play Romeo, throwing pebbles at the glass to get my attention. It was actually quite romantic.

I kicked off the covers almost instantly, not even bothering to put on a heavy coat. Wearing only a thin sweater, I scrambled out of bed. I couldn't wait to pull back the blinds. Bitter cold wind surged into the room in an instant, carrying icy snowflakes that whipped against my face.

I looked down. Standing right in the center of the backyard lawn was a tall, blurred shadow. The snow was falling too thickly, and the streetlights in the distance had been knocked out by the blizzard half an hour earlier, leaving the world submerged in a thick, viscous ink-blue darkness.

The silhouette stood there in the snow, raising one hand and slowly waving at me.

“Jack!”

I called out in a hushed voice. He seemed to notice me and kept waving continuously.

I turned and bolted out of the room, leaving one of my slippers behind in my haste. I sprinted down the narrow wooden staircase, urgency burning through my nerves like fire—I wanted to see him so badly, desperate to hear what he had to say to me tonight.

Just as I reached the very last step, my foot slipped. I lost my balance and crashed heavily forward. In the pitch blackness, my forehead slammed hard against a jutting edge.

I sprawled awkwardly on the freezing hardwood floor, my body aching all over. The wound on my forehead didn't hurt much, likely just a scrape. The rush of excitement overpowered the pain, so I mindlessly wiped my cheek with my sleeve, stood up, and limped toward the back door.

It was freezing out. The snow had already drifted past my ankles, crunching softly underfoot with every step I took. Something suddenly dripped down from my forehead, blurring my vision into a smear of crimson.

The figure in the center of the lawn was still standing there.

Wiping my eyes with my sleeve, I hurried toward him. But as the distance closed, an unsettling sense of wrongness suddenly seized my ankles like a lead weight.

He was too quiet.

Normally, the moment Jack saw me come out, he would laugh and run over. In his distinct, slightly nasal voice, he’d call my name, ask why I had come outside wearing so little, and drape his coat over my shoulders.

But right now, he just stood there. He raised his arm, waved three times, lowered it, and then raised it again—repeating the motion at an almost mechanical frequency.

Why wasn't he calling out to me?

And... had Jack always been this tall?

Jack had a habit of slumping his shoulders slightly, so when we walked together, he usually seemed only a head taller than me. But the shadow before me—if that thing could even be called a "person"—was as massive as a small fortress. He was at least seven feet tall, with absurdly broad shoulders and a thick, heavy frame that almost completely blocked out the bushes behind him.

It looked as though he was carrying a massive sack on his back. With every wave of his arm, the bundle twisted slowly, as if holding some dying, struggling creature within.

The biting chill finally swept away the excitement in my brain, and my pace slowed down.

“...Jack?”

I called out tentatively. My voice sounded frail and thin in the desolate, snowy night. I tried to stop, to turn back and run into the house. But by the time I realized it, it was too late—I was already standing right before him.

A sliver of moonlight pierced through the cloud cover, casting a pale, slanted glow across the lawn.  
I finally saw clearly what was standing in front of me. That was not Jack.

I didn't know how to describe it. It was a hulking, blood-red humanoid entity. On its head rested a tattered red pointed hat. Where its face should have been, a jagged fissure split open, revealing two rows of yellow, saw-like bone spurs.

It was smiling at me.

“Merry... Christmas.”

A low purr, like that of a giant feline, reverberated from its chest. It seemed immensely satisfied with my arrival.

Then it turned, placed the heavy sack on the ground, and dragged it right up to my feet.

“Present... for... you...”

I was too terrified to comprehend what might be inside. My only thought was: if I don't do what it wants, I will die.

So I opened the bundle. The fabric was sticky and damp, soaked through as if drenched in some heavy liquid. The cut on my forehead suddenly began to bleed again, streaming directly into my eye, but I didn't even dare reach up to wipe it away.

Through a blurry, crimson haze, I finally recognized what was packed inside—a massive Christmas stocking, printed with crooked snowflakes and a rabies-afflicted reindeer. The stocking was bursting at the seams, stuffed tight with a massive, congealed heap of flesh, bones, and internal organs. A few splintered, pale bones jutted directly through the fabric.

I had never imagined a single stocking could fit an entire human being.

Gurgle. At the top of the stacked flesh, a faintly glossy eyeball suddenly rolled in the pool of blood. A clear blue iris, like a deep pool of water. It was Jack’s eye. It seemed to be looking at me, or perhaps merely twitching in an unconscious reflex.

“Very... lucky...”

The monster stood nearby, its massive frame stomping its feet in excitement, pressing deep, icy craters into the snow. In that strange, grating accent, it began to chatter endlessly. Its speech was slurred and broken, and with every word, a plume of sulfur-scented steam spewed from the fissure on its face.

“He... was... right... next door...”

The second-floor bedroom window of Jack’s house was standing wide open, pitch-black and dead silent.

“Quick... gift... no need... to go... far... to find...”

The slit across its face stretched wider and wider, almost reaching its temples. It was beaming with pride over its own efficiency and good fortune.

“You... wrote... a note...”

“Good child... wants... a gift...”

It extended a thick, gnarled finger, pointing down at the burst-open, steaming stocking of flesh and bone on the ground.

“So... I brought... him to you...”

“Nothing... wasted... all... yours...”

−

I don't know how I survived.

On that cursed Christmas Eve, looking at that twitching blue eye and the burst stocking, I let out the loudest scream of my life.

He seemed somewhat confused by my violent reaction. It tilted its massive head, letting out a disappointed grunt. But soon, dog barks echoed from down the street, and lights in the neighbors' houses began flicking on one by one.

The monster turned. With a lightness that defied its colossal size, it cleared the yard wall in a few strides and vanished deep into the night.

When the police arrived, they quickly sealed off the scene. Jack’s parents fainted on the spot the moment they saw the "gift" on the lawn. Initially suspecting a horrific murder and dismemberment, the police interrogated me for dozens of hours, believing I was an accomplice in Jack's death.

But logic proved that human tools couldn't possibly crush and grind a five-foot-ten adult male into a meat paste small enough to fit inside a stocking within a mere thirty minutes. What’s more, there were no signs of a struggle in Jack's room—he had walked out of his house willingly.

In the end, the case went cold, and having suffered a complete mental breakdown, I was committed to a behavioral health facility.

The doctors said I had severe PTSD and hallucination syndrome. They insisted the towering monster was merely a defense mechanism my brain concocted to cope with trauma, claiming it was just the work of a sick serial killer.

Maybe. Maybe the doctors were right. Maybe there really were no Santa Clauses or monsters in this world, only psychotic killers lurking in the dark. I almost convinced myself to believe them.

After five years of therapy, I have mostly recovered. I am twenty-one this year. I'm no longer afraid of Christmas, nor do I fear the color red. So on Christmas Eve this year, I was finally released from my straightjacket.

I sat by the window of my room, staring at my reflection. The wound on my forehead had left a pale red, centipede-like scar. The doctor said it would be hard to fade, and I might have to live with it for the rest of my life.

It's fine. I am ready.

I let out a long sigh and thrust my hand into my pocket. My fingers suddenly brushed against a piece of paper.

The black watercolor ink had smeared, making it hard to read. I squinted, trying to decipher the handwriting.

It read: Please give him to me.

It was my handwriting. And it was only then that I realized I had spelled 'Santa' as 'Satan'.

Trembling all over, I flipped it over. On the back of the paper, written in a dried, brownish liquid, was a line of crude, jagged, and heavily pressed script that hadn't been there before:

“You’re welcome.”
