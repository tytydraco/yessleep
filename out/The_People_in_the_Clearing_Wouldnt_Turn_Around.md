I’ve gone back and forth on posting this because there are parts of that weekend I’m no longer confident I remember correctly. That’s not me trying to make this sound mysterious. Marcus and I have disagreed about conversations we were both standing there for, and Leah remembers things I have no memory of at all.  
I’m going to stick to what I’m reasonably sure happened.  
This was last September. I was twenty-nine and had been hiking fairly regularly since university. Nothing extreme. Mostly established trails, a few nights at a time, occasionally somewhere remote enough that I could pretend not having phone reception was a lifestyle choice rather than an inconvenience.  
There were four of us: me, my girlfriend Leah, my friend Marcus, and Marcus’s younger brother Ben.  
Leah and I had been together a little over three years. She originally hated hiking. Our first overnight trip together ended with her telling me that voluntarily carrying your bedroom up a hill was proof men had too much free time. Then she bought better boots, discovered she liked birds, and somehow became more obsessive about route planning than I was. By last year, if Marcus and I disagreed about which direction to go, we usually ended up doing whatever Leah said.  
Ben was twenty-four and had been dumped about a month earlier. Marcus decided, without consulting the rest of us, that three days without phone reception would fix him.  
“I could be depressed somewhere with plumbing,” Ben said about twenty minutes into the first day.  
That was pretty much his attitude toward the trip.  
The first day was completely ordinary. It rained for maybe forty minutes, Marcus learned that his supposedly waterproof boots were only waterproof in advertisements, and Leah spent half the afternoon using a bird-identification app that identified almost every sound as either a raven or “uncertain.”  
That night Ben managed to burn one side of our dinner while leaving the other side cold. Marcus accused him of doing it deliberately. Ben said cooking was a colonial construct. We drank some whisky we'd brought in a plastic bottle and went to bed beside a creek.  
I remember all of that because afterward I kept going back through the first day looking for something that should have warned us. There wasn't anything.  
The next morning we reached a washed-out section of trail. A large chunk of hillside had collapsed into the ravine below, and while we probably could have crossed it, none of us particularly wanted to try with full packs.  
Marcus had an old topographic map downloaded onto his phone. According to it, there was another trail about a kilometre behind us that eventually reconnected with our route farther north.  
I asked whether it was maintained.  
“It exists enough for the government to have drawn a line,” Marcus said.  
Leah looked at the map and said, “That is not what maintained means.”  
We went anyway.  
The old trail was difficult to follow. Fallen branches crossed it in several places, ferns covered most of the ground, and the few marks we found cut into trees looked old enough that none of us could agree whether they were actually trail markers.  
At one point Ben found a child's blue mitten in the mud. It was faded almost grey on one side, and he stuck it on the end of a branch and made it wave at Marcus until Marcus told him to grow up.  
I remember the mitten because for months afterward my brain kept trying to turn everything from that day into foreshadowing. Nothing happened with the mitten. It was probably dropped by some kid years ago.  
After another hour or so, we smelled smoke.  
That was reassuring at first. We thought we'd found another campsite.  
Then we heard people singing.  
Ben later got annoyed when one of the police officers called it chanting. He kept insisting it wasn't chanting, and he was right. It sounded more like a hymn, although I couldn't understand the words. There were a lot of voices, perhaps thirty, all singing quietly enough that the forest nearly swallowed the sound.  
We rounded a bend and saw the clearing.  
There were around thirty people there, maybe a few more. The first thing I noticed was how ordinary they looked. Nobody was wearing robes. Nobody had shaved their head or painted symbols on themselves. There were fleece jackets, hiking trousers, expensive trail shoes, two older women in raincoats, and a man wearing a university baseball cap. Camping chairs had been stacked beside a blue tarpaulin. There were coolers, water bottles and a cold firepit.  
Without context, it could have been a large family reunion.  
Except everyone was standing.  
And every person in that clearing had their back turned toward the mountain.  
The clearing ended at a wall of exposed rock, perhaps thirty metres high. A narrow crack ran vertically through the middle of it. It was wide enough near the ground that a person could probably squeeze through sideways.  
Nobody faced it.  
Even while singing, nobody turned around.  
Ben leaned toward me. “This is fucking weird.”  
Leah told him to keep his voice down, and Marcus raised a hand and called, “Hey?”  
The singing stopped.  
There was no fumbling finish or final note. Thirty voices simply went silent at once.  
A few people looked over their shoulders at us, but their feet remained pointed away from the rock.  
An older woman came toward us. She was maybe sixty-five, with short grey hair and glasses. There was dried mud running up one leg of her hiking pants. Someone called her Ruth later, so that's what I'll call her.  
“Where did you come from?” she asked.  
Marcus pointed behind us. “South trail.”  
Her expression changed.  
“There isn't a south trail.”  
“There is on the old map.”  
Marcus showed her his phone. Ruth stared at it, sighed, and rubbed the bridge of her nose. She seemed irritated more than frightened, which made the whole thing feel less sinister.  
Marcus explained that the main track had washed out and asked whether there was another way north.  
“You should go back,” Ruth said.  
“We can't.”  
“You can.”  
“We'd rather not climb across a landslide with full packs.”  
She swore quietly. For a moment she looked toward the other people, although she did it without turning far enough to face the rock.  
Finally she pointed west. “There's another track down to the river. Follow that north and you'll meet your trail again.”  
“Great,” Marcus said. “Thank you.”  
As we started to move, Ruth added, “Don't go near the opening.”  
Marcus looked automatically toward the crack.  
Ruth grabbed his wrist so quickly he laughed from surprise.  
“Sorry,” she said, releasing him. “Just don't look in there.”  
Ben, naturally, asked why.  
Ruth studied him for a moment.  
“I don't know you.”  
That was her entire answer.  
At the time, I assumed we'd stumbled onto some religious group with a sacred site. Strange, obviously, but people believe stranger things than a holy cave.  
Leah wanted to leave immediately. Marcus wanted to refill our water before we did. Ben wanted a photograph.  
We told him not to take one.  
He took one.  
It wasn't even subtle. He pulled his phone out and snapped a picture of the clearing.  
A man in a red jacket saw him and came toward us quickly.  
“Delete that.”  
Ben lowered his phone. “Yeah, sorry.”  
“Delete it.”  
“I said I would.”  
The man stood over him while Ben deleted the photo and then made him empty the recently deleted folder as well.  
I thought that would be the end of it, but the man asked, “Did you zoom in?”  
Ben frowned. “No.”  
“Did you look at the opening through the screen?”  
“I took a photo.”  
“That isn't what I asked.”  
Ruth returned when she heard them arguing.  
“Enough,” she told the man, then looked at Ben. “Did you see inside?”  
Ben shrugged. “I saw a black crack in a mountain.”  
For several seconds she kept looking at him.  
Then she said, “All right. Go.”  
So we did.  
We joked about them for the first half-hour because that's what people do when something unsettles them but doesn't yet qualify as dangerous.  
Ben called them the Mountain Scientologists. Marcus said that if they followed us, we were giving Ben back because he'd pissed them off in the first place.  
Leah didn't join in.  
She kept checking the map, and after a while she said, “Did either of you actually see one of them turn around?”  
Marcus told her to stop trying to make it creepy.  
“I'm serious. They looked sideways at us. None of them turned their backs on the trees.”  
I said maybe facing away from the opening was part of whatever ceremony they were doing.  
Leah looked at me.  
“Then why put the important thing behind them?”  
I didn't have an answer. At that point I didn't think we needed one.  
We reached the river in the late afternoon and made camp.  
Ben seemed normal. He ate two packets of noodles, complained about his shoulders, and spent ten minutes holding his phone in different positions trying to find enough reception to see whether his ex had messaged him. When Leah caught him, he claimed he was checking the weather.  
After sunset he became quieter.  
Eventually he asked, “Did any of you see how far it went?”  
Marcus was cleaning a pot. “How far what went?”  
“The opening.”  
I knew exactly what he meant.  
“No.”  
Ben stared into the fire. “I thought I saw daylight.”  
Leah looked up. “Through the mountain?”  
“I don't know.”  
Marcus threw a little piece of bark at him. “Stop trying to make this into a ghost story.”  
“I'm not.”  
Ben rubbed at his eyes.  
“When I took the photo, I looked at the screen for a second. The inside wasn't black.”  
“What was it?” I asked.  
He took a surprisingly long time to answer.  
“Far away.”  
Marcus laughed, but Ben didn't.  
“I know that sounds stupid. It looked like there was distance in there. More than there should have been.”  
Leah asked whether he still had the picture.  
Ben reminded her that the man had watched him delete it.  
That should have been the end of the conversation. Marcus started talking about work, Leah complained that the root underneath her sleeping mat was going to put her in traction, and eventually we went to bed.  
I woke sometime before dawn because Leah was shaking my shoulder.  
“Ben's gone.”  
His sleeping bag was empty. So were his boots.  
At first Marcus assumed he'd gone to the toilet. After ten minutes of calling his name, he stopped believing that.  
His footprints were easy to find in the damp soil.  
They went back up the trail.  
None of us had to say where he was going.  
We left most of our gear at camp and followed.  
It was just getting light when we returned to the clearing. The people were still there. Maybe some had changed places during the night, but I couldn't tell.  
They were standing with their backs to the mountain.  
Ben was standing among them.  
Marcus yelled his name.  
Ben turned around.  
The reaction from the others was immediate. Two people grabbed his shoulders and tried to turn him back. Marcus ran at them, somebody shouted at us to stop, and within a few seconds everyone was pushing and yelling.  
Someone hit me in the mouth with an elbow. Leah was swearing at a man twice her age while trying to pull Ben free. Marcus shoved someone hard enough that they went down.  
Then Ruth shouted over everyone.  
“Let him face away from it.”  
For some reason, that stopped the fighting.  
Ben stood maybe ten metres from the rock.  
His back was toward us.  
His face was toward the crack.  
Ruth approached him slowly.  
“Ben?”  
He didn't move.  
“Turn around.”  
“There are people in there,” he said.  
Nobody answered him.  
Marcus stepped closer. “Ben. Come on.”  
Ben lifted one hand toward the opening.  
“They're looking this way.”  
I could see the crack from where I stood. It narrowed quickly beyond the entrance. There was darkness inside, the kind of ordinary darkness you'd expect inside solid rock.  
At least that's what I saw.  
Ruth stepped between Ben and the opening and put both hands against his cheeks.  
“Look at me.”  
His eyes didn't move.  
She slapped him.  
Not hard enough to knock him down, but hard enough that the sound carried across the clearing.  
Ben blinked and focused on her.  
“What the fuck?”  
She immediately turned him around.  
“Keep him this way.”  
Marcus pushed through and grabbed his brother by the arm.  
“We're leaving.”  
Ruth shook her head. “You can. He shouldn't.”  
Marcus stared at her. “You're not keeping my brother.”  
“He looked.”  
“So what?”  
Ruth opened her mouth and then closed it again. I remember that because I expected the speech. This was the part where the strange old woman was supposed to explain the curse or tell us what ancient thing lived inside the mountain.  
Instead she said, “I don't know what to tell you that you'll believe.”  
“Try.”  
She glanced toward Ben. “Don't let him turn around.”  
Marcus told her to go fuck herself and started walking.  
Nobody stopped us.  
That reassured me more than it should have. If they were a cult planning to kidnap somebody, I figured, surely they wouldn't simply let us leave.  
Ruth followed us only as far as the edge of the clearing.  
“Marcus.”  
He ignored her.  
“Please.”  
He stopped.  
Ruth looked exhausted.  
“If he starts forgetting people, bring him back.”  
Marcus's expression changed slightly.  
“What?”  
She didn't answer the question.  
“Just bring him back.”  
We left.  
For the first twenty minutes Ben barely spoke. Then he threw up beside the trail.  
Marcus gave him some water and asked what he had been doing at the clearing.  
Ben rinsed his mouth.  
“I don't know.”  
“You walked back there in the middle of the night.”  
“I know.”  
“Why?”  
Ben stared past him.  
“I could hear them.”  
“The people singing?”  
Ben shook his head.  
Before Marcus could ask more, Ben turned his head toward the mountain.  
Marcus grabbed his chin and turned him back.  
“Stop.”  
Ben pushed his hand away.  
“I'm not doing it on purpose.”  
That was the first time I believed something might actually be wrong with him.  
We kept moving. I took over navigation because Marcus was too focused on Ben. According to the map, the trail should have curved northwest toward the river.  
After about forty minutes, Leah stopped me.  
“We're going east.”  
I checked the phone and told her that couldn't be right.  
She took it from me, compared the contours, and looked toward the slope.  
“You've been turning us.”  
“I haven't.”  
“You have.”  
We corrected course. Twenty minutes later she checked again.  
East.  
This time she took the lead herself.  
Leah was better with a map than any of us. I'd watched her navigate us out of fog when I couldn't see fifty metres ahead.  
She walked west for nearly half an hour.  
When she checked our position again, we had drifted east.  
Toward the mountain.  
She didn't say anything for a few seconds.  
Then she handed the phone back to me.  
“I want to go home.”  
Ben was smiling.  
It wasn't a huge grin or anything unnatural. It was almost the expression someone makes when they hear a familiar song playing from another room.  
Leah saw it too.  
“Ben?”  
He looked at her.  
“What?”  
“Why are you smiling?”  
He touched his mouth as if he hadn't known he was doing it.  
“I wasn't.”  
Marcus swore.  
Ben's expression collapsed.  
“I said I'm not doing this.”  
He looked genuinely frightened then, which was worse than the smile.  
Marcus put a hand on his shoulder. “Okay. It's okay.”  
Ben stared at him.  
The silence went on just a little too long.  
Then he asked, “Are your eyes brown?”  
Marcus's hand dropped.  
“What?”  
“Your eyes.”  
“Yeah.”  
“I knew that.”  
Nobody spoke.  
Ben rubbed his forehead.  
“I knew that.”  
Marcus tried to laugh. “Congratulations.”  
Ben didn't laugh back.  
We reached a rocky overlook sometime around midday. The valley spread below us, and through the trees behind us you could still see part of the mountain.  
We stopped there because Leah insisted we needed food.  
Ben sat with his back toward the valley.  
He wasn't looking at the view. He had positioned himself so he could see the mountain.  
Leah walked over and sat directly in front of him.  
“What did you see?”  
Ben looked at her.  
“I told you.”  
“No. You said there were stars.”  
His face changed.  
“I said that?”  
“Yes.”  
He looked embarrassed.  
“I don't remember.”  
Leah glanced at Marcus.  
“What was your mother's name?”  
Marcus immediately said, “Leah.”  
“No. Let him answer.”  
Ben frowned at her.  
“What does that have to do with anything?”  
“What's her name?”  
He laughed once.  
Nobody joined him.  
“Come on.”  
Marcus crouched beside him. “Just answer.”  
Ben's irritation disappeared.  
His mouth opened.  
He looked at Marcus.  
Then at me.  
Then back at Marcus.  
“I know Mum.”  
“I know you do.”  
“I know her.”  
Marcus's voice became softer. “What's her name?”  
Ben started crying.  
That was when Ruth appeared behind us.  
I still don't know how long she had been following. She had a small backpack and was breathing hard from the climb.  
Marcus stood.  
“You.”  
Ruth looked at Ben and seemed to understand immediately.  
“How long?”  
Marcus didn't answer.  
“How long has he been forgetting?”  
Leah said, “He doesn't know his mother's name.”  
Ruth closed her eyes.  
“Okay.”  
Marcus stepped toward her. “Tell me what you did to him.”  
“We didn't do anything.”  
“What is happening?”  
Ruth looked toward the mountain without fully facing it.  
“I don't know.”  
Marcus laughed angrily. “That's convenient.”  
“No. It isn't.”  
She sat on a rock and took out her water bottle. Her hands were shaking slightly.  
“My grandfather brought me to that clearing when I was nine. His father brought him. I've spent part of every year there since I was old enough to take a turn.”  
“A turn doing what?”  
“Standing.”  
“Why?”  
“My grandfather said it was better if all it ever saw was the same thing.”  
Marcus stared at her.  
“What does that mean?”  
“Backs. Trees. Rock. Nothing looking back.”  
I asked whether they worshipped it.  
Ruth actually looked offended.  
“The singing is for us.”  
“What?”  
“Do you know how quiet it gets standing there for six hours?”  
It was such an ordinary answer that for a second none of us knew what to say.  
She drank some water.  
“We don't pray to it. We don't know what it is. I don't know if it knows we're there in any way you'd call knowing.”  
Leah looked at Ben.  
“But it saw him.”  
Ruth nodded.  
“And now he's forgetting people?”  
“Sometimes that happens.”  
“Sometimes?”  
“There aren't enough cases for me to give you statistics.”  
That sentence stayed with me because of how absurdly normal it sounded.  
Marcus crouched in front of his brother.  
“Ben, what's Mum's name?”  
Ben was still crying.  
“I know who she is.”  
“What's her name?”  
“I fucking know who she is.”  
Marcus wiped his face with both hands.  
Ruth said, “Bring him back.”  
“What happens if we don't?”  
She hesitated.  
“He'll keep turning toward it.”  
“And then?”  
“I don't know.”  
“Stop saying that.”  
Ruth's voice hardened.  
“Would you prefer I make something up?”  
Marcus said nothing.  
She pointed at Ben.  
“I've seen two people get like this. One got better. One didn't.”  
“What happened to the other one?”  
“I don't know.”  
Marcus nearly shouted again, but Ruth cut him off.  
“I mean it. He walked out of the clearing one night. Nobody found him.”  
Ben had stopped crying.  
He was looking past us.  
Toward the mountain.  
This time Marcus didn't argue.  
We took him back.  
The route was easy in that direction.  
None of us commented on it.  
When we reached the clearing, nobody celebrated our return or behaved as if some ritual had begun. Someone gave Ben a camping chair and turned it away from the crack. An older man brought him soup. A woman found a blanket because he'd started shivering.  
The normality of it frightened me more than robes would have.  
Marcus sat beside his brother.  
Every few minutes Ben tried to look over his shoulder.  
Nobody yelled at him. Whoever was closest would touch his arm or move the chair slightly until he faced forward again.  
Leah and I sat near the tree line.  
After a while she took my hand.  
“You remember my face?”  
I looked at her.  
“What?”  
“Just answer.”  
“Of course.”  
“What colour are my eyes?”  
“Green.”  
“They're hazel.”  
“They're green enough.”  
She almost smiled.  
“Asshole.”  
I squeezed her hand.  
It was the closest either of us came to joking that day.  
We stayed there until well after dark.  
At some point Ben stopped trying to turn around.  
He slept for about an hour.  
When he woke, Marcus asked him their mother's name.  
Ben answered immediately.  
Marcus asked their father's middle name. Ben told him to fuck off.  
Marcus laughed so hard he started crying.  
That made several people nearby smile.  
Before we left the next morning, I spoke to Ruth again.  
I asked what happened if everyone simply stopped coming to the clearing.  
She looked at me for a while.  
“My grandfather said they tried that once.”  
“When?”  
“Before I was born.”  
“What happened?”  
“I don't know exactly.”  
I waited.  
She eventually added, “People started coming back.”  
“What people?”  
Ruth shook her head.  
“You keep asking questions like somebody wrote all this down somewhere.”  
That embarrassed me enough that I stopped.  
We hiked out that afternoon.  
We went to the police afterward, mostly because Leah insisted. I don't know what we expected them to do. We told them we'd found an unmarked gathering in the woods and that one of our friends had become confused after going there.  
Ben was taken to hospital. Apart from dehydration, exhaustion and a few minor abnormalities that meant nothing to the doctors, he was fine.  
Police went looking for the clearing.  
They found it.  
That surprised me.  
I half expected the cliché where everything had disappeared.  
The people were still there. They weren't armed. Nobody was wanted for anything. The land wasn't private, and standing in the woods facing the wrong direction isn't illegal.  
The police told us to stay away from them.  
That seemed reasonable.  
For a while Ben had trouble sleeping, but otherwise he recovered. He remembers the trip, although there are gaps. He doesn't remember walking back to the clearing. He doesn't remember saying there were people inside the mountain.  
Marcus never went hiking with me again.  
Leah and I lasted another five months.  
For a long time I told people the breakup had nothing to do with what happened. That's not really true.  
After the trip I started asking her stupid questions.  
What colour was my jacket when we met?  
Which tooth had I chipped?  
Which side of my face had the little scar beside the eyebrow?  
At first she played along. Then one night she asked why I kept testing her.  
I told her I wasn't.  
She said I was.  
Eventually she started asking me questions back, and somehow that was worse.  
We both became tired.  
There are ways to destroy a relationship without anybody doing anything unforgivable.  
I hadn't spoken to Ben in a couple of months when he messaged me earlier this week.  
He said he'd been going through old photos.  
Apparently his phone had backed up the picture from the clearing before the man made him delete it. He hadn't noticed it before because he had thousands of photographs in cloud storage.  
He sent it to me.  
I almost deleted the message without opening it.  
I wish I had.  
The picture looks exactly how I remember the clearing. People in hiking jackets and raincoats stand facing away from the mountain. Ruth is near the edge of the group. You can see Marcus's shoulder in the foreground because Ben took the picture from behind us.  
And you can see the crack.  
There is something inside it.  
Not close.  
Very far back.  
It looks like a person standing where there shouldn't be enough space for a person to stand.  
I zoomed in once.  
The photograph doesn't have enough resolution to show a face. I want to be clear about that. The pixels break apart before you can see anything useful.  
I closed it.  
A few hours later I was brushing my teeth when I realised I knew what its face looked like.  
I don't know how else to explain that.  
I hadn't seen it.  
There wasn't enough information in the photograph.  
But I could picture it.  
I could tell you the shape of its forehead. I could tell you how far apart the eyes are. I could tell you that its mouth is smaller than I expected.  
I'm not going to describe it any further.  
The next morning I tried to picture Leah.  
I could remember her hair. I could remember the little line beside her mouth when she was annoyed. I could remember her complaining about the bird app, the way she tied her boots, the exact sound she made whenever I stole the blanket.  
I couldn't put those things together into her face.  
I found a photograph of her on my phone.  
Of course I recognised her.  
That's not what I mean.  
I just couldn't summon her without looking.  
I told myself that happens. People forget details. We'd been apart for months. I was working myself up because of the photograph.  
Then Ben called last night.  
We talked about nothing for a while.  
Eventually he asked, “Do you still remember what I look like?”  
I said yes.  
There was a pause.  
I realised I had answered too quickly.  
Ben asked me what colour his eyes were.  
I couldn't remember.  
I opened his contact photo while we were still on the phone.  
Brown.  
I told him brown.  
He was quiet for so long I thought the call had dropped.  
Then he said, “I've been thinking about what happened up there.”  
I told him he needed to stop looking at the photo.  
“I did.”  
“Delete it.”  
“I did that too.”  
Neither of us spoke for a while.  
Then Ben said, very quietly, “I don't think remembering it is the problem.”  
“What do you mean?”  
He breathed into the phone.  
“I think it remembered me first.”
