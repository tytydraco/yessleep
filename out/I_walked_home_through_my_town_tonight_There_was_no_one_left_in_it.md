The mists swallowed the docks, but even without them I would have known. Sound carries far over water, and tonight there was none.



There was no need for me to go back there. I needed to get home. My hands still stung, and there was a taste in my mouth like ash and struck matches that I could not swallow away.



Cecile was probably home alone, crying for her daddy, wondering where he was. Don't worry, baby, I kept thinking. Daddy would be home soon.



Out of the cave, I climbed toward the road, long since swallowed by weeds, following a light in the distance – a billboard by the old highway. On it, a sun-faded sunrise over water, and far out, a robed, hooded figure standing where no one could stand. *NOTHING IS EVER TRULY LOST. WHAT CROSSES OVER CAN BE CALLED BACK.* I had read it a hundred times. I could have said it with my eyes shut. It should have given me comfort, but I could not rest until my little one was back in my arms.



I came out onto the road, as empty as the docks. A few streetlights threw down funnels of light onto the fog, and between them the dark was total. A man could easily lose themselves out here. I only needed to reach the town.



I breathed easier when the old road fed into the concrete highway and the glow of the neon signs in the distance started bleeding through the fog. I ran straight through the middle. Down the centerline. No headlights, no horns, and it never once crossed my mind to watch for a car, because somewhere underneath I already knew none were coming.



The diner came up out of the fog first. A car idling out front, and through the window I heard the radio murmuring to no one. Steam still curled over the cup of coffee. No people. I knew something was wrong before I was close enough to see, and the thing in my gut told me it was worse than I thought. It shouldn't have reached this far–



I stopped the thought before it finished and kept walking. 



A few steps on, a sound far off stopped me in tracks. Sweat went cold on my back. There was a sound where it shouldn’t be. I stood there for a long moment until I recognized it. The old harbor bell, swinging on the wind. That was all. It had to be. Yes. That was all it was.



I went on through the town, shrouded in smog and silent, empty of every living thing. Not even a dog barked. More cars sat idling, doors ajar on dark houses, TVs still talking to empty rooms. A child's stroller stood alone in the middle of the road. Any other night it would have terrified me. Tonight I was terrified for another reason.



Finally I reached the crossroads, and the road that climbed up the hill. So close now, my love, I kept thinking. We'll get in the car and I'll make you pancakes with the coconut on top and pour the apple juice, just how you like. By now my calm had thinned to nothing, and my jog broke into a run.



"Ceci!" I shouted into the yard, waiting for her to shout back. Her bedroom light was on, but I was the one who left it burning. "Daddy's home!" I called again, already grabbing the door handle.



She was not in the living room. That was all right, I told myself. She likes to hide.



"We'll play hide and seek next time, honey, you win! Just tell Daddy you're okay," I called, going room to room, every one of them empty. "Please. Please tell me you're okay. Please tell me you came back–”



The last room was hers. I knew what I would find before I opened the door.



The doll on the floor. And above her bed, still burned into the wall, the silhouette of a robed, hooded figure.



No sign of her.



It was all in vain. All of them, gone, for nothing.



Do you ever wonder what a parent would do for their child?



Would they give themselves? Would they kill a stranger? Would they trade a whole town for one more morning with her?



Now you don't have to.
