The late afternoon sun cast long shadows across the General Cemetery of Guayaquil, painting the labyrinth of mausoleums and corridors in unsettling hues of gold and deepening gray. My aunt, her two daughters, her son, and her son-in-law stood somberly with me around my grandmother’s grave, heads bowed in prayer. The cemetery, vast and disturbingly quiet, was nearly empty save for our small group. The stillness was broken only by an occasional rustle of leaves, which sounded almost like whispers in the wind.

As we murmured our final prayers, an eerie sound reached my ears: a steady, rhythmic clip-clop. At first, it was so soft I thought I had imagined it, but as the seconds passed, it grew louder, echoing off the concrete walls. The sound was unmistakable. Footsteps, deliberate and slow, approaching from somewhere unseen.

We exchanged uneasy glances, our prayers faltering. My aunt was the first to lift her head, her gaze nervously darting between the shadowed corridors, scanning the rows of graves and niches as if expecting something sinister to emerge.

Then an unnatural chill swept over us, a cold breeze that carried a hollow, whistling sound, echoing from nowhere and everywhere at once. The chill cut deep, enough to make us instinctively huddle closer together.

And then my aunt saw it. A second later, so did I.

From the far end of the corridor, a lone dark figure emerged, moving steadily toward us. At first, it appeared as a blurred shadow, its edges undefined, shifting unnaturally against the dim light. As it drew closer, its form sharpened, revealing a woman. She was impossibly thin, draped in a long black dress that trailed behind her like a shadow come alive. The sleeves hung far beyond her arms, obscuring her hands entirely, and the hem was ragged, shredded into tatters that swayed as if caught in a non-existent breeze.

A long, flowing veil concealed her face, leaving only a hollow void where eyes should have been. Her movements were unnervingly deliberate, slow yet precise, as if time itself twisted and warped around her. The echoing clip-clop of her footsteps grew louder and more resonant with each passing moment, filling the silence and making my breath catch in my throat.

There was something profoundly unnatural about her presence: a weight in the air, a sense of wrongness that made the hairs on my neck stand on end. No one spoke, but our fear was palpable, an unspoken pact of dread binding us together.

One thought sliced through my mind: There’s no way a sane woman would be visiting this cemetery at this time, alone and unaccompanied. My imagination spiraled into dark possibilities. What would she say or do when she caught up to us? Would she greet us with a hollow “hello” or a chilling “good evening”? Or perhaps she would silently acknowledge us in a way that would unsettle our very souls as she glided by.

And then it struck me. Wait… was she even walking? The figure’s approach felt wrong. Her movements didn’t align with the faint clip-clop that had first alerted us.

My thoughts turned darker still. Would she lift the veil and reveal a face too horrifying to comprehend? Or deliver a message from beyond, a revelation no living soul was meant to hear?

We looked at one another, wide-eyed, our fear mirrored in every face. It was an unspoken agreement: we needed to leave. But for a brief, agonizing moment, we stood rooted in place.

“Let’s go,” my aunt whispered, her voice trembling. Without waiting for a reply, she turned and began walking briskly down the corridor, and the rest of us followed. Our footsteps echoed hurriedly, a sharp contrast to the deliberate rhythm of the figure behind us.

We resisted the urge to break into a run, unwilling to cause a scene in a sacred place, but our fear grew with every step. The corridors seemed to stretch endlessly, a maze of identical walls and niches. I silently prayed we would find an exit soon.

Behind us, the clip-clop continued, unrelenting and steady. My cousin dared a glance over her shoulder. “She’s still there,” she breathed. “She’s closer.” Her voice cracked, and she quickened her pace. I didn’t look back. I didn’t want to know how close.

The sky darkened as dusk fell, casting the cemetery in shadow. We turned corner after corner, searching for the nearest exit. The oppressive silence was now broken only by our hurried footsteps and the relentless clip-clop behind us. It was as though she was herding us, driving us deeper into the labyrinth, feeding off our fear.

Finally, we spotted the archway of an exit up ahead. Relief flooded my heart as we quickened our pace, practically breaking into a run. The oppressive feeling began to lift as we crossed the threshold and stepped out into the open street. Breathless and trembling, we turned to look back. My cousin, overwhelmed and pale, clung to my aunt’s arm, burying her face against her. “Mommy!” she cried, her voice quivering with raw fear.

We had all seen it.

She was no longer coming toward us. She was darting from one corridor to the next, back and forth, side to side, flickering in and out of view. She wasn’t walking. She was gliding, hovering just above the ground, her impossibly long arms stretched out in front of her, fingers hidden beneath the tattered sleeves, groping blindly in the dark.

She wasn’t chasing us. She was searching.

Nobody spoke. Somewhere beneath the fear, I felt something far worse: pity. A deep, aching pity for whatever she had lost, and a terrible certainty that she would never find it.

My aunt crossed herself and pulled us away from the gate. We haven’t been back to that cemetery since. Last night, I heard it again, faint, from somewhere outside my window. Clip-clop. And then it stopped, right beneath it.
