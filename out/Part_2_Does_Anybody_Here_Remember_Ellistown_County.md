Right then and there I packed up my things and went back to the campsite, thinking the entire time about the hat in my right hand. Sign from God or not, Milos and Bailey would have thoughts.  

 

 The Lunks were also in the back of my head. Creatures, which I’d known from my time bouncing in other cities, were not normal. I doubted the Lunks were behind Frank’s hat, but I had a suspicion that the same thing that created them had created this.  

 

Milos and Bailey had crossed paths while going back to the site, so they both arrived at the same time, twenty minutes after I did. I showed them the hat and when it set in what it was, they looked like they’d got flashbanged. I told them how I’d found it.  

 

“No fucking chance this isn’t to do with the Lunks,” Bailey said. “I’ve heard stories from kids about strange stuff happening and kids would always say it was the Lunk’s fault. But I’ve never heard anything like this”  

 

“You shouldn’t have picked that up, Will,” said Milos. “It’s not good to mess with stuff you don’t understand. It’s why I don’t go near the fields. Can’t trust ‘em.”   

 

We talked about the hat for the rest of the evening in low voices, but after a while we got over it. All of us were hungry and we decided that now was as good a time as any to eat. Milos and Bailey said they’d gotten some pretty good batches. So we began setting up. Milos had some pots and pans that he set up over a fire they’d started, and he also set up a small wooden board he’d found at the start of the trip. Bailey took the first fish out of his bag, carefully placed it down and began cutting. Bailey was different than Frank in that sense; Frank would just twist off the fish from filet in quick use while Bailey took his time. He penetrated the skin, and we watched as he flinched a little and his face went pale. He held the fish up in our direction. 

   

There were lead and wires in the fish.  

 

That’s the best way I can describe it. It was like someone took the lead pencil-sticks you get in the store for the mechanical models and cut them up into chunks and stuck ‘em in the fish.  There were wires too, that ran around the fish’s body. Its blood was black, like the ink in a pen cartridge.   

 

We cut through all the fish, hoping to find anything edible, to no avail. All fish were affected. Some even had these black slug-like things sticking out of their eyes. When we cut open these fish the slugs retracted and turned white, like a maggot dying, much to our disgust and interest. We ended up throwing all the fish into the ocean. The great pounds of black ink rose to the surface momentarily before going back down. From there we went back to our campsite to try and pack up and go home. It was obvious there’d be no chance of food. That’s when we realised our boat, and the rock we’d tied it to, was gone.   

 

\*\*\*  

 

We checked along the coast for an hour and a half or so, before we realised it wasn’t going anywhere.   

“I reckon we get out the sleeping bags,” said Bailey. “None of us is finding anything now at night and there’s no way a thousand-pound rock could disappear.”    

We listened to Bailey’s advice and settled down.   

  

\*\*\*  

 

The next thing I remember, Bailey was up and looking around our beach spot while I was still laying down in my sleeping bag. He told me that Milos was gone without a word to Bailey. Milos’s bag was gone, and a few minutes later, I realised Frank’s hat was gone too. I waited at the camp and Bailey went to find Milos. He came back after thirty minutes or so, without a sign of Milos on both my end and Bailey's. We shared some peanut brittle, which was the only food we’d thought to bring. I was so hungry I probably would’ve gone through the whole box if Bailey hadn’t reminded me to save some for Milos.   

 

From there, we didn’t do much else. Bailey decided to write an SOS, and I drew stars in the sand.  

 

\*\*\*  

 

Milos turned up a bit after sundown. We were thankful that he was even alive, but when we asked him, he didn’t want to talk about what had happened. That was strange. Milos was always the one reciting his adventures. He did return with the hat, though, which we were again surprised to see. 

 

We offered him some of the brittle, but he said no. We were both surprised by that, and we even mentioned we’d saved it for him and asked if we could eat it. He said sure, and the final bits of brittle were gone in two minutes flat. After that I went to bed, and the others followed suit. 

 

\*\*\*  

 

I remember waking up to the sound of Bailey and Milos arguing, and it was on the verge of getting physical. When I fully stood up, I saw why. Milos’s bag overturned near me, and I could see fruit spilling out. Mangoes, watermelons, and it all looked fresh. I tried to intervene, mainly to give Milos shit too. He did tell us what happened, though he kept on repeating that we wouldn’t believe him. We pressed for answers enough for him to tell us. We interrupted him a few times through it but it became clear quite fast that Milos was serious.  

 

He said he’d had a dream, the night before he went missing. He was in the fields for the first time in ten years. There was corn surrounding him, and all the Lunks were standing dead still in a circle and they were all singing. The sun was in the center of them all, and in turn, Milos. He said he saw a Barnhouse off in the distance that soon obscured by rapidly growing cornstalks and plants. He said from there he dropped next to a river, that a Lunk came out of, like it, rose straight upwards. And the river filled with dead fish, and soon it was more fish than the river. He said the Lunk had no arms and limbs, and it had a triangle on its head that shone bright. When Milos locked eyes with it his vision went dark and, in that darkness, he saw directions to a place on Ellis Island, where he could get all the food he wanted and eat fruit and vegetables, the kind he dreamed about and talked to us about eating. And then his vision went dark, and all he saw was Frank, who was looking at him now as an animated corpse. He had no eyes and thus he had black sockets, and his hair was overflowing and down to the floor.   

 

“It was strange,” said Milos, “You guys know how much he hated long hair on boys. He was wearing a white shirt, too. Short-sleeved. The type he hated. And then I woke up and all of you guys were still asleep. I picked up my bag and walked, even though I didn’t know how and I somehow ended up in a large plains with all the food I could grab. I spent the whole fucking day there, basking in the pleasure. And then I somehow ended up back here, on the island. I forget where I walked to, either.”  

 

“Did Frank say anything?” said Bailey, looking like he was about to cry.  

 

“No. He just stared.”  

 

After this there was silence, and it was then that we truly realised how badly we needed to get off this island. We agreed we’d look into what’d happened on the island when we were away from it.   

We again packed up all our things and walked around the coast. This time, we found our boat, still tied to the rock. And as we got into the rickety thing, it began to rain. We sailed off a few minutes later. It was only then I realised that Frank’s hat was no longer with me. I asked if anyone else had it. Bailey said no, and Milos kept silent.   

I looked back at the island, and I saw Frank, as Milos described him. Beside him was the Lunk from the river, sitting high above the island. Before I could even flinch, I blinked, and my sight returned to seeing nothing but the beach and the sky.  

 

And after that, my memories of Ellistown, fade down to a few snapshots and blurry moments again. And I tell you all this because I know that Ellistown is still out there, and to an extent, Frank and Bailey and the rest of them. And I know someone has the answer I was looking for. So I ask all of you, from the bottom of my heart, does anybody here remember Ellistown County?  

  

 

 

\- Will
