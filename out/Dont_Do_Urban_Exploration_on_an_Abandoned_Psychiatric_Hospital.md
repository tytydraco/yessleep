“Blood, bones and guts are nothing to be afraid of. If you really want to see something terrifying, don't go looking inside the human body… look inside the human mind.”

These words echoed in my head once again as I drove through the empty road. When I asked David—a friend of mine who’s a doctor—what was the worst thing he’d ever seen during his shifts, I certainly didn’t expect an answer like that: “The Human Mind…” A shiver raced down the back of my neck and along my spine, sharp, electric jolts that made me grip the steering wheel even tighter. Was this really a good idea? I suppose, from a purely logical standpoint, there was nothing to be afraid of; ghosts are not real, right?

I shifted in my seat. It was clear that I was letting my imagination run wild... But may be I should tell you a bit more about myself before I go on.

Like any young person who grew up in the 2000s, I was drawn to the various trends that defined that era—parkour more than anything-. Watching those impressive displays of skill and agility left a lasting impression on me—as did the scrapes, sprained joints, and all sorts of bruises-. I tried to replicate many of the things I saw online, a terrible idea, no doubt, but as the saying goes: to become old and wise, you must first be young and stupid. 

Though, in my case, I might not end up being either. 

After all, what kind of person decides to start a YouTube channel featuring urban exploration videos in abandoned places? My experience as a \**traceur*\*—if I can even claim the honor of being called that, after practicing parkour as a hobby for three years in my youth— gave me the confidence and the skills to enter and exit places where I wasn’t supposed to be. Add a portable camera to the mix, and becoming a content creator suddenly seemed like a good idea... or so I thought at the time. 

I had explored the usual spots: buildings on the verge of collapse, abandoned properties tied up in legal limbo where no one was around to report an intruder, and hidden corners of the city so obscure that people had long since forgotten they existed. I’d find exactly what you’d expect in such places: graffiti on the walls, broken glass on the floor, and the occasional unfortunate homeless who had made these sites their “home”. 

But ever since I learned of its existence, I couldn’t stop thinking about visiting the abandoned psychiatric hospital. 

It was David who told me about it; apparently, it’s sort of an urban legend among healthcare workers. No one likes to acknowledge the atrocities committed in the name of science, they only care about the medical advancements we benefit from today. Fortunately, in our modern times, mental health issues are treated for what they are: illnesses. 

But back then… well, you can imagine. 

For starters, the damn place is further than a two-hour drive from the city. Why? Because back then people treated the mentally ill like trash "Out of sight, out of mind". 

They shunned them like the plague, as if their psychiatric conditions were contagious. Then again, perhaps they did it because seeing these individuals reminded them of their own struggles; they were terrified to see their own reality mirrored in someone else. 

Watching a human being crumble under the weight of life inevitably forces you to wonder: what would it take for the same thing to happen to you?

Anyway, the best idea they had was to store them away—starting in the early 1950s—subjecting them to all the atrocities imaginable at the time: ice-cold baths, electroshock therapy, lobotomies, and anything else they thought could drive out the demons from these beings, who were deemed worthless to society. 

David told me that just as these treatments became a source of shame for the medical community, so too did the facility where they were carried out. Consequently, it was abandoned overnight, and the few remaining patients were relocated to nearby towns or to special wards within the city’s “regular” hospitals. But something doesn’t quite add up, and that is the origin of the legends surrounding the site. 

David explained to me that progress in mental healthcare was not swift; rather, it was slow and tortuous. Why would the government close a psychiatric hospital overnight? It’s not as if lobotomies and trepanations were considered state-of-the-age one day and senseless barbarism the next. Furthermore, given the financial investment poured into the facility, one would expect something to have been done with what remained, right? 

Well, it seems it was generally accepted that the location was unsuitable for renovation, and that attempting to bring a hospital—situated two hours away—back into operation would be prohibitively expensive. Besides, in the event of an emergency, many patients would likely die during the long ambulance ride.

It sounded logical to a certain extent; given its remote location and dark history, it was largely unknown to anyone outside the field of health sciences. Therefore, no one gave it much further thought—but you can bet it always comes up whenever the hospital´s staff tells horror stories.

An obscure site with a dark past, tucked away from prying eyes, likely well-preserved, free from the intrusion of vandals, and far enough from the city that the police would rarely, if ever, patrol the area, it felt like I’d hit the jackpot.

Or so I thought, right up until the moment I found myself driving toward it.

I’m not someone superstitious; I don’t really believe in anything paranormal. It wasn’t any sort of significant date—not October 31st, the winter solstice, Día de los Muertos, Krampusnacht, or anything of the sort. It was just a weekend where I happened to have enough time to capture some good footage. As I said, there was no reason to be afraid—the biggest risk was running into a wild animal—yet, despite this, a growing sense of unease began to take hold of me. 

I found myself dwelling on how terrifying the human mind can be, and on the horrific things that had once taken place there. It made me wonder if it was truly worth disturbing the site for the sake of a couple of views.

I steered from the main road onto an asphalt path, cracked and overgrown with grass pushing up through the cracks, it was clear that no one had been here for a long time. 

I arrived at the site at 6:00 p.m. My plan was to scout the location and identify safe entry and exit points while there was still daylight; however, I definitely wanted to go in after dark. It would be much safer in case another curious person spotted me and alerted the police, and it would also make for a much more interesting urban exploration video. 

I parked my car under a tree, with the front facing the road, just in case I needed to make a quick getaway from a security guard or any other unforeseen situation inside. I got out of the car, carrying only my camera, and began to record.

I found the main gate locked with a padlock and rusted chains. The perimeter of the facility was enclosed by faded walls. Honestly, I couldn’t tell if the yellowish, worn-out paint was due to the passage of time or if they had simply chosen the cheapest color they could find. 

Through the gated entrance, I could make out a small courtyard with space for ambulances; the overgrown grass was starting to swallow the facility. There didn’t seem to be an easy way in, yet the walls were surprisingly low—about the height of those found at any middle school—and lacked any additional security measures, like barbed wire or anything of the sort. Weren’t they afraid the patients might escape?

I circled the perimeter, looking for a gap in the wall or an easy way in. The damn place was much larger than I had anticipated, and the dense, wild vegetation further hindered my initial scout. I only managed to spot two guard towers: one at the main entrance and another toward the back, may be near some sort of sports area?

By the time I retraced my steps, it was 7:30 PM, and the sun was beginning to set. I decided I had no choice but to venture straight inside. I walked to the trunk of my car and pulled out a pack of green glow sticks —the kind you snap to produce light for a few hours-. I didn’t want to get lost on my way back, so I planned to use them to leave a trail. 

I snapped the first one and placed it on the driver-side door handle; it glowed faintly, as there was still some daylight left, but I knew this could be the difference between finding my way out that same night or wait until dawn. 

I then walked to the entrance, cracked another glow stick, and made sure it stayed firmly attached to the chain securing the gate. I tucked the rest into my back pocket—the pack had contained ten green fluorescent tubes; now I had eight left-. I jumped over the small wall next to the main gate, started recording again once I was inside, and I began filming the hospital’s exterior. 

It was clearly an old-fashioned structure: sharp, right-angled corners, several stories high, broken glass, and an unsightly architectural style that resembled a lot more a school rather than a hospital. Bathed in the orange light of dusk, it resembled one of those "liminal spaces" straight out of the Backrooms.

As I approached to the main entrance, I took a closer look at the windows; while some had shattered panes, those on the first two floors were all fitted with iron bars. I figured that was why the outer perimeter fences didn't need to be particularly tall, but at the same time, it made getting in and out much more difficult for me.

 Before trying the main entrance, I decided to scout some more around the psychiatric facility. 

I discovered a small shed, detached from the main building, positioned close enough that I could reach the second floor if I climbed onto its roof. Looking at the main building closely, I spotted several windows with missing or broken glass—one, in particular, — had its iron bars bent outward, leaving a gap wide enough for a person to squeeze through. I chuckled to myself, imagining the scene that must have caused such a deformation. I focused my camera on the window and set out to inspect the shed before climbing up.

The dilapidated door could be broken down with little to no resistance, but I was determined to cause as little damage as possible; I may be a lot things, but I still keep a certain amount of respect for the places I explore. 

This outbuilding, however, was different from the rest of the hospital, it lacked bars on the windows and appeared somewhat more modern. I took the opportunity to climb in through a broken window, inside I discovered rows of bunk beds, rusted metal lockers, and crumbling, dust-filled bathrooms that hadn’t seen a drop of water in a very long time. 

The space resembled the substandard break rooms David had once described to me; it is truly shocking how poor can be the living conditions for healthcare personnel. 

I looked through drawers and scraps of old metal, eventually finding what appeared to be a set of keys, so heavily rusted that it was not short of a miracle they hadn’t crumbled into dust. I took them and left the outbuilding, hoping they might open the main entrance. 

I cracked two glow sticks and threw one over the roof of the structure and then another near the window that had its metal bars bended outward; I didn’t want to miss any chance of getting in or out—if the keys didn’t work, I could always try climbing here-.

The rust from the keys stained my hands with orange dust, and none of them would even fit into the lock on the main entrance. I was about to give up when I tried pushing the two metal doors; one of them gave way a few centimeters. I repeated this action over and over until I had opened it wide enough to squeeze through my arm, and then my torso and one leg at the time. Once inside, I turned around and tried to open it again to ensure I could get back out... 

But the dammed, rusted beast wouldn't budge, the hinges had shifted when I entered, causing the doors to jam against each other. I might have been able to pry it open using a piece of metal, but that would have taken too much time and cost me the chance to explore the rest of the building. So, I made my choice: I would head out through the window I spotted earlier -The one with the iron bars bended out- and jump over to the shed next door when I needed to get out.

Before my bread crumbs (glow sticks) died out.

The daylight was gone, and the interior was plunged into darkness, so I switched on the light attached to my camera. What many people forget—myself included—is that a psychiatric hospital is, at its core, still a hospital. It contains everything you’d expect to find: syringes, X-ray machines, laboratories, a kitchen, a laundry room, and, of course, a morgue.

I checked my supply of fluorescent tubes—only six left-, and each one lasts about four hours. I couldn’t stay too long. I wanted to start filming from the main entrance. My heart began to race, partly from the excitement of what I might discover, and partly, from the fear of what I \*don’t\* wanted to find. 

I began filming the metal door, staging it as if I had just walked in. Everything around was pitch black. I turned around, and then…  
  
Suddenly, the lights flickered on.

The harsh, strained crackle of electricity and the low hum of old lamps filled the space. I immediately scanned the area, expecting to see a security guard—ready to call the police—or a motion-activated surveillance camera. Damn it! Why didn’t I wear a face mask or something to cover my mug…?

But there was no one there, no cameras, no staff. It made no sense for there to be electricity here… or did it? 

Perhaps they had finally decided to renovate the place, or maybe they were using that old trick—leaving a light on to make any intruder think someone was keeping watch—. It certainly would have worked on me; had I seen this light on, I wouldn’t have dared to enter without permission. 

I took a moment to calm my racing heart. It was likely connected to the public street lighting in some way, turning on only at night to scare people off. 

Nevertheless, it was far more convenient for me to be able to see where the hell I was stepping on, even if it would surely make the footage much less terrifying. 

I kept the camera light on and also popped a new glow stick, just in case the power failed, and I began to explore…

The reception area featured a large, semicircular desk and scraps of yellowed paper covered in the rigid font of an old-fashioned typewriter. In some places, the walls appeared intact, while in others, it seemed as though the slightest gust of wind might bring them down. 

Several corridors stretched out before me, alongside a set of stairs. As I passed them, I decided to head to the lower level first—naively assuming it would be the laundry room, a boiler room, or something along those lines—. I had no idea what was in store for me. 

Before heading down, I left the glow stick on the first step, then I walked down the stairs.

I came across several old, dilapidated rooms, their doors resembling those of solitary confinement cells. In some of them, the lights were off; in others, they flickered, emitting an electrical hum that made me feel as though I were being haunted by swarms of invisible insects. I stepped into one of the rooms, which contained a lone chair, a table, and yellowed papers—much like those I had seen at the reception desk— But unlike the typed medical records, these were handwritten, and for the looks of it, seemed as if they were inscribed with fury. 

The pen had been pressed strongly, damaging the paper, passed repeatedly, tracing over each letter perhaps a dozen times. Despite the poor condition of the pages, I could still make out a few phrases:

ADELA, WHY DID YOU FORSAKEN ME?

GOD IS NOT HERE, NEVER WAS, NEVER WILL BE.

SHUT UP, YOU DAMNED FOOLS!

YESTERDAY, BEFORE—BUT NEVER LIKE WHEN I WAS WITH YOU.

DOGS DOGS HOWLING YOUR FEARS ARE CALLING ME.

Some of the phrases made sense, while others did not; some were terrifying, others just sad. I could only imagine what kind of thoughts had been racing through the mind of their author.

After leaving the cell, I noticed a flickering light to my left. At the end of the corridor, past all the other rooms, stood a door left slightly ajar, from which blue flashes emanated. Intrigued, I walked toward it; as I drew closer, a sound inside my head grew louder—a distant, indistinct noise I couldn't quite identify. I opened the door, and my blood ran cold…

In the center of the room sat a gurney with worn leather straps, and on the wall, a machine with menacing cables snaking out, it was a goddamn electroshock machine... 

Then, the room’s lights began to flicker, blue flashes and black shadows, over and over, like lightning in a storm, freezing me in place.

One of the shadows casted upon the gurney took on a human shape and began to thrash around in fury. 

The cursed sound inside my head intensified; now I could make it out... Screams. 

Human screams, coming from everywhere. My legs went numb, and I could only turn my head. Amidst the lightning-like flashes, I saw the bricks of the room splitting open in half, to reveal mouths filled with broken, yellowed teeth. Each one screamed in a different voice—men, women, and children—all of them sounding as if they were being tortured in the deepest pits of hell. The echoes drilled into my skull, and fear washed over me like never before.

Not knowing what else to do, I covered my ears, squeezed my eyes shut, and screamed with all my might. I fell to my knees, trembling. 

When I finally stopped screaming, all the other screams ceased as well. I looked up—I was drenched in sweat, tears in my eyes—but the whole damn place was perfectly intact. Had I hallucinated it, or was that shit actually real? I got up difficulty, and retraced my steps; everything looked normal.

I began to doubt my own sanity more and more, so I decided to try and get out. I headed up the stairs where I had left my glow stick on the floor—but it was gone.

My heart skipped a beat. I counted the glow sticks I had left: Five. 

My hands were still shaking. I looked down, thinking the tube might have fallen down the stairs... 

But just then, the basement lights went out again, plunging the lower part of the staircase into total darkness. I shone my camera light toward the steps, but it was as if the darkness swallowed the beam, preventing it from reaching more than a meter in front of me. 

From the back of my mind, I could hear the voices returning. Like a distant scream that grew louder as the darkness surged toward me. The damn walls were screaming once again. 

Breathing heavily, I said "to hell with it all" and scrambled up the stairs, searching for the window I had planned to use as an exit.

But I was running so fast, and was so terrified, that I climbed one floor too high without even realizing it. The moment I reached the top step, the remaining lights failed.

"Crap!"

I yelled, nearly having a heart attack. I turned carefully to head back down to the floor below and find the only window through which I was certain I could escape…

But the sound of painful moans and ragged, gasping breaths froze me in place. 

I was paralyzed from the knees down, my heart pounding as if it were about to burst from my chest.

I turned around. The faint moonlight filtered through the window bars, falling upon the silhouette of a man, bare-chested and facing away from me, and he lowered a rope out the window. He was barefoot, his body entirely hairless, his skin a deathly, pallid white, whether from the moonlight or a lack of blood, I couldn’t tell.

He stopped and turned toward me.

A wave of nausea washed over me; the wretch stared fixedly, never blinking. His abdomen had been split open, his pants were torn, and his hands were soaked with dark, purple blood. The "rope" was, in fact, his own intestines, which he was feeding out through the bars.

He continued to move his hands, one after the other, lowering his viscera as if letting a bucket down into a well. He brought his blood-stained fingers to his lips and hissed: "Shhh."

His grin revealed broken, yellowed teeth, they were identical to the ones I’d seen in the bricks that screamed on the basement. 

He placed his head between the window bars and shoved himself forward with all his might; the bars pressed against his chest, and I could hear his ribs snapping and shifting. With his intestines removed, he was able to squeeze through the bars. He collapsed toward the ground, leaving a pool of blood where he had stood.

I couldn’t take it anymore, my mind shattered in that instant. My legs began to move on their own; instinct drove me toward the exit. In that moment, I had forgotten that the damned door was jammed, fear was the only thing that occupied my mind…

I don’t know what would have become of me had I not caught sight of that saving, fluorescent green light to my right, there it as was, the window with the bars bended outward, and below it, a shed.

I jumped without a second thought, landing with a loud crash. I rolled and then I was back on my feet in an instant, grateful that my weight didn’t collapse the roof. 

I felt as though the shadows of the trees were turning into hands, reaching out to snatch me.

I looked around desperately until I spotted the main gate —guided by that green light—and, further back, the light on my car door handle. Hope flared in my soul, as bright as that incandescent, fluorescent glow in the night. I jumped down once more, rolling, and running breathlessly through the tall grass. 

I dropped my camera before grabbing the bars of the gate and climbing over. My adrenaline was so high that I felt weightless.

I dropped down on the other side and hurried into my vehicle, grateful that I had left the keys in the ignition.

I ran away, tires drifting over the gavel, and got back onto the road. After fifteen minutes of driving like a madman, I forced myself to slow down; I hadn’t made it out of that place just to die in a traffic accident.

Those two damned hours—driving with my heart pounding in my throat—were the longest of my life.

\*\*\*\*\*

It took me more than two weeks to gather the courage to go back for my camera, with lots of daylight to spare, of course, and with company.

David and his brother offered to help; I had told no one but David what I’d seen. He suggested that perhaps my mind had played tricks on me, or that I’d inhaled some kind of hallucinogenic mold, something along those lines.

I... wanted to believe him, but the only way to be sure was to check the footage. 

We found the camera, lenses broken and out of battery, but the memory card was intact.

We took it back to my place and watched the video on my computer. What we saw, however, bore no resemblance to my memories. 

According to the recording, the lights never came on, I had gone down into the basement with nothing but the light from the camera, and there were no screaming walls or flashes of lightning. I did scream and flail about, just as I remembered, but…. Everything else seemed perfectly normal; there was nothing, and no one else in the building.

I felt a strange mix of emotions: relief, an unpleasant worry that I might be losing my mind, happiness that none of it had been real, and anger at all the suffering I’d endured because of it...

So... why am I telling you all this if it was just a product of my “imagination”?

Well, after I climbed out the window and dropped the camera, it kept recording, pointed toward the hospital entrance. 

You could hear my footsteps moving away, the sound of me climbing the gate, and the car starting up and skidding as I drove off… and then, in the distance, near the main entrance of the psychiatric hospital, a shadow could be seen rising from the grass, and patiently picking up a rope from the ground.
