The [last time](https://www.reddit.com/r/nosleep/comments/1wke7it/i_exterminate_sixinch_people_for_a_living/) I made one of these... there were nineteen Denen.

I made Lilith check after me. She thought I was worried someone might have fallen out of the collection tote on the drive north. I was.

Four days after we crossed the river, I counted eighteen.

The church kept its basement doors locked at night. The pastor had given us a key, but other families were sheltering upstairs, and people came down periodically during the day to use the laundry room. I'd gotten into a habit of checking on the Denens before bed. Esther didn't like me doing so. She was afraid I'd smother one of them in their sleep. I wouldn't have, but I can understand her distrust.

But I always checked, regardless of her insistence that they could count themselves just fine.

That night, one of the little homes against the wall stood empty. Its front door was wide open, and a cup no bigger than a button had been knocked off the table inside.

"Where's Reece?" I asked.

No one had an answer.

Reece was the old man who'd lost his right arm. He'd been at Hartwell. I learned his name the morning after we reached the church, when he asked me to move his house closer to the heating pipes. Since those houses weren't bolted into the wall, any normal-sized folk could pick them up no problem.

I'd picked up his house carefully, both hands underneath, while he stood beside it telling me which way to turn and exactly where to place it.

Esther came out of her house carrying her sewing-needle staff. She saw Reece's open door and stopped.

“Has he gone upstairs?” I asked.

“I wouldn’t think so."

Other Denens began coming out. Someone called his name into the space behind the wall. Lilith woke on her mattress and joined us.

I checked under the workbench first, then behind the boiler. The church had an old utility cabinet beside the laundry sink, and I opened it even though Reece couldn’t have reached the handle on his own.

The black collection tote was on the bottom shelf.

I’d kept it because the sleeves inside still had Town’s barcodes. I thought they might help prove what happened at my house. Esther had told me to throw them away.

The tote was open.

I took out one sleeve, then another. They were folded the way I’d left them. But the third wasn’t.

Reece lay inside it with the plastic pulled over him. His remaining hand was raised against the clear material. For a moment, I thought he was pressing it there and waiting for me to let him out.

Then I saw that the hand wasn’t moving.

Lilith was beside me before I could close the tote.

“Is-is he asleep?”

I didn’t answer quickly enough. She turned and ran back to her mattress. I heard her wake my wife with a shaky voice. She was crying. She loved these people like they were family.

Esther climbed onto the bottom shelf. She put her hand against the plastic near Reece's face, then looked at me. There were breathing holes near the seal. I had made them myself, back in Lilith’s bedroom. They were still there.

“He couldn’t have suffocated,” I said.

“I didn’t ask you how.”

I took the sleeve out. A faint sweet smell came off it as the plastic unfolded. Honey.

I looked toward the basement door. The lock was still turned. The windows were too high to reach from outside without a ladder, and neither of the small panes above the sink had been opened.

“Someone got in,” I said.

Esther looked at Reece. Then at the tote I’d kept.

“Someone did,” she said.

Esther reached for the sleeve. I moved it away before she could touch it.

"Damn it, let me see him," she said.

"I will; just give me a minute."

"No, you've had nine years staring at dead bodies, Freddy. I know Reece."

I set the sleeve on the workbench and pulled the plastic back from Reece’s face. His eyes were closed, and I couldn’t see an injury.

Esther climbed onto the bench by way of a length of cord Lilith had tied to its leg. I stepped aside. She knelt beside Reece and placed two fingers against his neck.

Behind us, the other Denens were gathering at the edge of their houses. Nobody spoke. I could hear the boiler running and my wife talking quietly to Lilith across the room.

“Was he sick?” I asked.

“No.”

"When was the last time you saw him?"

"Before supper. He brought Johnny some bread."

Esther lowered her hand. She looked at the inside of the sleeve, at the holes I’d punched near its seal.

“You said he couldn’t have suffocated.”

“He couldn’t.”

“Then tell me what the fuck happened.”

I lifted one of the folded sleeves from the tote and opened it. It smelled of plastic and the basement shelf. I checked another.

Nothing.

Reece’s sleeve still smelled sweet.

“Town Blue,” I said.

Esther pulled the plastic farther away from him.

“Are you certain?”

“Has to be; can't be any of the other Town Rainbows. They all got distinct smells. Town Pink is flowers, like clover. Town White is like rotten eggs. Town Blue is honey.”

I wished I hadn’t said that. She looked up at me, but she didn’t respond.

My wife came over while Lilith stayed on her mattress. I told her what I’d found. She looked from Reece to the basement door.

“It was locked when I came down,” she said. “I checked it.”

“Then whoever did this could still be here.”

At that, several Denens hurried back into their houses.

“No one goes into these walls,” Esther called to them. “Stay where I can see you.”

I went to the door and checked the lock again. The pastor had given us one key. His was upstairs. I hadn’t heard anyone come down, but I’d slept through Lilith getting out of bed plenty of times when we lived at home. The stairs were only a few feet from our mattresses.

I walked the basement once more. There was a drain in the laundry floor, a boarded opening behind the boiler, and a service door near the far end that led to the yard. All three had been there when we arrived. I checked them because they were the places I would have checked on a job.

The service door was bolted. The boards behind the boiler hadn’t moved. The drain cover was fixed in place.

When I came back, Esther was sitting beside Reece.

“Call the pastor,” she said.

I looked toward my wife.

“She’s right,” my wife told me.

I went upstairs and woke him. He came down in his slippers with a sweater pulled over his nightshirt. I told him someone had died. He didn’t understand until he saw who.

He knew Reece. He’d spent the previous afternoon finding a piece of thin rubber tubing so Reece could run heat into his house without having to ask one of us to move it for the third time.

The pastor stood at the workbench for a moment, looking at the tubing Reece would never get to use.

“I’m calling the police,” he said.

Esther nodded. I did not.

Town’s report listed Reece among nineteen Denens killed at my house. His name wasn’t in it, because I hadn’t known it then. All I had given Town was a number and a photograph. I had no idea what would happen when an officer tried to report the death of someone who was already recorded as dead.

The pastor noticed me looking at the sleeve.

“Freddy?”

“Call them,” I said.

He went upstairs for the phone.

I counted the Denens again while we waited. Esther watched me do it. This time, she didn’t object. Seventeen stood outside their houses. One was on the workbench beneath a sheet of plastic.

Eighteen.

I counted once more because I had to have missed someone.

Then Johnny’s little voice came from behind Lilith’s mattress.

“I’m here,” he said.

I hadn’t seen him. He’d been sitting on the floor beside her, holding the corner of the blanket she used to sleep. He came out when she asked him to, and I counted him with the others.

Eighteen alive and well Denens. One dead.

I looked at the basement door. It was still locked.

Then I looked at the small houses along the wall. Their doors were open now, with people standing in nearly every one.

The officer arrived twenty minutes later.

Her name was Bell. The pastor brought her downstairs, and she stopped on the last step when she saw the houses along the wall.

I showed her Reece. She asked who had found him and whether anyone had touched the sleeve. I told her I had.

She looked at the breathing holes, then at the cabinet where I’d found him.

“Who else has a key to this basement?”

“The pastor and me.”

“My wife knows where mine is,” I added.

Bell wrote it down.

Esther was beside Reece on the workbench. Bell asked her when she’d last seen him alive, and Esther told her about supper and the bread he’d brought Johnny. She’d just started giving Bell Reece’s full name when a cup struck the floor behind us.

I turned.

A Denen woman named Ruth was standing outside her house with one hand against the doorframe. Her other hand had opened, and the cup lay at her feet.

“Ruth?” Esther called.

Ruth tried to step forward.

Her leg folded underneath her.

Bell crossed the room before I did. By the time she reached the houses, Ruth had fallen onto her side. Her arms were still moving. She was trying to pull herself toward the edge of the shelf.

“Get her down,” Bell said.

I didn’t know how to do it without hurting her. Esther and two others climbed onto the shelf and carried Ruth to my hands. I laid her on a clean dish towel on the workbench, away from Reece’s sleeve.

She was breathing. I could see her chest moving beneath her shirt.

Then I couldn’t.

Bell asked what she needed. I told her I didn’t know. There was no Town equipment in the basement except the empty tote and the bags.

Ruth took one shallow breath. Esther called her name. She didn’t take another.

Nobody had come through the basement door. Bell had been looking at it when Ruth fell. I had been standing between the door and the houses the whole time.

I looked over at Ruth’s home. Her cup was still on the floor where she’d dropped it. All the other Denens were outside their houses now, staring at her.

Two dead.

Bell took out her radio and called for help. Her voice stayed steady until the dispatcher asked how many people were in danger.

I answered before she could.

“Seventeen.”

Bell made us leave Ruth where she was.

The other Denens stayed together at the far end of the workbench. Esther stood in front of them. Johnny was with Lilith on her mattress, his blanket pulled around his shoulders.

Two more officers came down. One checked the yard while the other went through the basement with Bell. They opened the service door, pulled the boards away from behind the boiler, and shone their lights into every space large enough for a human hand.

They found nothing.

Bell asked whether I could tell her how Ruth had been exposed. I couldn’t. I’d smelled Town Blue on Reece’s sleeve, but I hadn’t smelled it on Ruth. There was no canister, no tubing, nothing I could point to and say, *there*, *that's the murder weapon.*

Bell wanted everyone out of the church basement by morning.

“There’s a shelter in the next county with rooms built for Denens,” she told Esther. “They can take you today.”

Esther asked whether they could take all seventeen together.

Bell said she’d have to check.

That was the first time I saw Esther hesitate. She looked back at the houses along the wall, at the people waiting on the workbench, then at Lilith and Johnny. She’d spent months getting them to my house and another four days getting used to this one. Now she was being asked to move again.

“We’ll go,” she said. “But nobody gets separated.”

Bell promised she would ask. She took Reece and Ruth upstairs in two small boxes the pastor found in the church office. Before she left, she told us another officer would stay outside until she came back with an answer.

I watched him park across the front doors. He could see anyone who drove up. He couldn’t see the houses in the basement.

The pastor brought us breakfast. My wife sat with Lilith while Esther told the others to pack only what they could carry. Most of them had arrived at the church with little more than that.

Reece’s house was still beside the pipe. The tubing the pastor had found for him lay on the shelf outside his door.

I offered to carry it upstairs with the other homes.

But Esther said to leave it for now. So I did.

Around noon, I heard someone arguing on the upper shelf. The Denens had built two additional rows of houses into the space along the wall. The upper row was near a warm pipe that crossed behind the laundry room.

I started toward them. Esther was already going up the cord ladder.

“You don’t have to watch us pack,” she called down.

“I heard shouting.”

“We’re frightened. Shouting and cursing are probably all you'll hear for a bit.”

I stayed by the tables.

A few minutes later, the pastor came downstairs to tell us Bell had called. The shelter could take everyone. He was still giving Esther the address when someone screamed.

I looked up.

A woman named Mara stood at the edge of the upper shelf, clutching at the air. Someone had both hands against her back.

It felt like time stood still. Mara was high enough that a human falling would break their leg, a Denen from that high was equivalent to you falling off a damn three-story building.

A few Denen ran over to where Mara was standing, but by then it was too late. The man pushed.

Then she fell.

I reached for her, but I was too far away.

Mara struck the corner of a folding table on the way down and landed on the concrete beneath it. The sound was a small crack. And I could see Lilith tuck her face into my wife’s side.

The Denen who had pushed her ran along the shelf toward a gap beside the pipe.

For a second, I just stared at him. I’d spent the whole night watching doors a man could fit through. He was six inches tall, and he was already nearly out of reach.

I dragged the table away from the wall. Everything on it fell to the floor. I got my hand against the gap before he could enter it.

He turned and ran the other way.

Esther was coming up the ladder. He jumped over her head, landed on the shelf below, and kept going. I caught him against the wall with my other hand.

I didn’t close my fingers. I’d seen what a careless hand could do. How easily it can end a Denen life.

He stood inside the space between my palm and the wall, breathing hard. There was nowhere for him to go.

Below us, Lilith called Mara’s name.

The Denen looked down at the floor. Then he looked at me.

I knew his face. I’d counted him in Lilith’s bedroom, put him in one of the sleeves, and carried him across the bridge with the others.

I hadn’t known his name then either.

"Brennan," Esther said.

She had reached the shelf below him. Her staff was in her hand, but she made no move toward him.

"Is Mara alive?" she asked.

Brennan looked past me. I kept my hand against the wall, thinking he wouldn't run anymore. The pastor was kneeling beside Mara. Lilith stood a few feet away, now holding Johnny against her chest.

"No," he said.

Esther shut her eye. For a moment, she held on to the edge of the shelf with her free hand. Then she looked at Brennan again.

"Reece and Ruth?"

He said nothing. Then he nodded.

"You put Reece in that bag," she said.

"Yes."

I expected him to deny it. I think Esther had, too.

"How?" Esther asked, her voice dropping to a harsh whisper. "You’re six goddamn inches tall, Brennan. That utility cabinet was latched shut. That tote sits waist-high to a full-grown man."

Brennan wouldn’t look at her. He stared down at his boots, his jaw twitching.

"The kickboard," he muttered. "Behind the laundry drainpipe. Looks like mice chewed out the bottom corner decades ago. Reece... Reece found the run three days ago. He told me he went into that cabinet whenever the choir folks upstairs got too loud."

"Did you drag him up there?"

"I didn't have to drag him," Brennan whispered. A wet shudder escaped his lips. "I couldn't have lifted an old man if I tried. I... I laced the crust of bread he ate for supper. Then I told him Freddy left a spare heating element inside the tote. For his house."

Esther’s knuckles turned white around her needle staff.

"He walked in there on his own two feet," Brennan continued, his voice finally breaking. "He was digging through the plastic looking for that wire when his lungs seized up. I just pulled the sleeve over him so Lilith wouldn't hear him thrashing against the shelf."

Below us, the pastor called to the other officers outside. I heard the basement door open and heavy steps on the stairs.

"Get them away from him," I said.

Esther turned.

"Everybody off the shelves. Go to Lilith."

The Denens began climbing down. I watched them pass below Brennan, one by one. He watched them too. He didn't try to touch any of them.

When they were clear, I brought my other hand up behind him.

“I’m going to pick you up,” I said. “Keep your hands where I can see them.”

“No,” Brennan said.

“I’m not leaving you up there.”

“Then get something to carry me in.”

I almost laughed. After three people, he was worried about what *my* hands might do to him.

Then I saw what he was looking at.

A dark stain marred the front of his coat. It had spread down one sleeve while we were talking. He held that arm away from his body.

“Don’t touch it,” he muttered. “The vial in the lining... it cracked when I jumped.”

I backed my hand off the shelf.

That's when I heard Lilith scream.

I turned. She was still holding Johnny against her chest, except now his small body had gone rigid against her, his arms drawn up tight the way I'd seen a hundred times before.

"I saw him eating the bread," Brennan said quietly from behind me. "While they were packing."

"What bread?"

“The piece Reece gave him. I laced Reece’s slice before supper. Johnny broke some off and kept it.” His breathing hitched. “I didn’t think a piece that small would be enough.”

Lilith had already laid him on her mattress, her hands shaking so badly she couldn’t get the blanket untangled from his legs. His lips had gone blue. His chest was working too hard and fast, the way Ruth’s had right before it stopped working at all. Brennan had admitted killing her, but he still hadn’t told us how.

"How long?" I said. "How long ago did he eat it?"

"I don't know, twenty minutes, maybe more—"

I had nothing. No canister, no antidote, nothing built for something his size.

"The shelter kit," the pastor said, already moving toward the office. "We keep one. For accidental exposure, in case anybody upstairs ever—" He didn't finish it. Every church on this side of the river had learned to keep one. Town and their “accidental” environmental contaminants.

He came back with a case I recognized before he'd even gotten it open. It was the same auto-injector I'd carried in my own glove compartment, built to put a measured dose of counter-agent into a grown man's thigh in under three seconds.

Johnny weighed less than the needle.

"That'll kill him," I said. "That whole dose would stop his heart before it touched whatever's already stopping his lungs."

"Then what?" Lilith said. Her voice had started cracking. "What do we do, Daddy?"

I looked at the injector. Then at Esther, climbing down off the shelf as fast as her leg would let her. Then at Johnny, whose eyes were still open, still tracking Lilith's face for now.

"Bring me the smallest dropper you've got," I told the pastor. "And a clean dish."

He turned and left the room, coming back a few moments later.

I depressed the injector into the dish myself, watching the fluid pool there, trying to remember every fraction Town had ever trained into me; dosage per estimated body mass, milligrams per kilogram, numbers I'd used a hundred times to calculate how much poison it took to kill something Johnny's size.

"Lilith. Hold his head still."

"Freddy—" Esther muttered.

"I need you to trust me for about four seconds," I said. "I know that's asking a lot. But… please.”

She didn't answer, but she didn't stop me either.

I drew a single drop into the dropper. It was all I dared give him.

"Open," Lilith whispered to him. "Please, Johnny, please."

His mouth opened, just barely. I gave him what I had.

His chest rose once, then fell. Then it didn't rise again.

Lilith said his name. I kept watching him, afraid to touch him and more afraid that I’d waited too long. Esther leaned close enough that her hair brushed my hand.

Johnny drew in a breath so thin I almost missed it. We waited. He took another, rougher than the first. Lilith covered her mouth. Esther stayed beside him, watching his chest with me.

I couldn’t tell them he was going to live. His lips were still blue when I turned back toward Brennan.

“Tell her we have the person who did it. Tell her he’s been exposed to Town Blue.”

The officer looked at the Denen standing against the wall.

“Is that him?”

“Yes.”

He took out his radio.

Brennan looked at me.

“I didn’t mean to spill it.”

I remembered how he’d landed on the lower shelf after jumping over Esther. He had struck it hard, but he’d kept running. The coat must have torn then.

His arm began to shake. I pulled the folding table back against the wall and spread a dish towel across it. Brennan looked at it, then at Esther.

“Where did you get it?” she asked.

Brennan kept his eyes on his sleeve.

“A man brought it to the laundry room yesterday.”

“You met someone in this church?”

“He came in with the other people doing wash. He didn’t come downstairs.” Brennan swallowed. “I went up.”

The basement had been locked all night. I had checked every door, every window, every opening a human being could use. I have no idea how he slipped upstairs, even now.

“What did he want?” I asked.

Brennan looked up at me, and I saw that he was frightened.

“The same thing they want from you.”

“Names?”

He shook his head. “Bodies.”

His knees folded beneath him. Esther caught his shoulder, then pulled her hand back when she remembered the stain.

I brought the antidote over from Johnny’s mattress. Brennan watched me approach. He swallowed what I gave him, and after a moment his breathing seemed to ease.

I wanted to believe that meant it was working. But the poison had been soaking through his coat the whole time I was trying to save Johnny. I had no idea whether I’d stopped it or only bought Brennan time to talk.

“Why?” Esther asked.

Brennan didn’t answer.

She struck the table with the end of her staff, hard enough to make him look at her.

“Why, Brennan, why do this?”

“They still have Nell.”

Esther’s grip tightened around the needle.

I hadn’t heard the name before.

“My daughter,” Brennan said to me. “She was at the collection yard.”

Two Denens had escaped that yard before Lilith found them. He must have been one of them.

“She didn’t get out with us,” he said. “I thought she was dead until yesterday.”

“What did the man show you?” I asked.

“A photograph.”

That was all Brennan had. A photograph somebody from Town had brought him and a promise he was trying very hard to believe. Esther sat down across from him.

“You could have told me.”

“And done what?” he asked. “Gone south and taken her back?”

“We could have tried.”

“You have too many people here. You wouldn’t have risked them for one girl.”

Esther didn’t answer. I couldn’t have answered for her either.

Brennan's breathing changed. He took a quick breath, then another before he’d finished letting the first one out. I knew that sound. Heard it a lot with Town Blue.

“Bell’s coming,” I said.

He shook his head.

“How much did he give you?”

Brennan looked at the stain again.

“Enough.”

“Did you use it all?”

“No. Just enough for the bread, but… I couldn’t go through with it.”

“With what?”

“They wanted me to poison everyone. Now the rest of it’s soaking into me.” He chuckled lightly.

Bell walked over to us with another officer behind her. She saw Mara first. The pastor told her what had happened while she pulled on a pair of gloves.

Brennan tried to stand when she approached.

“Stay down,” she said.

“He’s contaminated,” I told her. “He needs treatment.”

“I’ve called for it.”

She asked Brennan his name. He opened his mouth, but no sound came out.

“Brennan,” Esther said. “His name is Brennan.”

Bell wrote it down.

Brennan looked at her notebook as though he didn’t understand why she needed it. Then he looked at me. His hand found the edge of Esther’s dress.

“Nell,” he said.

“I heard you.”

“If they come for her—”

“I heard you,” Esther said again.

She stayed beside him. She didn’t take his hand, and he didn’t ask her to.

Lilith had turned Johnny’s face against her shoulder. My wife was guiding the others to the far end of the room. They walked past the empty houses, carrying what they could. Brennan's breathing slowed. Then it stopped.

Bell checked him. No one said anything until she withdrew her hand.

The pastor brought another box downstairs. Bell asked him to wait. She photographed Brennan's coat where it lay, then had everyone step away from the table. The officer by the door said they’d need statements from all of us. Bell told him to start with the pastor and let the Denens finish packing. I asked her what would happen to Nell.

“We have a name and a location,” she said. “I’ll call the department on the south side.”

“Town runs that yard.”

“I know. It’s still worth a shot.”

That was as much as she could promise. She went upstairs to make the call.

Esther remained beside the table after Bell left. I stayed where I was.

“He killed three people,” I said. “I watched him push Mara.”

“I know it.”

She looked at the stain on his coat, and I could see a tear fall onto the fabric.

Johnny's lips were still blue. They stayed that color for almost an hour. 

That afternoon, we moved fifteen survivors to the shelter Bell had found. She kept her promise to Esther: they went together. Lilith carried Johnny in her hands because he wouldn't get into another bag, not even an empty one. We left the little houses for the pastor to bring later. Reece's tubing was still sitting on the shelf outside his door. I put it inside before we went.

Bell came by that evening, still in uniform. She asked to speak to Esther and me together, in the hallway outside the room where the others were settling in.

"I called it in," she said. "The collection yard, the name he gave us. Nell."

"And?"

"Town's records show no child processed through that facility under that name. No child matching her description, either, according to the file they sent over."

I felt my chest tighten. *Those fucking assholes.*

"Maybe she used a different name," I said. "Maybe she's still there, and they're lying."

"Maybe," Bell said. "I've requested the yard's intake logs going back six months. That'll take time. I can't promise you I have leverage to speed up."

Esther hadn't said anything. She was looking past both of us, down the hallway.

"Say it," she told Bell.

Bell hesitated before finally speaking.

"There's another possibility," she said. "That whoever contacted him inside that church lied about having her. Showed him a picture of a child, and let him believe whatever kept him useful."

"Why would Town go to that kind of trouble," I asked, "over one Denen and a photograph? And wouldn't Brennan know his own daughter?"

"He would," Bell said. "That's what makes me think the photo's real. Just old."

"Old, how old?"

"However old it was, clearly nobody could prove from looking at it whether it was taken last week or months ago." She glanced at Esther. "And Denen age faster than we do. If she was older when she got taken in—"

She stopped.

"Then she's not a child anymore," Esther finished. "She's... something 'useful'."

Bell didn't correct her.

"There are laws against what you're both dancing around," I said.

"There are laws against a lot of things Town does on the south side of that river," Esther said. "I've never once seen that stop them. Just seen them get better at making sure nobody could prove it."

Town had spent nine years teaching me to see everything with a tiny heartbeat as an object. A dead child would have been a tragedy Brennan could grieve and eventually let go of.

A living one, still breathing, still useful to somebody, still small enough to work somewhere no inspector would ever think to look... now that was a tragedy.

"So he's not being lied to," I said. "He's being played."

Bell didn't answer that either.

Esther didn't say anything for a long moment. Then she turned and walked back into the room where the others were, and I watched her stop in the doorway, not going any farther, just standing there looking at the fifteen of them getting settled into a shelter none of them had wished for. They just want a place to call home. One without Town or its Rainbows.

Lilith found me in the hallway a few minutes later. She'd overheard enough of it, I think.

"Is Nell real?" she asked.

I wanted to tell her yes. I wanted to tell her no, just as badly. I've spent most of my life inside a company built entirely out of lies, and I still didn't have a single truth to hand my own daughter.

"I don't know," I said. "I don't think Town does either. I think that might be the whole point."
