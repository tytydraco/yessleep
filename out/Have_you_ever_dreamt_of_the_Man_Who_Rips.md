I’m writing this in hopes that someone might have some insight into a situation that may be beyond my ability to understand. I’ve been a psychologist for over ten years, with a PhD from one of the most prestigious academic institutes in the world, and nothing from my previous experiences can make sense of the case I’m currently working on.

I started in clinical behavioural psychology early in my career before transitioning into working with children. The developing mind has always fascinated me, and the ability to work with individuals who have a changing cognitive capacity makes me feel like I can assist children in overcoming psychological conditions that may hinder them later in life.

I’ve owned my own practice for a long time, taking all sorts of clients. I hate to admit that I’m not cheap, but I do try to cut my costs for cases where parents come to me needing an immediate understanding of their child’s mental state. Mostly, it’s parents and guardians who contact my office. So, I was surprised when a detective from a small town in Nebraska reached out. His email subject line read: “Desperate for help.”

Now, clients usually travel to me, but for whatever reason, this detective informed me that I’d need to hop on the next flight if I was willing to take on the case. He explained that the mayor of their town had allocated resources in order to transport me into town as soon as possible. They had reached out to me due to the fact that I was one of the leading specialists in child psychology, and they needed someone with that specific experience to help find a solution to what he explained as “A strange phenomenon”. When I asked for further details, they kept it quite vague. The little information I did receive during our initial correspondence was that this situation was of grave importance, and that they were out of options.

The next morning, I got on a flight to Nebraska and read a few police reports on the plane that had been sent to me by the detective. Essentially, from what I could gather, what had been happening was that, for almost a month, parents in this small town (with a population of 726 people) had been consistently calling the police station to report something any other police department would overlook: a child’s nightmare.

At first, the police didn’t take much action, which was understandable. What could the police even do? But as the calls began flooding in from several parents, night after night, officers began being dispatched to homes after parents described their children as hysterical.

The curious thing was that this wasn’t one child having the same nightmare. It was every child in town. Not simultaneously, but consistently enough that, within a week of the calls beginning, every household in town that had a child between the ages of four and ten had contacted the police station in distress, reporting the same thing:

“My child’s hysterical after waking from  a nightmare.”

The ride from the airport to the town was almost three hours. The town was located in southwest Nebraska, just a few miles away from the Kansas state border. The detective picked me up himself. He was younger than I expected. A native of this small town.

We talked a lot during the drive, and as I waited for him to bring up the case, he never did. It wasn’t until I brought it up as we passed the welcome sign into town that I could see the despair behind his eyes. Whatever he thought was happening, it scared him.

Being a professional in my field means you know how to read people. So, I understood why he didn’t want to talk about it. Avoidant behaviour is a coping mechanism for many dealing with heavy situations. He needed a break from the reality of this “phenomenon” so I accommodated. We didn’t talk shop. But I could sense the fear growing in him as we arrived to our destination.

We stopped off at a motel they had arranged for me. It wasn’t anything special. A bare-bones room with a stiff bed and outdated wallpaper. I dropped off my bags and hopped back in the car to go to the police station.

The ride through town felt eerie. I couldn’t tell if this was just how it was all the time, or if the presence of these strange nightmares had turned the place into a desolate ghost town, devoid of community. I don’t think I saw a single person that entire drive.

I remember a cold chill passing through me as we drove past a playground in the middle of a park. A new addition to the town. A project headed by the mayor for the town’s children. The large, colourful jungle gym sat there cold and untouched. The empty swings swayed in the breeze. It was a strange sight.

At the police station, the detective and I sat with the mayor. It was there that they told me why they brought me out. The mayor wanted me to sit with each child in town and talk to them about their experience with this nightmare, in hopes that I could come up with some explanation for why every child was having, as the mayor theorized, the exact same recurring nightmare.

I was told that they’d provide whatever I needed for the sessions. But all I needed was a room. Preferably somewhere that felt a little warmer than a cold space where cops interrogate criminals. So, they said they’d set me up in the children’s gymnasium at the local elementary school.

When I got back to my motel, I looked over the police reports again. I wanted to see if there was any indication that suggested a correlation between each child’s nightmare. Something that could back up the mayors theory that these children were all experiencing the exact same nightmare. I’ve heard of the concept of “Dream Sharing”. The idea of a collective unconscious, but I’ve never encountered it in my work. If I was going to look for some sort of connection in these reported nightmares, I needed to find the similarities.

All the police reports depicted the same incident. Parents calling the police with concern over their child’s behaviour after waking from a nightmare. Almost each child was described as being manic, afraid, crying heavily, and hyperventilating. I’ve observed these intense emotional reactions in the past. Cases where children felt extreme fear. Whatever this nightmare was, it was triggering a real traumatic response from these kids.

There were no statements from the children. None of them were capable, or even calm enough to explain the details of their nightmare. The report described them as “Too afraid to discuss their dream out loud”. Follow-up reports described each child as almost comatose. Many of them became non-verbal. It was all very unusual.

One deputy described a strange moment in their report where a child raised his finger in a “Shh” gesture when being asked about their nightmare. Reading that left me a little uneasy before I turned off the lights and went to bed.

I woke up early to gather my notes and come up with a structured assessment strategy. A standardized protocol for each session so that I could compare each child’s responses without introducing inconsistencies of my own. That way I could pin point the similarities between each child’s account and form a list of collective experiences, if there were any at all.

I walked to the elementary school, wanting to get more of a feel for the place. As I passed a stretch of residential streets, about to cross the street to my destination, I couldn’t help but feel like someone was watching me. I’m sure anyone can relate to that strange, creepy sensation. That creeping notion that eyes are on you.

I looked around my surroundings, and that’s when I spotted it. I couldn’t make out any details of the distant figure staring at me through an upstairs window, but it was definitely a child. I waved, giving a warm smile, but the child just stood there stiffly.

I’ve never been particularly good with children, in a sense. Perhaps it was because I treated them more like adults. I never believed that embracing the playfulness people often use with children helped create a space where they felt comfortable sharing what was on their minds. Instead, I simply met them where they were, speaking to them in a way they understood. Direct. Honest. Doesn’t mean they always liked me.

When I arrived at the school, I was brought into a class room. Wasn’t long before parents shuffled in, their children in the classrooms down the hall with a teacher and police officers.

I stood at the front of the class beside the detective as the mayor introduced me to the parents. He shared my credentials with them. telling them exactly what I was there to do: talk to the children, examine the root of, what could possibly be explained as some sort of a shared psychosis, and hopefully find an answer to this strange phenomenon.

Some parents raised concerns and asked questions, but I assured them that I wouldn’t push their children to share anything they didn’t want to. My process was to guide them toward being comfortable enough to shed some light on what was going on in their heads.

When I asked if any of them had any information, or knew any details about the nightmare their children had, no one spoke. They all looked around the room, hoping someone knew something.

I walked into the gymnasium where an armchair sat in the middle of the large room, across a sagging beanbag chair. Wasn’t long before I heard footsteps approaching from the hall as I waited for the first child. The door opened, and the detective led them in.

I set my voice recorder on a small stool between myself and seven-year-old James Caraway. He was small for his age. Shaggy-haired and wide-eyed. There was a restlessness on his face. I could tell he was barely sleeping, if at all.

Below is a transcription of my recordings.

**Me**: Hello, James. How was your day?  
*(No response.)*  
**Me**: My name is Dr. Samantha Ross. Do you know why I’m here?  
*(No response.)*  
**Me**: I’m here to help. To make things better. But to do that, I need you to talk to me. Let me know what’s going on.

It was at this time that James looked up at me and slowly shook his head no.

**Me**: Your parents have told me that you had a nightmare a few weeks ago. You haven’t been sleeping much since. Is there anything you can tell me? Anything about your dream?

His breathing began to grow heavy. His eyes darted around the room, then he dropped his head and stared at the ground for the remainder of the session.

The next few children that entered the gymnasium responded the same way. Nothing too different.

Every child that sat across from me exhibited pronounced hypervigilance. Even in the safety of an adults presence in the gymnasium, their eyes repeatedly drifted toward the door, the windows, and the corners of the ceiling.

Before asking about the nightmares, I spent several minutes establishing rapport and observing each child’s baseline behaviour. Initially, I had suspected some form of social contagion. Children are remarkably susceptible to suggestion, particularly within close-knit groups. But that hypothesis was quickly dismissed.

Their physiological presentation resembled an acute stress response rather than the aftermath of an ordinary nightmare. A particular observation that I couldn’t make sense of.

I was starting to believe the sessions were hopeless. Until the detective brought in ten-year-old Sally Reynolds.

I found that the children weren’t responding to the plush beanbag chair, so I swapped it for a classroom desk. The ones with the seat attached. I asked a teacher to bring in some crayons and paper so the children could lose themselves in a task while I asked them questions.

I began my initial assessment with Sally, just as I had the other children. Not a word. Not a single response from her. She just doodled on the paper, ignoring anything that came out of my mouth.

As the detective walked in at the end of the session to escort Sally back to the classroom with the other children, Sally stood up and, as she walked past me, handed me the paper she had been drawing on.

I took it and looked at her, only to find that she was looking at me, holding a finger over her lips in a “Shhh” gesture. A moment that resembled what I had read in one of the police reports.

When I looked down at the paper, what I saw shook me. It sent a cold chill down my spine. It was eerie, but finally, it was something.

Sally had drawn a strange figure in black crayon, and in red, she wrote four words:

“The Man Who Rips.”

Now, I’ve spent years studying the fears children create. I know the difference between imagination and trauma. So, as the sessions continued, and I watched other children doodle the same entity Sally had shown me, it began to dawn on me that, for the first time in my career, I was documenting something I couldn’t categorize.

The mayor was right. His theory that the children were all having the same nightmare was now undoubtedly proven. I had a pile of crayon drawings of the exact same figure. An entity that now had a name, thanks to Sally. This is a start. Something J can use in the next sessions.

I’ve shared the pictures here and am sharing this with all of you to ask: has anyone else ever dreamt of “The Man Who Rips”?

Please share your experience if you have.  
Tomorrow, I’ll be returning to the school to run another series of sessions. I have a few ideas on how to get these kids talking.

I’ll report back with my findings.
