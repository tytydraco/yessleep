“My name is Doctor Chicken. I can fix anything!” 

The faux-feathers of his suit were caked with all manner of indescribable things, the ragged doctor’s coat he wore overtop even more so. He looked at me with dull, plastic eyes. He came from the space between the dumpster and the wall. He was disgusting. I sniffled, wiping a tear from my eye. My mother took me to the fairground. I’d wandered off, left her in the mini donut line, and now I couldn’t find her. I didn’t think I’d ever see her again, I thought I’d end up like the old man on the street corner with his coffee cup and coins. But I found this alleyway. 

I found Doctor Chicken.

I wasn’t scared of him. I should’ve been, but I was six. It didn’t register. I held my moo-cow tighter, his soft, plush fur comforting and clean. I sniffled again. “C-can you find my mom?” I stuttered, staring at the grimy floor. He crouched down, his coat dragging on the floor now, falling into old rainwater and pooled filth. I could smell him, the rank odor of forgotten things. He met my eyes—not with any eye holes on the costume, but with the scratched, faded plastic circles embedded in his head, too tarnished to make out the iris or pupil.

“Do you want me to fix that?” 

“You... you can?” I wiped my nose.

He nodded on a loose neck, slowly and methodically. “Of course! I can fix anything.” He stood up again, tall and looming above me. The way he moved wasn’t right, he didn’t have a... structure to him. Doctor Chicken was like a stuffed animal, but one that hadn’t been filled up all the way. Just enough loose space to lend him a deflated feel. His head hung lopsided, those blank eyes sizing me up from an unnatural angle. He lifted an arm, pointing to the space from which he’d emerged. “Come with me? I can fix you too.” 

I shook my head at that. “I can’t. I’m too big. I won’t fit.”

He paused for a moment. He didn’t say anything. Neither did I. I don’t know why, but everything I should’ve been feeling, it hit me at once like a cannonball. Like ice water, injected straight into my little veins with a needle far too big. Far too painful. Now I was scared. But... he didn’t *do* anything, he just nodded again. “Not yet.” He wasn’t looking at me anymore, he was looking at something else. Something far away that I couldn’t see at all. I opened my mouth, I don’t know if I had something to say—or maybe I was just going to cry. If he noticed, he didn’t say anything else. He just left me there, and with a final look, squeezed himself back into the gap until he disappeared into the narrow dark. I remember thinking it was weird that he’d filled his costume with sticks, especially if he was just going to break them all like that. I didn’t know that bones could snap. 

His voice, scratchy and distorted like a bad recording, muffled by the thick mask, crept its way out of the space one last time. “I can fix other things too. Our secret.” I didn’t know how to respond. If I should say anything. I opened my mouth again, when— 

“Jeremy?” My mom peeled around the corner. “Oh my god.” She ran towards me, wrapping me up in her arms. She told me that I shouldn’t run off like that, that she was worried sick, and she held me tighter than anything else, and I started crying. Not sadness, or at the fact that my favorite stuffy was now sitting in a dirty alley, but deep, shuddering sobs of relief. I *was* scared, but not of Doctor Chicken. I wasn’t scared anymore, because...

*He fixed it*, I thought at the time. *He made her find me. He can fix anything.

---

I was fourteen, the next time I saw him. I wasn’t the most popular kid in my school, but I wasn’t at the bottom of the ladder either. Perfectly average, in every single way. I was okay with that, it was easy. Never needed to wear a performative mask, and rarely had to deal with any of the ire and brutality that children can so casually bring upon those who have earned their disdain for any number of nebulous, nonsensical reasons. 

In that sense, things were good—but they could’ve been better. 

My mom believed I was much smarter than I was, which is probably a common occurrence. Unfortunately, she was incorrect, because I was bombing math. Big time. It wasn’t the standard stuff that was kicking my ass. I knew how to multiply, how to divide. It was geometry, and to a lesser extent, algebra. I *wanted* to understand, but my brain just didn’t work like that—spatially. I needed everything in a nice, linear, simple equation. I wasn’t getting that anymore. 

Now, I wasn’t *failing* failing, I was just close. I *was* passing, and I would continue to be passing as long as my grade held up. This was mostly because I did the homework. No one cared if you used a calculator at home. The tests were a different story. Flop after flop. I didn’t know what would happen if I failed—would I repeat the grade? Would they force me to stay back? Now I know they probably wouldn’t have done anything at all, but at fourteen, this was the difference between moving on to high school, or being stuck behind while all my friends went on without me. 

It was stupid, it didn’t matter. It wasn’t a big deal. But to a middle schooler, it sure was. It was the most important thing in the world.

So one night, after the school had closed, I decided to answer the voice that called from the locker room drainpipe. The voice that had called from dark spaces since that day back then. The voice that told me it could fix anything.

He was worse now, squeezing through the dark like a bloated insect, waterlogged and dripping with decay and the smell of mildew and wet rot that only intensified when he moved. I fell backwards when I saw him, my ass hitting the soaked tile and sending the garbage can spilling onto the floor, paper towels scattering into the murky water that trailed off of his coat and feathers in thick, dark pools. In the eight years that had passed since, my mind had sanitized the experience. Sanitized him. I scrambled back, my heart beating with the cadence of a pulsing sore. 

But he didn’t do anything. Dripping and horrible, he looked at me with the same lopsided, inscrutable expression he had years ago. If you could even call it an expression. 

My mouth felt dry. This wasn’t right, it... It wasn’t. I took a step away, and then another. “D-don’t come any closer, I—“

“My name is Doctor Chicken. I can fix anything!”

I stopped. “Okay.” I took a breath. He made no attempt to reach me, no attempt to advance. He stood in the shadows of the unlit shower, the motion lights flickering on and off with only my own movements, reflecting off his head mirror like a strobe. “Okay.” I shivered, but I didn’t know what I expected. This is what I wanted. I was the one who called on him, after all. I was the one who answered him, the one who heard that wavering, scratchy voice from the drain and told him yes. Yes he could fix something. 

I climbed slowly to my feet. I took another breath, deeper. “I’m not going to pass my math final. Can... you fix that?”

He nodded, the costume’s jaw flopping up and down while the eyes, now ringed with black, wet blotches, never broke contact. “Anything.” 

“Okay...” I said for a third time. I blinked. “Um... do that. Thanks.” I started to back away again, my own eyes meeting his. He didn’t blink. Of course he didn’t. *This was a mistake*, I thought. I was almost at the door, when he raised his arm again—not high, but just barely above his side, limply hanging. With a stained, damp glove, he pointed downwards. Towards the drain. 

“Come with me?”, he croaked. 

My mouth went dry again. I put my hand on the door handle, and shook my head. “N-no thanks. I... I won’t fit.” I gave him the excuse again. Like last time, all those years ago, he just stood there in the dark and nodded. 

“Not yet.”—and then he was gone. Down the little drainpipe. I exhaled, a sharp, harsh sound. The motion lights flickered to life, and stayed on this time. I opened the door, but turned back, only for a second. I thought I’d heard a final, blubbering whisper from the drain.

“Our secret.”

Whatever innocence clouded my eyes as a young child, it was long gone now. I vowed then and there, that I would *never* go with him. That I would never call on him again. I’d made a mistake, inviting... whatever the fuck that was back into my life. I should’ve taken the F and walked away, that’s what I thought at the moment. 

Then, the final exams came along—except, not all of them. Science, English, Social Studies, a few weeks after classes officially ended, we were dragged back test by test. Math was the last one, it loomed in the distance at the end of the exam week like a veritable colossus on the march. But it never came. Instead, my mother received an email the morning of. Mr. Leslie, our teacher, would not be coming in. I found out later, months later through the endless, winding connections of the gossip network that connects any school, that it was a traffic accident—he was drunk. He was dead before they reached the hospital.

It was too short notice to arrange anything, anyone to cover and grade the tests... and thus, they were canceled. Our final grade pre-exam would be our final grade overall. 

I got a C. 

Doctor Chicken did it again. 

At least, that’s what I *told* myself. I’d called on him twice—well, once. The first time, he was the one who came to me. Both times though, I got my wish. In spite of what he looked like, I was convinced that, one way or another, he was benign, or benign enough anyway. Benign enough to where, when I heard him whispering through the vents in the car, or when I could see the yellowed white of his plastic eyes in the space below the deck, I didn’t really care. He was creepy, but I thought he was looking out for me. Sure, Mr. Leslie probably didn’t deserve to *die*, but it wasn’t like Doctor Chicken force fed him liquor, strapped him down in the car, and sent him on his way. He made his choice, fate had just twisted it together to benefit. 

So I broke my vow. I did call on Doctor Chicken again. I called on him after a bad date—a date that’d eventually turn into a marriage, even though I spilled my soup across the table. I called on him before college applications, and despite my poor Math grades (those, unfortunately, persisted through my high school years), I got into the school of my dreams. He always asked if I’d come with him, but he never forced anything. He always agreed—not yet—and he’d go on his way, back to wherever he came from. Wherever he “lived”, I guess. Somewhere in the dark between things. I didn’t know what he was, but I knew what he could do. 

Whatever our arrangement was, whatever he was getting out of the whole thing, I could put up with it. I would put up with it. Scary though he may have been, my life was unquestionably better—and I owed it all to him. He wasn’t as bad as he looked. Far from it.

That’s what I thought, anyway.

---

I was thirty-five. Our daughter was four. She had leukemia. She wasn’t doing well. It had been a long time since I had called on Doctor Chicken. Since I’d seen him. After a certain age, you just start thinking you can handle everything alone. I was wrong.

We were at the Children’s Hospital—my wife, Lauren, and I. Anxiously sitting in the waiting area, doing what one does in such a place. Waiting for news. Good, bad, or inconceivable. Our daughter had crashed hard the prior night, collapsed on the kitchen floor. She was supposed to be getting better, the treatments were supposed to work. She wasn’t, and they didn’t. That’s how it goes, isn’t it? Our dear Katherine, my little Kathy. I loved her almost as much as she loved her moo-cow, the toy I’d loved when I was her age, worn and weathered though it now was. We didn’t know if she’d survive the night, they’d taken her into emergency almost seven hours ago, by my count—and we hadn’t heard much since.

Our marriage wasn’t in the best position, not anymore. This kind of thing, it draws on you. Emotionally and physically. It takes your very soul and drags it across a forest of broken glass. There was no love left, only exhaustion.

That’s why, when the doctor gave us the news that our daughter’s chances of surviving were low—not just because of the leukemia, but because of the way she hit her head when she fell, the way we’d eased off treatment when she seemed to be improving. The toll that the chemotherapy had already taken on her. I didn’t have comforting words for Lauren as she cried, and cried. I only felt cold. So, so cold. 

I must’ve looked like a monster to my wife, staring blankly ahead at the wall. I must’ve looked worse when I told her that I knew someone who could help, when I told her that “Doctor Chicken” might be able to save her life. I’d never told her about my strange friend before, and as the years went by and our relationship deteriorated, I She looked like she wanted to saw my head off with a steak knife. She must’ve thought I was worse than a monster—that I was either insane, or using our daughter’s plight to set up some cruel joke at her expense. Maybe both. 

But I wasn’t, I was dead serious. I think she could tell. 

“Doctor... Chicken.” She had a level of disdain in her voice, but stayed measured. Calm. I think she was too tired to argue. “Is that someone at the hospital? I can... I can get someone to page him.” She asked, finally breaking the silence. Her eyes were still red.

“No,” I told her. “It sounds stupid—maybe it is, but I’ll need you to come with me. Trust me.”

Her brow furrowed. “Come with you where? We’re supposed to wait for updates, if we leave, we might—“

“It doesn’t matter. Come on.”

“Come *where?*” She asked again, anger creeping into her voice.

“Somewhere dark.”

I don’t know why she stopped asking questions and followed me—by all accounts, I did sound crazy. Maybe she was desperate, or maybe she was an idiot. God knows I was both. 

It was the middle of the night. We went outside. To the playground at the far end of the hospital, isolated and quiet. Just like every other time. She was nervous. She didn’t understand what I was doing, she wanted to go back. What if we missed some news? What if they needed something? Quiet, I told her—and we waited. 

He came from the playground, from an open-topped tube meant to be spoken into, twisting and squeezing and cracking his way through the opening. He was looking rough, compared to the last time I had come calling. The rot had seeped in deeper, thick and oozing from the eyes of the suit, which was marred now with all manner of depressions and gouges each dripping with the same foulness. But I recognized him the same. The distant hospital lights glinted across his dull eyes, almost as though in spite of the suit’s static face, he was smiling in recognition. I smiled too. Smiled at my old friend.

Not Lauren. Lauren started to scream. 

“What the fuck?”, her voice hoarse between wails. “What the *fuck* is that!?”

“Just wait, I told you. He can help her, he can—“

She didn’t let me finish. She turned tail and tried to run. I grabbed her arm, my fingers tight around her wrist as she wrenched and pulled at my hand, digging her feet into the ground. “Let go!” She cried, “Let me fucking go!”. I held my grip tighter. 

“Doctor Chicken”, I nodded at my old friend. “We need your help. Our daughter... can you help her?”

Doctor Chicken stared blankly ahead. “I can fix anything. My name is Doctor Chicken.” His voice was worse too, like a tape overwritten too many times—interspersed with repeating sounds and wavering volume. I felt bad for him. Lauren started hitting me now, her fists balled up tightly as she sobbed deep, terrified sobs. I didn’t care. I was doing what I had to do. He was harmless, he was—

“Come with me.” Doctor Chicken pointed towards the tube with a rotting, matted arm, as he had the countless other places from which he’d slithered. 

As always, I answered in turn. “You know I won’t fit.” It’d almost turned into an inside joke at this point, a joke between me and Doctor Chicken. Until he said what he said next, and then it wasn’t funny anymore. Within three words, I was sent back to when I was six, to what I’d felt when I first denied his request. Pure, abject dread.

“You will now.”

For the first time since I’d met him, Doctor Chicken lurched towards me like a gelatinous mass. I leapt back in surprise, releasing Lauren’s arm. She fell on the ground, her foot kicking off the playground gravel and sending a shower upwards. “Whoa, whoa—hey!” I held up my arms and stepped further back. “What the hell? This isn’t—“ He continued his advance, lurching forward in huge, exaggerated strides before the spray of gravel hit him clean in the face and, to my surprise, sent him reeling backwards to the ground, where he hit the grass like a wobbling thud. 

I rushed over to Lauren, stumbled through the dew-slick grass and tried to help her up—but Doctor Chicken started to move again. He didn’t get up. He didn’t run. He didn’t even move a limb. He didn’t have to. With an unnatural pop, his head wrenched sideways and he started to *slither,* dragging his body, that decaying suit, behind him like a tangle of vestigial limbs. I pulled my wife to her feet, almost yanking her arm out of her socket, and we scurried away from the playground and the *thing* I’d thought was my friend—then, she fell again. She fell hard, and I could tell why. 

Something long and segmented was wrapped around her leg, glistening and reflective in the moonlight. My eyes traced it back across the grass to the mangled figure pursuing us, it emerged from Doctor Chicken’s rubber beak, now angled sideways and flopping about erratically like the mandibles of an insect. The... tendril? The arm? Whatever it was, it started to pull, dragging her backwards across the grass as she howled an unholy mixture of pain and fear. I grabbed her again, trying to fight it—trying to pull her back. I kicked at the appendage, I yanked her by the torso, and then... I stopped.

From behind her, from Doctor Chicken, I heard him repeat the promise that I’d heard him give me time after time.

“I can fix anything!”

I looked Lauren in the eyes, wide and brown. Tearing up with a fear I didn’t think a person could feel. We’d been together ten years—eleven this year. As of late, we’d hated each other. Every interaction we’d had was a snide remark directed at the other, only putting on the veneer of cooperation for the sake of our daughter. Everything we’d done, was for her. For our Kathy. Looking into her eyes, I understood that. 

And I understood that I still had a chance to save her. To save the person that the both of us had dedicated our lives to. 

“I’m sorry.”

I let go.

Doctor Chicken dragged her backwards, another shriek of fear and despair struck across the field like thunder as she was dragged across the grass, through the gravel—and sure enough, she went with Doctor Chicken. Down the tube, screaming something truly unearthly until it deteriorated into a wet, manic gurgle. If I was still six, I would’ve thought it was weird that she was filled with sticks. As an adult, I knew that bones could snap. 

They were gone. Silence—save for a faint echo, a sound I had to get close to the tube to hear.

“Our secret.”

I pulled my head away. A thin, red line dripped from the opening. I didn’t want to look at it. 

I walked back into the hospital, we’d only been gone maybe twenty minutes at most. I told the nurse that my wife had gone out to get us food, and that she’d be back. In spite of everything, the waking nightmare that I had just played host to, I felt... at peace. I told myself that Lauren would’ve done the same—would’ve sacrificed me in a heartbeat to save our little girl. But it was alright now. Doctor Chicken would help her. Doctor Chicken would save her life. It’s what she would’ve wanted.

Then, I got the news. Kathy had passed. 

Doctor Chicken didn’t fix her. He couldn’t fix anything. 

I’ll spare you the details. Nobody should know what it’s like to deal with this kind of thing, filling out paperwork for the death of your own child. They took her to the morgue, to keep her until I made arrangements with a funeral home for a transfer of care. I felt numb, like someone’d taken a ladle and scooped out my insides. Like nothing would ever be okay again. But that was better—better than what I felt next, when I saw it in the lobby. 

It was just a little TV in the play area, put up so kids would stay entertained while their parents waited around. I didn’t care about that. I cared about what was on the screen. It was some educational video, it looked like it was from the 70s. It was old, old enough that it’d probably played on a thousand screens over the years. Old enough that if someone or something was watchful enough, they could memorize every detail of over the course of its existence. At first, it didn’t mean anything to me. Just some bad child actor, pretending to cry, his arm scuffed up with some red makeup. Then I saw him. He walked into the scene.

Doctor Chicken. 

Not the Doctor Chicken I knew, the filthy, horrible thing. A friendly, clean, warm-looking mascot. A mascot who leaned down to speak to the child, and spoke those same words.

“My name is Doctor Chicken! I can fix anything!”

He pointed to the kid’s injury. “Do you want me to fix that?”. The kid enthusiastically shook his head up and down. Doctor Chicken gave two thumbs up to the camera. “Great! Come with me!”

I was transfixed. The scene changed and the two were sitting in some... doctor’s office. Looked like *Blue’s Clues*, or something like that. Doctor Chicken was wrapping the kid’s arm with gauze, and the kid had stopped crying. “Am I better? Will I be better soon?” he asked. 

“Not yet—but you will be! I can fix other things too!”, the chicken exclaimed, pointing a finger in the air. “Like an upset stomach, a fever, or some things you might not even know about!”

“Wow, thanks Doctor Chicken!”

The chicken handed him a lolipop as he hopped down the doctor’s table, the same kind they stock every doctor’s office with. He turned to to the camera and patted the kid on the head when he popped it into his mouth. Doctor Chicken winked. “Our secret!”

It was nothing, it was a PSA telling kids to go to the doctor. But while I watched it, this five minute long *nothing*, everything fell into place—and I almost fell to the floor and cried like I did when I lost my mom at the fairground.

See, I don’t think Doctor Chicken could fix anything. I don’t think he ever could. It was coincidence all the way down, this whole time. I recognized that voice, I recognized every spoken word and every line that came out of his mouth on the TV. Like a bad recording. I think Doctor Chicken—*my* Doctor Chicken—had seen this tape too, once upon a time. I think he thought it was... an approachable character. An approachable guise to wear while he lured me and maybe countless other kids in. While he waited until I was big enough to go with him, down to wherever black pit he crawled out of. 

He wasn’t my friend. He wasn’t granting my wishes. He was fattening me up.

And I gave Lauren to him. For nothing. I guess he was happy to take whatever he could, when it came down to it. He’d waited long enough. 

In an ideal world, I’d say that I went about my life. That I never saw Doctor Chicken again, never heard from him. But that’d be a lie, that much is predictable. I still hear the whispers, the same sort of whispers that I’d heard my entire life, crawling out of crevices where no living thing would ever dwell. I can hear them now, from the space beneath my box spring. 

Only, they don’t sound like Doctor Chicken anymore. They sound like my wife. 

I think... I might go with her. Maybe it'll make things right, in some grand cosmic sense. Or maybe I can't fix anything either. 

That'd make two of us.
