We lived with our father in a rotting, yellow two-story house on the edge of town where the streetlights died. My room and my sisters faced each other at the top of the stairs, separated by a narrow strip of threadbare hallway carpet. I never had an issue with my space, but my sister treated hers like an active threat. She claimed the room had a "weight" to it—a localized gravity that made it hard to breathe. To drown out what she called the "wet scraping" in the silence, she kept a static-choked radio blaring 24/7.  
Then came the accusations. She grew aggressive, her eyes bloodshot and hollow, claiming I was clawing at her doorframe at 3:00 AM or trying to trip her down the steep wooden stairs. I have chronic insomnia; I roam the house at night like a ghost myself, trying to exhaust my body into submission. But I never touched her door. My father, checked out and perpetually exhausted, ignored the screaming matches. Eventually, my sister stopped fighting. She simply locked herself inside, drowning in radio static, until the day she packed her bags and left.  
I took her room immediately, eager for the extra space. My cousin temporarily moved into my old room across the hall.  
The first night in my sister’s room was the last time I ever left a door open.  
Around 11:00 PM, after playing cards downstairs, I lay in bed. The room felt freezing, despite the summer heat. The only illumination came from the sickly blue glow of my phone and a single, jaundiced streetlamp bleeding through the window. The silence wasn't peaceful; it was heavy, pressing against my eardrums like deep water.  
At midnight, the bedroom door gave a low, agonizing creak. It swung open roughly halfway.  
A dark silhouette poked its head around the frame. My heart misfired, a spike of pure adrenaline shocking my limbs into ice, until the figure spoke.  
"Wanna play another round?" My cousin’s voice whispered.  
Relief washed over me, leaving me angry. I snapped at him to go to bed. He slunk back into the dark. Five minutes later, the thin walls carried the rhythmic, comforting sound of his snoring from across the hall. I shook my head, laughing off the scare, and sank back into my phone.  
By 3:46 AM, the insomnia had won. My eyes burned. I slid my phone under the pillow, plunging the room into the dim, yellow streetlamp shadow.  
That’s when I realized the door hadn't been closed. It had stayed in the same position my cousin left it.   
The air in the room suddenly curdled. The ambient noise of the night—the crickets outside, the distant hum of the highway—simply vanished. The silence became absolute, a vacuum.  
The door shifted an inch.  
Another head peeked around the wood. My brain, desperate for safety, tried to logic it away. *My cousin again. He needs to go to bed.* I opened my mouth to curse him out, but the words died in my throat.  
The figure didn't just step out. It *poured* into the room.  
It was terrifyingly tall, stretching toward the ceiling, a silhouette cut cleanly out of the fabric of reality. It wasn't just dark; it was an iridescent, oil-slick blackness that seemed to suck the ambient yellow light into itself. It didn't walk. It drifted, hovering inches above the floorboards, its movements completely frictionless and horrifyingly fast.  
Before my brain could process its mass, it was at the foot of my bed.  
Panic became a physical weight. A crushing, unseen hand slammed down onto my chest, pinning me to the mattress. I tried to scream for my cousin, for my dad, for anyone, but my throat closed up. The muscles in my neck strained so hard I could hear the tendons creaking in my own skull, but only a dry, whistling gasp escaped my lips. My fingers clawed at the mattress, embedding my nails into the fabric until my knuckles turned white, desperately trying to anchor myself to reality.  
The entity just loomed. It had no face, yet I could feel its gaze—not looking at me, but *into* me, mapping out my fear, feeding on the frantic, ragged pounding of my heart against my ribs. The air around the bed tasted like copper and old dust. I realized then what my sister had been living with. It wasn't a ghost. It was a predator that hunted in the paralysis of the night.  
My lungs burned for oxygen. My vision began to blur at the edges, tunneling into blackness. I fought the urge to close my eyes, terrified that if I blinked, it would lean down.  
Human biology failed me. My eyelids snapped shut for a fraction of a second.  
A gasp tore from my throat as my eyes flew open.  
Blinding, aggressive morning sunlight was pouring through the window, searing my retinas. I sat up violently, drenched in cold sweat, my chest aching as if someone had been standing on it. My fingers were stiff and cramped from gripping the bedframe all night.  
The room was empty. The house was quiet.  
But across the room, the door was still resting exactly halfway open, staring at me like a dead eye.
