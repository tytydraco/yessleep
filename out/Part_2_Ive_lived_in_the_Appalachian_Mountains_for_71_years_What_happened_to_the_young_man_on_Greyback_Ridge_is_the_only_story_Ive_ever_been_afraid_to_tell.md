He counted the stones three times to make sure he had the number right.

Forty-seven.

He left them where they were. He didn't touch them. He went back inside, locked the door, and stood at the fire finder for a long time, not looking at it — just standing near it, thinking.

Then he picked up his notebook and turned to the page where he'd been logging the fire finder readings. The unexplained ones. The bearings that appeared without any corresponding smoke.

He had sixteen entries over fifty-two days. He'd dutifully noted each one — date, time, azimuth — and he'd never reported any of them, as Gary had instructed.

Now, for the first time, he looked at them carefully. Really looked.

Fifteen of the sixteen bearings pointed in different directions — various points of the compass, seemingly random.

But the sixteenth. The one from three nights ago, the night before the storm.

He got out the topographic map that was pinned to the wall beside the fire finder. He took the bearing — two hundred and sixty-two degrees, almost due west — and he traced it out on the map with a pencil, extending the line from Greyback Ridge outward.

The line crossed two other ridges. It passed over a small unnamed creek. It went through a section of national forest marked as restricted access, no trails, no facilities.

And then it terminated at a location marked on the map with a small symbol he didn't recognize — not a trail marker, not a shelter icon, not a fire lookout symbol. Something else. A small black circle with a line through it.

He pulled out the map legend.

The symbol meant: historic site, access by permit only.

He stared at it for a long time.

Then he called the ranger station.

Not Gary — Gary wasn't on duty. He got a woman named Sandra who handled administrative inquiries.

"I'm looking at my topo map," he said. "There's a symbol at approximately these coordinates — " He read them off. "Historic site, access by permit. What is that?"

A pause on the line. Longer than he expected.

"Can you hold for just a minute?" Sandra said.

He held for four minutes.

When Sandra came back, her voice was slightly different. More careful.

"That's an old structure," she said. "Pre-dates Forest Service ownership of the land. It's closed to the public. Structurally unsound."

"What kind of structure?" he asked.

"I don't have the details in front of me," she said. "Is there something else I can help you with?"

He thanked her and hung up.

That night, he didn't sleep.

He sat in the chair by the fire finder and he watched the northeast corner of the summit through the glass and he thought about the trail. About the bend in it. About the two or three degrees of temperature drop and the sudden silence of the birds.

Around three in the morning — the radio static had come and gone and the silence after it was total — he saw something move at the treeline.

Not on the trail. Just at the edge of the trees, where the darkness under the boughs met the darkness of the open summit.

He couldn't see what it was. It was too dark, and it was too still. It wasn't moving anymore. But he had the overwhelming sense of something standing there. Watching the tower the way he watched the treeline.

He sat without moving until first light.

When the sky began to pale, the sense of being watched faded slowly, the way a sound fades — not stopping, just receding until it was beneath the threshold of perception.

He called Gary that morning.

Gary was quiet for a moment when Daniel told him what had happened. The stones on the catwalk. The thing at the treeline. The historic site on the map.

"You followed the rules?" Gary said.

"Yes. All of them."

"You didn't go out after the bell."

"No."

"You didn't look through the glass when you heard the footsteps."

Daniel paused. "No," he said. "I closed my eyes."

Gary let out a long breath.

"Okay," he said. "Listen to me. What happened last night is — it happens, sometimes. Not often. Once, twice in a season. You did everything right. You're safe. Do you hear me?"

"What is it?" Daniel said. "What's out there?"

Silence on the line.

"Gary," Daniel said. "I've been up here for fifty-three days. I deserve to know."

Another long pause.

"I'll come up Saturday," Gary said. "I'll bring your supplies and we'll talk."

Saturday was four days away.

The next three nights, Daniel slept with the light on. The rules stayed quiet. The bell didn't ring. The footsteps didn't return.

But each morning, when he went out onto the catwalk, he found one more stone.

Not forty-seven. Not a row. Just one stone, placed neatly in the center of the doormat he'd brought from home.

Three mornings. Three stones.

He put them in a line on the windowsill, where he could see them.

He told me he didn't know if that was wise. He just wanted to be able to see them.

Gary came on Saturday, as promised. He arrived an hour earlier than usual. He didn't bring just supplies — he brought two other men that Daniel hadn't met. One was a ranger. The other wasn't in uniform, and Gary didn't introduce him by job title. He was an older man, heavyset, with wire-rimmed glasses and a canvas hat, and he carried a notebook and a small device Daniel didn't recognize.

The three of them came up the stairs and into the cab and looked around without speaking for a moment. The older man's eyes went immediately to the bell. Then to the photograph on the south wall. Then to the three stones lined up on the windowsill.

He picked one up and turned it over in his fingers. Set it back down.

"Tell me everything," he said to Daniel. "Start from the first night."

Daniel told him everything.

The man — Daniel called him the Professor, and I'll call him that too, because that's what he seemed like — listened without interrupting, writing steadily in his notebook. When Daniel finished, the Professor was quiet for a moment.

Then he asked: "The bearing on the fire finder. Two-sixty-two. You traced it on the map?"

"Yes," Daniel said. "It pointed to a historic site."

"Did you look up what the site was?" the Professor said.

"I asked the ranger station. They said it was an old structure. Closed to the public."

The Professor nodded slowly. "It's a mill," he said. "Or what's left of one. Built around 1831 by a family named Coulter. Grain mill, operated by a water wheel on the creek. The Coulters ran it for about twenty years. Then, in 1851, something happened."

He paused. Set down his pen.

"The census records show the entire family — seven people, three generations — living at that location in 1850. By 1852, they'd all been removed from the census. No death records. No tax records after 1851. No land transfer. They simply stopped appearing in any official documentation."

"What happened to them?" Daniel said.

"Nobody knows," the Professor said. "The mill itself was intact when the Forest Service acquired the land in 1936. All the equipment still in place. The family's personal belongings still in the dwelling attached to the mill. Everything exactly as if they'd just stepped outside and never come back."

He picked up his pen again.

"The trail on the northeast side of the summit," he said. "The one you were told not to follow. Do you know where it goes?"

Daniel said he didn't.

"It goes to the mill," the Professor said. "About four miles downhill, following the creek drainage. It's the original access trail from the Coulter property up to the ridge. It's been there for nearly two hundred years."

The cab was very quiet.

Daniel looked at Gary. Gary was looking out the window.

"What's been coming to the tower?" Daniel asked. "What left the stones?"

The Professor didn't answer right away. He looked at the bell again. At the photograph.

"The short answer," he said, "is that we don't know exactly. The long answer is that certain locations in these mountains — locations that have held concentrated human presence, especially human suffering or loss — seem to develop a kind of residual — " He paused. Chose his next word carefully. " — attention."

"Attention," Daniel repeated.

"They notice things," the Professor said. "Particularly isolated individuals. Single points of warmth and light and thought in an otherwise empty landscape." He glanced at the windows. "A fire lookout tower on a ridgeline is, functionally, a beacon. You've been here for fifty-three days. Whatever is associated with the Coulter site has had fifty-three days to become aware of you."

"The stones," Daniel said. "What do the stones mean?"

The Professor looked at the three stones on the windowsill for a moment.

"In our experience," he said carefully, "the stones are an invitation."

The word sat in the room.

"An invitation to what?" Daniel said.

"To follow the trail," the Professor said. "Past the first bend."

Nobody spoke for a moment.

"And the people who follow it?" Daniel said.

The Professor looked at Gary. Gary was still looking out the window.

"The lookout before you," Gary said finally. "Hendricks. I told you he's not the lookout anymore." He turned from the window. "He followed the trail on his second season. We found his gear at the first bend. His notebook. His radio. His boots." A pause. "We didn't find him."

Silence.

"He'd been given the rules," Daniel said.

"He'd been given the rules," Gary confirmed.

The Professor stood and picked up his notebook.

"You have thirty-eight days left on your assignment," he said. "The rules will protect you, but only if you follow them exactly. The stones will continue. The attention will continue. You may hear your name again — if you do, do not respond. You may see something at the treeline — if you do, do not acknowledge it."

"And when the assignment ends?" Daniel said.

"Rule Nine," the Professor said. "Follow it to the letter." He looked at Daniel steadily through his wire-rimmed glasses. "And do not look back."

He and the unnamed ranger went back down the stairs. Gary stayed for another moment, standing by the door.

"You're going to be okay," he said. "You followed the rules. That matters."

"Why does it matter?" Daniel said. "What do the rules actually do? Why does not answering my name make a difference? Why does not opening the door protect me?"

Gary thought about this for a moment.

"I think," he said slowly, "it's about consent. About engagement. Every time you don't respond — don't answer your name, don't open the door, don't look through the glass — you're refusing to engage. You're saying: I know you're there, and I'm choosing not to participate."

He paused.

"Whatever is at the Coulter place has been here for a very long time. It knows how to be patient. It knows how to wait. But it needs something from you to do what it wants to do. And as long as you don't give it that — as long as you keep refusing — it can't take you where it took the others."

"Others," Daniel said. "There have been others besides Hendricks?"

Gary put his hand on the doorframe.

"The Coulter family was seven people," he said. "The records show the mill had a hired hand who lived on the property as well. That's eight people who disappeared in 1851."

He looked at Daniel.

"There have been fourteen more since then. Hunters. A survey crew in 1923. Three hikers in the seventies who tried to follow the Coulter trail all the way down. Hendricks."

He pulled on his hat.

"Thirty-eight days," he said. "Follow the rules."

And he went down the stairs.

Daniel stood in the empty cab for a long time after he heard the sound of the truck fade down the mountain road.

Then he picked up the three stones from the windowsill.

He went to the door. He opened it and stepped onto the catwalk. He walked to the northeast corner — the corner that faced the trail — and he leaned over the railing and dropped all three stones off the edge of the summit. He watched them fall out of sight into the trees far below.

Then he went back inside. He locked the door.

He picked up his notebook and wrote: Day 53. Stones returned. Thirty-eight days remaining.

And then he made himself lunch, and he read his book, and he watched the ridges, and he counted the days.

The next morning, there was a stone on the doormat.

He left it where it was.

The next morning, there was another.

He left that one too.

The mornings continued. The stones accumulated. By the end of the second week after the Professor's visit, there were fourteen stones on the doormat. By the end of the third week, the mat was covered, stones piled two and three deep, and they had begun to spread outward onto the catwalk grating.

He stopped counting.

He stopped going out onto the catwalk in the mornings. He'd open the door a crack to let in the morning air and then close it again without looking at what was out there.

The thing at the treeline became more regular. Most nights now, if he stayed up past two AM, he could see the shape of it — and it was always exactly at the place where the trees met the open rock, always at the same distance, and it never moved while he was watching. But he would blink, or turn away to pour water, and when he looked back it would be slightly different — not closer, exactly, but more present somehow. More substantial.

He stopped staying up past two AM.

He slept with earplugs and a sleep mask and he tried to make his mind as blank as possible.

He called his mother once a week from the ranger station radio on Gary's supply visits. He didn't tell her anything that was actually happening. He told her the views were beautiful and he was reading a lot and the weather had been good.

He held on.

And then, on the eighty-eighth day, the photograph fell off the wall.

He was sitting at the table eating dinner when he heard it — a small sound, a pop, and then the dry slap of the frame hitting the floor.

He looked at it lying there, face down on the floorboards. The nail it had hung on for however many decades was still in the wall. The wire on the back of the frame had simply let go.

He sat at the table for a long time looking at it.

Rule Eight. Do not turn it over.

He got up and walked to where it lay and he stood over it.

He hadn't touched it. He hadn't turned it over. It had fallen on its own.

He crouched down. The frame was face-down. The back of the frame was a piece of cardboard, old and warped, and around the edges of the cardboard, in the narrow gap between it and the frame itself, he could see the back of the photograph.

And on the back of the photograph, in handwriting so small he had to lean very close, were words.

He read them without touching the frame.

Without turning it over.

He has never told me exactly what the words said. I've asked him, more than once. Each time he goes quiet, looks at his hands, and then says something like: "It was a name, and a date, and a direction." And then he stops.

Once, after I'd known him for a while, he said: "The name wasn't mine. But the date was the date I arrived. And the direction — " He stopped. Shook his head. "The direction was northeast."

He left the photograph on the floor for the last five days of his assignment. He stepped around it every morning. He did not pick it up. He did not turn it over.

On the last night — September first — he followed Rule Nine exactly.

He lit the lantern and placed it in the west window, facing out into the dark. He cooked a full meal — rice and beans, the last of his food stores — and he put a full plate of it on the catwalk, setting it down without looking at the stones that covered the grating, without looking at the treeline, without looking at anything except the plate in his hands and the floor in front of his feet.

He went back inside.

He locked the door.

He lay down on the cot.

He didn't sleep, but he lay still and kept his eyes closed and breathed slowly and waited for morning.

At three AM the radio static came and went, as it had every single night for ninety-two consecutive nights.

At four AM he heard the footsteps on the catwalk.

He kept his eyes closed.

The footsteps went around — north, east, south, west — and then they paused at the door. He heard the careful, polite knock. Three times.

He did not move.

Then, after a moment, he heard something he hadn't heard before.

A sound like a long exhalation. Not quite human. Not quite not.

And then silence.

And then, very faintly, the sound of footsteps moving away — not around the catwalk, but down. Down the stairs. Down the mountain. Toward the northeast.

Fading.

Fading.

Gone.

At first light, he gathered his belongings. He put on his pack. He took one last look around the cab — at the lantern still burning in the window, at the photograph still face-down on the floor, at the bell hanging motionless and tarnished above the door.

He went down the stairs.

He started down the trail.

He did not look back.

He told me he walked those forty minutes to the parking area in an absolute blank — no thoughts, no feelings, nothing but the trail under his feet and the morning light through the trees. He found his truck where he'd left it three months ago. He got in. He started the engine. He drove down the mountain.

He stopped at a diner in the nearest town and ordered the largest breakfast on the menu and ate all of it. He used the diner's wifi to call his mother on his phone — the first time he'd used a smartphone in three months — and he told her he was coming home.

She asked how it had been.

"Quiet," he said.

Now.

I've told this story the way Daniel told it to me. I haven't added anything. I haven't taken anything away. I promised him that, and it's a promise I intend to keep.

But I'll add one thing of my own, because it seems relevant.

About six months after Daniel came back down from Greyback Ridge, I drove out to the area where the Coulter mill is located. Not to find it — I wasn't about to go poking around a restricted site in an area I'd been told doesn't want visitors. I just drove the forest road that passes within about two miles of the location, the way I used to do when I was younger and less knowledgeable about certain things.

I parked the truck and I sat with the window down and I listened.

The birds were quiet for about a quarter mile in every direction. The air was still in a way that had nothing to do with wind. And I had the feeling — the particular, unmistakable feeling I've had in certain places in these mountains over seventy-one years — of being seen.

Not threatened. Not menaced.

Just seen.

I rolled up the window and I drove home.

These mountains have a long memory.

And they are very, very patient.

That's all for tonight. You stay safe out there. And if you're ever in the Appalachians — if you ever find yourself on a trail that gets cold a little too fast, where the birds go quiet in a way they shouldn't — you turn around.

You turn around and you don't look back.

You hear me?

Good night.
