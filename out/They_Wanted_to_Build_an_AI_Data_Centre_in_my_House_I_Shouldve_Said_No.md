I was thirty-four years old when my mother passed away.  

At the time I was unemployed, friendless, and drowning in debt.  

I'd dropped out of university at twenty-two, hoping to be the next big entrepreneur. I started an import business that failed. Then a delivery app that failed harder. Then an e-commerce website, one that failed so quietly that I didn't even notice until the domain expired.

Every failure cost me money I didn't have, and friends I couldn't keep. Eventually, it gave me the kind of reputation that screamed ‘avoid this guy at all cost’. It was so bad that relatives stopped inviting me to weddings, because they didn't want to explain who I was to their in-laws.

When Ma passed, the only thing she left me was the farmhouse. It was a big and beautiful home that was practically useless. It was virtually in the middle of nowhere- forty minutes from the nearest proper market, with no reliable internet, and no neighbors close enough to borrow sugar from. Also, it had way too many rooms for a single inhabitant.

I tried to sell it. When that didn’t work out, I tried to rent it. Everybody liked the house itself, but nobody wanted to have it. And if I'm honest, nobody wanted to deal with me either. I have a way of making people regret trying to help me. Ma used to say that too, before she stopped saying anything to me at all.

Three months in, I was sitting in the house alone, calculating the debt I had to repay by the end of the year. That’s when I had an unexpected visitor, wearing a pale green shirt, sleeves rolled exactly twice.

He introduced himself as Rafiq, Senior Manager of Aldermore & Associates, a multinational corporate consultancy firm.  They were working as a "strategic infrastructure partner", his words, for a startup called Shobar AI. He explained that they’re expanding their computing network into residential properties. He said my house, because of its isolation and size, was "an ideal candidate" for a residential data center. He said I’d be paid daily for the operation, should I choose to accept their offer.

Not monthly, not upon completion, but daily. That part sounded so good that it made me raise an eyebrow.

The other thing that raised my eyebrow was the name itself. **‘Shobar AI’** translates to **‘everyone’s AI’** in my native language. It sounded too on-the-nose, even for an AI startup.

*“The startup aims to deliver the power of AI to everyone on the planet, regardless of who they are and where they are from,”* said Rafiq.

I cringed at how corny that slogan was. My reaction was visible enough for Rafiq to notice.

*“It’s the company’s motto sir, not mine. I am just the one connecting you with them*.” His tone was defensive, but professional nonetheless.

He handed me a sophisticated business card with **‘Aldermore and Associates’** embossed on top. He requested me to give him a call when I was ready. He smiled warmly before taking his leave.

Before I agreed to anything, I did try to look into it.

It didn’t help that I’m kinda hopeless with technology. I just don't understand most of it. I never really understood computers, not the way people my age were supposed to by now. I still get USB and USG mixed up half the time, and I learnt very recently that motherboards are not the boards Ma used to cut vegetables on.

But I wasn’t going to stop that from assessing what I was dealing with.

I downloaded the Shobar AI app, opened an account, and clicked *‘I accept’* on the large window that appeared. I typed *"Hello"* on the chatbox. It took almost a full minute to respond. Not a good start, I thought.

When I asked it to list the last five American presidents, it gave me a list that included ‘George Floyd’ and ‘Donald Duck’. I laughed out loud, alone in my kitchen, at how bad it was.

But the app looked expensive. The interface was sleek, dark, and professional. Buggy or not, it looked like real money had gone into making it look like real money. And real money was exactly what Rafiq was offering me.

I didn't ask why a company with that much visual polish would ship a product that couldn't name the president correctly. I didn't ask a lot of things, to be honest. I just wanted to stop being the butt of all jokes at every family gathering. I wanted, for once, to be one of the cool guys- the ones that actually earned money instead of throwing it down the gutter…

The contract was four hundred and eighty pages long. I know because I counted the number on the last page, out of morbid curiosity, after I'd already signed it. I barely read the first twenty. Every page had a set of digits on it- a fee, a clause, a percentage. Convenience fee. Safety deposit. Site preparation fee. Liability waiver fee. House modification allowance.

By the fifth cluster of numbers, I stopped trying to understand what I was agreeing to. I was getting paid, and the numbers were meant for me. That was all that mattered. Halfway through, I stopped even pretending to read, and just started looking for the signature line. Rafiq sat across from me the whole time, smiling, nodding, telling me not to worry, that it was "standard boilerplate," that his firm had done this a hundred times before.

I signed everything. In fact, I gave them the whole house, almost. Specifically, they had access to every room except the master bedroom, the dining hall, and the kitchen. I figured that the more space I gave up, the more I'd get paid. I wasn't wrong about that part, at least.

The technicians arrived on a Tuesday in six white vans. They got to work immediately. I heard them talking about CPUs and GPUs. Both terms sounded equally foreign to me, like they were speaking a language I'd never bothered to learn. Every time they’d finish working on a particular room, they’d attempt to explain what they’d actually done. They described the installation using words like "thermal load" and "inference nodes" and "immersion cooling architecture," I just nodded along, the way I always did, pretending I understood more than I actually did.

I'd pictured a data center the way I'd seen them in documentaries: endless rows of humming white boxes, blinking lights, cold air blasting from vents. What they installed instead was kinda different. And for some reason, it unsettled me, in a way I couldn't articulate at first. The server racks were dense, black, and etched with patterns I initially mistook for decorative branding. When I looked closer, they weren't logos. They were symbols- intricate and layered, almost like calligraphy pressed directly into the metal casing.

I asked one of the technicians about it. He barely looked up from his tablet.

*"Third-party components. Chinese supplier. They like their designs fancy."*

He said it like he'd answered the question a thousand times before, in the exact same bored tone.

I nodded and let it go. But something nagged at me. Years ago, in one of my many failed attempts to reinvent myself, I'd tried to teach myself Arabic. I thought it might help me break into import business with the Gulf. I never got past the alphabet. But those symbols on the servers, whatever they were, didn't look Chinese to me. They looked closer to that alphabet I never learned. I told myself I was imagining things.

I told myself a lot of things back then.

At the center of it all was the tank. A massive, transparent immersion chamber, filled with a clear, faintly shimmering liquid, submerging what I was told were the core processing units. It was installed in what used to be my mother's sewing room. The technicians called it "the heart of the system." I thought that was a strange thing to call a server. I didn't think much else about it.

By the time the technicians finished, the house resembled the place I grew up in. Whole walls had been gutted and rebuilt around thick bundles of reinforced steel piping, running from floor to ceiling like a second skeleton. The sewing room was gone entirely, replaced by a sealed, soundproofed chamber housing the immersion tank, its glass walls faintly glowing under blue lighting that always stayed on. Server racks lined what used to be the living room and guest bedroom, arranged in tight black rows, their surfaces etched with those strange calligraphic symbols. Every hallway had been re-routed to accommodate the cabling and cooling infrastructure, narrowing them into tight corridors that curved around machinery instead of memories…

For the first month, everything was perfect. Money hit my account every single day, more than I'd made in a month of any failed business I'd ever run. I bought a new phone the week the servers went live. A new car two weeks after that. I stopped looking at price tags when I shopped for clothes, something I hadn't done since I was a teenager living off my mother's salary.

I called cousins I hadn't spoken to in years, not to reconnect, but to tell them about the car. I visited an old business partner who'd once told me, not unkindly, that maybe entrepreneurship wasn't for me. I made sure he saw the watch on my wrist before I left. I didn't do it because I was happy. I did it because for the first time in over a decade, I had something other people didn't. I wanted them to feel the way I'd felt every time one of my businesses collapsed and they'd quietly pitied me. Looking back, I understand now why nobody came looking for me later, when things went wrong. I'd spent that entire month making absolutely sure nobody would want to.

The only strange thing, during that whole golden month, was the lights. Every few days, for no more than a few seconds, every bulb in the house would flicker. Not dim, not surge, just flicker, all at once, like the whole house had blinked. I mentioned it to Rafiq once. He said it was “normal calibration” and there is nothing to worry about.

But I did start worrying by the end of the second month.

It started with several things, arriving on top of each other, until I couldn't tell where one ended and the next began.

The hum from the server room, which had been negligible so far, grew louder. Not overnight, but gradually, the way a headache slowly creeps up on you. Within two weeks it was audible from the hallway. Within three, I could hear it in the kitchen, through two closed doors.

Around the same time, the house started running hot. But it wasn’t the typical high temperatures caused by the weather. I live in a South Asian country, where summer heat is simply a fact of life, something you build your whole schedule around. This was different. The AC in my bedroom would run at full power, and the room would still feel like a sealed oven. The hallway outside the server room became so stifling I started avoiding it during the day. One evening, I reached for the doorknob leading into what used to be the sewing room, and yanked my hand back in pain. The metal was scalding, like it had been sitting in direct sunlight for hours. Except it was night, and the room behind it was supposed to be climate-controlled to near-freezing temperatures.

All of this was getting a bit too difficult to overlook.

I called Rafiq about it. I also called the emergency line that the technicians had given me. Both times I was told, very politely, that the monitoring systems showed no faults. Temperatures were within range. Everything was "nominal." I was, they implied, overreacting for no good reason. Rafiq even asked me if I was getting paranoid, because he thought I was imagining things.

I did give them the benefit of doubt. Maybe I was just stressed out. Maybe I was, indeed, blowing things out of proportion. And I might have been imagining some of those things, I told myself.

But I sure wasn't imagining the cracks.

I found them on a night I couldn't sleep because of the heat. I got up from bed to the sound of someone knocking. It didn’t sound like a knock on the door. Rather, it sounded like someone slamming their fist on something solid. It wasn’t forceful. It wasn’t gentle either.

I got out of the bedroom, hoping to find the source of the sound. It was loudest near the server room, the one housing the immersion tank. The sound stopped as soon as I opened the door. I was greeted by a gust of cold air, blue light illuminating a transparent tank. The tank was filled with liquid that was bubbling, with rows of servers submerged in it.

Something immediately caught my eye. The tank had a spiderweb of cracks, running across a single section. The cracks didn’t look too deep, and they didn’t seem to be random. They were shaped, unmistakably, like a hand. I could make out a palm, and five fingers, pressed outward from the inside, as if someone had shoved their hand against the glass with everything they had.

The "heart of the system" , as they called it, was freezing to the touch. I pressed an intact section of the tank to be sure. Then I touched the cracked section. It was hot. Like, boiling hot, scalding hot, simmering hot. Everywhere else on that tank, my hand went numb from the cold within seconds. But in that one section, my hand almost burned.

I called Rafiq again the next morning. This time, a technician did show up. He looked at the tank for less than two minutes. He told me that the cracks were "surface cosmetic damage, not structural".  He left before I could ask why cosmetic damage on a supercooled tank would be radiating heat…

That night, I heard growling for the first time. It was a low, guttural growl, coming from somewhere inside the newly installed pipes. I got up, heart pounding, and followed the growl down the hallway. The coolant pipes were thick, with reinforced steel, running along the baseboards where the walls had been opened up during installation.

They had always carried a faint blue glow, due to the liquid that flowed through them. That night, I swear to you, I saw a streak of orange bleeding through the blue. The orange hue was moving along the pipe, like something alive was traveling through it. The growl moved with it, room to room, fading as the color faded, growing louder as it brightened.

I told myself that I hadn't slept properly in weeks. I told myself that the heat was making me hallucinate.

I’ve never wanted, so badly, to be right about anything in my life.

Over the following days, everything got worse.

The hum became a physical presence I could feel through the floor. The heat spread from the server room into the hallway, into the guest rooms, inching toward the parts of the house I'd kept for myself. The orange in the pipes stopped being a streak and became a stain, spreading further along the metal every single day. It was visible now, even in daylight, even without the growling to announce it. And the cracks in the tank multiplied. What had been one outstretched hand became two. Both hands seemed to be slamming against the glass, fingers spread wide. To my absolute dread, some of the fingertips now punched clean through into small, ragged holes, like something was trying to claw its way out…

Three months in, the alarms went off for the first time. I was told that the alarms would fire automatically if the server temperature exceeded a certain limit. In that case, I was supposed to turn it off manually and inform the technicians immediately.

By the time I had located the manual switch and turned off the alarm, a team of technicians were already at my front door. They said there was a ‘remote monitoring system’ that notified the team, even before alarms in the house detected the temperature spike. The team dispersed throughout the house, moving with an urgency I hadn't seen before. They refused to answer my questions, and refused to make eye contact for longer than necessary. One of them, young and clearly new, started to tell me something, before an older colleague pulled him aside.

Rafiq arrived a little over an hour later. He walked straight past me, into the server room. He stood in front of the immersion tank for an unreasonably long time, just looking at the cracks. I watched him from the doorway. He didn't touch the glass. He didn't call anyone over.

*"I apologize for the inconvenience,"* he finally said, turning to face me. *"This shouldn't have happened. Not so fast, anyways."*

He reached into his jacket and produced an envelope, and inside it, was a cheque.

*"Compensation. For the unforeseen circumstances tonight."*

I glanced at the number. It was more than I'd made in the entire first month combined.

For one humiliating second, I felt that familiar warmth spread through my chest. The same feeling I got every time a new payout landed. Some part of me was already thinking about what I'd do with that number.

But I didn't take it.

*"I don't want the money,"* I said. *"I want you to tell me what's happening in my house."*

Rafiq's expression didn't change. He lowered the cheque slightly.

*"What do you mean, sir?"*

*"You know what I mean."* My voice was shaking more than I wanted it to. "*The cracks. The heat. The sounds in the pipes. I've seen orange, Rafiq. I’ve seen it moving, inside the walls. And I've also heard growling. Your technicians won't even look at me when I ask about it."*

He was quiet for a moment. Then he sighed, the way you'd sigh at a child who won't stop insisting the closet monster is real.

*"With all due respect, sir,"* he said gently, *“Alcohol does play tricks on your eyes.”*

I opened my mouth to argue, and then closed it again, because he wasn't wrong. I had been drinking. More than I liked to admit, more nights than not, ever since the payouts started.

Rafiq continued, his tone almost kind.

*"The ears too, sometimes. Stress, isolation, a drink too many… the mind fills in gaps with things that aren't there."*

I didn't push back. I wanted to. I had a dozen things to say, and none of them came out,

He set the cheque down on the counter beside me, since I still hadn't taken it, and gave a short nod to the technicians filing out behind him.

*"Get some rest,"* he said. *"We'll be in touch if anything changes."*

And then they were gone, taillights disappearing down the long dirt driveway, leaving me alone, with a cheque I never asked for, and an explanation I never received…

That was five nights before the worst night of my life.

I got home past midnight, a little drunk, after a reunion with an old contact who wanted to hear about my "data center business." I unlocked the front door and stumbled into the house.

I stood there a moment, keys in hand, realizing that something was terribly wrong.

It started with the pipes. Nearly every visible inch of coolant line in the house had turned orange. It was the deep, pulsing orange I'd been watching for weeks. But this time, it wasn't a stain moving through the metal. It was the metal itself, glowing from within, like the pipes had become veins carrying something molten. The hum from the server room, which had grown into a constant presence in the walls, had stopped.

The heat that followed was unlike anything I'd felt in that house before. It was suffocating, pressing against my skin the second I stepped into the hallway, thick enough that I found myself gasping for air.

The lights started glitching. Not the gentle flicker from the good month, but violent, rapid bursts, on and off, in intervals of less than a minute, like watching my own house through a broken film reel.

And then I heard the growling again. Except this time, it wasn't coming from the pipes.

It was coming from the walls themselves. It was everywhere, low and guttural, seeming to breathe in and out, like the house itself had grown lungs.

I was in the kitchen when the stove ignited on its own. All four burners roared to life simultaneously, flames leaping far higher than any gas stove should produce. I stumbled backwards, my heart slamming against my ribs.

As I got closer, a gust of heated air rushed past me, so hot that it felt like standing in front of an open furnace. And underneath it, unmistakably, I felt something else. It was the distinct sensation of being watched. Not from the front. From behind. Across the kitchen counter, exactly where I couldn't see without turning around.

I grabbed the nearest knife off the counter, my hand shaking so badly that I nearly dropped it. I spun around.

There was nothing there.

Nothing I could see, anyway. But the feeling didn't leave. If anything, it got stronger, closer, like whatever had been standing across that counter had simply taken one step forward.

*“Who’s there?”* I shouted.

The growling stopped.

*“Who the fuck is there? Show yourself!”*

For one full second, the entire house was silent, in a way that felt worse than any sound it had made all night.

Then I heard the laugh.

I need you to understand that I’ve heard people laugh cruelly before. I’ve been the target of cruel laughter, more times than I care to count. From relatives, from business partners, from people who watched me fail and couldn't hide how satisfying they found it. None of that prepared me for this. It wasn't a human laugh dressed up to sound frightening. It was something that had learned what a laugh was supposed to sound like, and reproduced it without any of the humanity. And it reeked, in a way I felt more than heard, of pure malice.

Then every pipe in the house exploded, all at once.

I don't have a better way to describe it. The walls themselves seemed to convulse, a chain of muffled cracks tearing through every hallway simultaneously. From the direction of the server room came a single, deafening crack, louder than all the rest combined. I heard glass shattering. I heard a wave of liquid hit the floor, with enough force to rattle the walls. Within seconds, smoke was pouring from beneath the sewing room door.

I ran.

I don't remember deciding to run. I remember being in the kitchen, and then I remember being on the front lawn, lungs burning. I remember watching the orange light, but it was different this time. It was real fire now; not the strange glow in the pipes, but actual, roaring flame. I saw them climb up through the windows of my mother's house, engulfing it in the process.

And then I saw **it**, emerging from the flames…

It was a sharp humanoid figure, tearing through the collapsing wall where the server room used to be. The figure was tall, easily seven feet, black as pitch. It was humanoid in the way a shadow can look humanoid, before your mind fills in details that aren't really there. Except this one had details. Veins ran across its entire body. Veins that looked scorched and cracked like old volcanic rock. And through every single one of those veins, pulsed the same orange light that had crawled through my coolant pipes for weeks.

It staggered out of the burning house, one heavy step at a time, grunting with each footfall. It didn't look at me. Instead, it looked straight up, at the night sky. Then it let out a growl. It was a growl so loud that I felt it in my sternum.

And with that single growl… it vanished. It never ran away. It never walked away. It was just gone, the way a candle flame disappears when you pinch it out, leaving nothing behind. Nothing but the smell of smoke, and something underneath the smoke, something I still can't identify. It was something that smelled metallic and horribly wrong…

I don’t remember when the fire trucks arrived. I don't remember the ambulance ride. I don't remember most of the week that followed. I remember burns on my arms, that the doctors said were consistent with "extreme, but brief, thermal exposure."

The one thing I remember, with painful clarity, is Rafiq standing at the foot of my hospital bed the next morning.

*"You're going to be alright,"* he said, folding his hands in front of him, like he was delivering a eulogy.

*"This was unfortunate. An electrical fault, we believe. Entirely unrelated to the data center."*

I was still too dazed to string a full sentence together, my throat raw, my arms wrapped in gauze. But I found the strength to utter a single word-

*"Liar."*

His smile didn't waver. If anything, it grew warmer, which somehow made it worse. He pulled the chair beside my bed closer and sat down.

*"I understand you're upset,"* he said. *"But you're not in a position to call it anything else. Not publicly, at least."*

I didn't understand what he meant, not right away. He explained it to me slowly, the way you'd explain something to a child. Buried on page three hundred and something, of the contract I'd barely skimmed months ago, was a clause. A non-disclosure agreement, to be precise, triggered automatically in the event of "unforeseen incidents." I had signed away my right to discuss the installation with the press, with authorities, with any third party at all. In exchange, a "safety and discretion allowance" had already been deposited into my account.

*"There's something else,"* he added, and for the first time his tone shifted, just slightly, into something colder underneath the politeness.

*"Since the fire has been officially ruled unrelated to our equipment, the damage to Shobar AI's infrastructure falls under your liability. The servers, the tank, the installation itself. These are considerable losses, structurally speaking."*

I stared at him, waiting for the part where he told me how much I owed. It never came.

*"I'll speak to the company,"* he said instead, standing, folding the sleeves of his shirt exactly twice.

*"I'm sure I can convince them not to pursue that route. Given the circumstances."*

He said it kindly. He said it like a favor. But I understood, even through the haze of painkillers and disbelief, exactly what he meant, and exactly what would happen to me if I ever spoke a word of this to anyone who mattered.

He left without waiting for a response. I didn't have one to give him anyway…

Shobar AI's website still exists. It is operating under a new name now, a name that I cannot reveal thanks to my circumstances.

I moved. Of course I moved, there was no house left to move back into even if I'd wanted to. I found a small flat in a different city, hundreds of kilometers from the farmhouse, from the ashes, from everything.

I promised myself a fresh start. I still had enough money in my account to live comfortably for years, maybe longer if I was careful. I promised to forget everything that happened inside that farmhouse. I promised to convince myself it was nothing more than a bad dream, one I'd eventually stop having.

I also promised myself I'd never tell this story to anyone.

But there's a reason I'm breaking that promise tonight.

I still hear the growl.

Not every night. Not even every week, at first. But it's there, low and patient, coming from somewhere in the room with me. There are no pipes here. No tanks, no servers, no data center humming behind the drywall. There is nothing mechanical in this apartment that could explain it.

Two nights ago, I heard the laugh again. The same one from the kitchen. The same one that came right before every pipe in my mother's house exploded.

I don't know what followed me out of that fire. I don't know if it ever really left at all. I don’t know if the fire was never the ending I thought it was, just the moment it stopped hiding.

If anyone reading this knows anything about Aldermore & Associates, about the one called Rafiq, about Shobar AI, about what those symbols on the server casings actually meant, please reach out. I'm not looking for validation anymore. I'm not even looking for someone to believe me, though I'd take that too.

I'm looking for anyone who can tell me how to make it stop following me.

Please tell me I can make it stop.

Please help me make it stop.

Please help me…

Please.
