I write this in the tenth year after my deliverance, and I write it against my judgment, for I am a man of science and I know what the world does with testimony it cannot file. My trade, in those years, was the orderly reduction of the sea's harvest to card and jar and binomial at a marine institute whose name I withhold; and I was accounted good at it, which is to say that nothing the nets brought up had ever declined to be what I named it. In the winter of which I write, the freighters began to come home holed beneath the waterline — not stove by ice nor gnawed by any teredo, but torn cleanly, at lengths exceeding that of a door, by something which the underwriters, in the simplicity of frightened money, required to be an animal, since an animal might be hunted and priced. My professor pronounced it some deep-water form grown enormous, and a survey vessel was fitted to find it before it found a third hull. I packed my jars and presses, and a notebook in which I had already ruled a page for the creature beneath the three headings I had never needed to exceed. I did not know that the page would be the voyage's first casualty, and the least of its losses.

We found it on the second night — or, as I have since come to believe, it ceased to avoid us. It ran ahead of the ship lit from within, not as a lamp is lit but as a furnace is, with an interior glow that no phosphorescence of my acquaintance has ever counterfeited; and our harpooner, a Canadian of great mirth and greater arm, put his iron into it for the joy of the throw. The harpoon rang. I have heard bells with less certainty in them. Then the sea rose and took the professor, the harpooner, and myself off the deck as cups are taken off a tray, and after an interval of drowning, we were drawn up, half-dead, upon a back of riveted steel, beneath which boilers murmured and a crew went about its morning with the dreadful ordinariness of men in a kitchen. A hatch opened. No one expressed surprise at our arrival. It frightened me then more than the water had, and I understand it now: surprise would have meant that we were an accident. We were not an accident.

There exists — I choose the tense deliberately — a boat that travels beneath the sea, which no navy lists and no shipyard will admit to having built; I lived in her ten months and never saw a name upon her plate or bell. Her captain kept a saloon fitted as museum and library in one: twelve thousand volumes, cases of shells laid out like good silver, instruments burnished to a surgical cleanliness; and in the hull's side a great window, shuttered in panels, which opened upon water lit by the vessel's own lamps. Beyond the reach of that light the darkness continued in the manner it had continued before there were eyes, and it did not arrange itself for ours. Once, in the first days, something crossed the far verge of the illumination without entering it, unhurried, and kept our pace for a while, and never came closer; I was grateful for its manners, and I have never believed that they were kindness. The professor, with a scholar's directness, asked the captain his name. He smiled — the first time I saw the expression reach him — and answered: "Nobody." His country, he said, was the sea. I believed him as one believes a man who points out where the floor is weak: he was warning us, not performing. That night I attempted his entry in my best hand. I got no further than the ruling of the lines.

We walked upon the bottom in weighted boots and copper helmets, our air descending to us in hoses, through a forest that grew as no forest on land grows — straight upward toward the light, wasting nothing sideways: sponge and stone-coral reared in green and perpetual weather. I named what I knew beneath my breath, as a man counts change, and the names came in their accustomed order and comforted me, until a spider crab unfolded itself out of the green upon legs taller than the professor and departed at a pace so deliberate that it resembled judgment. I knew the creature. It was Macrocheira kaempferi, whose kind haunts the deep slopes off Japan, and whose span from claw to claw exceeds the height of two men; the name fitted, the name was correct, and the correctness was the worst of it. The forest was not hostile. I set this down as the most frightening sentence I know how to write. It simply continued, and we were three warm objects passing through it on the sufferance of our air.

Off Ceylon we lay over the pearl banks of Mannar, where men dive as their fathers did, in a manner I set down exactly. A diver stood with one foot upon a flat granite stone; two ropes ran from him upward into the hands of his mate in the boat — one for the stone, one for the net at his waist — and before the fleet sailed, a hereditary charmer of that coast had bound the sharks for the day only, at a twentieth of the take, the charm to dissolve at nightfall. Down the man went, standing upon his own falling: sixty seconds on the bottom among the oysters, and at his tug the ropes brought him up, sometimes with a thread of his own blood at his mouth, which no one remarked. Then, one morning, a shark came for him as he gathered; and before my fear had finished forming, the captain was in the water with a knife, and it was done at arm's length in a clouding of sand and blood, and the diver came up living on the rope his mate had never let go. The captain placed in his hands a pouch of pearls beyond a lifetime of such mornings, and made no speech, and did not wait for thanks. The man's wage for those mornings was a share of unopened shells, the Crown taking two parts in three; loose pearls were a currency in which he had never once been paid. I wrote his entry entire in one line. It is the only finished entry of the voyage.

One night the captain took us down to show the professor a city. It had lain beneath the sea so long that the water had ceased to treat it as news: streets entire, columns prostrate like felled trees, a temple overturned, and above the ruins a volcano burning red and unsteady, by whose light we walked. The steps were wrong for my legs; I knew it in my knees before my mind consented to know it, as a man knows an unfamiliar chair by sitting — the place had been measured for a people whose measure I could not take. I searched, being what I am, for the dead. A place of that antiquity should have kept its dead somewhere. There were no bones. In the forest the names had come in their order and comforted me; here they came and fastened on nothing. The captain walked those streets as a man walks in a house he formerly inhabited — not studying, visiting; he knew the turns without looking.

Going north — the city still in my hands, a thing I could not set down — the sea closed over us and laid the ice upon our backs, and for two days the whole economy of our lives was air. The captain rendered the figure without cushion, as a grocer renders a weight. We chopped in turns, crew and guests together, the lamps burning the very element we were husbanding; I counted breaths, counting being the liturgy of my hands when they are afraid, and near the end I abandoned the count deliberately, that I might not know. The professor's air failed first; the harpooner and I gave him of ours and did not tell him — there were no speeches; a valve, a held breath, a friend not told. We got out. By what agency I cannot say, and after ten years I have ceased to require the saying before the fact. When the professor thanked him, the captain answered only that the sea had finished with us for the moment. For the moment. He chose his words as other men choose their footing upon ice.

We were upon the surface one night, taking air, when they came up about us grey as wet stone — I write they, for there is no honest singular. They were in no haste, and the absence of haste is the portion of that night which has kept its cold longest: we were weather to them, a brief phenomenon of the surface. The crew fought with axes. A sailor whose name I had never asked — the not-asking shames me yet — was taken from the rail before my eyes, lifted, held a moment in the air, silent, and gone. No man shouted. Eleven men had axes in their hands, and the only sound was the water settling back into the shape out of which he had been lifted. When it ended I saw the captain at the rail with his face in the ruin of his hands. I wrote nothing that night. Some days a notebook is an obscenity.

A warship found us not long after. Whose she was he never said, and by then I had stopped requiring to be told. He did not flee her. He went about her once, patient as weather about a hill, and then through her, beneath the waterline. She settled by the stern with her guns loaded and her men in the water, and the captain stood at the great window and watched it entire, and had the panels kept open until the water above her held nothing but wreckage. Then he ordered dinner, in the voice he used for soundings. I ate at that table, and the food was warm, and it was good, and I ate. That night I opened my notebook to his page and shut it again upon the ruled lines.

The harpooner had been contriving our escape as other men breathe, steadily and without discussion. Off Norway there is a water that turns in a circle and renders back nothing that it takes; the boat strayed to its margin with the three of us half-gone in the small boat, and the turning took us. I retain a horizon standing at an angle no horizon is permitted, a noise as of the world's hinge, my professor senseless, and the harpooner shouting what I then took for prayer and have since decided was seamanship. The captain could have had us back; I believe that as I believe few things in this account. I saw him at the rail as the water took us. He gave no order that I could discern, or he gave the other one. Then there was a fisherman's hut, and soup, and a fire, and outside the door the sea lying flat as a table. The fishermen asked what we had struck. I answered: "Something." It is the answer I have given ever since. The harpooner confirmed it, went home, and died an old man without once amending it.

People ask why I publish this stripped of every checkable particular, and the answer is that I checked first: there is nothing in these pages that can find him — no port, no date that holds, no name save the one he gave us, which is none. He suffered us to go when recalling us would have been the easiest order of his year; the least I can do is let him stay gone. I still sort specimens at the same bench. Everything that comes up in the nets receives its card, in my ordinary hand. Everything except one. In the drawer of that bench there lies a label, written for no jar, bearing nothing at all. Some mornings, before the day begins to belong to everyone, I take it out and look at it, and put it back. It is blank.
