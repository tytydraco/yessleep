I've gathered together the accounts of myself (Clayton), my wife, Rachael, and our son, Dean, as we attempt to piece together what happened to us as a family.

What follows are our recollections of those events, told from each of our perspectives.

**Clayton Hymes' Account:**

The television flickered to life. Static hissed across the screen like a distorted downpour. Coloured lines split the image apart before vanishing again. Somewhere inside the VCR, a mechanism clicked, and the screen went black.

White splotches, declaring the age of the film, flashed across the empty space. They jumped up and down, as though the picture itself were trying to leap out of the television frame.

Eventually, the reel settled. What appeared was a child's interpretation of a hellish landscape. Crimson mountains rose against a blood-red sky, while a frozen fire burned beneath them. Stalagmites hung from the ceiling like the fangs of some hungry beast.  
Letters slowly materialised in the centre of the artwork.

**The Devil Made Me Do It.**

An orchestra swept into a melody reminiscent of those old Looney Tunes introductions. The sort you'd hear from a time long since passed.

The lettering was peculiar. A curved horn protruded from the left-hand side of the first T, with another curling out from the right side of the final T.  
The title screen eventually faded to black. The orchestra reached a crescendo, and every instrument played a single note in unison before falling silent.

For a moment, the only thing visible was the faint electricity dancing behind the screen. It seemed to bounce and flare within the darkness. A strange limbo of light caught between wanting to illuminate and wishing to remain hidden.

Then, colour began creeping back into the picture.  
A demonic goat hand puppet appeared.  
Blood-red horns jutted from either side of its head, curling backwards into spirals. Black hair framed a pair of yellow eyes that stared directly into the camera. They never blinked. Their pupils were thin, like those of a venomous snake.  
Despite its rather sinister appearance, the puppet had a fuzzy exterior that made it look almost cuddly. Like something a child might take to bed with them.

"Greetings, children and parents," it hissed.  
The puppet held onto the S sounds at the beginning and end of the sentence, its forked tongue darting out and retreating with a blink-and-you'll-miss-it motion.

"It's me. Your unconventional friend, Old Nick. Although I must say, I look quite exquisite for my age. Today, I wish to tell you a story. Could you grant me that?"

Old Nick leaned towards the camera, one ear perked up as though waiting for a response.

The image briefly froze. Then continued.

"You know, a soul came to me the other day. Speaking about how he did something he never would've done. Shall we set the stage?"

The scenery began to pull itself apart around the puppet. The mountain range dropped out of view, replaced by a hand-drawn road with a city backdrop. The orchestra picked up again, accompanied by a chorus of birds singing their little songs of warning.  
It was almost as though the devil himself were willing an entirely new world into existence.

A sickly-looking postman floated into view.  
His bulbous purple nose drooped from his face. Like Old Nick, his skin had a woolly texture, and he wore traditional postman attire with a one-strap bag slung over his right shoulder.  
His entire posture was miserable. His head hung low, his shoulders slumped, and he carried himself as though the weight of the world had been placed squarely upon him.

"Why so green? You look like death itself," Old Nick hissed.His dead eyes seemed to be staring at something other than the ghostly figure.

"I feel sick," the postman coughed, his body flailing like a rag doll being thrown across a room.

"Now, why is that? Have you not gone to see a doctor?"

"No. I don't think a doctor would be able to help me."

"And why is that? Modern medicine fails mankind once again, hmm?"

"Well, I'm deader than a doorknob, for starters. All because I mistakenly poisoned myself."

"What?! Poisoned?!"

Old Nick's mouth hung open, and his entire body came to a sudden stop.

"Yes, you heard me right, mister. I drank from the wrong cup. It was meant for my wife."

"Now, why would you go ahead and do that to someone you ought to love?!"

"Because I heard you whispering to me. Telling me to DO IT."

"Why would you even think of such a thing?!"

"She was always nagging me about something. The laundry..."The postman hurled a bundled-up pile of laundry at Old Nick. Socks and dirty underwear scattered everywhere.

"...The dishes..." He reached into his bag and pulled out a single white plate. With a flick of his wrist, he tossed it through the air like a frisbee.

Old Nick's head followed its movement until it disappeared above the screen with a loud crash. He ducked as fragments of the plate rained down, bouncing off his head in an exaggerated cartoon fashion.

"...And that I never sweep the house."

The postman looked up, his jaw hanging open.

"She always said it was like the guests waltzed through the living room and had their feet seasoned."

"But you don't possess any feet."

"And neither did she!" he snapped. "Or any guests, for that matter! It was just piling up and up. And I just... snapped."

"But why are you in hell and not her?"

"Because I mixed up our drinks. The cups, they both looked the same."

"Well, then you're an idiot. Lived as one, and now you're as dead as one."

"Well, I listened to you! You made me do it."

"I don't recall telling you to do that!"

"Yes, you did. Now I'm dead, and she is probably with a different man, smooching him in her new car."

"Collected your insurance, I would imagine. Tsk, tsk. Reality can be a terrible thing."

"Boy, would you know it."

The postman slumped over. A few letters slipped out of his slightly open bag and floated towards the ground.

"Well, that is a woeful tale. But my hands are clean. I didn't tell ya to poison your wife. Nor did I make you drink the poison yourself like a nimrod."

"I heard you!"

The postman suddenly stiffened, pointing his head towards Old Nick like an accusing finger.

"You were whispering. It was faint, but also clear. Only for me to hear. You told me to kill her. And you told me to do it with poison!"

He reached into his bag and pulled out a can with a skull emblazoned on its side. He shook it like an aerosol can.

"Why, it seems you got what you deserved."

"And why did I deserve this? I didn't do it...

He leaned towards the devil until their eyes were practically touching.

"...YOU did! You did! You did! You did! YOU DID!"

His voice grew louder with every repetition

Old Nick raised a hoof-like hand, attempting to push the postman away.

"Have you ever heard of personal space, man?! The finger you're pointing at me has three more pointing back at you. You chose this. The evil you're looking for is staring back at you in the mirror—"

Old Nick suddenly stiffened.

His mouth remained open, but the final word never came.

The postman pulled away slightly.

The orchestra continued playing.

For a few seconds, neither puppet moved. Old Nick's smile remained fixed in place. His yellow eyes stared straight ahead, unblinking.

"Mirror?" the postman asked, almost as though reminding him of his next line.

There was no answer.

The music seemed to crawl through the speakers, gradually transforming into a strange gargling sound.

I noticed a faint tremor running through the arm connected to Old Nick. It was so slight that I initially thought I had imagined it.

"Nick?" the postman asked.

His voice had changed. There was something uncertain about it now. A distant thud echoed from somewhere beyond the set. Old Nick's eyes suddenly shifted. They moved independently of his head, focusing directly on the postman.  
A muffled groan filled the room. It sounded as though someone were trying to speak with their head submerged underwater.  
The cheerful orchestra of chirping birds continued in the background, although it was becoming increasingly difficult to hear over the noise.

Old Nick's mouth began forming words. Nothing came out. The groaning grew louder, becoming more frequent. The postman leaned closer.

"What?"

Beneath the demon, the hand began shaking violently. The fabric surrounding it bunched together and twisted.

"Are you alright?"

Something about the postman's voice caught my attention

The exaggerated, high-pitched cartoon voice was slowly disappearing. Beneath it, I could hear a deeper voice making an appearance.

"What did you say?" the postman asked.

The groaning abruptly transformed into a scream.  
A woman burst into view, clutching a sharp knife. She lunged at the man operating the postman puppet and began stabbing him repeatedly.  
Blood sprayed across the set like squid ink. A few droplets struck the camera lens and slowly trickled downwards.  
I could hear the man's screams and desperate pleas from somewhere off-screen. With every thrust of the knife, they grew weaker. The screaming became incoherent, then dwindled to a whisper.  
Eventually, all I could hear was a horrible bubbling sound.

Then nothing.

The picture abruptly cut away.  
A new image appeared, displaying the words:

**TECHNICAL DIFFICULTIES. STAY TUNED.**

A loud, continuous beep filled the room.

I sat there, staring at the screen.

The VCR swallowed the rest of the tape with one final click.

The television went black.

For a moment, all I could see was my own reflection staring back at me.

To the average person, the disturbing imagery contained in the footage would have been enough to turn their stomachs to knots. But for me, it had become easily digestible. I'd consumed the media so much, frame by frame, that the taste of its bloodied contents had transformed into a morbid comfort. Similar to how a historic tragedy would eventually be rinsed down to a nursery rhyme to be sung to an ignorant child.

That inescapable fact scared me. Time had carved a gap between me and the event so vast that the haunting had become a fascination.

I clutched the VHS in my hand, feeling its outer corners with the tips of my fingers.  
My gaze remained trapped by the text written in permanent Sharpie on the front label.

'S4 Ep21 Copy'.

I placed the tape next to an opened notepad, with rough writings scattered throughout the page. The pulsating static from the television illuminated the words.

*Why did she do it?*

I stared at the question, my pen lingering at the question mark. I rubbed my eyes, collected my notebook and pushed myself across the room in my desk chair, spinning halfway before slowing myself down to meet the back wall.

With my finger, I found the light switch. The room came into view. A dingy space with moist, carpeted floors that carried the scent of rotted wood. Dust particles swarmed as I pushed through them on my way across the weathered room. My eyes traced the tattered wallpaper, with yellowed pages taped across it. Like a detective trying to draw connections using photographs as a physical mind map.

Drawn upon these pages were old storyboards.  
A wave of nostalgia hit my chest upon seeing the artist's handiwork. I recalled seeing them being drawn in person. From sketch to the final iteration, and yet, devastatingly, they'd never be translated to film, remaining a lost media of their own kind.

As I continued scanning the papers, I came across the colourful character designs of the puppets. An array of anthropomorphised animals to funny-looking human beings. A diverse collection of designs to stimulate the imaginations of children.  
One design, however, distinguished itself from the crowd of cartoony mascots.

Old Nick. The demon goat. The starring character of *The Devil Made Me Do It.*

His piercing yellow eyes had a habit of burrowing into me whenever I washed my hair in the shower. But when I'd twist around to catch sight of him, I'd discover it was all a conjuring of my mind.  
Or had he simply disappeared the moment I decided to look?

Maybe I too was beginning to go mad.

A funny memory suddenly dawned on me, an attempt to soothe my paranoia. Separate from the tragedy, but still rather controversial.  
Our studio had, at one point, received over 68,000 signed petitions from enraged parents calling for the cancellation of the show. The majority of them Christian.

Old Nick had been the trigger for the outrage. His design, his voice. Just everything about him freaked the parents out. The very thought warranted a chuckle from me.

Their main concern was that the show attempted to convert children to Satanism.

Preachers in churches across various states of America spouted about how the show comprised of Illuminati imagery, circling around hand signs caught in a single frame that were supposedly calling cards to those within the cult.

The most ridiculous claim I remembered was when preachers played certain select scenes from the show backwards. They translated the garbled phrases for the confused public, such as 'Hail the Lord of Flies!' and 'Beelzebub has got a plan for you', to name a few.

Those churchgoers seated in the pews quickly spread the word around like a gasoline fire. The ability for people to conjure something out of nothing still baffled me.

While adults worried for their kids, however, the show received much different feedback from the youth of America. You see, children enjoy being frightened, provided they know they're safe.  
The design of Old Nick, for instance, tittered between being scary, but not too scary that it would be unbearable. I felt, while writing the show, that I had something to say. Every decision you make is made by you, and can't be blamed on an external party. In this case, the devil.  
The children clearly got the message, but obviously the parents had not.

The controversy, however, would only evolve into something uncontainable when the murder on set occurred.

Many of those paranoid parents now felt justified in their fear, and Sarah Slater was hailed as someone affected by the devil on set. The media tore the show apart in pursuit of answers.  
And me, the man who ran the show, bore the brunt of it.

At least, that was what I told myself every day since.  
Those who wanted the show cancelled finally had their wish granted.

I continued gliding across the room towards my desk. Placed at the centre of its surface was my laptop, which I promptly opened, allowing the ghoulish blue light to brighten my face.  
Immediately, I pulled up Sarah's case file within my favourites tab.

I skimmed through it rabidly. Images rose on the page, causing me to lift my finger from the centre mouse wheel.

A picture taken at a staff party. The cast, crew and even I were all linked together, arms draped around each other's shoulders. We wore colourful pointed hats with miscellaneous shapes and patterns.  
Sarah, petite in frame, her scalp covered in auburn hair and wide-brimmed glasses reflecting the camera flash, could be seen towards the far-left corner. She stood out from the crowd of monotonous tailored suits and ties with her vibrant pink shirt, dappled with flowers.

I scrolled further down, discovering another image of Sarah holding a fluffy white rabbit pressed against her cheek. Her head tilted towards it in a loving gesture.

I recalled this photograph being pasted upon the front page of every newspaper at the time, with everyone all thinking the same thing.

*How does someone like this become a murderer?*

I leaned closer, placing two fingers upon the ghostly blue screen, tracing the edge of Sarah's cheek. I continued staring into her eyes, feeling as if she could see me.

My heart rate increased, and my eyes darted from side to side to notice if anyone was observing me.

Quickly, as if I would soon be discovered, I moved my hand to the outer side of the laptop screen to close it, then drew my hand back to cover my mouth.

"Clayton! Can you come down here!"

My wife's muffled call travelled through the old wooden door to meet my ear, breaking me out of my haze.

"I'll be down soon! Just finishing up in here!" I shouted back in a tone that could be heard, but not to the point where it came off as confrontational.

"I need you now."

"Alright. I'm coming.

I shut my notebook on the table. *The Devil Made Me Do It Draft Book.* The words were handwritten on the front cover.

Moving at a quicksilver pace, I scampered down the creaking birch steps and turned to walk through the arched threshold leading into the kitchen area of the house.

Our small black-and-white Boston terrier, Lucy, lay on her cushioned bed on the floor. As I entered, her ears perked upright. She leapt from her bed and waltzed gleefully towards me, her rear swaying from side to side.

I bent over to scratch her back, a joyous grunt escaping me as her hind leg began kicking at the air, her nails tapping the floor in an almost rhythmic pattern.

Rachael approached me with an envelope, drawing my gaze before pressing it against my chest.

"What's this?" I asked, standing up.

Lucy whined for further attention, but I ignored her.

"Open it and find out."

Her voice carried a hint of annoyance.  
It was addressed to me, with my postal address and today’s date, 06/27/2010, stamped on the front. The top had already been torn off.  
I rotated it over, allowing the letter to slip into my hand.

'Dear Mr. Hymes.

We regret to inform you that your mortgage account is currently in arrears due to missed payments.  
Unless an outstanding balance of $11,956.00 is paid within 30 days of the date of this letter, we may commence legal proceedings to recover the debt. This may include placing your property up for public auction in accordance with the terms of your mortgage agreement and applicable law.

If you've already made payment, please disregard this notice. If you're experiencing financial hardship, we encourage you to contact our Mortgage Services Department as soon as possible to discuss the options available to you.

We appreciate your prompt attention to this matter,

Yours sincerely,

Mortgage Collections Department

Memory Bank LTD.'

I read through the letter, every word raising my heartbeat. Heat generated behind my cheeks.

"One month! We've got only one month! Those bastards. They'd be happy to assist us onto the streets with our bags, wouldn't they?"

"How many do you think we'll get before they stop asking politely?"

"I'll handle it."

"When? You've been saying that for months."

"I know."

"No." She shook her head. "You don't."

She snatched the letter out of my hand, placing it atop the kitchen island layered with grease.

"I'm this close to finding the answer," I said, pinching my fingers together, allowing a small, ant-sized gap between them.

"You said that last year."

"This is different."

"How so?" she asked, with folded arms and a finger tapping against her arm.

"I've started noticing the patterns."

"In what?"

"Everything."

"Oh, come on." She threw her hands up. "Give it a rest already, Clayton."

"Give it a rest?" I nervously chuckled, wiping the cold sweat developing on my forehead. "I'll give it a rest when this damned book is done."

"And what will that bring? You can't keep this up. Look at what it's doing."

I pulled back, resting my body against the kitchen counter, turning my attention away from my wife.

Lucy rubbed her body against my leg.

"You've barely slept a wink in what... five days. You're all cooped up in your cramped room, typing away."

I paused to exhale deeply, my whole body heaving from the sigh.

"What day is it today?"

I frowned.

"What?"

"What day is it?"

"Friday?"

She nodded. "And what else?"

"Look, I really don't have time for these riddles—"

"Dean's birthday. In case you forgot."

I looked towards the hallway, bent my head down and scrunched up my face.

"You should be up in his room wishing him."

"Why're you on my ass? You know how much money we could get if I could present to the public the reason why Sarah did it. Why she killed Patrick."

"Please, don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't pretend this is about the money."

I didn't answer.

A car rolled past outside.

Rachael leaned in, angling herself until she was directly in my view.

"If someone approached you with a bag of a million dollars and asked you to stop looking... would you?"

I didn't answer.

A stone replaced the Adam's apple in my throat.

"Look at me when I'm talking."

I couldn't. Instead, I focused on Lucy, scratching absentmindedly behind her ears.

Rachael let out a humourless laugh.

"I knew it."

I still didn't answer.

"How many times have I watched you drive to that prison to go and talk to her? How many times has she given you the same answer?" She paused. The room still bellowed from our ongoing argument. "It was the hand puppet."

"Look. I know how it sounds. Trust me. I know."

"How it sounds? She's crazy, Clayton." She jabbed her fingers into the temples of her head. "Why do you think she is in the nut house? It can't be because all the lights work up there."

"There has to be a reason. There's always a reason why."

"Listen to us. We're fighting over a woman who believes some creepy hand puppet told her to kill that poor man. And that she listened to it."

"She didn't just listen. She said it compelled her. Like it possessed her and made her do it!"

"And you see. That's why she's been locked away from the rest of us."

I twisted in place, slamming my fists down on the kitchen counter, causing it to reverberate beneath my hands.

"Goddammit. I know that!"

My hands convulsed from the impact.

Lucy began barking hysterically, feeling the tension constricting the air in the room.

"Mom. Dad."

A low voice travelled from the hallway, meeting the two of us. Lucy continued to bark, each one erupting through the house and travelling down every corridor.  
Rachael and I slowly rotated our heads towards the direction the voice had emanated from.  
Our son, Dean, stood beneath the arched entrance. Nine years old to the day.  
A mask of swallowed fear neutralised his pale face. With him, he carried a backpack almost as big as he was, swung over one shoulder.  
A deep scar beginning from his lip and running towards his left eye was illuminated by the morning light filtering through the windows.

I immediately bent down to soothe Lucy, stroking her back until her anxious barks disappeared.

"Oh, honey. Honey, honey, honey."

Rachael repeated the words as she walked towards him, kneeling to kiss him on the forehead.

"Why're you guys always fighting?" Dean asked, his voice shaken.

I shamefully lowered my head to avoid having my face seen, locking my vision on Lucy's attentive face, her wet tongue sticking out as she panted away.

"Oh, honey. We weren't fighting. We were just—"  
Rachael cut herself off, her eyes moving to the left as she searched for a reply. "...having an adult chat. I'm sorry you had to hear that."

"It's okay."

He said it with drooped shoulders and a saddened, limp head. Rachael ran a hand through our son's dirty-blond hair.

"You want breakfast?"

"Not really. I don't feel hungry right now."

"Alright." She moved a strand of hair behind her ear. "Not a problem. Let's get you to the car."

She gently ushered Dean towards the front door, turning her head to look at me and gesturing to our son with her eyes.

I pushed myself away from the counter, moving towards my wife and son.

"Hey, birthday boy!" I raised my voice. "Excited about your party tomorrow?"

Rachael cracked a slight grin.

"I guess."

Dean tilted his head downwards.

"You know. Mark and Jason will be there."

"I dunno. Mark doesn't seem to like me anymore.

And I don't see Jason a whole lot either."

"Oh. Well. Why do you think that?"

My face tightened with concern.

"Well. Gabriella said I look like a freak with this scar on my face. And Mark says he doesn't wanna be seen with a freak."

Rachael and I looked at one another. Our brows scrunched, and our eyes quivered. For a moment, it was as if we were psychically linked, understanding exactly what the other was thinking.  
I rested a caring hand on Dean's shoulder.

"Well, I'm going to need to add money to the swear jar, because I think Gabriella’s a dumbass."

Rachael's eyes widened. She covered her mouth to hide a snicker, air escaping from her narrowed nostrils.

"You aren't a freak. You know why?" I smiled.

"Why?"

"Because only warriors have scars." Dean smiled a little at this newfound understanding.  
"Ah, I see a smile! Quickly, Rachael, take a picture before it goes back down.” I chuckled. All three of us laughed harmoniously, our stomachs near aching as the laughter began to fizzle out.

"Alright, now get to school, and when you get back, we'll let you off with no homework," I said with a smirk.

"Ah, yeah! Thanks, Dad!"

He leapt with every step as we all neared the family car.

I opened the car door for my son and wife.

Rachael looked at me, her body halfway between entering the car and standing outside the vehicle.

"Thank you, Clay." Her voice was like silk.

"Eh. It's a non-issue."

I leaned on the driver's door, resting my chin on my crossed arms.  
Rachael quietened for a second, turning the car radio on to drown out the noise outside. Then she shut the door and slowly eased the car off the driveway.

I lingered in place, rubbing the tips of my fingers together, watching as my wife and son drove down the road before disappearing behind the houses as they turned down an adjacent street.

Immediately, as if a man possessed by an unexpected ghost, my demeanour changed.  
I headed back inside the house in a hurried motion.  
My footsteps echoed through the house as I scrambled for my keys. Lucy wandered behind me, but her pleas for attention went ignored. She was little more than a spectator in my world, sidelined by everything else entering my mind.

I found my keys and slipped my wallet into my back pocket before shutting the door without breaking my focused state.  
Lucy pawed at the door, whining as my presence grew more distant with every step I took towards my car.

And before long, I too was heading down the road, but in the opposite direction my wife had gone.  
I was driving faster than the legal limit.

Wherever I was going, I intended on getting there quickly.
