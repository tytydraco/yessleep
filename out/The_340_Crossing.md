I've worked the graveyard runs on the Sound for four years, the last sailing out, the first one back, the hours when the boat is more empty than full and the deckhands outnumber the walk-ons. I like the night crossings. I like the quiet. I liked it, anyway, before the

Tuesday I'm about to tell you about, and I want to say up front that I've requested a transfer since, and I want to say up front that I don't think it will matter.

The last sailing off the island leaves at 3:40 in the morning. Always has. It's a short run, twenty-five minutes dock to dock, mostly empty this time of night, a couple of shift workers, maybe a drunk or two coming back from the last open bar in town. My job is simple.

Count heads. Match the count to the ticket scanner. Radio the number to the captain before

we pull out.

That night the fog was doing the thing it does out here in November, not drifting in but... arriving, whole, like a curtain dropped from above. Visibility maybe thirty feet past the bow lights. I did my walk-through same as always, clipboard in hand, counting the huddled

shapes in the covered seating.

Fourteen. I counted fourteen twice.

The scanner said fifteen.

I want to be clear that this happens sometimes, a walk-on badges through and then decides to wait for the next sailing, forgets to tell anyone, and the count runs high by one. It's not unusual. I radioed it in as a discrepancy, logged it, and went back to my second count just to be sure, because the captain likes the numbers clean before we leave the dock.

Fourteen people. I could see all of them. An older man asleep against the window. Two guy in reflective vests, off a construction shift, sharing earbuds. A woman scrolling her phone in the far corner, the blue light the only clean color in the whole gray cabin. I know because I looked at every single face, because something about that number sitting wrong in my chest made me look twice as hard.

Fourteen faces. Fifteen bodies of heat.

I don't know how else to explain what I felt when I walked the center aisle a third time. There's a thermal strip near the stairwell, an old maintenance leftover, meant to flag heat buildup near the engine housing, mostly decorative at this point, nobody really watches it. I glanced at it out of habit and it was reading warm all along the row of seats at the back, like every seat back there had a body in it, radiating.

There were three seats back there. All three empty. All three warm.

The temperature on the covered deck dropped so fast my ears popped, the way they do on a descending plane, and underneath the diesel and salt smell of the boat, something else came through, copper, thick and wet, like the inside of my own nose after a hard hit.

I stood at the end of that row of empty, warm seats and I did not want to move. I want you to understand that this wasn't a decision. My body simply refused the order. I've been in a few genuinely dangerous situations working this job, rough water, a fire in the galley once, and none of them locked me up the way three empty seats did.

The boat's horn sounded the two-minute warning for departure, and it startled me enough to break whatever had ahold of me, and I turned to head back to the bow, and that's when I saw the door.

There's a maintenance hatch at the stern, painted the same government gray as everything else on that boat, bolted shut, alarmed, never opened during a sailing. I've worked that boat four years and never once seen it stand open.

It stood open. A black rectangle in the gray, no light behind it, no light finding its way in from the fog outside. Just black, the kind that seems to have a texture to it, the kind your eye keeps sliding off of because it doesn't want to focus there.

I did not see it open. I looked away for maybe four seconds, the horn, my body turning, and when I looked back it simply was.

Nobody came out of it. Nobody went in. It just was, and something about knowing it was there, at my back, the whole rest of that crossing, put a cold sweat down my spine that didn't leave until we docked.

I radioed the discrepancy again on our way across. Told the mate about the hatch. He said it was closed and alarmed when he checked it not ten minutes before, said the alarm never tripped, said I should get some coffee in me. I didn't argue with him. I didn't have the throat for it, my mouth had gone so dry I could hear my own tongue when I tried to talk.

I watched that hatch the rest of the crossing instead of doing my job. I'm not ashamed of that. Twenty-five minutes is a long time to stare at a piece of the dark that shouldn't be there, waiting to see if it moves.

It didn't move. Not the whole way across. But somewhere around the halfway point, and I know this because I checked my watch out of nerves, 3:52, thirteen minutes underway, a thought arrived in my head that wasn't shaped like my own thoughts. It didn't come with a

voice, exactly. It came the way a word gets stuck behind your teeth right before you say it, already formed, already yours, except it wasn't.

\*You found it. Now you belong to the crossing.\*

I did the only thing my body would let me do, which was nothing. I stood very still and stared at black nothing at the far end of the boat and waited for the lights of the dock to show up in the fog, and when they finally did, something in my chest unclenched so hard I nearly went down on my knees.

The hatch was closed when we tied up. Bolted. Alarmed, same as always, the mate checked it himself this time, mostly to shut me up, I think, and it read fine on every gauge they've got for it.

I did the second run of that morning too, because I needed the money and I needed to prove to myself it had been nothing, exhaustion and fog and a bad thermal strip. Fourteen walk-ons. Fourteen on the scanner. Clean count, easy crossing, no hatch, no cold, no smell.

I've done maybe sixty crossings since that night. I count heads. I match the scanner. Most nights it's fine.

But every so often, maybe one sailing in twenty, always the last one, always somewhere around the halfway mark of the Sound, the thermal strip near the stairwell reads one body warmer than the seats show, and I've stopped checking which seats they are.

I count the living passengers now and I let the rest of the boat keep its numbers to itself.

Last week, off the coast a few miles south, a King County news brief ran about a walk-on who badged through the scanner on a late crossing and was never seen leaving the dock at either end. Missing, presumed a fall overboard, currents unforgiving this time of year.

I didn't watch the segment. I didn't need to. I already know what the manifest said that night, and I already know what the count did not.

Fifteen.
