For twenty-three nights, my house used exactly eleven gallons of water between 2:14 and 2:17 a.m.

Not approximately eleven. Eleven, every single time.

I know because my water company recently installed smart meters and started offering hourly usage reports through their website.

I live alone in a small two-bedroom house. No dishwasher, no irrigation system, no water softener. Just me, a washing machine, and a bathroom with a toilet that occasionally needs the handle jiggled.

When I first noticed the nighttime usage, I assumed the toilet was leaking.

I put food coloring in the tank, waited, checked the bowl. Nothing. Replaced the flapper anyway.

Eleven gallons the next night.

I started checking everything. Turned off the washing machine's supply valves. Checked the faucets. Looked around the foundation for damp spots.

Nothing.

The strange part wasn't even the amount. Eleven gallons isn't an enormous quantity of water. It was the consistency.

Every night at exactly 2:14, the meter registered usage. Three minutes later it stopped.

I called the water company.

The woman suggested an appliance running an automatic cleaning cycle. I explained I didn't own anything that could be doing that.

She suggested hiring a plumber.

So I did.

His name was Rick. Nice older guy who spent about an hour checking the plumbing, looking under sinks, and inspecting the crawlspace access at the back of the house.

He couldn't find a leak.

He said it might be something happening in the service line, but the regular timing didn't make much sense.

Before leaving, he suggested I watch the actual meter during one of those three-minute periods.

"At least you'll know if the numbers are real," he said.

That night I went outside at 2:10 with a flashlight and a folding chair.

The meter is in a ground-level box near the driveway.

At 2:14, the little flow indicator started spinning.

I watched it for almost three minutes.

Then it stopped.

I photographed the reading before and after. Eleven gallons.

I was actually relieved. Whatever the problem was, at least it wasn't some weird reporting error.

I was putting the lid back when I heard something inside the house.

A dull thump.

Then another.

It sounded like somebody moving a heavy box across the floor.

I went inside, turned on every light, and checked both bedrooms.

Nothing was out of place.

I decided the noise was probably related to the plumbing. Old houses make noises.

The following afternoon, I noticed something strange in the hallway.

There's a narrow linen closet between the bathroom and the spare bedroom. The door had started sticking.

When I pulled it open, the bottom shelf was damp.

Not soaked. Just slightly wet, like someone had wiped it with a dishcloth.

I took everything out.

The towels on the bottom shelf were damp too, but the wall behind them felt completely dry.

I assumed there must be a slow leak somewhere inside the wall.

Rick came back on Tuesday.

He checked the bathroom plumbing again and used a moisture meter around the closet.

Every reading was normal.

He removed a small access panel behind the bathroom sink to inspect the pipes. Dry.

He said he could bring someone out to do a more thorough leak detection, but it would be expensive.

Then he asked me something odd.

"Has that closet always been that deep?"

I said I thought so.

He measured it.

Twenty-eight inches from the door to the back wall.

He checked the bathroom wall on the opposite side, then went outside to look at the dimensions of the house.

When he came back, he said the measurements didn't quite add up, but old houses sometimes had strange framing and unused spaces.

He asked if I'd ever had renovations done.

I hadn't.

The house is a rental, and I've only lived here fourteen months.

He told me to contact my landlord and ask about previous plumbing work.

I called my landlord that evening. He said he didn't know anything about a hidden space behind the closet, but he'd check the old inspection paperwork.

I didn't think much about it until Thursday.

That morning, I opened the linen closet and found all six towels from the bottom shelf on the floor.

They were neatly folded.

Still damp.

I knew I'd left them on the shelf because I'd spent twenty minutes drying the wood and arranging everything the night before.

There were also two shallow indentations in the back wall.

They looked like somebody had pressed their fingertips into wet plaster.

Except the wall was painted and completely solid.

I took pictures and sent them to my landlord.

He asked if I could wait until Monday for someone to inspect it.

I said fine.

I wish I'd said no.

Thursday night I decided to stay awake and listen.

At 2:10 I sat at the kitchen table with the lights off.

I could see the hallway from where I was sitting. The linen closet door was closed.

At 2:14 I heard water moving through the pipes.

Not the normal rushing sound of a faucet running.

It was a slow, irregular gurgle, almost like someone drinking from a bottle.

Then came the thumping.

Three heavy knocks from the direction of the closet.

I stood up.

Another sound followed.

A soft scraping noise, moving slowly down the inside of the closet door.

I called out, "Hello?"

The scraping stopped.

For a few seconds I stood there feeling incredibly stupid.

Then the closet door moved.

Not much.

The handle didn't turn. The door just bowed outward slightly, as though something very heavy had leaned against it from inside.

I ran to the front door.

By the time I got outside, the noises had stopped.

I slept in my car in a supermarket parking lot.

The next morning I called Rick.

He couldn't come until Saturday but told me to shut off the main water valve inside the house.

I went back in daylight.

Everything looked normal, except the closet door was sticking worse than before.

I turned off the water at the main shutoff and opened the kitchen faucet until it ran dry.

Then I pushed a dining chair against the closet door.

I didn't go near it again.

Saturday night, Rick called to say his previous job had run late. He'd have to come Sunday.

I was furious, but there wasn't much I could do.

I decided to stay at a friend's apartment.

Before leaving, I checked the closet one last time.

The chair had moved.

It was only a few inches, but I was certain I'd pushed it against the door.

I pulled it aside and tried the handle.

The door wouldn't open.

I pulled harder.

It felt like somebody on the other side was holding it shut.

Then something pressed against the door from inside.

I heard the wood creak.

I let go.

A wet mark appeared near the bottom of the door.

It spread upward.

At first I thought it was water seeping through the wood, but then I realized it had a shape.

Five long fingers.

A palm.

A handprint, pressing outward from the other side.

And then another one appeared above it.

Higher.

At about the height of my face.

The door shuddered in its frame.

I backed away, grabbed my keys, and ran outside.

I called my landlord from the driveway and told him something was inside the closet.

I didn't mention the handprints. I just said I thought somebody had broken into the house through a hidden space in the wall.

He called the police.

Two officers arrived about twenty minutes later.

They checked every room.

They opened the linen closet.

Nothing.

No hidden person. No opening in the wall.

The back panel was solid drywall. The shelves were ordinary wooden shelves.

The officers looked at the photographs on my phone and agreed the handprints were strange, but they couldn't establish that anybody had entered the property.

They advised me to have the landlord inspect the wall.

One of them suggested the marks might be moisture damage.

I didn't argue.

I went to my friend's place and stayed there overnight.

Sunday morning, Rick finally came to the house with my landlord.

I wasn't there.

Rick called me around noon.

He sounded different from the previous two times we'd spoken.

He asked if I was somewhere safe.

I told him I was.

Then he said they'd removed the shelves and cut into the drywall at the back of the closet.

Behind it was a narrow cavity between two walls.

No pipes.

No electrical wiring.

Just empty space.

Except it wasn't entirely empty.

The wood framing was wet.

Not rotting. Not moldy.

Wet like someone had just poured water over it.

And there were marks on the inside of the drywall.

Rick described them as finger impressions.

Dozens of them.

All facing toward the closet.

He said he'd never seen anything like it and wasn't going to pretend he had.

Then he asked me whether I'd turned the water back on.

I said no.

He told me to keep it off.

I asked why.

He said the cavity had been measured at just under nine inches wide.

And there was a mark in the wood at the bottom, where the floor met the wall.

He said it looked like somebody had been standing there.

Not a footprint exactly.

More like the outline of two bare feet pressed into soaked wood.

Then he went quiet.

I asked him what was wrong.

He said, "There isn't enough room for a person to stand in there."

I haven't been back.

My landlord said he'd arrange for a more thorough inspection, but I told him I wanted to terminate the lease.

I don't care about the deposit.

I don't care about the furniture.

I've got my documents, my laptop, and enough clothes for a few days.

Last night, the water company sent me an automated notification.

For the first time in twenty-three nights, my house hadn't used eleven gallons of water.

It hadn't used any.

I should have felt relieved.

Instead, I kept thinking about what Rick had said.

About those footprints.

About how narrow that space was.

And about how, every night, something inside my walls had been getting exactly eleven gallons of water.

At 4:30 this afternoon, my landlord called.

He said he needed to tell me something before I came back for my things.

Someone had opened the linen closet from the inside.

The door was hanging off one hinge.

There was water all over the hallway floor.

And there were wet footprints leading from the closet to my bedroom.

I asked him if he'd called the police.

He said yes.

Then I asked where the footprints went after that.

He didn't answer immediately.

Finally he said, "They don't come back out."
