I’ve been flying this plane for nearly 14 hours, and we’re starting to run out of fuel. 

Every now and then, we get internet for a few minutes, and we can send messages to the outside world. I thought it was best if I sent out some sort of warning. I hope the experiences I’ve detailed here can help you figure out some sort of solution if you ever find yourself on a flight like this one. 

The rain started a few hours into the flight.

“I told you we shouldn’t have fucking taken off in this weather. I’m going to say it. They should’ve just delayed this flight. Tokyo isn’t running away. Anything for customer satisfaction.” 

My copilot Hanson was a nice guy, but he could be a little much sometimes. Still though, I could admit this was a little strange. Through the windshield, I could see there was barely a drizzle outside, yet the plane was shaking and the air was dense with fog. 

The movie Hanson was half-heartedly watching was kind of a strange one, and didn’t seem to have much of a plot, but Hanson liked his movies that way. He says when they don’t demand too much of your attention, you can afford to do your job and still get the full experience.

I turned on the aviation intercom and began to speak.

*“Flight attendants, please be seated. All passengers, we’re experiencing some unusually rough air here. Please return your seats and ensure your seatbelts are fastened. Thank you.”*

I thought back on some of the passengers I had seen entering the plane. It was an unusually empty flight. There was a tanned woman in a suit who typed away furiously on her phone, refusing to look up for even a moment. Behind her, a child tapped at some brightly colored game on an iPad. After them, there was a group of maybe 10 to 15 college kids, all wearing jumpsuits that read “UCLA VOLLEYBALL.” They were engaged in an avid discussion on what the girls in Tokyo would be like. At the back, a middle-aged man in a hoodie made a show of rolling his eyes, despite the wide grin he wore.

About an hour later, the rain seemed to subside for a few moments. I looked at Hanson. 

“See? I told you it was going to be fine.” But Hanson wasn't looking at me. He seemed confused, and nervously glanced around, as if he couldn’t even hear me. I began to repeat myself, until I realized I couldn’t hear myself either. All sound had vanished in such a perfect silence that it was nearly unnoticeable. Instinctively, I tried to speak up. A sense of panic rose in my chest when once again, nothing came out.   
  
The sound that proceeded to shatter the silence into a thousand pieces and return to me my hearing was so loud, so encompassing, and so absolute that initially I had no reaction. It was only when I heard the sudden violent downpour of rain against the plane, accompanied by the screams of the passengers and furious thunder, did I realize that we were now in free fall. I began to scream too.

The entire plane had lost power. Strange thuds ricocheted throughout the hull of the plane, and the rain only seemed to grow stronger. Suddenly, the power returned, and I gripped the yoke and pulled upwards. Hanson spoke over the intercom.

*“All passengers, the situation is under control. I repeat, the situation is under control. I am contacting the nearest tower for an emergency landing. Please, do not panic.”*

A wet thud, the loudest of them all, broke my concentration. Something had splattered against the windshield. At first, I thought it was a bird, until I noticed the wings. Four of them, transparent and still twitching. It resembled a locust, albeit impossibly large. It was two feet long, had stubs where its antennae were supposed to be, and thick matted fur running along its abdomen. It lay there, broken against the glass for a moment or two before the force of the storm’s wind threw its body up and over the side, leaving a sickly looking residue.

Hanson looked at me, and I looked at him. The impacts to the body of the plane continued to echo throughout the hull. 

“Do… Do you think the other sounds?”

I returned my gaze to the cockpit and turned on the radio.

"Oakland Center, Pacific Four-Twelve, MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY. We just took a massive hit, lost power, and dropped altitude. Possible emergency ocean landing. Sending position, stand by."

A chorus of static responded. I took to the microphone again.   
  
"Oakland Center, Pacific Four-Twelve, MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY. I repeat, we momentarily lost power, and dropped altitude. Attempting emergency ocean landing once we reach less turbulent waters. Sending position, stand by."

We waited a few minutes, no response. There was a hesitant knock on the cabin door.  
  
As I got up to open the door, I asked Hanson to keep trying to reach someone. It was Sandy, our senior flight attendant, followed by the tanned woman in the suit from earlier. Sandy began to speak but was quickly interrupted.  
  
“Just what the hell is going on here? Are you even remotely qualified to-” Sandy ushered the woman to sit. “Please, ma'am, be seated.” She entered the cockpit and closed the door behind her, leaving the woman looking quite frustrated.   
  
“I understand that you’re trying to figure out what’s happening here, but I need something to tell our passengers. There are… things… on the windows.” 

Hanson spoke first. “Look at this. Our altitude. Despite being in free fall for nearly 40 seconds, we are somehow at 44,000 feet. That’s why we were in free fall before. We were suddenly so high up our plane no longer had the lift to support itself. Not to mention, I think I know why we’re not getting a response. The navigation display is showing no nearby ATC towers nearby. In fact, it’s not even showing any satellites. I have no idea where we are.”

Just then, the intercom came to life. A strange series of clicks escaped it. Still, it was a response. I spoke back.  
  
“Attention unknown aircraft, this is Pacific Flight 412, requesting identification.” The response came back in something that sounded like English, but was indiscernible. Something like this:

“Hako? Ifki por sunna funfun. Pacifica ight fourtu elve! Ifki lor sun nagan.” The voice was high pitched, raspy, and speaking in an incredibly fast manner. 

For the next six hours, we continued to cruise, occasionally being impacted by the insects and surprised by strange voices on the intercom. I told our passengers that we had been “knocked off course” by the storm, and that we were going to make an emergency landing at the nearest landing strip.

With our fuel supply being steadily depleted, I began to consider an emergency landing, even in this weather. The intercom came alive again. I was about to shut it off, when I heard a sound so beautiful I was nearly brought to tears. English.  
  
“Pray, is someone there?” Hanson scrambled to answer.  
  
“Yes, yes, hello? Who is this? Do you know where we are?”

“Thank heavens! Yours is the first English-speaking vessel we have encountered in a fortnight!"  
  
Slowly, with an air of confusion, Hanson replied.  
“How have you been able to travel for two weeks?”  
  
“I confess, I am quite bewildered by your question. Upwards of two months is the standard duration for a flightcraft’s reactor.”

Throughout our conversation, we learned many things about each other. They came from a place they called “The Federated Republics of Pretania,” which they said was west of the Mexica. They were flying across the Pacific Ocean, when just like us, they went through turbulence, and ended up here. They detailed tales of creatures far larger than the ones we had encountered, all winged and monstrous. They said that this world was far larger than ours, and was covered by sea and unrelenting storms. Shortly after, we lost contact with them.

That was around an hour ago. I fear what awaits us in the seas below, but I know that we cannot fly forever. This place, I believe, is where most missing flights end up. Please, if you too are stuck in this place and this message reaches you, contact me. 

I’m going to try and make another post in a few hours to share if I’ve learned anything new.
