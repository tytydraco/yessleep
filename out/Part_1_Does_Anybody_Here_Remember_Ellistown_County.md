While I’ve grown up and had a happy life, Ellistown County has always been in the back of my mind.   

It was a beautiful place off the Coast of California, one of the many places I lived as a child. I was one of those kids who was always bouncing across the States with my mom while she found work in different fields (And before you ask, my mother died a few years ago, bless her soul. She told me very little about Ellistown while she was alive). It had a large beachfront and a marina, where my mother worked at a fish bar and where we lived. There was also hillier countryside up north, near downtown. 

There’s virtually no information available about my childhood town. A few weeks ago, I tried to do a deep dive with the little information I remembered, and came up with nothing, even when I tried searching on Google Maps. That’s to be expected, I guess. I can’t even remember the surrounding towns. 

I got a job at a grocery store in Oklahoma a few weeks ago, and it's left me plenty of extra time to try and search for any information about the place.  I figured you guys would know a thing or two and maybe help me track down the county, or anyone who could’ve possibly stayed there. I had quite a few supernatural experiences there, just as many of the locals had. 

We came to the town when I was 11, but I can only remember a few snippets and memories of my 8-Month-Stay there. Granted, most of those snippets are still fresh in my mind. I’ll try to tell them as best as I can. 

 

 

\*\*\*\*\*\* 

 

 

The most supernatural thing about the town that was well known were these things we called Lunks.  

They were strange creatures, tall and lanky and looming, three times higher than the mountains they walked across. We rarely got good looks at them, us kids. Which was ironic, because it seemed to all of us kids that only we could see Lunks.   

My clearest memory of them was when a few other kids and I were exploring the hillier parts of The County, and at some point out of the corner of my eye, I spotted a great mover. It was tall and lanky and black and covered with what looked like large rusty and used wires. It had the build of a human if it was stretched out like Play-Doh and its head was replaced with a white star. I’m not joking when I say it looked like it had a piece of the sun on its head. It took no notice of us faraway specs, continuing until it literally faded out of sight. 

We just stopped and watched. I don’t recall being particularly scared. We never were of the Lunks. And to our knowledge, this great race only existed on land and took no notice of its lowly spectators on the ground.   

Both ideals were disproven when my best friend died.  

 

 

\*\*\*\*  

 

 

As I said, I grew up on the Marina side of town. The Fisherman’s District, as the locals used to call it. It had a diving wall that doubled as a fishing block and the buildings sported colorful murals I’d sometimes stop and look at on my way to school. If you walked far enough out, you’d get to a sketchier part of the dock. That’s where Me, Bailey, Frank, and Milos would meet every Saturday to go fishing. We’d go out on Milos’s rickety old boat he’d saved for and bought from some senile old dude and, in true Milos fashion, rarely cleaned. We’d row out to a fish-dense area with lots of rocks to hide from Game & Fish (I’m always shocked we got away with fishing so many times) and just see what we could catch.   

 

Me and Milos and Bailey caught stuff all the time. It was Frank that always came back with nothing. And I mean nothing. He was a talented fisher, but he had the kind of luck that would ruin your life. To an extent, I think it did. But he redeemed himself through his cooking. I don’t remember why, but on one of our trips we decided to let Frank fry the food. And when we’d finished our meal that day, it was unanimously decided that Frank should be our cook. To this day, I’ve never been able to recreate the flavor that Frank could find in your humble filet. You’d give him your catches, and he’d fillet and fry them, and you’d have the most beautiful fucking fish plate you’d ever tasted in front of you in under five minutes. I even suggested, on a day when Frank was coincidentally not with us for some reason, that we get him a job with my mom at the fish bar. Milos told us not to. He said that Frank’s cooking needed to keep to ourselves. And so, we did, and it was a beautiful arrangement. We’d catch, and Frank would cook. He even started bringing a little notebook to take notes on new recipe ideas he had for different fish. He was the best with bass, I think.  

And then came a Saturday in October, when the rain was hitting down hard. I was the first to get to the dock as always, and I instantly knew that it was going to be a bad day. The wind was so strong that my feet were barely sticking to the dock, and my bag of pounds-worth of supplies was hanging on by a thread. The rain wasn’t helping our case, either; it even got to the point where, when everyone else arrived, we considered calling off the entire operation. In true Milos fashion, he hadn’t put a tarp over the boat, and the entire thing was wet and slippery. But we kept on and left the dock thirty minutes after I first arrived.  

The dock was a bit far away from our usual rocky area, and with the added pressure of the weather, it took twice as long to even get close. We were about fifty yards away from our usual area when we heard Milos scream bloody murder, as a surprise wave went against the boat’s side. The entire thing flipped over, and we were in the water. Somehow, I wound up on one of the rocks that we were trying to make and were way over there. It took around twenty minutes for another passing boat to pick me up and search for the others. They found Bailey, and Milos had held onto the boat long enough to be rescued by another vessel.  

That was the last time any of us saw Frank alive. 

 

 

\*\*\*  

 

 

The next few weeks were blurry in my mind. I remember a few snapshots, mainly of the funeral, and Frank’s coffin, which I knew was empty. Despite the town’s searches, nobody was able to find his body.   

The next clear memory I have is of me, Milos, and Bailey sitting near the rock wall and looking out onto the horizon. It seemed as though everyone else was trying to avoid the spot of water where our boat went down, while I was drawn to it. Through grief and morbidity alone, all I could think about was where Frank's body had gone.  

I didn’t take my gaze off the rocks until Milos started talking about Ellis Island, which I later realized he’d been gazing at the entire time. Ellis Island is small and only accessible by boat. Frank always talked about going there. Frank was a big fan of Tom Sawyer, and every once in a while, usually when the island bank was just viewable on clear days, he’d start talking to us about one day going there and staying the night to catch fish and fry them over a campfire, over a spit-roast, which was something he’d always wanted to do. Milos always pushed back, as he’d say it would be too much work to do. And that’s not even to mention the island was famous for having terrible catches. So we never really gave Frank what he wanted. And Milos seemed to be thinking pretty hard about those wishes. 

He started talking about a tribute trip. And after a few minutes of talking, a fishing trip to Ellis Island started sounding better and better. So that is where we set our sights.   

 

\*\*\*  

 

 

  

It took us a good few months before we could pull off the trip, mainly because of our parents. The news of Frank’s death was, as you can imagine, big news. And while I don’t remember exactly how we got our parents on board, the trip ended up happening. Milos used some extra money he’d borrowed to get a new boat, this one even more rickety and uncomfortable than the last. It had these great wooden boards with splinters everywhere, and by the time we got to the shore of Ellis Island, we were covered in cuts. It wouldn’t have been such a big deal if we’d been talking and joking on the way over, but for us, though we didn’t realize it at the time, this was more of a second funeral. 

We grounded the boat on the shore and tied it to a rock and walked around the island for a bit. We found a place to set up camp a few minutes away from our boat, and we set everything down, and then we walked along the island coast for a little bit. Milos kept going on about Frank, and his lisp had grown even worse in the past few weeks. For a bit while we were walking and Milos was talking, it dawned on me that Milos was probably suffering the worst out of all of us. He’d known Frank for his whole life, and they’d grown up fishing and talking together long before Bailey or me came along. I think what hurt Milos the most was that there wasn’t even a body underneath Frank’s headstone. Milos was always obsessed with graves. And he was pretty religious, in his own way. He believed deeply in the power of praying when a body was nearby.  

I was thinking about all this even as we stopped to rest, though eventually I died down. We sat by the coast for over an hour, in pure stillness. After that we got up and went to find a stream.   

It took us a bit, but we found a stream leading into a lake, and we fished. It didn’t take us long to realize that three people were overkill for such a small batch of fish. And after a while, Bailey said we should split up and find different water bodies to catch more fish. Then we could meetup at sunset and begin cooking. None of us were planning on sleeping, anyway.   

I decided to go to the center of the island, and it took me about fifteen minutes to walk there. Halfway through, it started to rain pretty hard. The worst of it came just as I found a riverbank. And it was as I was setting up my rod that I noticed a baseball cap with white stripes and red spots sitting a few yards away. I took some river water, wet my hand, and used it to clean off the dirt from the hat. When I looked underneath the cap, I saw what my mind had been thinking about, but my eyes refused to believe. Frank’s signature. Just as I’d suspected, Frank’s hat, which he’d been wearing a few days before his death. At the time, I saw it as a sign from God, a sign that things would heal and get better.   

 

It’s strange looking back and realizing how wrong I was.
