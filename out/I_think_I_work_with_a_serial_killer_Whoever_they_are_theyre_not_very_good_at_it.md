I work in fast food. No one in this line of work wants to admit to it, even though it’s not particularly damning. Lots of people get paid to sit on their ass and click a mouse a few times a day. Lots of people get paid to shuffle papers around. Well, I get paid to make sure the fries get thrown away every seven minutes and that nothing we serve falls into the “temperature danger zone.”

I’ve learned a lot about temperature and danger zones in the last 24 hours.

Call me Jules. Not my real name, but close enough. I *want* to say I’m a writer, but my self-published romantasies and smut books haven't been exactly a gold mine. Overall, being a fast-food manager isn’t the worst gig. You meet every type of person at this kind of job. High school kids, college students, parents trying to give their kids the best possible life, stoners, retirees, twenty-somethings just trying to keep their heads above water, hoping this job is only the stage change between the great set pieces of their lives. People like me.

I won’t tell you the name of the company I work for. You’d know it if I even identified the meat we sell. We’re a tiny franchise location, directly across from a suburb, and nothing but forest behind us for miles. Nothing glamorous. 

This all happened last night. I’d come in at 11 AM and it was verging on 11 PM. I had two call-outs; we were getting slaughtered, at least a hundred transactions over projection; one of the trainees put a foil-wrapped sandwich in the microwave; and I was ready for a beer.

Mike and I were the last people left in the store. I had just finished my nightly paperwork, and Mike, an angel, had shut down the fry station and the kitchen.

He was currently wiping the tile walls. “Go home, Mike,” I said. “Day shift can deal with that.”

Mike turned and smiled. He was a good guy, worked harder than anyone else. Almost triple my age and in a just world would be retired by now. He wore thick glasses, had tired, sunken-in eyes, and a face dappled with liver spots. But even though he was clearly deep into his sixties, his arms were corded with muscle, and he towered over me. I’m not an athletic guy. Mike could probably snap me in half. He wiped the sweat from his brow. “But there’s still boxes in the freezer I gotta break down,” he protested.

“Fuck the boxes. I got them. Your wife is going to kill you for getting home so late.”

“She’d kill me if I *was* home. But if you’re trying to shoo me away…” He waggled his hands.

“I am. Go home. I’ll see you tomorrow.”

“You’re opening, right? It’ll be like you never left. Might as well sleep here.”

I snorted. I’d get five hours of sleep tonight, if I was lucky. But I’d get off at three tomorrow, so I didn’t mind. “Don’t remind me. Get out of here, Mike!”

“Good night to you too!” He winked.

I can remember this conversation so clearly. I replay it in my head, over and over, like someone tumbling a stone in their hands. Did I miss a double meaning to his words? Was there a shadow moving behind Mike in the kitchen I didn’t see? Something in his eyes?

I can’t remember.

He clocked out and I adjusted the labor hours accordingly. I pretended not to see him slip into the walk-in (probably to grab a bag of shredded cheese or something, but hey, you help me, I help you), and I looked over the day's sales summary. I sent the summary to my district manager and then I remembered the boxes in the freezer.

I should’ve left it for the morning. Hell, I’d *be t*here in the morning. I could do it myself bright and early at seven. But I don’t like leaving things undone. Just a couple of boxes. I could get it over with on my way out. I had to lock the freezer anyway.

Mike’s car was still in the parking lot, but I didn’t care. He was always there when I left. At first, it confused me, but then one of the kids let it slip that Mike liked to stay late when I was here, just to make sure I got home okay. I found that sweet.

We had a detached freezer, a little white building directly across from the back door, which meant the fryer person had to go out in the rain or snow to get their fries. A combo lock hung off the latch because we kept at least a grand's worth of chicken patties and fries out there. The digital thermometer on the outside read six degrees. I didn’t look at it very hard. I was ready to go home.

Would you believe it if I told you there were only *three* boxes in there to break down? *Three?* I’d brought my box cutter for the job. Checked my watch. 11:20. My chances of getting any serious sleep were certainly dwindling. I tucked the boxes under my arm and twisted the doorknob.

Nothing. No give.

My heart fluttered, but I tamped it down. I tried twisting the doorknob the other way. Maybe I was so tired I had gotten my directions crossed or something. Nothing. I pressed my shoulder up against the door. Threw myself against it until my shoulder ached. The cold was starting to make my face hurt. The door didn’t budge. I might as well have been slamming myself against a vault wall.

“Mike?” I called. This is a somewhat common prank, especially among the teens. Ask someone to go in the freezer and then lean against the door. Wait for them to start screaming and then fling the door open. As a teen, I’d been both the perpetrator and the victim of this prank many times. I forced a laugh. I was too old for this, and losing time to sleep. If I was lucky, I’d be asleep by twelve and up at six. I’d feel like shit tomorrow. “You got me! Mike?”

Nothing. I fumbled for my phone, but phones didn't work in the freezer. I knew this because any time I put the truck away, my audiobook would cut off as soon as I stepped past the door. Like usual, ‘no signal’ crawled along the top of the screen.

Now it was time to start screaming. And banging. I was wearing a black polo and khakis, and my clothes were starting to cling to my skin. *The sweat.* I was gross after scrambling for the past hour to close everything. My cheeks were already hardening with cooling sweat and grease.

My eyes darted around the freezer. Mist from the fans made everything foggy. Metal shelves lined the walls, piled with ten- to twenty-pound boxes of fries, tenders, nuggets, and slabs of meat. I screamed my throat raw, and then whatever was left of my vocal cords allowed me to sputter into a laugh. I was so panicked I’d forgotten the safety release, a big crank beside the door.

I pulled it, hard, my teeth starting to chatter.

*I’m boiling. I’m in a soup. It’s so hot my skin’s starting to melt off.*

Would you believe me if I told you that didn’t help?

The door moved—and then stopped. It didn’t open, didn’t let in even a breath of outside air. I kicked the door so hard in my flimsy no-slip shoes that the pain shot through my foot and up to my shin. This wasn’t possible. I’d pulled the safety release, but—

The lock. Someone had locked the freezer.

My eyes slashed to my watch. 11:24. It was so cold that even time had become sluggish. I pounded on the door with both fists now. I called Mike’s name so many times the word lost meaning. I even threw in a few ‘Michael’s for good measure. He wasn’t going to save me, and I’d lost my voice.

My mind pinwheeled. I lived alone. No one would notice I wasn’t home. No one was due in to work until seven. My teeth chattered, and I was shaking. I wasn’t going to make it to seven. I’m no scientist, but with my body reacting like this, I knew I had maybe a couple of hours at most. And ‘at most’ was being generous.

I checked my pockets. My phone. My house keys with a Hello Kitty charm. My box cutter.

I flicked the blade out and hacked at the hinges. My fingers were stiff and painful around the blade. *The sun. You’re standing on the sun. You’re rotating on a spit. You’re incinerating.* Maybe a few metal chips fluttered down. There was no way I was going to saw through those hinges. There had to be a safety shut-off button, but where?

I looked behind me at the tubing on the wall. There was one, wrapped in black foam, zip-tied in about a thousand places. Despite visiting this freezer almost every day, I didn’t know much about it. If I hacked through the tubing, would that shut off the fans and save my life? Or would it flood the place with coolant and noxious fumes? I wasn’t sure and I couldn’t risk it.

I had to try to find the emergency shutoff. It had to be hidden behind one of the shelves, maybe on purpose, so no one would bump into it. I climbed the shelves on the back wall and hurled a half-empty box of onion rings to the ground. It landed with a heavy thud. I had to stop between boxes to rub my hands together, wasting more time. When the shelf was finally empty, I snatched the shelf’s metal bars and hissed as the cold surface bit into my palms. It took three heaves to pull the shelf even a few inches off the wall. Nothing but smooth plastic behind it.

I would’ve screamed, but I was tired of screaming. I probably couldn’t have screamed if I tried. I moved to the next shelf, but the boxes on top were pushed so far back I had to climb, my hands now bright pink and trembling. I stretched as far as I could to reach the farthest box, but no dice. I lifted my knee to build momentum, and I must’ve kicked something because the shelf teetered forward.

I had less than a second to drop down and roll to the side. Boxes smashed and dented on the ground, frozen chicken everywhere, and the smell of blood. First the smell, then the pain. The shelf had come down hard on my leg and split the meat of my calf. Mist rose from the pooling blood, not quite steam, but the byproduct of nearly a hundred-degree liquid hitting a frozen surface. Before I could stop myself, I rubbed the warm blood all over my hands and face, anything for a little extra warmth.

I don’t know when I started crying. I was going to die here. Buried under fucking chicken tenders. I was squinting against the pain, the blood and tears rapidly cooling to my skin and caking on my lashes. I was going to die coated in my own blood, sweat, and tears at my shitty job. How poetic.

I lifted my head, pushed the crust out of my eyes, and focused on where the shelf once stood. There was a little green button. Maybe it wouldn’t save me, but it might buy me just a little more time.

I pushed myself up, but the shelf's weight still pinned my leg. The bleeding had staunched a little, but the pain throbbed through me. I was going to die, but I had to try. I thought of all my half-finished stories and then thought of my cat. I inched forward on my elbows, found the wall with my fingertips, and just barely reached the button.

The whirring of the fans stilled. I breathed out a sigh, but the cold leeched in through my clothes and my blood was still pooling on the ground. My hands had turned sticky, and the image of me being stuck to the ground with my own blood shot through my brain. Even with the shut-off, I might be in worse shape than I was even a few minutes ago. I checked my watch. 12:02. I couldn’t afford to wait.

In the fall, one of the shelf’s legs had bent, the one that had pinned my calf. I thought of Percy again, my cat, his sleepy green eyes blinking at me as I tapped away on my laptop. I thought about all the books I hadn’t read. I know, I know, silly stuff as motivation to *live.* I even thought of pan pizza with extra pepperoni and a cold can of Miller Lite, though *cold* wasn’t exactly enticing right now. I lifted the shelf with a soundless scream and tore myself free.

‘Hurt’ is an understatement. ‘Exhaustion’ is just a word. For a moment, my head was empty, no words, no thoughts at all. Nothing to describe the cold and the pain and the crushing, horrible hope that propelled me forward. Hope is at the bottom of Pandora’s box, after all.

I sawed at the dent in the shelf’s leg with my box cutter. It felt like hours; it must’ve been minutes. When that snapped, I dragged my injured, trembling body toward the door. From the corner of my eye, I could see the trail of blood I’d left. The bright red was already fading to a scary rust color. Probably something to do with the temperature.

I squeezed my makeshift weapon so tightly that its ragged edge sliced into my palms. Under normal circumstances, I’d be worried about tetanus, especially because with every swing of the shelf’s leg, the edge buried itself deeper and deeper into my skin. But I was so filled with adrenaline, and so numb from the cold, that it didn’t even cross my mind.

I must’ve swung at least a hundred times. I lost myself in the motion, my life reduced to the freezing cold and the arc of the swing. The first hinge clattered to the ground, and I didn’t even pause to gather myself. If I stopped, I knew I would lose the momentum. I’d slump against the door and that would be it. I bashed in the second hinge. The third was too high. I pushed with all my strength and caught a crack of darkness. The latch held the middle of the door firmly in place, but the bottom….

I laid on my stomach, the cold leeching in through my shirt from the floor. I beat and beat and beat at the kickplate on the bottom of the door. This was my last chance. I couldn’t feel my arms or legs, and I could barely open my eyes against the blood that crusted on my lashes. The door lurched forward another inch, sending a breeze of fall air that felt like tropical heat. But it wouldn’t be enough. I squirmed my hand through the opening and fumbled with the lock.

I was dizzy and could barely remember the numbers. The code was simple. 333. When Vera opened it, she only flicked each number left once: 222. But Dave was more thorough. Dave scrambled the lock. No matter how hard I searched my memory, I couldn’t remember the numbers on the lock when I entered the freezer, or even who opened today. I prayed, though I don't know what I prayed to, pushed each number one digit to the right, and pulled.

You’ll have to forgive the line break. I’m a writer, and it felt appropriate.

I don’t remember falling asleep, but I must’ve passed out from exhaustion the moment I crawled out of the freezer. Dave found me curled up outside the busted freezer door, clutching a length of metal like a teddy bear.

I’ve gotten a lot of stitches today, four in my palm and six in my leg. Percy is sniffing at them experimentally as I type this, and I have to hunt-and-peck the keys with my left hand, so it’s been slow going.

When I woke up this morning, Dave was standing over me. He’s the type that talks with his hands, so he might as well have been signing at me. “Do you want extra hours? Mike’s in the hospital. We found him on the walk-in floor. Don’t know how he’s alive; his brains are everywhere. We might have to open late today.”

Dave paused and scratched his stubble, his shadow lengthening over my body. “You alright, Jules? You look like shit. You want me to get you a coffee? We got a lot of work to get done today!"
