I’ve done this job all my life. Moved over four hundred houses. Only one left a blood trail. Only one wanted me dead.

Most folks think the job starts with a crane and ends with a demo crew. Wrecking-ball jockeys are a whole different breed. They get paid to break shit. Somehow, they get paid twice as much.

It’s a lot harder to move a house without putting a scratch on it. And a hell of a lot more complicated. Hell, I’ve had people ask if we fly houses around with helicopters. Like, what kinda gas-station bath salts are they smoking? Maybe they’ve seen that movie with the old fart and all the balloons one too many times.

No Bugs Bunny crap. Truth is, it’s diesel, mud, and busted knuckles.

House moving starts in the dirt. Underneath. You crawl into a space built for pipes, spiders, and men a whole lot skinnier than you. You dig under the foundation by hand, slide steel beams through the mud, then jack the whole place up one inch at a time.

And that’s the easy part. The hard part is the people.

Moving someone’s home is a little like doing surgery on the family dog. You save him, you're a hero. You screw up, you're the son of a bitch who killed Sparky.

“Never forget, we're hauling more than what's sitting on those beams.”

Pops never lost a house. Mostly because he knew when to say no. Bad route? No. Rotten foundation? Fuck that. Client trying to shave two days off the schedule? Hell to the no.

I said “no,” at first. Then the phones stopped ringing and the bills kept coming. After a while, “bad idea” starts sounding a whole lot like “maybe.”

Mrs. Carol Haskins was one of those maybes. Retired schoolteacher. Little white Cape Cod outside Frederick, Maryland. Built in 1949 and sitting exactly where the county wanted to widen a road. The kind of job I normally would’ve passed on. Tight lot. Bad access. Not enough room to work. Too many ways for a small problem to become an expensive one.

But work was slow.

Her grandson bought a lot three miles away, Mrs. Haskins called us, and suddenly we were moving the whole damn thing. Porch. Shutters. Flower boxes. Even the pencil marks in the pantry where she’d measured her kids growing up.

We’d raised it the day before and spent the morning easing it onto the Buckingham dollies. Steel beams carried the house across sixteen independently steerable wheel assemblies, hydraulic lines snaking underneath like the veins on some old mechanic’s hands.

My brother-in-law Tony lay on his back beneath the rear beam, welding a support bracket. Sparks bounced off his leather jacket.

“Your ass is on fire,” I told him.

“Then quit staring at it.”

Tony was forty-one, built like a refrigerator, and usually looked like he’d just been accused of something he probably did. He’d done three years in Jessup for stealing construction equipment, which he maintained was bullshit because most of it had eventually been recovered.

My sister married him six months after he got out.

Pops called that piss-poor judgment.

Tony called it true love.

And he loved that woman hard. Quit drinking. Mostly quit fighting. Even started showing up at church and pretending Jesus had something to do with it.

Then Renee got sick. Some long-ass medical name the doctors could pronounce but couldn't do a damn thing about. Tony stayed through all of it. Appointments. Hospital chairs. Bathing her. Feeding her. Turning her at three in the morning. Reading her trashy romance novels when she couldn’t hold the book anymore.

Prison never broke Tony.

Losing Renee came damn close.

He started drinking again. Fighting again. Some Mondays he showed up with a black eye or split lip. But he always showed up. Work and church. I figured a man was allowed to be fucked up if he kept showing up.

And whatever else Tony was, the bastard could weld. Give him enough steel and profanity and he could fix damn near anything.

He slid out from beneath the house and inspected the smoking hole in his jacket. “I love this jacket.”

A voice behind us said, “Uncle Tony smells like hot dogs.”

My daughter Grace stood nearby in a yellow safety vest over a purple Ravens sweatshirt, clipboard under one arm. She was fifteen then. Born with Down syndrome, which is usually the first thing people noticed and about the least interesting thing about her.

She could identify every truck in our fleet by its engine, kept better inventory than most adults I’d hired, and cheated at Uno like there was money on it.

She’d also probably saved Tony’s life once by noticing he’d buried a fire extinguisher under three cases of water and making him dig it out right before a hydraulic line blew beside his welding rig.

After that, Grace made us label everything.

“Hot dogs smell good,” Tony said.

“You don’t.” Grace handed me the clipboard. “Front chains checked. Two silver hooks missing.” She glared at Tony.

Tony sighed and produced them from his jacket. “I needed them.”

“Then you sign them out. Don’t be a child.” She clipped them back onto the rack and walked away, shaking her head like a disappointed supervisor.

Tony rubbed the little hummingbird tattoo on his forearm. Renee used to draw it on every birthday card. I’d seen him do that a hundred times when he thought nobody was looking. Figured it was a little prayer or tribute or something. Never asked. Guys like me rarely did.

My better half, Stacy, was inside doing the final walk-through—cabinet latches, loose junk, anything that might start flying once the house moved. She handled permits, routes, utilities, payroll, and the thousand other things keeping Suarez and Sons alive. She only came out for complicated jobs. She said it was because she didn’t trust us. In her defense, she’d met us. When Stacy came out the front door, the house was five feet off the ground. She stepped onto the temporary stairs holding an old ceramic rooster. “Found this on top of the refrigerator.”

Mrs. Haskins appeared behind her. The old woman hugged the rooster to her chest. “My husband bought this on our honeymoon.”

Tony leaned toward me. “Imagine being married sixty years and that’s the honeymoon story.”

Uncle Tito, the graybeard of the crew, gave the last chain a hard tug, listened to the steel answer, then gave me a thumbs-up.

I liked pretending I ran the company. Truth was, I didn’t move shit until Tito said we were ready. He’d worked beside Pops back when the whole company fit in one parking space.

Tito could hear a hydraulic pump change pitch and tell you which corner was low.

He also smoked beside the diesel tank.

Family businesses are complicated.

Every diesel barked awake.

Something behind my right eye barked with them.

Grace rubbed my scalp like she was trying to buff out a dent.

“Better?”

It wasn’t. The headaches had started about a month earlier. Doctor gave me migraine pills and told me to hydrate, sleep eight hours, and cut down on coffee. Real comedian.

“Let’s roll,” I said.

Grace rode shotgun and called clearances. Stacy took the rear escort. Tony walked the dollies. Tito handled the hydraulics.

We squeezed the Cape Cod through one intersection with less than four inches on either side. People lined the shoulder taking pictures. A kid followed us on his bike for almost a mile.

Three miles took six hours. Every turn mattered. Every slope. Every branch, traffic light, mailbox, power line—and every jackass in an F-150 who saw a house taking both lanes and still tried to squeeze past.

Grace waved like she was in a parade. At the new foundation, we rolled the house dead center.

Mrs. Haskins cried.

We finished just before dark. By the time I got back to the yard office, the headache behind my right eye had grown sharp enough to blur the security lights.

I sat in Truck One with the engine off and waited for it to pass. Swallowed two pills dry and swore to Jesus I’d throw away my Keurig if He’d just make the pain stop. This was about as close as I got to a moment of zen.

I hated walking into the office these days. Moving a six-hundred-ton house was easier than opening another goddamn past-due notice.

Every drawer had become a hiding place. Permits. Tax forms. Past due. Final notice. Final-final notice. I’d go looking for a bridge permit and find another electric bill threatening to shut off the lights. Every morning I half expected to pull into the yard and find a foreclosure notice taped to the office door. Maybe that’s what I needed. Somebody to tell my stubborn ass it was finally over. To put the whole damn thing out of its misery.

My phone buzzed. Stacy. She stood twenty feet away beside the office door, holding her own phone to her ear. Zen was done for the day.

I answered. “Hey babe.”

“You planning to come inside?” she asked.

“Minute.”

“You’ve been sitting there ten minutes.”

“You’ve been counting?”

“Since the minute you pulled up.” She gave a long pause. “There’s somebody here, somebody in a suit.”

The office sat in a converted double-wide at the back of the equipment yard, between our maintenance garage and a row of rusting dollies we kept promising to rebuild.

My father’s original company sign hung above the front desk. SUAREZ & SONS STRUCTUAL MOVING. Structural was misspelled. He’d noticed the mistake three days after paying for it and refused to buy another one. Said people who were all worried about spelling were welcome to move their own damn houses.

Stacy was waiting in the doorway. She rubbed my face. “You look pale.”

“You like pale.” I kissed her before she could keep looking at me and went inside.

The man sitting in our office was indeed wearing a suit. Charcoal. Perfect fit. Not a wrinkle on it.

Tony was behind the desk eating Oreos from the emergency hurricane kit. Grace was organizing invoices. Luis had already gone home. He had a newborn at home and a rule about never staying past sunset unless someone was paying overtime.

The silver-haired man rose. “Mr. Suarez.”

I shook his hand. Mine still had grease worked into the creases. He looked down at it for half a second before wiping his palm against his trousers.

“My name is Mercer,” he said. “This is Ms. Vale.”

Vale looked like she'd taken a wrong turn on the way to somebody's funeral. Black clothes. Black shoes. Dark hair pulled straight back. Not a speck of dust on her. She gave me a small nod.

“How did you get in?” I asked.

“Your side door was unlocked.”

“It wasn’t,” Grace said. Locking up was her job, and like most things, she took it seriously.

Mercer let that hang there. “We represent a private property consortium seeking a structural relocation specialist.”

“You need a house moved,” I said.

Mercer opened a leather folder. “We were told your organization has experience with difficult relocations.”

“We’ve moved churches, barns, three lighthouses, and a house that weighed six hundred and eighty tons.”

Grace lifted a finger. “Six hundred seventy-eight.”

“Our structure is considerably smaller than six hundred tons,” Mercer said.

Stacy moved closer to the desk. “What kind of structure we talking?”

Mercer paused like an actor remembering his lines. “It’s known as Payton House. It’s a three-story Victorian. Approximately seventy-six feet long, fifty-two feet wide.”

“Stone?” Tony asked through an Oreo.

“Wood frame. Brick veneer in sections. Structurally sound.”

“Foundation?” Stacy asked.

“Fieldstone. Separated at the sill.”

“Where’s the house?”

“Pennsylvania. Near the western border. A suburb called Bellwether.”

“And where’s Payton House going?”

“West Virginia.”

Tony stopped chewing.

I laughed because the other option was asking him if he'd lost his fucking mind. “You want us to drag a fifty-two-foot-wide house through the West Virginia mountains?”

Mercer’s expression didn’t change. “I was told men in your line of work were paid to do the impossible.”

“Yeah, well, impossible takes time. Impossible costs money. Every county wants a permit. Every state has different axle limits. Then you’ve got bridges, grades, power lines—”

“The route has been arranged,” Mercer interrupted.

“Arranged by who?”

“Our organization.”

“Then let’s see it.”

“You’ll receive the directions in stages. The first set will take you to your initial waypoint. When you reach those coordinates, the next instructions will be transmitted automatically.”

I turned to Stacy. She was already looking at me, eyebrows raised. The fuck is this?

“You want us to move a house across state lines without telling us where we’re going?”

“You know where you’re going, Mr. Suarez. West Virginia.”

“That’s a state. I need roads. Bridges. Clearances.”

“You’ll have everything required for each leg of the journey.”

“What about police escorts?” Tony asked.

“Unnecessary.”

I actually laughed. Who was this asshole to tell me what was unnecessary? I’d spent half my life waiting on cops who couldn't figure out which end of an intersection to block. “Utility crews?”

“No.” Mercer nodded to Vale. “We’ve selected a route that provides the necessary discretion.”

Discretion. Another word I didn’t like.

Vale opened a black case and spread paperwork across the desk. County maps. DOT permits. Oversize-load route books. Laminated turn sheets. Bridge surveys.

Real stuff. All covering the first leg.

I looked at the turn sheet. No interstates. No cities. Mostly county roads and old state highways. At the bottom, instead of an actual address, there was a set of GPS coordinates.

Apparently, when we reached those coordinates, the next set would update on our phones automatically. Three million dollars to play the world's most expensive scavenger hunt.

“One more thing,” Mercer said. “Keep your phone on throughout the journey. Location services enabled. We'll be monitoring your progress.”

“Cool. Cool,” I said. “Anything else? My Netflix password?”

Tony grabbed one of the route maps. His eyebrows went up. “This is batshit crazy. In my professional opinion.”

Mercer reached inside his jacket and pulled out a sheet of heavy cream paper, folded once. He opened it and laid it between us.

“Whatever that says, it doesn’t matter,” I said.

He stared at me.

I stared back.

Fine.

I looked.

STRUCTURAL RELOCATION FEE: $3,000,000

Tony stopped fucking with maps. Stacy inched closer. Even Grace looked up from her iPad.

I laughed. More of a cackle.

Three goddamn million dollars.

Trucks paid off. Insurance caught up. Payroll without moving money between accounts and praying nobody cashed a check early.

Stacy sucked in a breath.

Mercer didn’t blink. He knew exactly what he’d put on the table.

“Three million dollars will be transferred to your account when Payton House reaches its destination.”

“Bullshit.”

I started to stand.

“To demonstrate good faith, we’ll pay three hundred thousand now.”

Vale lifted a suitcase onto the desk like they’d rehearsed it.

Clicked it open.

Cash.

Stacks of it.

More money than I’d ever seen in one place.

Tony leaned forward. “Is this Monopoly money?”

“I assure you it is not.”

Stacy and I shared a look.

The whole thing smelled dirty. Nobody walked into a double-wide with three hundred grand in a suitcase because everything was on the up-and-up.

Stacy was the last person on Earth who’d ignore that. She renewed permits early, kept insurance cards in three trucks, and once made Tony drive back because his safety vest was the wrong shade of orange. No chance she was buying this.

“What’s the destination?” she said.

“A private site,” Mercer said. “Suitable.”

“That’s not an answer,” Stacy said.

“It’s the only answer I’m authorized to provide.”

I leaned back. Truck One needed a transmission. Insurance was due. Payroll was Friday.

Three hundred grand would keep us alive. Three million would raise the fucking dead. Sorry, Jesus.

“What’s the catch?” Tony said.

Mercer folded his hands. “The owners require several nonnegotiable protocols.”

Ms. Vale placed another page on the desk. The list was short.

1. All exterior doors and windows MUST remain sealed during transport.
2. No member of the moving crew MAY enter the structure.
3. No personal property MAY be removed from the structure.
4. The structure MUST remain on the designated route at all times.
5. The move MUST be completed within forty hours of the crew's arrival at Payton House.

“Forty hours?” I said. “You want us to agree to a deadline when we don't even know where the hell we're going?”

“You know the destination state.”

“West Virginia. Great. That narrows it down to an entire fucking state. How far? What kind of roads? How many hours are we actually looking at?”

“The schedule is achievable.”

“According to who?”

“Our logistics team.”

Stacy shook her head. “We can't verify any of this without the full route.”

“I'm afraid the operation requires a certain degree of compartmentalization,” Mercer said. “You're being compensated quite generously for it.”

“Compartmentalization. That's a fancy word for keeping us in the dark.”

“You'll receive everything you need when you need it.”

“And we only get the rest if we pull this off.”

Mercer glanced at the suitcase.

“Three hundred thousand dollars is sitting in front of you, Mr. Suarez. The balance is contingent upon successful delivery.”

“It’ll take us two days just to lift it, brace it, and get it onto dollies.”

“That work has already been completed by a previous contractor,” Mercer said.

I looked up from the map. “Then why aren’t they hauling it?”

“They are no longer available.”

Bullshit. Nobody does two days of the hard part, gets a house sitting pretty on steel, then leaves their truck and walks away.

Mercer closed the folder. “The house is loaded and ready. The clock starts when you arrive at the site.”

“What’s inside?” I asked.

“Nothing that concerns you.”

“If I’m moving it, it concerns me.”

“You have everything you need. The terms are not negotiable.”

“Then neither is the job.” I slid the contract back. The headache pulsed behind my eye. For a second, the office seemed to tilt. Not much. Just enough that I put one hand on the desk.

Stacy noticed.

Mercer did too, but played it cool. He reached for the suitcase.

Tony slammed a hand on top of it. “Would you mind giving us a few minutes?”

Mercer looked to Ms. Vale. Checked his watch. Then back to us. “Of course.”

The two of them stepped outside, quietly shutting the door behind them.

Tony waited until the door closed.

“We're doing this.”

“No,” Stacy said.

“Three million fucking dollars.”

“It's not real,” she said. “It can't be.”

“That suitcase looks pretty goddamn real.”

“Forty hours?” Tito shook his head. “On roads we haven't even seen?”

“That's the part that bothers you?” Stacy said.

“It's the part that'll kill us.”

Tony looked at me. “Dan?”

I didn't answer.

Stacy waited until everybody started arguing again, then stepped closer.

“We don't have to do this.”

Sure. We could always declare bankruptcy. Close the shop. Tell everybody who depended on us to go find another paycheck. Maybe lose our house while we were at it. Hell, maybe DoorDash was hiring.

Five minutes later, Mercer was back inside, and I was signing.

The banks were closed, so I spent the night with three hundred grand in a suitcase shoved under my bed. Didn't sleep for shit. Every set of headlights through the blinds was Mercer's imaginary ski-mask crew coming to take it back.

Next morning, Stacy and I were outside the bank before it opened. The second they unlocked the doors, we deposited the money, drove back to the yard, and pointed five trucks toward Pennsylvania.

I drove Truck One with Grace beside me, Mercer's route binder across her knees. She punched the first set of coordinates into our commercial GPS. Mercer had promised the next set would arrive automatically when we reached the first waypoint.

No final address. No complete map. Just another pin to chase.

Our workhorse was a faded red Peterbilt 389 with enough miles that the odometer felt more like a suggestion. It smelled like diesel, old coffee, wet Carhartt, and the Marlboro Lights Tony swore he wasn't smoking on overnight hauls.

The wheel was cracked everywhere except beneath my hands, where years of driving had worn it smooth. Behind the seats, the sleeper bunk disappeared under moving blankets, rain gear, a cooler, and a dozen mismatched gloves.

Up front, Stacy ran point in the pilot truck, anywhere from fifty yards to half a mile ahead. Her job was to find trouble before I did—low wires, bad turns, construction, some idiot parked where the survey said nobody would be parked.

Behind me came the power truck, basically a diesel engine, hydraulic pumps, and a nest of black hoses bolted to a flatbed. Tito drove. The previous contractor had left their dollies under the house, and if they matched our system, he could tie in and control the whole load—raise a corner, drop another, steer the wheels.

Tony had the equipment rig, an old white Freightliner loaded with welding gear, chains, cribbing, spare steel, and whatever else he figured we'd desperately need halfway up a mountain.

Bringing up the rear was Luis Ortega in our pickup. Thirty years old, three little girls at home, and not much use for words unless they were overtime, raise, or when’s payday? He watched the load from behind and carried enough tools and parts to keep us moving unless something was actually on fire.

We rumbled into Bellwether in a line.

A house-moving convoy isn’t subtle. Five diesels on a residential street make enough racket to shake pictures off the walls—engines growling, chains banging, air brakes coughing, amber strobes flashing through windows.

Usually half the neighborhood was outside before we made the first turn.

**Nobody came out.**

Bellwether sat at the end of a subdivision so new the asphalt was still black instead of gray. The houses were new too, but the lawns had gone long and shaggy, like nobody had cared for them in weeks.

There should have been kids. Hell, there should’ve been somebody.

Cars filled driveways. Garbage cans sat at curbs. A basketball rested beneath a hoop.

FOR SALE signs leaned in waist-high grass. Weeds split the sidewalks. Kids’ bikes lay beside driveways. One house still had Christmas lights clipped along the gutters.

It was October.

Then I noticed the crosses. Not every house. Just enough. Bolted above the front doors.

Security cameras hung beneath the eaves. Every damn one pointed the same direction. Toward the far end of Bellwether. Nobody said anything over the radios. Just five diesel engines echoing off the houses. Air brakes hissing. Chains knocking against steel.

Something cracked beneath the truck. Soft. Brittle. Like running over a handful of dry twigs.

Then again. Crunch. Crunch.

The road ahead was littered with dead birds. Some still had color in their feathers. Others had been there long enough to become feathers, bones, and dark smears across the pavement.

Power lines overhead. Maybe they’d hit a transformer. Maybe somebody sprayed for mosquitoes. Bad feed. Disease. There had to be an explanation.

The radio crackled.

“We’re not going to talk about the birds,” Stacy said.

Apparently not. Nobody answered. We kept moving.

I kept my eyes on Stacy’s taillights. Grace plugged both ears with her fingers.

Then the neighborhood ended.

The neat little yards and attached garages stopped on both sides, gave way to weeds, old trees, and one oversized lot nobody had bothered to tame.

The road kept going another hundred feet.

Straight toward it.

Payton House sat alone at the far end of Bellwether, looking like it had been there a hundred years before the subdivision showed up. Normally, developers tore places like that down. This one, they built around. Everything stopped short of its lot, like nobody wanted to touch the ground it sat on.

Three stories of blackened wood and faded brick. Chimneys crowded the steep roof. Narrow windows stretched too tall. One corner of the wraparound porch sagged, its railings black with rot.

I eased off the throttle.

The convoy settled behind me. Diesels dropped to idle. Air brakes sighed. Everybody climbed out to stretch and stare.

Up close, the bastard seemed even bigger.

But the thing that stopped me was underneath it.

Just like Mercer said, it was ready to move. Beams already ran beneath the foundation. Thirty-two hydraulic dollies carried the load. The previous crew had done beautiful work. Days of cutting, lifting, cribbing, welding, balancing.

“Well,” Tony said. “Ain’t that convenient.”

Fifty yards away sat a white Ford F-250 with its driver’s door hanging open. Magnetic signs covered both sides: “HARBOR STRUCTURAL MOVING. YOUNGSTOWN, OHIO”

I pulled onto the shoulder and set the brake. “Hold here.”

By the time I reached the Ford, the others were climbing out.

Tony nodded at the sign. “You know these guys?”

“By reputation.” I kept looking at the truck. “They’re good.”

Tony took in the open door. The empty road. “Then why’d they leave their truck?”

The keys were still in the ignition. A paper coffee cup had collapsed in its holder from months of heat and cold. A clipboard sat on the passenger seat beneath a route survey.

Tito checked the open gang box in back. Chains. Shackles. Hydraulic hoses. Cutting torch. Welding rods. Everything a crew leaves behind when they expect to come back after lunch.

“They wouldn’t leave this,” he said.

“Maybe somebody got hurt,” Tony said.

Stacy came up beside us and looked toward Payton House. “Companies walk off jobs. They don’t leave the trucks.”

She was right, and I knew it. I wish I could tell you that was enough to make me leave.

My phone rang. Unknown number.

“Mercer?”

“Mr. Suarez. I assume you’ve arrived.”

“About ten minutes ago—”

“I strongly suggest you begin immediately.”

“We haven’t inspected the rigging.”

“I assure you the rigging is satisfactory.”

That got under my skin. “Yeah? You crawl under houses for a living now?”

“I recommend you be gone before dark.”

I looked at the sky. “It’s not even noon.”

“Your forty hours have already begun, Mr. Suarez.”

The line went dead.

Stacy watched me lower the phone.

“Nice guy. Real people person.” I pocketed the phone and grabbed my hard hat. “Come on, party people. Daylight’s burning.”

I ducked beneath the house first. I didn’t trust anybody else’s work until I’d put my own hands on it. Tony and Luis crawled in behind me. At the power truck, Tito checked Harbor’s inspection tags against the hydraulic pressures while Stacy spread the permits and route survey across the pickup hood. Grace trailed me with her clipboard.

Harbor knew their shit. Every beam sat where it belonged. Cribbing square. Cross-bracing overbuilt, if anything.

“These welds are gorgeous,” Tony called from underneath. He slapped one of the steel beams. “I hate these guys.”

At the rear dollies, Tito crouched beside a hydraulic manifold. “They replaced this line.”

“So?” I asked.

“So they were working,” he said. “Right up until they weren’t.”

“Dad.”

Grace stood on the trailer beneath one of the first-floor windows. Luis was up there with her, shining his flashlight through the narrow gaps between the boards.

I climbed up beside them. Thick oak planks covered the window, bolted straight into the framing with barely a quarter inch between them. Up close, the wood was black with age and moisture, green mold fuzzing the edges and something yellow and waxy growing in the cracks. It looked soft enough to take a thumbprint.

Broken glass lay caught between the bottom board and the window frame. More glittered on the trailer deck beneath us.

“Somebody try to break in?” I said.

“The glass is out here,” Luis said. “If somebody had broken the window from outside, most of that glass should’ve been inside the house.”

Luis leaned closer, his face almost against the boards.

“Careful,” I said. “There’s mold right by your eye.”

He shifted the flashlight, then stopped. “There’s something on the back of this board.”

Luis tried another angle. No good. Then he pulled off his glove.

I knew where this was going.

“Luis—”

“I got it.”

Of course he did.

He turned his hand sideways and started feeding his fingers into the gap.

You ever drop something important into a garbage disposal? You know the switch is off. You can see the switch is off. Still, the second your hand goes down that drain, your brain starts screaming that the blades are about to come alive.

Yeah, that.

He pushed deeper.

Then suddenly stopped.

“What?”

His face tightened. “Grooves.”

“Grooves?”

“Scratches.”

He felt around. I hated that he kept feeling around.

“Deep ones.” His fingers moved again. “I can put my fingers right in—”

Luis screamed and ripped his arm free.

His knuckles slammed the oak.

Skin tore.

Blood striped the board.

“FUCK!” He stumbled backward, clutching his wrist, and damn near went off the trailer. “Fuck. Fuck. Fuck.”

“What happened?”

“I don’t know. Something bit me.”

He finally opened his hand. A black splinter was buried deep in the heel of his palm, thick as the end of a skewer. Blood ran down his wrist.

I’d pulled a thousand splinters on job sites. I’d never seen one like that.

“Stace!” I yelled. “First-aid kit.”

Stacy looked up from the other side of the trailer, saw the blood and started moving without asking questions.

Luis pinched the skin around the splinter.

“Don’t.”

“I can get it.”

He squeezed harder. His face changed. He groaned. It was painful to even watch.

“It’s not coming out,” he said between gritted teeth.

“Then quit digging at it.”

Stacy hurried up with the kit. “Shit. What happened?”

“House bit him,” Grace said.

Luis gave her a look.

“Splinter,” I said.

But I knew better.

That was the first time Payton House drew blood.

“Come on. Sit in my truck.” Stacy took Luis by the elbow, then looked at me. Her eyes flicked past my shoulder toward Payton House before she led him away.

Tony and Tito were watching too. Guys who work around heavy equipment notice bad omens. We’d already seen dead birds, boarded windows, Harbor’s abandoned truck, and now this—and it wasn’t even lunch. Usually we laugh them off. Nobody was laughing.

“Finish the inspection,” I called. “We leave as soon as Tito gets the power unit connected.”

I ducked under the rear beam and checked a chain binder I’d already checked twice. Gave it a tug. Then another. Anything to make it look like business as usual.

When nobody was watching me, I wandered over to Stacy’s truck.

Luis sat on the running board with the first-aid kit open beside him. The splinter was still buried in his palm. Ugly thing. Black, wet, and driven deep enough that only the tip showed.

The skin around it had already gone purple. The puncture looked swollen and slick. When Luis squeezed, blood and cloudy yellow fluid welled up around the wood.

He clamped the tweezers onto the tip and pulled.

The skin came with it.

The splinter didn’t move.

“Fucker.”

He reset and pulled harder. Groaned. Strained. Still nothing.

“Jesus.”

Luis pinched the heel of his hand.

More blood came up.

Then something thicker.

Gray-yellow.

It slid around the splinter and into the crease of his palm.

Luis went pale.

“Easy,” I said.

He swayed once. I caught his shoulder and eased him back against the truck before he folded.

Then the splinter moved. Just a fraction. Its black tip sunk beneath the swollen skin like something had tugged it from the other side.

**I won't bullshit you. That scared the shit out of me.**

“Damn.” Luis stared at his hand.

I pulled out my pocketknife. “You want me to open it up?”

He looked at the blade, then at his palm.

“Nah. I’ll soak it and wrap it. It’ll work itself out.” He grabbed the antiseptic. “It’ll be fine.”

Yeah. Sure.

Luis swore he was fine and refused to sit out.

I knew better. I also needed him. The next four hours were all work.

We stripped off Harbor’s hydraulic hookups and connected our own.

Tito tested the dollies one at a time. Tony repaired a questionable brace. Stacy checked the permits and turn sheets. Luis checked chains with his glove back on. I let myself forget about his hand. Grace logged every tool we touched.

By four-thirty, Payton House belonged to us. At least mechanically.

Tito brought the dollies alive.

I climbed back into Truck One with Grace.

Stacy moved out front.

Tito brought the power truck in behind us. Tony and Luis fell in behind him.

We were ready to roll.

I called Mercer. Again, he answered immediately.

“We found damage around one of the windows,” I reported.

Mercer went quiet. “Was the interior exposed?”

“No.”

“Did anyone enter?”

“No.”

“Did anyone remove anything from the house?”

“No. Nobody went in.”

Mercer exhaled. “Then proceed.”

“Mercer—”

He hung up.

I stared at the phone for a second, then dropped it onto the console.

“Okay,” I said. “Let’s get this bastard out of here.”

Behind us, Tito brought the hydraulics up.

The dollies turned.

I eased Truck One into gear and felt the slack come out of the connection behind me.

Then Payton House moved.

An inch.

Another.

Steel groaned beneath it. The porch shook. Chips of old mortar dropped into the grass.

I gave Truck One a little more throttle.

The house left its lot.

We crawled back through Bellwether past the dead birds, the crosses, the cameras, the boarded windows.

When the last dolly cleared the subdivision, Luis came over the radio. “House is clear.”

For the first few miles, nothing happened. The house rode smoothly. The dollies held pressure. We cleared the first turn with six inches to spare and passed beneath two utility lines without stopping.

We were ten miles outside Bellwether when the sound began.

At first, I thought it was part of the song playing through the radio.

A distant, hollow thud beneath the guitar.

I reached over and turned the volume down.

THUD.

Grace looked at me. “What was that?”

I took my foot off the accelerator. The convoy slowed behind us. For a moment, there was only the grind of diesel engines and thirty-two sets of tires rolling across pavement.

Then—

BANG.

The sound came from inside Payton House.

Three hard blows came from somewhere behind the boards.

BANG. BANG. BANG.

Not wood settling. Not a branch. Too evenly spaced.

Everybody stopped.

BANG. BANG. BANG.

Someone was inside Payton House.

And we’d been promised three million dollars not to open the door.
