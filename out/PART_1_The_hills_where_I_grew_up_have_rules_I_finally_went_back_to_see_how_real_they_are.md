Some of the hills in the Southern Balkans are more than believed to be haunted. Out here, everyone either knows someone who lived through something, or has lived through it themselves.



Long story short, during the rule of the Ottoman Empire, these were the hills where the two religious factions fought, and they fought bloody. Corpses of Christians and Mulsims were left on stakes to send a message. Bodies were mutilated. Never burried. The hands of the rich Ottoman fighters especially, so people could pry the rings off their bloated fingers. I won't bother you with the rest of the details, but you get the idea. They are gruesome.



If any place on earth had grounds to be haunted, it would be this one.



As a kid I thought all of it was real. Older, I decided it was just a scary story people told. Older still, I heard the stories from people I knew, serious people, the kind who would not lie about a things like this. And in the end, I got to experience some of it myself.



One rule everyone here knows by heart is to always carry a piece of bread, or at least the crumbs of one. In Christianity bread is the body of Christ, so this way you are quite literally bringing Him with you. And believe me, on some of these roads, you want Him with you.



I went up to my late grandfather's house a while back, God rest his soul, to spend a night and check some of the stories for myself, and to ask the locals about the rest. Piece of toast in my pocket, of course. I will be honest, I was more excited than nervous, though I brought a bottle of brandy either way. By the time I finished work and got up there, it was late afternoon, but it did not feel anything like afternoon.



It is an unapproachable place, almost as if it wants to stay that way. The car only gets you so far. When the cocnrete road ends, there was a kilometer or two on foot to the house where my father lived, and his father, and his father before him.



To reach the village, I had to descend all the way to the bottom of the valley and then climb out the far side, up the hill, along a trail. Nobody had walked that trail in a long time, and I nearly lost it more than once. It was nowhere near evening yet, but down at the bottom of the valley it may as well have been.



It was a strange thing, to be swallowed by all that thick green and feel completely exposed at the same time. I felt eyes on my back the whole way down. Anyone standing at the top of the hill could see me plainly, and could step behind a tree or a bush before I ever finished turning my head. I will admit it, when the thought crossed my mind that not just someone, but *something* could be there, I was close to running for the village.



Now, when I say village, forget what you are picturing. There was no cluster of houses packed shoulder to shoulder. Out there, five houses spread a kilometer or more apart was considered a village. So there was always a chance it was just a shepherd or a woodcutter up on that ridge. But this was not that. This was not a human presence.



Finally, after a glass of brandy, I settled in the house. It was just in time for dusk. Up on that hilltop the sunset is a beautiful thing, and by this time of the year the leaves had turned from green to yellow and red, so the whole hillside looked as though it were on fire. I did not stay out to watch it finish. My grandfather always said that I everyone should be in the house before the sun is gone, and so I was.



I locked the door and drew the curtains shut. Another of my grandfather's rules: something out there will always try to catch your eye, and you must never let it. Meet its gaze and it is bound to your soul. Silly as it sounds in daylight, I was not about to test it after dark.



I turned on the old TV, one of those black boxes you have not seen since childhood, maybe not even then if you are young enough. It played only one station. I left it on for the company more than anything.



I must have dozed off after half the bottle, because a loud sound snapped me awake. At first I thought I had dreamed it. Then it came again. Something was out there, banging around in the old pig house. I recorded it on my phone, and on the recording it sounds like nothing but wind. In the room, though, you could hear the difference exactly. It was deliberate. A set of sounds made with intent, meant to draw a person out. Some call it a vampire. Out here we call it a *drekavac*.



Funny-sounding name. It translates to something like "the screamer," and you understand why the moment you hear it. That squeal, like a hurt animal, or an infant. The good thing was that it could not come inside. Not without an invitation, a permission, if you will. So instead, it stands out there and screams in borrowed voices, trying to talk you into opening the door yourself.



Then, the banging stopped. A muffled sound came, closer and closer. Not only could I recognize that it was crying for help, I could recognize the tone too. Even though it had been three years since he was put in the ground, I knew that voice by heart. The son of a bitch was using my grandfather’s voice. 



I am not ashamed to admit I shed a tear there. As evil and bizarre as circumstances were, hearing the voice of your long-lost loved one wakes something inside you and kills it in the same breath. 



Still, I did not answer, the way I was raised not to.



I had come prepared. Curtains shut, door locked. I knew how this would go down, and still, it is a different thing to sit with something on the other side of the wall. I opened the old cabinet and there it was, my grandfather's double-barreled hunting gun, still loaded. They say nothing touches a *drekavac* but silver or sunlight. The gun would not have done a thing. It gave me some comfort anyway.



I realize how this sounds, how cliché. It is a close cousin to your vampire, yes. But the word vampire is ours, South Slavic. We were the ones who dealt with them first. And I have always believed we got the oldest and nastiest of the lot.



I tried distracting myself for the rest of the night with a book and that dying TV, and every so often it would start up again, a clatter, a baby's cry, the name at the door. It only truly stopped when a rooster called from somewhere far down the valley and the dark began to thin. Another very well known thing was that the cry of a rooster brings the morning and with it, the sun. It seemed that *drekavac* knew the rule too.



My eyes were burning by then. But nothing had gotten in, and I knew that was going to happen, as long as the rules were held.



I pulled the curtains apart. The sun was just breaking the horizon.



I was exhausted, but the day had to be used. If I was going to get the locals talking, it had to be in daylight. There would be time to sleep later, with the sweet lullaby of *drekavac* a few meters from my window.
