My name is Silas Black. I have spent most of my life working alone in places where other people do not want to stay. I have watched fire towers, lighthouses, mines and mountain stations. I thought I knew every kind of quiet there is. I was wrong. There is a kind of quiet that does not come from empty space. It comes from something that is listening. This story is about the sixty days I spent guarding a forgotten bunker in the Bitterroot Mountains of Idaho, and about the five rules I was given before I went down there. I broke one of them on the eighth evening. I have never forgiven myself.  
  
It started with a letter. It was not an email or a phone call. It was a real paper letter, and it came to my old post office box in the middle of September 2021. The paper was thick and cream colored, and the writing was done with a fountain pen. It said that a man named Walter Calder owned a piece of land in the Idaho forest, forty miles from the nearest town. On that land there was an old guard cabin. He needed someone to live in the cabin for sixty days, from the first of October through the twenty-ninth of November, and keep an eye on the property. The pay was twenty-four thousand dollars. Half would come before I left, and half would come when I finished. At the bottom of the letter he wrote one more line. It said, "You must be a man who can sit with silence and not fill it."  
  
Twenty-four thousand dollars for two months of sitting. I have worked for much less. But I have also lived long enough to know that when a job pays too well, the job is rarely the real price. I read the letter three times. Then I called the number at the bottom. A man answered on the second ring. His voice was dry and slow, like old paper. He did not say hello. He said, "Mr. Black. You got my letter." I said I did. He said, "Then you are interested." I said I was curious, and that curiosity is not the same thing. He gave a short laugh. "Curious is good," he said. "Curious men read the rules. Come and see the place. If you do not like it, I will pay for your gas and you can go home."  
  
I should have said no. I know that now. But my knees hurt, and my small house had a roof that needed fixing. So I packed my truck. I took my lantern, my wool coats, my fedora, my winter boots, two boxes of books, a thermos and a heavy knife. I took my old notebook too, because I always keep one. Four days later I drove north into the mountains.  
  
The last town was called Elk Prairie. It had one gas station, one diner and a church with a white tower. I stopped for gas. The woman at the counter looked at my license plate and asked where I was headed. I said I was going up the Selway road to a cabin owned by Walter Calder. She did not smile. She did not frown either. She just stopped moving for a moment, the way a person does when they hear a name they have not heard in years. Then she gave me my change and said, "Well, you have a good winter, mister." I noticed that she did not say "stay safe." People say that to strangers going into the mountains. She did not.  
  
The road after Elk Prairie was gravel. After twenty miles it became dirt. After thirty miles it became two lines of dried mud between tall pine trees. The trees grew so close that the sky became a thin gray strip over my head. I saw no other cars. I saw no houses. I saw one old sign nailed to a tree, so faded I could barely read it. It said, "U.S. Forest Service. Road Closed in Winter." Under it somebody had hung a rusty chain, but the chain lay on the ground. I drove over it.  
  
Walter Calder was waiting at the end of the road. He stood next to an old green truck with a rifle rack in the back window. He was a tall man, around seventy,  with white hair cut very short and a face that looked made from a dry branch. He wore a brown canvas coat and good boots. He shook my hand. His grip was strong, but his hand was cold in a strange way, even though the afternoon was mild. He looked at me for a long time, and I felt like a horse being judged at a sale. Then he said, "You are older than I expected. That is good. Young men want to talk to the dark. Old men have already said what they wanted to say."  
  
Behind him, up a short hill, stood the cabin. It was small and made of gray logs, with a green metal roof and a stone chimney. It looked like any old Forest Service guard station. There was a porch with a broken rocking chair, a woodpile covered with a blue cover, and a fuel tank on a steel stand next to a small generator shed. Behind the cabin the forest rose up in a dark wall. There was no other building in sight. Calder told me the nearest neighbor was a ranger post twenty-two miles to the east, and it had been closed for years. The only way out was the road I had come on. In winter, he said, the road could close in one night.  
  
"Does the cabin have a phone?" I asked.  
  
"No," he said. "No radio either. The last man who stayed here had a radio, and it caused trouble." He did not explain what kind. "I will come up the road on the twenty-ninth of November. If the snow is bad, I will come on a snow machine. You will see me. Until then, you are the keeper."  
  
"Keeper of what?" I asked.  
  
He turned and walked toward the cabin without answering. I followed him.  
  
Inside, the cabin had one room. There was a wood stove, a narrow iron bed with a gray wool blanket, a small table, one wooden chair, one tin cup, a shelf of canned food and a wall of firewood stacked to the ceiling. Four oil lamps hung from hooks on the beams. A big black logbook lay open on the table with a pencil tied to it by a string. The room smelled like pine smoke, old dust and something else. It was a cold, flat smell, like wet stone, like the inside of a cellar or the air near a cave. It seemed to come from the floor.  
  
In the middle of the floor, under a faded old rug, there was a square iron hatch.  
  
Calder pulled the rug away with his boot. The hatch was about a yard wide. It was painted dark green, and the paint had worn off around the handle. It had a heavy bolt on one side and a padlock on the other. In the center of the iron lid, someone had fixed a small steel plate with words stamped into it. I will never forget how those words looked in the yellow light from the window. They were cold and plain, like a sign in a government office.  
  
I read them out loud. I always read things out loud when I am nervous. The plate said, "One. The hatch stays closed from sunset to sunrise. Two. Write in the log every night. Write only what you see, never what you hear. Three. Never knock back. Four. There is one cup, one chair and one bed. If you find more, do not use them. Five. Never say your name near the hatch."  
  
I looked at him. He looked at the hatch.  
  
"What is under it?" I asked.  
  
"A shelter," Calder said. "Built in nineteen sixty-one by the mining company, with help from the county civil defense office. When people were afraid of the bombs, the mine owners wanted a place for their workers and families to hide. Forty bunks, a kitchen, a generator room, a water tank, an air filter. A long concrete hallway with steel doors. It was never used the way they planned. The war did not come." He stopped. "Something else came."  
  
"What?" I asked.  
  
He took a moment before he spoke. "I do not like to say. When I say it, it gets stronger. Let us say that the shelter is not empty. It has not been empty for a long time. My job, and now your job, is to make sure that it stays where it is."  
  
I want you to understand how strange it was to hear this in that quiet room, with the afternoon light coming in gold through the dusty window, and a stove that smelled of pine sap and a man who seemed completely sane. I have heard many stories in my life. I have heard drunk men in bars swear that the mine was haunted. I have heard rangers say that the lake was cursed. I always listened with respect and never believed more than half. But Calder did not seem to be telling a story. He spoke like a man reading a weather report.  
  
"Why do you need a keeper?" I asked. "Why not put a stronger lock on the door and go home?"  
  
"I have tried," he said. "The lock holds. That is not the problem. The problem is that the place needs to feel watched. When the cabin stands empty, the hatch starts to come loose. Leave it long enough, and it opens by itself." He walked to the window. "The first time it happened, nobody was here for nine days. When we came back, the rug was folded against the wall, the padlock was lying on the floor and the bolt was pulled back from the inside. And the cabin was full of footprints. Bare footprints. Very small ones. As if forty children had walked around the room in the night."  
  
The stove ticked. The wind moved a little in the pines.  
  
"Are you telling me the truth?" I asked.  
  
"I am telling you what I know," he said. "I have kept this place for almost forty years. I am too old to do it alone now. The man before you stayed six weeks and left in the night. I found his truck at the end of the road with the door open. I never found him." He turned and looked at me directly. "I will not lie to you. Four men have worked for me in the last ten years. Two finished. One left early. One has not been found. You can take your money and leave, and I will not be angry. But if you stay, you must follow the rules. They are not a joke. Every rule on that plate was written because someone broke the one before it."  
  
I stood there and thought about my roof, my knees and my small bank account. I thought about the forty bunks under my feet. I thought about the woman at the gas station who did not say "stay safe." I am not proud of what I thought next. I thought, this man is old and lonely, and he has built a ghost story around his land to keep people coming. I thought the footprints were a lie, or a trick, or a memory made bigger by years. I thought sixty days was only sixty days. Twenty-four thousand dollars was twenty-four thousand dollars.  
  
"I will stay," I said.  
  
Calder nodded slowly. He took an envelope out of his coat and gave it to me. Inside was twelve thousand dollars in cash, in twenties and fifties. "The second half is in a safe at my house," he said. "You will have it on the twenty-ninth of November." He pointed at the shelf. "There is food for sixty days. The generator runs the pump for the water and the one light in the shed. Keep the tank above a quarter. Cut wood every morning. Read nothing aloud near the hatch. Do not open the hatch for any reason, at any hour, ever. The plate says sunset to sunrise, but I tell you now, that is only the rule for beginners. A man who is wise does not open it in daylight either." He paused. "One more thing. If you hear someone call your name from outside the cabin, it is not a person. If you hear someone call it from below, it is worse."  
  
He walked to the door. At the door he stopped and turned around, and for the first time he looked old and afraid.  
  
"Mr. Black," he said. "It does not hate you. That is what makes it dangerous. It only wants to be counted."  
  
Then he went out. I heard his green truck start. I stood on the porch and watched it go down the dirt road between the trees until the dust settled and the sound faded. I was alone.  
  
The first night was easy. I will say that honestly. I cut some wood, lit the stove, cooked a can of beans with a little onion and sat in the one chair with my lantern and a book. The cabin was warm. The wind was soft. Somewhere in the forest an owl called twice and was quiet. I did not look at the hatch. The old rug covered it again, and I put the iron kettle on top of the rug, partly as a joke and partly because it made me feel better. At nine o'clock I opened the logbook and read the first pages.  
  
The log was very old. The first entries were written in a careful, upright hand and dated in nineteen eighty-three. They were short. "Cold. Wood cut. All quiet." Over the years the handwriting changed, because the keepers changed. Each one wrote in his own way. I read three hundred pages by lamp light. Most of them said the same few things. Weather. Wood. Fuel. Quiet. But now and then there was a different kind of line. "Rule three broken today by new man. He knocked back. Heard the answer. He will not stay." Or, "I did not sleep. Counted twelve knocks. It is learning." Or, in shaky letters, "Do not write what you hear. I wrote what I heard. Now I hear it all the time." I noticed three names in later entries, each beside an unfinished account of a keeper who had stopped coming upstairs: Thomas Reese, Amos Vickery and Julian Ward. I did not yet know what those names meant.  
  
I closed the book and sat still for a while. Then I picked up the pencil and wrote my first entry. "October first. Clear and cool. Cut wood. Fuel tank at three quarters. All quiet. Silas Black." I looked at my own name on the page and felt a tiny cold touch on the back of my neck, though I could not say why. Then I blew out the lamp, lay down on the iron bed, pulled the gray blanket to my chin, and listened.  
  
Nothing. No wind. No owl. No branch against the roof. The silence was so deep that I could hear my own heart. And somewhere under it, far below, so faint that I told myself it was the stove cooling, I heard something that sounded like a long, slow breath being let out.  
  
I did not write that down. I fell asleep.  
  
The next morning I woke before sunrise, feeling stupid. In daylight, with gray light on the windows and the stove ticking as it cooled, the breath I had heard seemed like nothing at all. Old buildings make noises. Old iron pipes make noises. I told myself that, and I mostly believed it. I put on my coat and went out to cut wood.  
  
That is how I spent most of my days at first. I cut wood, carried water, checked the fuel tank, cooked a simple meal and walked the land. The forest was beautiful in the way that wild places are beautiful when you are not afraid of them yet. The pines were tall and straight. The air smelled like cold stone and sap. In the mornings frost lay on the grass like salt, and by noon it was gone. I saw an elk once at the edge of a meadow, standing so still that I thought it was a stump until it lifted its head. I saw two ravens that followed me from tree to tree, croaking, as if they had a long opinion about me. On the third day I stopped to watch them and noticed something. They never flew over the cabin. They went around it. They circled wide to the east and came back from the west. In all my years in the woods I have rarely seen a bird avoid a roof where there was food and warmth. Ravens like to be near people. These ones did not.  
  
On the third night I heard the knocking for the first time.  
  
It was a little after three in the morning. I know the time because I had a pocket watch with a faint glow on the numbers, and I looked at it as soon as I woke. I did not wake because of noise. I woke because the quiet had changed. It had become thick, as though the air itself were holding still. I lay on the iron bed with my eyes open in the dark. The stove was almost out, and a red line of coals showed through the door. And then, from directly below the floor, I heard it.  
  
Knock. Knock. Knock.  
  
Three knocks, soft and even, like a knuckle on a wooden door. It was not loud. It did not sound angry or hurried. It sounded patient, the way a neighbor knocks when he is sure you are at home. I did not move. I did not breathe. I thought about rule three, and I understood in that moment why it was rule three. Everything in my body wanted to answer. When someone knocks, a person answers. That is how people are made. It is not courage or politeness. It is a deep old habit, older than language. The knock asks a question, and the body wants to give the answer.  
  
I pressed my hand over my mouth, and I did not knock back.  
  
After a minute or two the silence returned. Then, very faintly, I heard something I have never heard before or since. It was the sound of someone writing. Not with a pen. It sounded like a fingernail drawing slowly across stone, forming letters. It went on for maybe ten seconds. Then it stopped. I lay awake until the windows turned gray. In the morning I opened the logbook and wrote, "Cold night. Wood stacked. All quiet." I did not write what I had heard. I had read what happened to the men who did.  
  
On the fourth day I decided to find out what I was really guarding. I do not like to sit still while a question is open. So after I cut the wood, I took my knife and a flashlight and walked the land in a wide circle, looking for anything that belonged to the shelter. The map in the cabin showed nothing, but I knew where to look. A shelter for forty people needs air. It needs a way in and a way out.  
  
I found the air first. About two hundred yards behind the cabin, in a stand of young fir trees, a rusted steel pipe stuck out of the ground. It was as wide as a barrel and stood about three feet high, with a metal cap on top shaped like a small roof. A cold wind came out of the pipe. I stood there a long time with my hand over the opening. It was not the steady breeze you feel from a vent. It was slow. It came out and stopped, came out and stopped. And it came exactly with the pace of the breath I had heard on my first night. I took my hand away and walked back. I noticed that my hand smelled like wet rock.  
  
The way out took me the rest of the afternoon. Half a mile to the north, at the bottom of a small valley, I found a concrete box half buried in dirt. It had a steel door in the top, rusted and covered in moss. Four thick bolts held it shut, and across the bolts someone had fixed a steel bar. The metal joint was old, but it was not as old as the door. Someone had come here, not long ago, and made sure it stayed closed. On the bar, scratched into the rust with a knife or a nail, there was a row of short lines. They were counting marks, in groups of five. I counted them. There were forty. Under the last mark someone had scratched one more line, very thin and very new, standing alone, as if waiting for a friend.  
  
I did not like that. I walked back to the cabin faster than I walked out.  
  
On the fifth and sixth days, nothing happened. I want to say that clearly, because it is the part people never believe. Nothing happened. I cut wood and cooked and read my books. The days were calm and bright, and the cold smell in the cabin was weaker. I started to relax. At night I heard no knocks. I slept deeply, and in the mornings I felt almost foolish. I wrote small boring entries in the log. "Clear. Wood. Fuel. Quiet." My handwriting got neater. It was almost a pleasure, the way a routine can be a pleasure. I began to think that maybe Calder was a lonely old man with a story, and maybe the knocking on the third night was the stove, and the fingernail on stone was a mouse, and the pipe breathed because that is what pipes do when the wind changes.  
  
That is exactly the kind of thinking that a place like that wants you to do. I know it now. A good trap is never loud. A good trap is comfortable.  
  
On the seventh night the knocking came back, and it was different.  
  
It started a little after midnight. This time there were not three knocks. There were three groups of three. Knock knock knock. A pause. Knock knock knock. A pause. Knock knock knock. Then silence, long enough for me to think it was over. Then it began again. I lay in the dark and counted. It repeated three times. Twenty-seven knocks in all, in nine patient groups. When it finally stopped, I realized I had been counting along with it, silently, moving my lips. That frightened me more than the knocking. I had not decided to count. My mind had joined in by itself, like a man who walks into a room because he heard his name.  
  
I thought about what Calder said at the end. It does not hate you. It only wants to be counted. I did not understand it then. I thought he meant that it was lonely. Later I understood that he meant something much worse. It was not looking for company. It was taking attendance.  
  
On the eighth day it snowed a little in the afternoon. It was a thin, dry snow, the first of the year, and it did not stay on the ground. I spent the day inside, reading and feeding the stove. I will tell you what I was doing, because it matters. I was not afraid. I was bored and a little proud of myself. I had made it through a week, and I thought the worst was behind me. I had cooked a good stew with canned beef and potatoes. I was sitting in the one chair with the tin cup in my hand, enjoying a quiet evening. The lamps were lit, and the stove was warm. The window was dark blue.  
  
At about seven o'clock I heard a child crying.  
  
It came from under the floor. It was soft and thin, the crying of a very young child who has been alone for too long, and who has stopped expecting that anyone will come. It rose and fell. It shook and broke. It said, in a tiny cracked voice, "Mama? Mama?" And then it cried again, a little louder, in a way that closed my throat.  
  
I stood up. I knew what it was. I want to be clear about that. I knew. I had read the rules. I had walked to the sealed door in the valley and counted the marks. I had heard the breath of the pipe and the knocking and the nails on stone. I knew that nothing living could be under that floor. A voice from below was exactly what Calder had warned me about. But I was an old man with a daughter I had not seen in nine years, and a granddaughter I had never met, and the sound of a child crying in the dark is not a sound that the heart can follow rules about.  
  
I walked across the room. I stopped about three feet from the hatch. The kettle sat on the rug, and I stared at it. The crying went on, small and broken. I felt my throat working, trying to hold back words.  
  
Then the child said, "Please. Is somebody there? I am so cold."  
  
And I said, "It is all right. I am here. My name is Silas. You are not alone. I am right here."  
  
I heard the words leave my mouth. I heard them hang in the air of the cabin like smoke. And I understood, too late, what I had done.  
  
The crying stopped at once, as if someone had turned a switch.  
  
The cabin was silent. The stove ticked. The lamps burned straight and still, with no flicker at all. I felt every hair on my arms stand up. I waited. I did not know what I was waiting for, but I knew that something was about to happen.  
  
Under the floor, very softly, the child's voice said, "Silas."  
  
It said it the way a person tries a new word. It said it with careful pleasure, the way you say the name of someone you have been waiting a long time to meet.  
  
Then another voice said it, an older girl. "Silas."  
  
Then a man. "Silas."  
  
Then a woman, and then more, and more, until the whole space under the floor was full of whispers. They said it softly, together, like children learning a lesson. "Silas. Silas. Silas." There were dozens of voices. I stood frozen in my own cabin and listened to forty people under my feet practicing my name.  
  
Then, all at once, they stopped. And one voice, close to the hatch, almost under my boots, said in a clear, calm, friendly voice, the voice of a teacher who is pleased with a student:  
  
"Thank you, Silas. That is one."  
  
I do not remember going back to the chair. I remember sitting in it with my knees pulled up and my coat around me, holding the tin cup in both hands, staring at the kettle on the rug. I remember that the lamps did not flicker until sunrise. And I remember that when the first gray light came through the window, I opened the logbook, picked up the pencil and found that my hand was shaking too much to write.  
  
I wrote only three words. "Eighth day. Quiet."  
  
It was the first lie I told in that book. It would not be the last.  
  
For three days after that, I did everything right. I got up at dawn. I cut wood until my shoulders burned. I walked the long circle through the forest, because I could not stand to be inside, and I came back before the light turned orange. At sunset I closed the shutters, lit the lamps, ate my dinner standing up and did not go within ten feet of the hatch. I read until my eyes hurt. I wrote small dull lies in the log. "Ninth day. Cold. Wood. Quiet." "Tenth day. Clear. Quiet." The thing under the floor did not speak. It did not knock. I almost wished it would. Silence was worse now, because I knew what it was doing with the silence. It was listening to me live.  
  
On the morning of the tenth day I found the footprints.  
  
There was a thin layer of frost on the porch boards, and in the frost, going from the door to the edge of the steps, there was a line of small bare footprints. They were about the size of a child's. There were seven of them. They did not come back. They simply stopped at the edge of the porch, as if whoever made them had stepped off into the air. I stood and looked at them for a long time. The sun came up and the frost went away, and the prints dissolved one by one, until there was only wet wood. I did not tell the log.  
  
On the twelfth day, as the light was going, I heard my name in the forest.  
  
I was coming back from the woodpile with an armful of split pine. The sky was dark orange behind the trees, and the shadows were long and blue. I was thinking about nothing at all. And from somewhere out among the pines, not far away, a man's voice said, clearly and kindly, "Silas."  
  
I stopped. The voice was my own.  
  
It was not like a recording. It was not like an echo. It was my voice, my exact voice, the slightly rough, slightly slow voice that I have heard in my own head for most of my life. It said my name with my own tired patience. "Silas. Come out here a minute. I want to show you something."  
  
I stood with the wood in my arms and my heart beating hard against it. Calder had told me. If you hear someone call your name from outside the cabin, it is not a person. I did not look toward the trees. I looked at the cabin door. I counted my steps to it, twelve, and I walked each one without hurrying, as if the voice were a barking dog I must not show fear to. I went inside. I shut the door. I put the wood down. I locked the door and pushed the chair against it, though a chair would not stop anything.  
  
"Silas," my own voice said, outside, gently. "It is cold out here."  
  
I put my hands over my ears and sat on the iron bed and I stayed there until it stopped.  
  
I have thought about that voice many times since. It could have sounded like anyone. It could have sounded like my daughter or my dead wife. It chose me. It chose the one voice that I would be least able to doubt. And that is when I understood something about the thing in the shelter. It was not just repeating. It was learning how to be a person. It was trying on pieces of me, like a child trying on a father's coat.  
  
On the fifteenth morning I found the second cup.  
  
It was sitting on the shelf, next to my tin cup. It was the same kind, tin with a blue rim, and exactly the same size. It was clean and dry. I do not know how long it had been there. I only know that when I went to bed the night before, there was one cup. I stared at it for a good while. Then I thought of rule four. There is one cup, one chair and one bed. If you find more, do not use them.  
  
I took the cup outside and dropped it into the creek behind the cabin. I watched the water carry it down over the stones until it disappeared. That night, when I came back from my walk, the cup was on the shelf again. It was dry. It had a small dent in the rim that I had seen the creek stones make.  
  
I did not touch it. I left it there. Three days later there was a second chair.  
  
It stood on the other side of the table, facing my chair. It was a plain wooden chair with a worn seat, an exact copy of mine. I found it in the morning when I woke up. And what I remember best is that it was pulled out a little from the table, as if somebody had just stood up from it. The seat was cool when I put my palm on it, but there was a faint smell in the air of the cabin, a smell of wet wool and rain. I carried the chair to the corner and turned it so its back was to the room. In the afternoon, when I returned, it had been turned around again and pushed in to the table, neatly, the way a polite guest does when he is leaving.  
  
By the twentieth day there was a second blanket folded at the foot of the iron bed. It was a gray wool blanket, the same as mine, with the same worn edge. I slept in my coat on the floor that night rather than touch it. In the morning the blanket was not folded anymore. It lay spread out on the bed, smooth and flat, and in the middle of it was the shallow shape of a person who had lain there all night.  
  
I want to tell you that I left. I want to tell you that I was smart enough to pack my truck and go. I tried. On the twenty-fifth day I woke up before dawn, put everything I needed in the truck and drove down the dirt road toward Elk Prairie. I told myself I did not need the second half of the money. I told myself a man should walk away from a bad job. I drove fast, and the road ran under the trees like a gray ribbon, and I felt lighter with every mile.  
  
Eleven miles down, a tree lay across the road.  
  
It was a huge old fir, with roots torn out of the ground. It lay across the road at an angle, thick as a barrel, with the bark fresh and wet. There had been no storm. There had been no wind. I got out and looked at it. I saw no tracks and no cut. I stood there beside my truck, looking at a tree that could not have fallen, and I knew that I could not cut through it with the small saw in my truck bed in less than a full day.  
  
So I did what a stubborn man does. I took out the saw and cut. It took me five hours. My hands were bleeding at the end. When I finally rolled the last piece aside and got back in the truck, I felt an old rough pride. I started the engine and put it in gear. And I had gone only another half a mile when I remembered what Calder had said. If the cabin stands empty for too many days, the hatch opens by itself. How many days? He never said. He said nine. But the first time it happened, nobody had been in the cabin for nine days. I had been gone for only five hours. The nine days he had mentioned were no comfort now. Whatever was down there had changed since I spoke my name.  
  
Still, I turned the truck around.  
  
I told myself that I was being cautious. I told myself that a man who leaves a job should at least say goodbye to the man who hired him. But that was not true. The truth is that I could not leave. Something in the middle of my chest pulled me back, the way a hook pulls a fish. I did not know if it was duty, or fear, or something else. I drove the whole road back at a crawl with my hands tight on the wheel, and when I came out of the trees and saw the gray cabin on the hill, I felt relief and horror in the same breath.  
  
The front door was open.  
  
I had closed it. I had locked it. Now it stood wide open, moving a little in the wind. I got out of the truck and walked up the hill very slowly, with my knife in my hand. I stepped onto the porch. There were small bare footprints on the boards, dozens of them. They went in the open door. They did not come out.  
  
I went inside. The rug was folded back against the wall. The kettle lay on its side across the room. The padlock was on the floor, open, with the metal loop bent. The iron bolt was drawn back. And the hatch was closed, but it was not locked. It sat flat in its frame, as if someone had closed it politely from below. And all over the wooden floor of the cabin, in the thin old dust along the floorboards, there were prints. Small bare feet. Hundreds of them. They went around the table, around the bed, around the stove. They went to the shelf and to the cups. They went to the window. They went to the second chair and stood a long time around it. They had walked all over my home like people at a party.  
  
I put the rug back. I found a spare padlock in the generator shed and fastened it with shaking hands. I picked up the kettle and set it down on the rug. Then I sat in my chair and understood why Calder had a keeper at all. It was not to keep things out. It was to keep one man in a room, so that the thing below would not walk freely in the empty house. I was not a guard. I was a lid.  
  
In the log that night I wrote, "Twenty-fifth day. Drove for supplies. All quiet." I looked at those words for a long time. They were getting easier to write. That was the dangerous part.  
  
The next two weeks passed like a bad fever. Every night the knocking came. It was always three groups of three, then silence. But after the thirtieth day, on the nights when I did not move or sleep, there was a tenth knock, quiet and slow, at the end of the pattern, as if somebody wished me goodnight. I stopped sleeping well. I lost weight. I saw my face in the small window glass in the morning and did not like it. The skin hung loose under my eyes. My beard looked whiter. I found that I was talking to myself while I cut wood, saying nothing important, just muttering, the way a man does when he is trying to keep his own voice company.  
  
On the thirty-fourth morning I woke up with cold feet.  
  
I do not mean that my feet were a little cold. I mean that they felt like stones from the bottom of a creek. They were gray and stiff. The soles were covered in a fine gray dust I had never seen in the cabin before. There was a mark of the same dust on my hands. My knees ached as if I had been walking down stairs for a long time. The iron bed was warm under me, and the second blanket was folded neatly at my feet. I lay there and looked at the ceiling beams and tried not to think about what it meant.  
  
I told myself that I had walked in my sleep. People do it. I had done it once as a boy. But the hatch was shut, the bolt was pushed home and the padlock was in place. I checked three times. The key was in my pocket. Everything was exactly as I had left it.  
  
On the thirty-seventh morning I woke with the same cold feet, and the same dust.  
  
On the fortieth day, in the afternoon, I opened the logbook and began to read my own entries from the beginning, because I wanted to see how many lies I had written. I turned to the page for the thirty-fourth day. I had written, "Thirty-fourth day. Cold. Wood. Quiet." That was what I remembered. But under my entry, in the same pencil, in handwriting that looked like mine but was a little too careful, a little too straight, there were four more lines.  
  
"Went down at midnight. Counted the bunks. Thirty-nine are full. One is waiting. The number is nearly right."  
  
I read it three times. I turned the page. The thirty-fifth day had the same four lines. The thirty-sixth, the same. The thirty-seventh, the same. The thirty-eighth, the same, except that the last line said, "The number is nearly right. He is almost ready." On the thirty-ninth, it said, "Keeper has stopped fighting. Good." And on the page for the fortieth day, the day that was not yet finished, a new line had already appeared, in pencil, under the heading I had not yet written. It said, "Tomorrow he will meet us."
