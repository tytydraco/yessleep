Shortly after my mom passed away, I set up email forwarding on her account so that I could help take care of her unfinished business. For several months after her funeral, I was constantly cancelling subscriptions, closing accounts, and passing on news of her death to her friends and acquaintances. Now, almost three years later, I very rarely get messages addressed to her, and thus I almost dismissed the email as a scam.

"FINAL NOTICE!!" was the subject line. "OVERDUE RENT—UNIT 208". When I opened the email, I saw that it was addressed to my mom. Apparently, she had been listed as a backup contact on a storage unit in Mississippi. The primary contact, surprisingly, was my dad, who I have not spoken to in over a decade. He had stopped paying and answering the phone (sounded about right), and had exactly 24 hours to settle his balance before his unit was auctioned off.

I phoned the facility the next morning and explained that my mother was gone and my father hadn't been reachable in a long time. The kindly old woman on the phone was sympathetic. She said she couldn't legally hand over his property to me, but if I could make it down there in the next few days, she'd let me into the unit myself, off the books, so I could grab anything I needed before the contents got auctioned or thrown out.

To combat boredom on my impromptu roadtrip, I spent a lot of time on the phone with my youngest sister. Carly had been only six when our father walked out; she barely remembered the man, and I had few happy memories with which to fill that gap for her. When family did come up, we mostly talked about Brielle, our middle sister, who had struggled for most of her life with addiction and mental health issues. Carly mentioned that she hadn't heard from Brielle in a while; that in itself wasn't so unusual, but I still made a mental note to check in with her when I got the chance.

I got to the facility after dark and quickly found my dad's unit on an outer row of corrugated steel containers. The ten-by-ten space was packed. Plastic storage totes were stacked four high along both walls. In the center sat an old gray desk, and behind it was a tall metal filing cabinet with four drawers. Most of what I found was junk, but the desk held an old Canon PowerShot. I found the charger at the bottom of the drawer and, curious to see if the camera still worked, plugged it into my power bank. I left it on top of a tote to charge while I worked on the filing cabinet.

In the third drawer, sandwiched between appliance manuals, I found Brielle's birth certificate. I felt relief, and then a wave of disgust. Years ago, it had been a complete bureaucratic nightmare to get Brielle a certified copy of her "lost" birth certificate, and here it had been left to rot in a storage unit all this time. I put the document in an unused folder from the filing cabinet and gently slipped it into my bag, wondering if my dad had taken it by mistake or out of malice.

By then, the camera's charging light had turned green. I clicked the battery back in and powered it on. The lens whirred out, and the screen lit up. I navigated to the gallery.

The first photo was me, maybe three years old, sitting on a carpet in a dinosaur shirt. A few more of the same—me passed out on the floor next to a half-eaten bowl of Cheerios, me rolling a red firetruck toy over my mom's leg. I was more than a little surprised that the camera hadn't been wiped and sold.

I called it a night not long after that, happy to have found the camera and birth certificate, but frustrated that there was nothing else of value.

I found a room at some run-down motel. It was past two by the time the night clerk handed me a key for, coincidentally, room number 208. After failing to fall asleep for twenty minutes, I sat up, cracked open a beer, and pulled the Canon out of my bag.

Scrolling past the photos of myself that I'd already perused, I saw the first video clip thumbnail in the gallery. I hit play.

My mother filled the tiny screen. She was sitting on the edge of our old floral couch, holding a toddler-aged version of me on her lap. She was beautiful—younger than I could ever clearly remember her being—but her expression was solemn. There was an extraordinary heaviness to her gaze; her eyes looked like those of a soldier who had just returned home from war. She barely smiled as I babbled and tried to grab at her necklace, her movements slow and mechanical.

I scrolled further down the timeline. A series of still shots appeared next—close-up, slightly blurry photos of a newborn wrapped in a pink blanket. Chronologically, it had to be Brielle.

Then came another video file. I pressed play and heard my dad's voice.

"You ready to hold your sister for the first time?"

The scene opened on the same living room. The lighting was low, the room illuminated mostly by the harsh glare of a television set off-camera. My mom sat on the couch with infant Brielle resting across her knees. I was bouncing on the cushion next to her, leaning in close to stare at the baby. Admittedly, the sight pulled on my heartstrings a little. Brielle was always small for her age, but she was so tiny in that video that she almost looked like a stuffed animal.

"Show him how to hold her head, Mary," my dad said. My mom didn't answer. She barely blinked. She just sat there staring straight ahead at the TV screen, her jaw loose, her eyes unfocused and glassy.

"Mary," his voice came again, sharper this time. "Let Austin hold his sister."

Slowly, almost painfully, she turned her head toward me. Her posture was slack, her limbs heavy and uncoordinated, like someone moving underwater. She lifted Brielle off her lap and transferred the baby into my waiting arms. The toddler version of me beamed at the camera, grinning ear to ear as I proudly cradled my new sister. I opened my mouth to speak.

"How are you gonna make her stop—"

And then the video ended abruptly.

Bizarre. For as long as I can remember, I've had a specific image in my head when I think about meeting Brielle for the first time: warm summer day, bright light pouring in through the windows, my dad seated next to me on the couch, my mom beaming as she transferred my little sister into my arms. I didn't remember my mom looking so forlorn, and I certainly didn't remember my dad filming the whole thing. Actually, I didn't remember my dad filming anything during my childhood whatsoever. He wasn't exactly the sentimental type; I had a hard time imagining him whipping out a camera to capture precious domestic moments. And yet, here was a gallery full of photos and videos of myself and my siblings.

Pulling my phone off the charger, I texted my sisters, knowing I wouldn't receive a response until the morning at the earliest. To Carly, I sent a simple question: asking her if she remembered our dad taking photos and videos around the house. When texting Brielle, I had to be a bit more strategic. I couldn't jump right into questions about family stuff; instead, I sent a generic "how've you been?", hoping she'd take the bait and open the door to our first real conversation in months.

Outside, a car pulled into the motel parking lot. I heard a car door open and slam shut as I set down my phone and refocused my attention on the camera.

The next dozen files were all photos, and every single one was taken in that same living room. The background was unmistakable: the faded sofa, the wood-paneled walls, and the scratchy carpet I constantly used to burn my knees on.

My dad was in a few of the files, framed in the edge of the mirror or caught in a reflection on the dark TV screen. He looked and sounded exactly how I remembered, down to the way he chastised us kids whenever one of us made a mistake in front of him.

Conversely, looking at my mother through adult eyes was disorienting. In my memory, she had always been a solid, quiet presence—a little worn down by my dad, sure, but steady. In these photos, though, she looked frail. Her collarbones stuck out sharply against her shirt and her cheeks were hollow. I stared at one picture of her sitting on the arm of the sofa, wondering if she had really been that thin back then. Maybe my brain had just smoothed over all the signs of her distress to make my childhood feel safe.

The next short video started with off-key singing. My dad's flat baritone was leading the song, joined by my own tiny, squeaky voice. The camera was pointed at the kitchen table, where a little girl in a party hat was sitting behind a cake with two burning candles.

It was Brielle's second birthday.

In the video, I was leaning over the table, helping her clap her hands. We were both grinning, completely captivated by the candles.

"Blow 'em out, Bri," my dad's voice came from behind the lens. The camera panned slowly to the left, tracking away from the cake to capture my mother sitting at the end of the table. The sight of her left me more than a little confused.

She was heavily, visibly pregnant. As the camera locked onto her, she planted her elbows on the tabletop, dropped her head into her hands, and covered her face. Her shoulders began to heave—shaking violently, as if she were either laughing uncontrollably or sobbing in complete silence.

Before I could figure out which it was, the video cut to black.

Carly was born when Brielle was four years old. Certainly, my mother could have had pregnancies in between them that she just never told us about. If she had suffered a traumatic miscarriage, it made sense that she wouldn't want to talk about it with her young children. But then … Why didn't I remember any of that? I was six years old on Brielle's second birthday. If I could remember holding Bri for the first time, I should have remembered my mother miscarrying so late into her pregnancy.

I hit the back button. After some trial and error, I figured out how to open the file details. I pulled up the metadata on the video file. The timestamp at the top of the file read: OCTOBER 14, 2006 - 4:12 PM.

Something was wrong. In October of 2006, Carly was already a few months old. Brielle was turning four that year, not two. And I had been an eight-year-old third-grader. I backed out of the file and checked the metadata on the earlier photos—the ones of infant Brielle, the ones of toddler me in the dinosaur shirt. In the earliest photo of me, in which I couldn't possibly have been more than three, the date on the metadata suggested I was six years old.

Old digital cameras keep time with a tiny backup battery, and when it dies, the clock usually resets to some default date. If Dad had never bothered to set it right, every file on the card would be stamped with nonsense. That had to be what was going on.

My phone buzzed. Looking down at the screen, I was surprised to see a message from Carly:  
*hmm no i don't remember him w a camera, but like i said earlier i don't remember much lol. are you in mississippi still?*

Before I could type anything back, a muffled commotion rose up from the floor below—someone shouting at the night clerk about something I couldn't make out. Maybe it was some drunk trying to book a room and getting turned away. It went on for a minute or two before trailing off into silence.

By the time the shouting subsided, my mind had drifted from Carly's text back to the camera. I scrolled through the remaining files, my thumb slamming the dial as I studied the background of the photos.

Every single one seemed to have been taken in the downstairs area of my childhood home. There was the narrow kitchen pass-through, the exposed radiator in the living room corner, and—one of the more unique features of the home—a door right at the top of the stairs to the second floor. The placement of that door in the stairwell had always struck me as hazardous, though it was an architectural quirk that my mom always defended, claiming it kept the sounds of rambunctious children from waking whoever slept upstairs.

Once I confirmed that the photos had indeed been taken inside my old home, I unzipped my bag, pulled out Brielle's birth certificate, and held it under the dim bedside lamp. I stared at her birth date, then looked back at the camera screen. I wasn't going insane; I wasn't misremembering dates. The story told through the photo gallery simply didn't match up with reality.

Through the thin motel walls, I could hear someone moving around outside. I heard footsteps, followed by someone knocking on a door down the hall. If I hadn't been so distracted by the camera, I might have connected the disturbance to the shouting I had heard minutes before.

I continued clicking through the files, watching the years pass across my own face. The atmosphere inside that living room deteriorated with every new video clip. Expressions grew more and more solemn. The room grew noticeably dirtier. My mother was absent for long stretches, and when she did appear in videos, she was often lying face-down on the sofa, unmoving for minutes at a time. I watched Carly grow older through a series of still shots, but something felt off about her—her hair was a deep brown, darker than I remembered, and so was mine. Brielle, aside from looking a little thinner than the sister I knew, mostly matched up with my memories.

The final file on the card was a video.

My dad had set the camera down on a shelf, creating a fixed, wide-angle shot of the living room and the staircase leading up to the second floor. In the frame, I sat on the floor while he sat in his old armchair, trying to teach me how to play chess (yet another thing I never remembered him doing). Brielle sat nearby on the carpet, silently watching.

The scene went on for twenty seconds. I played tentatively, hand hovering nervously over the board. My dad sat rigid, his posture tightening every time I made a wrong move. I recognized that look—the jaw clenching, the anger building up behind his eyes. The tension in the video grew so thick it made my own chest feel tight in the motel room.

Suddenly, the door at the top of the stairs pulled open.

Everyone in the frame froze, their heads snapping toward the noise. A blinding torrent of daylight poured down the stairwell, washing into the living room. The sudden glare flared the camera's lens, turning the screen pure white for a few seconds as the sensor tried to adjust. When the exposure balanced out, a figure was standing at the top of the stairs, framed by sunlight.

It was a girl. She looked around six years old, wearing a familiar blue dress, staring down at the living room(?) in open-mouthed shock. My dad's voice roared, booming with rage.

"Brielle, close the fucking—"

The video cut off.

God. Just thinking about it makes me feel sick all over again. I sat with what I'd seen for a minute, then rewound the video. I zoomed in on the girl at the landing. It sure looked like Brielle, but then again, so did the little girl sitting next to me at the bottom of the stairs.

Before I had fully processed the implications of the video, there came a knock at the door to my room. I stilled. It was four in the morning at a run-down motel in the middle of nowhere—I doubted that whoever was outside had good intentions.

I got out of bed as quietly as I could and walked to the door, not intending to open it, but to look out through the peephole. Much to my chagrin, the floorboards creaked under my feet as I reached the door; if the person outside didn't know the room was occupied before, they certainly did after that. I looked through the peephole, but strangely, a wall of solid black looked back at me. Something must have been covering it on the other side.

For a long time, there was no sound from outside. Only when I started to wonder if the person had somehow left without me hearing did the squeak of the doorhandle make me flinch. I watched in the dim light as the handle moved down slowly until the deadbolt caught with a click. It held there for a moment, then returned to its original position.

Another long silence followed. Standing there in the dark, separated from whoever was outside by centimeters of old, splintered wood, the quiet was far worse than any noise would have been. Finally, after what felt like five minutes of staring at the blocked peephole, I heard the faint echo of footsteps, growing quieter as the person made their way back down the hall. I crept over to the window, pulling the edge of the cheap curtain back to look out at the parking lot. Unfortunately, the floor of the outdoor hallway obstructed much of my view. I didn't see anyone, but a few minutes after the knocking at my door, I did see a beat-up white sedan leaving the parking lot.

As soon as the sun rose, I packed up my things and high-tailed it out of that motel. I'm sitting at a coffee shop a few towns over right now, trying to figure out my best course of action. Despite my many attempts to get a hold of Brielle this morning, I haven't yet received a response. Hopefully she'll hear my panicked voicemails and realize that the situation demands more urgency than my normal attempts to check up on her. I need to know what she remembers, what she saw that day. She's the key to making sense of what's on that camera, I'm sure of it.

But until I can get in touch with her, I'm not sure what to do. Go to the police, maybe? Couldn't hurt to start a paper trail, even if this doesn't turn out to be as sinister as I initially thought. My route back home also takes me through the area of Texas that I grew up in. Maybe it's a crazy thought, but while I'm waiting for Brielle, I'd like to try to visit the house I grew up in. If it hasn't been torn down, maybe the current owners will let me look inside. It might give me some peace of mind (or if not, then it might give me some theories) to see how the actual interior of the house compares to both my memories and the files on the Canon.

I'll post an update here if I learn anything new. In the meantime, if any of you have suggestions as to what I can do next, I'd greatly appreciate the advice.
