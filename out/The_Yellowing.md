I’d only read a quarter of the way through the story, but I groaned before finishing the next sentence. Something about immense mountains and vast glaciers. I was immediately pulled from what little immersion the story offered simply because the author never bothered to research the place that they chose to write about. There are no mountains. There are no glaciers. There is nothing.

I was being jaded, of course. I was reading a story about the Arctic while physically present in the Arctic. A place where the overwhelming majority of people would never come close to seeing with their own eyes. Roughly 50 miles from the edge of the world, if I wanted to be dramatic about it. I wasn’t open to being humble or graceful about my reaction. I wasn’t open to romanticized, trendy, half-fledged ideas. Camp life was exhausting and the land was unforgiving, and not in ways that people expected. I was tired of shitting in a bag and tired of the drone of generators. I wanted to go home. 

I closed the story and stared out the crank-style window, watching a scraggly herd of caribou simply exist on the horizon in the dwindling sun. The animals only moved to tiredly scrape the snow, searching for moss underneath. Otherwise, the basic action of turning their heads to look at the world cost them energy that they didn’t have. So they just stood there, pitifully, tortured by the wind and staring blankly at the same cold gray horizon that I also watched. 

There were four animals yesterday, five today, and a part of me contemplated joining their ranks to quell the growing cabin fever in my mind. But a knock on the door to the steel 40x10’ camp structure pulled me from daydreaming with my stoic companions.

“Supplies came in,” Ryan grunted through the partially cracked door. 

He didn’t wait for my response, nor did I have one. I threw on my jacket to retrieve the order that I had made two weeks ago and moved outside, stuck in a lull of monotonous muscle memory. 

Two boxes waited for me in the cargo towed in a sleigh behind a towering, red Steiger machine. The breeze blew the hot stench of exhaust into my lungs, and the fumes hit like smelling salts against the frigid air as workers tossed things from the transport vessel. I grabbed mine and tucked one under each arm before plodding back to my space to log the items.

Neither box had much weight, I thought. “I wonder what they forgot to send me this time,” I sighed and sliced open the first box, quickly sifting through its contents and bitterly accepting whatever fate the expeditor had sent me. When I was finished, I flattened the boxes and set them aside, promising I’d take them to the Incinerator later. That’d be something to do in the quiet of the night shift. Maybe I’d read until then.

Life ran on repeat for some sixty-odd days in the oil field hitches: twelve hour shifts, over-salted food, industrial walls dragged across the tundra every week or so, bereft of any semblance of humanity in a desolate, wind whipped territory that looked the same for endless miles surrounded by thirsty men that would oogle a stick figure if you drew a pair of tits on it. And every living thing with an ounce of self respect left the tundra in the winter. Even the sun left the tundra. Yet… there I was. At least, you could get out of your head for a while during a hitch with a good story. Except for the fact that for whatever god forsaken reason, the Arctic was a buzz word in the stories this time. I couldn't escape it, even in fiction. 

I scowled at the next story I chose. The soulless, generative formula felt like a wet fart and entertained about the same. The piss colored “artistic” glow of digitally aged varnish of some bullshit cover art was the straw that broke the camel's back. I needed a walk. I sneered and clasped my laptop shut with a touch of force and a generous dollop of frustration. We ate enough slop for meals… slop wasn't going to cut it now.

I pushed myself off my chair and grabbed the collapsed cardboard boxes, slamming the door as I left.

 

“Ten more days,” I warily hissed under my breath. “Just ten more days, give or take a few… or several. Fuck.” 

Any other day I’d probably gain some enjoyment in trolling the “author,” but I was festering and needlessly angry. Camp life wore one down after a dozen days, let alone the fifty I had endured.

I plodded toward the Incinerator, a solitary structure designed to burn trash - and shit - at the edge of camp where we didn’t have to smell it. Fire was the most logistically sound option for garbage in the Arctic, and, suffice to say, a 200 man camp produced a lot of rubbish - and shit - each day. So the fire always churned, flickering in gold as its attendants fed it waste, and what couldn’t be immediately burned was stored in a massive, iron cage to keep animals from scavenging it. Clear bags for trash. Black bags for shit. All caged far from immediate acknowledgement. And, on windless days, the smoke of our transgressions against that lonely land would patiently slither across the sky like a greedy, dark snake. 

It wasn’t uncommon to leave the fire unattended. It’s not like a miserable, frozen bog had much fire risk after all. Tonight was no different. Alone, I placed the cardboard in the barrel and stared into the black expanse beyond. I felt my temper cool beside the fire. The fever of frustration simmered in the cold shadows where the halogen bulbs of camp couldn’t reach. The melancholy of repetition eased its infectious chokehold. The eternal hum of the machines that kept us alive weren’t as loud beside the Incinerator, and so, too, my mind grew quiet. My peace, however, was interrupted by a peculiar clicking in the black beyond.

I frowned, craning my neck to listen. I caught movement on the threshold of visibility in the moonless night of the tundra. Something was approaching and clicking with each step. 

Suddenly, the two toned Bear Alarm echoed through camp, the frozen equivalent of an air raid siren. I bolted to the cage, the closest haven to protect myself from the hooked and unforgiving claws of a brown or polar bear, locking the grated door and stumbling over garbage. The figure became more clear as it approached, but, adding to my confusion, it was revealed to be multiple figures: the caribou. The small herd had multiplied to hundreds, and now they anxiously cut through the side of camp to escape something unseen.

“Martha, do you copy?” J’s synthetic voice was muffled through my winter coat.

I struggled to pull the portable radio from my pocket, “go for Martha.”

“Is that you by the Incinerator? Are you ok?”

“A-firm.” I spoke with a twinge of concern, watching the animals.

“We don’t know if there’s a bear, but something spooked ‘em and they don’t do this for nothing, not even the wolves. We thought we'd play it safe.” The radio clicked silent momentarily. “Are you warm enough? You might be out there a while.”

“Yeah, I’ll be fine. It’s not that cold tonight.” I pouted.

“Stay put, we’ll get you out of there as soon as we clear camp.”

“Copy.”

He didn’t respond. 

The clicking grew louder as a torrent of startled deer approached the Incinerator. It was a normal noise that they made, but it was no less unnerving to know that it was the sound of countless ankle tendons snapping into place over bone, a peculiar adaptation that caribou evolved to keep track of each other’s locations in blizzards and in darkness. Lonesome nomads, the animals spoke with sinew, beckoning each other with the mechanics of their bodies, and I was surrounded by their foreign correspondence.

Wisps of cold breath steamed through their wide nostrils, and their legs chirped with every purposeful step. This was the most activity I’d ever seen them perform in the winter. It was still too much energy for them to stampede or to run, but there was haste behind their snapping gait, and each animal anxiously pushed at the others in front to put greater distance behind whatever pursued them.

I moved in the cage. A few animals briefly acknowledged me but ultimately continued, more pressed by their pursuer than by me. The herd peeled around the burn barrel and I looked at one animal illuminated in the shimmering glow. Its pupil had constricted in the light to a narrow, horizontal slit against the iridescent blue of its iris, and fear was visible in its fleeting gaze.

After a few minutes, the herd passed the Incinerator. Their ankles clattered into the darkness and past my auditory range. But one straggler approached long after the others. Head hung low, the animal was haggard even by the most desperate caribou’s standards. It moved its legs without coordination, and every joint in its body cracked as it moved. 

As it closed the distance, I grew colder and more worried. Abruptly, It stepped into the fire light and stood still with its head cocked as if it were blind and unaware, waiting for a prompt. Its eyes were dull and yellowed, and its normally light coat was stained the color of acidic bile as if it had rolled in crude oil that bubbled through the tundra. It felt… fake. Soulless, even.

I held my radio to my face to inform that the herd had passed, “hey J-”

The animal whipped its head to my direction with a harrowing crack, loud and distinct enough to make me flinch. I stared at it in silence and felt its hollow eyes study me. It approached the stairs with a splayed and awkward pace, and, to my shock, it clumsily ascended the grated steps to the cage. Emaciated, it smelled of sour meat and moved like a marionette.

My mouth fell open as I watched the sickly animal advance. I no longer felt like it was part of the herd fleeing from a predator, but that the herd was fleeing from it all along. It stood with a wide stance on the central floor in front of the furnace. Methodically and noisily, it lowered its antlers with a vibrating crunch, dragging the dulled, malformed ends across the door to the cage, searching. 

I had already started climbing further into the trash, black bags tumbling over me, but now I was desperately scrambling. The uncanny caribou arched its neck up, folding its left, front leg with a sickening pop, and pursed its jaundiced lips. Its yellowed teeth clanked against the door as it explored. Its wet mouth seemed to glow independently of the nearby fire. 

“Hey, hey!!!” J yelled as he jogged toward the Incinerator waving his arms to shoo the animal.

The caribou jumped, jerking sideways and over the Incinerator railing. It fell to the ground with a deep thud and a chorus of painful clicks, and it flailed briefly before it took off with a noisy sprint into the night. 

“I know we joke that you’re trashy, but get out of there.” J winced at the sight of me.

“Did you see that thing???” I screamed, unamused. 

“Just a mangy caribou,” J shrugged. 

“It looked possessed!” I sneered.

“You’ve survived… worse.” He unlatched the door and frowned as he eyed the medley of refuse that covered me. “Jesus, go clean up.” He jabbed.

The original small herd of caribou did not return by the next day’s light. My wary companions were gone. A crew in the field reported that the herd had been spotted miles away at the base of a 100’ bluff, some still dragging paralyzed limbs weakly across the ice of the Colville River below after presumably falling to their demise.

The higher ups of camp management were abuzz trying to appease the Inupiat tribal reps that permitted our exploration on their land, promising that no one had harassed the animals to the point of suicide off the cliff. It was a freak accident and mere coincidence, they pleaded. But whatever fines and conclusions they drew were beyond my pay grade. I stepped outside for a breath, hoping I could find someone to share a drag from a cigarette. 

I cursed to myself when I found no one, cursing louder as I dodged yellow snow where I walked. 

Urine didn’t burn, so everyone capable of pissing upright without attracting attention had to pee outside. It was always a source of contention in camp, and management was constantly scolding grown men not to pee in the primary paths. To add insult to injury, the nasty shades of yellow were the only contrast of color against the expanse of dingy gray and white. I was sick of looking at it and sick of knowing the camp's hydration status based on the glaring colors of frozen piss.

I glanced upward to see Ryan standing with a generous stance in the middle of the path. I thought he was taking a leak, adding to the disgusting stains.

“Ryan,” I jeered.

He was silent.

“RYAN? Go take a piss somewhere else, for fuck's sake!”

Something about him seemed stunned rather than like a man relieving himself. He swayed lightly and his head lulled back. He stumbled briefly, returning to his tired posture like a Portland streetwalker stuck in a lean. 

Ryan stirred and turned to face me and revealed his forlorn face. The white of his eyes had turned sickly bronze and his skin reflected a similar hue. He mumbled something incoherently. His words were slow and confused. His gaze was vacant. He just stood there: vacant, hollow, and sickly. 

I flinched, alarmed by his appearance. “Ryan, are you ok?”

But Ryan simply stared for a long while before he staggered off on unsure footsteps, leaving me silent and equally perplexed.

Over the next few days, camp fell ill. We chalked it up to the “Camp Crud,” a quaint colloquialism for the cold virus when it inevitably found its way into our isolated population and ran amuck. Lethargy, mild confusion, dingy skin… the symptoms, initially, were nothing outlandish but they were obvious. Ryan must have caught it too, hell, maybe he was the first.

But as it progressed, those afflicted grew agitated and, for lack of a better way to put it, yellow and waxy. Not waxy and swollen like you’d expect some illnesses where fluid builds, but waxy like frostbitten skin looks a little less alive when it finally thaws and ultimately mummifies if it doesn’t putrefy… amber facsimiles of something previously alive. Their skin sagged and bunched in peculiar ways. But it was just the Crud, or so we thought.

I was reading the comment section of a plagiarized story, more enthralled by the roasting that the thief received than the plot itself, when J rushed the door to my space. 

He gestured his face in a quick jerk towards the window, too short of breath to speak. A large group of workers, all previously too ill to move, slowly amassed and weakly marched to the edge of camp and moved southward.

The last person to leave camp on foot, years earlier, had abruptly discontinued their antipsychotics and declared that he intended to walk into the tundra to find angels or some other wild shit. His delusions were infamous and people still talked about it, but now, sure as shit, J and I watched half of camp stagger to some quietly determined exodus.

No one touched them, too afraid that whatever spawned their shared hysteria might be contagious. So we watched them slowly stagger one by one, and, simultaneously, we quietly weighed the guilt of inaction until the last of their silhouettes faded beyond the veil of icy fog to unspoken but predictable fates. 

\~

Evacuation wasn't formally announced after that, but we weren't going to wait for permission nor a plan to escape either. Management tried to keep things calm, but the remainder of camp exploded in a simmered worry. Many grabbed machines and fled while workers filled the available seats, but there was some degree of coordinated departure.

 

I donned my Arctic bib and clutched the jacket for later, grabbed my backpack with the bare essentials, and I readied to escape. I didn't care if I ever saw the rest of my belongings again. I could only think of that sick, deformed caribou, and the possibility that it brought some disease to camp. I imagined it hunting angels like the man years earlier, driving its comrades to shared insanity and a hasty demise. 

Could psychosis be contagious? Could caribou even go mad? I didn't think cabin fever was something animals caught in their preferred environments, then again, I couldn't guess why anything would prefer to stay in that god forsaken land year round. It had its beauty, surely, but it was far from a land of abundance or comfort. They chose to live in that misery, I guess. But there wasn't time to dwell on those concerns. 

The door to my bunk room always stuck in its crooked frame, so I dropped my shoulder to ram it open when I was ready. It swung free with a thud and, to my surprise, knocked Ryan to his ass as it opened. Rather, the door knocked something that loosely resembled Ryan on the other side.

The figure spasmed on the hard packed snow below, clicking and groaning agonally. I caught myself before I could habitually apologize, realizing it wasn't who - or what - I thought I had caught off guard.

 

Whatever wore his poorly replicated face hadn't noticed me, or perhaps it forgot I was there in the first place. I kept my movement low, hiding behind the open door frame and concealed in the dark, miniscule space.	

I watched its extra limbs and long mats of patchy hair flail. Its expressions shifted in amorphous, bulbous patterns, and it twitched angrily as it tried to right its tangle of parts and concepts. It looked like a fleshy, dying beetle stuck on its back.

I was frozen by fear. Rhythmically, it rotated its clicking extremities, budding and atrophying new appendages before my eyes. A ghost of Ryan's visage emerged on a gelatinous protrusion where its head should be, crowned in a single, lumpy antler before it popped like a festering blister.

There was no way I would be able to sneak past the mess in front of me. It wriggled immediately at the base of the stairs, and despite how slowly and uncoordinated it moved now, I was certain it could muster vitriolic speed and precision on a whim if I caught its attention. I cautiously reached through the open door instead. I needed to close it to try to protect myself.

Slowly.

Fearfully.

I held my breath, hoping it wouldn't see me or hear me. A singular eye rolled purposelessly in its oversized socket, the sclera stained a dramatic, pale gold. It moved blindly, however, waiting for something to demand its attention and command its action. It moved with primitive, scripted concern.

I noticed now that whatever was in front of me was bigger than I originally thought. Bigger than any man should be, with too many fingers and the boundaries of its body ambiguously blurred. And as I inched forward I felt increasingly vulnerable and exposed, praying quietly that if I could just go unseen now, that it wouldn't know how to open the door when I shut it. Maybe it lacked object permanence. Maybe it couldn't learn that much.

The door squealed with a shrill note when I pulled it, and as if on cue, the man-thing jerked to rigid attention. I quickly slammed the door in response. It shot forward against it like a viper, propelled on newly formed, spring-loaded legs. But the door closed before it could get inside.

I was swallowed in the blackness of the small room. The only window had been boarded shut so that what little daylight illuminated the winter could not penetrate that room, and, in turn, my only other escape was blocked by a handful of screws, foam board insulation, and a thin panel of wood.

The monster scraped across the exterior of the door, reminding me of the caribou at the Incinerator. I fumbled in the dark to find the lamp, casting pale light into the space when I hit the switch. I shoved the table towards the door, and pulled my multi tool from my pocket. Everything took too long. Seconds dragged while I fumbled to extend the Phillips head bit. I struggled to steady my trembling hand, and every twist to loosen each screw felt like an eternity. 

 

I dropped the first screw when it freed. I dropped the second. By the third, I could hear some increased and coordinated effort at the outside of the door as if Ryan's copy was finally understanding the latching mechanism. A fourth? A fifth? Please god, make this faster.

 

I tried prying the board, but I couldn't get enough leverage to break it free without removing at least one more screw. I was hasty and my tool slipped repeatedly during my sloppy attempt, but eventually another screw fell to the ground with a soft ping.

I pulled desperately at the plywood barrier. The wood splintered and cracked and bent. Grasping the plier end of my tool, I plunged it into the secured foam, digging desperately to expose the fragile glass beneath just as the door creaked free and light spilled in.

With one final, frenzied attempt, I smashed my tool into the window. Searing pain shot through me as glass shards pierced into the flesh of my arm and hand. I couldn't stop now. I pulled at the brutal edges to expose my escape and the glass bit back more. My blood looked like oil in the low light as it sprayed from me in a pulsing pattern and splattered around me. I had severed my radial artery.

 

I'd figure it out, I thought. I had time to stop the bleeding on the other side, I assured myself. I just had to escape. If I didn't try, that thing would surely kill me before I'd bleed out... I just had to escape first.

The monster tripped over the table, stumbling with a roar as extra appendages spilled forward to catch its fall, and I threw myself head first out the broken window at the same time. The fall onto the hard snow below was a painful drop, and I knocked the wind out of myself on impact. Exposed in the cold white of the tundra, my blood splashed in harsh compliment to the pale nothing of endless snow, more distracting than the piss puddles could have ever been. I clutched my arm desperately and gasped like a fish as my chest spasmed and my vision blurred.

 

The lookalike wailed in the window, forcing its elongated limbs through the narrow, sharp hole. I staggered forward, trailing red behind myself. My head spun.

 

I collapsed some yards beyond and rolled to my knees, watching Ryan clumsily fall from the window in a shifting mass of tissue and simulated parts. It bellowed, sickly mouth glowing with some unknown, amber heat deep in its gullet. 

Despite my weakening state, I was relieved to see it completely unaware of our human mortality and foresight. It may have tried to look like us, but it failed to realize the slowly advancing threat behind it. Every second counted as my consciousness increasingly faded and as the Steiger advanced towards the disastrous and furious facsimile while it advanced to me.

The timing didn’t favor me, however. It swung a massive limb at me, casting me like a ragdoll, just as the giant machine drove over its fragile body. I closed my eyes while it screamed and thrashed, splattering rancid fluid the consistency and color of honey in thick globs under the tracks of the Steiger.

I woke up in the small clinic of the primary oil operations facility, far from our remote camp. My arm throbbed beneath the plush gauze bandages encapsulating my injury. To say I felt weak was an understatement. I felt like Death himself took insult to my existence. 

I assumed there were others that escaped, including whoever had rescued me, staunched the bleeding, and carried me to haven. I assumed there was some chaotic and desperate conversation - a plea - about the caribou and the yellow madness that infected our group. But I'd have to wait to hear more for the time being. Until then, I assumed I was safe. 

A clinician fussed on the other side of the curtain. She was speaking with another patient, unaware that I had finally woken. Her voice was full of worry.

“We're going to have to fly you out,” she began. “We're flying out another patient in the other room in a couple of hours. The medevac is en route now, and we can arrange your simultaneous transport. But you’re going to need a proper hospital. We just can’t manage this here. We don’t know what’s causing your jaundice, and your cognition is deteriorating quickly-”

My heart skipped to hear her. I was too weak to holler and protest her plan, too weak to warn her that it started the same way in our remote camp: the yellowing. That she'd be sending it to civilization where it could spread and take root, whatever it was.

 

I had to warn people. I grabbed my phone and started typing, “I work in the Arctic. You've got it all wrong. The cold isn't the most dangerous thing in the tundra...”
