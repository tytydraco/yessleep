When my parents visit, the house instantly thaws. I can almost hear the familiar cadence of their voices echoing down the hall, a comforting, radiant buffer against the quiet of the evening. But every time the door swings open to let them in, I catch a fast, shadowy blur near the floorboards out of the corner of my eye. A stray cat, slipping through the gap before the heavy wood clicks shut. I never manage to catch it. Death is nothing new to me—it is a cold, familiar roommate I’ve long since learned to tolerate—so I compartmentalize the intrusion, focusing instead on the warmth of my family.

The reality of that intrusion only sets in at night, when the house is fortified. I sleep surrounded by rows of warm string lights and stacked curtains, my bedroom door and my children’s doors wide open so my vigilance never rests. There is a large mirror in my room, positioned deliberately to my right so I never face it. I don’t drape it; that’s a cliché trick that doesn’t change the nature of the glass. I simply refuse to acknowledge it. But the violation doesn't start in the silver backing. It starts on the mattress. Just as my exhaustion dips into sleep, I feel it. A delicate, distinct weight lands on the edge of the bed. *Thump.* Four phantom paws step lightly across the duvet. Panic spikes, sharp and adrenaline-hot. I lunge in the dark, my hand violently smacking and sweeping across the mattress, desperate to strike solid fur and prove my sanity.

My palm only hits empty blankets.

But the air shifts. There is movement in the peripheral glow of the nightlights—a shadowy presence distinct from the warm illumination. A separate, terrible chill ignites in the room. It’s the same chill I feel when I walk into my children's rooms in the dead of night, only to find them sitting bolt upright in bed, their eyes wide open, staring at the empty doorway while completely fast asleep. There is a parallel consciousness sharing our home, operating on a frequency I can only feel, testing the boundaries of our sanctuary.

Then, the illusion shatters. I wake up gasping, tears stinging my face as the brutal, crushing reality hits me all over again: my parents are dead. The visits are just dreams, beautiful, desperate moments where the veil thins enough for us to laugh and converse again. But when they cross over to comfort me, the threshold stays open a fraction of a second too long. That is how the cat gets in. That is what slithers into the slipstream of their love.

Tonight, as my heart pounds and I feel the mattress depress, the icy chill of the intruder floods the room. But this time, it is met with an overwhelming, radiant heat. I can feel my mother in the room. She isn't bringing the shadows with her; she is screaming—not in agony, but in a ferocious, deafening fury. It’s a warning, and a barrier. I keep my eyes fiercely glued to the floorboards, refusing to look at the dark glass in the corner. I finally understand that she isn't trapped in the mirror. She is standing guard in front of it, holding the line, ensuring that whatever slipped through the door behind her never makes it to my children's rooms.
