The first time my daughter drew the house, I thought it was cute.

She was four years old, sitting at the kitchen table with a pile of crayons scattered around her. I was making dinner while my wife answered emails in the living room. It was a normal evening, the kind you forget until something happens that makes you wish you could go back.

“Look, Daddy,” she said.

I turned around.

The drawing was simple. A square house. A triangle roof. A little tree beside it. A yellow sun in the corner.

Except there was one thing that bothered me.

There was a person standing in the upstairs window.

A little black stick figure.

“Who’s that?” I asked.

My daughter looked confused.

“What do you mean?”

“The person in the window.”

She stared at the drawing for a few seconds.

“That’s not a person.”

I laughed. “Okay. What is it?”

She picked up a red crayon and colored over the figure until it disappeared.

“That’s where she looks from.”

I stopped smiling.

“Who looks from there?”

My daughter shrugged.

“The lady.”

I remember feeling a strange little chill crawl up my arms.

Not fear. Not yet.

Just the uncomfortable feeling you get when your child says something they shouldn’t know.

“Have you seen this lady before?” I asked.

She nodded.

“She watches me sleep sometimes.”

I immediately called my wife over.

She laughed when she heard.

“She probably saw someone on TV,” she said. “Or maybe she’s just making up stories.”

And she was probably right.

Kids say weird things.

Kids imagine things.

That’s what I told myself.

Until she drew the house again the next day.

Same house.

Same tree.

Same upstairs window.

Same black figure.

Only this time, there were two people inside.

I pointed at the drawing.

“Who’s the other person?”

My daughter looked at me like I was being silly.

“That’s me.”

I felt my stomach tighten.

“Why are you in the house?”

She looked down at her crayons.

“Because she wants me to come back.”

I didn’t sleep much that night.

I know how this sounds. I know people hear stories like this and immediately jump to ghosts or imaginary friends. But there was something different about the way my daughter talked about it.

She wasn’t scared.

That was the part that bothered me most.

She talked about the lady like she was an old friend.

A few weeks later, my wife and I took her to my parents’ house for the weekend. My parents live in an older neighborhood, full of houses built in the 1950s. My daughter spent most of the day playing outside.

That evening, she came running inside holding a piece of paper.

“Daddy!”

I looked at the drawing.

My blood went cold.

It was the house again.

But this time, there were details.

The porch.

The cracked window upstairs.

The tree beside the driveway.

I looked up at my parents’ street.

The house directly across from them was identical.

Not similar.

Identical.

I slowly walked outside.

My daughter followed me.

“You know this house?” I asked.

She nodded.

“The lady lives there.”

I stared at the abandoned building across the street.

I had lived with my parents for eighteen years and never noticed it.

The windows were covered with boards. The grass was dead. The mailbox was hanging open.

Nobody lived there.

At least, nobody alive.

I asked my father about it later that night.

His face changed when I mentioned the house.

“What did you say?”

“The house across the street. Why is it abandoned?”

He looked toward the window.

“You shouldn’t talk about that place.”

I laughed nervously.

“Dad, what?”

He didn’t answer.

My mother walked into the room and immediately knew what we were discussing.

“Your father told you not to go near it when you were little.”

I stared at them.

“Why?”

My father sighed.

“Because there was a family there.”

I waited.

“They had a daughter about your age. She disappeared.”

The room went quiet.

“What happened?”

“Never found out.”

My mother looked uncomfortable.

“Her mother spent years sitting by the upstairs window waiting for her to come home.”

I looked back toward the dark shape of the house.

“What happened to the mother?”

My father didn’t respond.

He didn’t need to.

The next morning, I woke up to find my daughter sitting at the kitchen table.

Drawing.

My heart immediately started racing.

“Sweetheart?”

She looked up.

“Daddy, she wants you to stop being scared.”

I walked closer.

The drawing was different.

It wasn’t the house anymore.

It was our house.

Our front yard.

Our upstairs bedroom.

And in the window was the same black figure.

Except now there was another figure beside it.

Me.

My hands started shaking.

“Why did you draw me?”

My daughter tilted her head.

“Because you’re coming too.”

I grabbed the paper and threw it away.

My daughter started crying.

My wife came running in, asking what happened.

I tried explaining, but even as I spoke, I realized how insane I sounded.

A few minutes later, my daughter stopped crying.

She looked toward the hallway.

“She’s here.”

I turned around.

Nobody was there.

Then my daughter smiled.

“She says you finally remembered.”

I froze.

“What does that mean?”

My daughter looked at me.

“She said you lived there before.”

I laughed.

A nervous, forced laugh.

“No, I didn’t.”

My daughter frowned.

“Yes you did.”

She pointed toward the trash can.

At the drawing.

I don’t know why, but I picked it up.

I looked closer.

The house wasn’t just our house.

It was my childhood home.

The one my parents sold when I was six.

The one I had completely forgotten about.

And standing in the upstairs window was the little girl who used to live next door.

The girl who disappeared.

The girl I suddenly remembered playing with every day.

The girl my parents told me I had imaginary friends with.

The girl who used to whisper that one day she would show me her secret room.

I dropped the drawing.

My daughter looked at me.

“Daddy?”

My voice barely came out.

“What?”

She smiled.

“She says she’s glad you’re coming back.”

That night, I searched through old family photos.

I found one from when I was five.

I was standing in our backyard.

Beside me was a little girl.

I had no memory of her.

But written on the back of the photo, in my mother’s handwriting, were four words.

“Do not remember her.”
