The Abyss is a Long Dead Weight

The deep end of the YWCA pool was my first abyss.  
I was six years old, under the tepid instruction of an elderly, sun worn woman who kept her hair bound in a bandanna, a terrestrial keeper of a chlorinated sea. It was midsummer. Up to that point, my aquatic world was strictly defined by where my feet could touch the painted concrete at the bottom of the pool. When the lesson ended, I asked my stepmother to stay. She waved me off, relishing her temporary emancipation from the nagging weight of my existence.  
Excitement surged through my small frame. I rushed the poolside, desperate to immerse myself in the cool, blue envelope I craved in the suffocating heat of the southern summer. I leapt. I braced for the familiar, jarring impact of the bottom, but it never came. There was only an endless, suspended blue. It was too much, too unexpected. A silent panic seized my chest, but an older, primal instinct took the helm. I kicked, broke the surface for a desperate gasp of air, and put my face back into the water, thrashing my way to the wall.  
I DID IT!  
I clung to the edge, chest heaving. No one saw. No one noticed. No one cared. I felt a transgressive thrill, the dark joy of having cheated death and authority in one fell swoop. I should have been punished for leaping into the deep, for flirting with the abyss. Instead, I realized I had the run of the water. The pool seemed endless. I let go of the wall, pushed off, and swam the entire width. By the time we left that day, I had graduated from the shallow end to an hour of jumping off the diving board. The, so far, thrill of my life. 

The water never frightened me again. I gave myself to it with a fearless, reckless abandon. But the water, I would soon learn, has a memory. And it has hands.

When I was eight, we lived in an old, brooding house on Vineville Avenue in Macon. It was a home layered in shadows and history, divided by French doors fitted with heavy crystal doorknobs and veiled in wispy lace curtains. They opened to a stairwell that led up to a second set of doors guarding the hallway to our bedrooms. When those doors were closed for the night, the house would wake.  
Every night, from the dark of our beds, we listened. Footsteps. Slow, deliberate pacing circling the dining room table downstairs. The creak of the floorboards, the distinct swish of the swinging door to the kitchen opening and closing, pushed by unseen, heavy palms. The first night it happened, my stepfather charged down the stairs, racking his shotgun, ready to blow a hole in a trespasser. He found only still air and moonlight bleeding through the lace.  
Eventually, we just let it go. The entity became a strange roommate; it moved things, shifted our belongings, but seemed harmless. A phantom tied to the wood and plaster.  
Until the winter.  
It was a freezing afternoon. A friend and I were playing at the apartment complex next door, where the swimming pool had frozen over. We were pushing a ball across the thick ice. A dozen times, I had leaned over the edge, reached down, and retrieved the ball without incident. But on the thirteenth time, as I leaned over the frozen expanse, I felt it.  
It wasn't a slip. It was a nudge.  
A firm, deliberate pressure against my shoulder that sent me crashing through the ice and into the freezing depths. I gasped, the frigid water seizing my lungs, panic flaring as I scrambled out, soaking my good school jacket. I got in trouble for falling in, scolded for my carelessness. But I knew the truth. The thing from the dining room didn't just walk the floors. It had followed me.  
As I grew older, my intellectual curiosity gave me a terrifyingly easy advantage in manipulating the world, but I longed to chase the burst of adrenaline from challenging my body to perform. Skateboarding, roller skating, break dancing, surfing, water skiing. I lived for the extreme edge of motion. But the entity from Vineville Avenue was always there, waiting in the margins, trying to finish what it started at the frozen pool.  
It never manifested as a ghost I could see; it manifested as physics inexplicably breaking.  
When I was skateboarding down a steep grade, bombing a hill with perfect balance, my back wheel would violently jerk, not from a rock, but from an unseen hand yanking the axle, sending me tearing into the asphalt. When I was break dancing, mid-flare, a sudden, unnatural shift in gravity would pull my legs down, slamming my knees into the concrete as if a weight had been dropped onto my hips. While surfing, a wave wouldn't just crash over me; I would feel a distinct, icy grip wrap around my ankle leash, pulling me down into the churning wash and holding me there just a few seconds longer than the ocean ever could. It was always a pushing, a pulling, a deliberate tripping up.  
But it was in the subterranean waters that it nearly had me.  
I was cave diving in Florida, exploring tunnels so suffocatingly narrow we had to remove our scuba tanks just to fit. We pushed the heavy cylinders ahead of us in the blackness, our flashlights cutting thin beams into the ancient rock. I gripped the regulator in my teeth so tightly my jaw ached, inching forward like a worm in a stone artery.  
Suddenly, the air stopped.  
I inhaled, and the regulator delivered nothing but a dead, suffocating vacuum. I tapped the purge valve. Nothing. My pressure gauge read full, but there was a distinct, crushing sensation wrapping around the rubber hose, pinching it shut. The water around me dropped ten degrees in a second. In that tight, pitch-black coffin, the entity was sitting on my chest. My lungs burned. The edges of my vision starred. It held the air back for almost too long, a silent, suffocating eternity where I felt the familiar weight of the dining room footsteps pressing into my spine.  
I didn't thrash. I closed my eyes and reached for the only weapon I had ever found that could break its grip. The Word.  
I didn't need a church crowd, and I had abandoned the social spotlight. What I had discovered was the ancient, binding power of Scripture. In the suffocating dark, my mind screamed the verses I had memorized like incantations. \*“Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil…”\*  
The instant the verse locked into my mind, the unseen hand recoiled. A rush of cold, sweet oxygen blasted through the regulator, filling my lungs. I survived the dive, but I knew the war had escalated.  
This chase led me to the deep ocean. I joined the Navy submarine fleet, plunging into the very domain where the abyss is absolute. I became a nuclear missile tech and teams coordinator. Under the veil of ultimate secrecy, I loaded Navy SEAL teams and their gear onto the boat, slipping them into the dark while the rest of the crew remained blind to who they were or where we were taking them.  
The ocean has a way of making you feel the weight of eternity. I remember standing in the hatch at the top of the sail earlier in the patrol, on a cloudless, moonless night in the middle of the Atlantic. The submarine slid silently through the ink-black, still ocean. The water was like glass. Stars stretched from horizon to horizon, their reflections burning so clearly in the sea that the line between water and sky vanished entirely. I felt a part of a universe vast beyond human imagination, suspended in a cosmic void. I thanked God for the devastating beauty of it.  
But as I looked down into the starlit water brushing against the steel hull, I felt that familiar, creeping cold. It was down there. The thing that walked the floors in Macon, the thing that choked my air in the subterranean caves. It was waiting in the fathomless trenches, biding its time.  
It made its move during the most critical moment of our deployment.  
The mission required a lock-out dive. I climbed into the cold, steel belly of a nuclear missile tube alongside a four-man SEAL element. It was a metal coffin, suffocatingly tight, packed with hardened men, rebreathers, underwater propulsion vehicles, and lethal ordnance. As the teams coordinator, I was the final check, the conductor of this claustrophobic orchestra. Surrounded by the crushing pressure of the deep Atlantic, we sealed the inner hatch.  
I initiated the flooding sequence to equalize pressure so the outer door could open. The cold seawater rushed in around our legs, rising fast. The ocean began to swallow us.  
Then, the mechanical hum of the hydraulics ground to a violent, shuddering halt.  
The water level froze at our chests. The red operational light on the panel flickered, then died. The pressure gauge needles began to violently twitch, the ambient pressure in the tube spiking erratically. It wasn't a mechanical failure; it was a physical impossibility. The temperature of the water plummeted instantly, biting through our thick neoprene suits like liquid nitrogen.  
In the dim emergency glow of the tube, I saw the eyes of the SEAL team leader widen behind his mask. These were men who didn't panic, but they knew the math. If the pressure didn't equalize, the tube would become a compression chamber. We would be crushed, our lungs ruptured before the steel gave way.  
Then, I felt it.  
The heavy, deliberate pressure against my shoulder. The exact same sensation from the frozen pool in Macon. The water in the tube seemed to thicken, pressing against my chest with the weight of unseen hands. It wasn't just a system failure. The entity had followed me into the steel belly of the boat. It was gripping the outer hatch, holding it shut against thousands of pounds of hydraulic force, trying to trap us in the dark.  
I closed my eyes against the freezing water and the rising panic of the men beside me. I didn't reach for the manual override; I reached for my armor. In the crushing dark of the missile tube, my mind screamed the ancient words.  
“He that dwelleth in the secret place of the Most High shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty. I will say of the Lord, He is my refuge and my fortress: my God; in him will I trust.”  
I repeated the Psalm, weaponizing the scripture, hurling it against the dark water, against the phantom weight on my chest. My refuge and my fortress.  
A deafening crack echoed through the water. The hydraulic whine instantly smoothed out. The unseen grip shattered. The outer door swung open flawlessly, revealing the endless, black maw of the Atlantic. The water equalized, the pressure stabilized, and the SEALs deployed into the void, vanishing into the ocean without a backward glance.  
Three days later, the retrieval went off with eerie, mechanical perfection. The tube flooded, the outer door opened on command, the team slipped back in, and the water drained away like clockwork. The system operated exactly as it was designed to.  
Later that night, in the dim, red-lit quiet of the sub's mess, the SEAL team leader sat across from me. He was nursing a lukewarm coffee, his face etched with exhaustion. He stared at the metal table for a long time before speaking.  
"That doesn't happen," he said, his voice a low, gravelly whisper.  
"What doesn't?" I asked, keeping my tone perfectly neutral.  
"The lock-out. The failure." He looked up, his eyes locking onto mine. "This boat is pristine. Checked, double-checked, and checked again. It’s a top-secret deployment platform. Things don’t just stall and then miraculously fix themselves when the outer hatch is engaged." He leaned in closer. "I've been on a hundred lock-outs. When that door jammed... the water felt wrong. It felt like something was pushing back from the outside. Like a physical weight holding the steel shut."  
I held his gaze, nodding slowly, offering a subtle shrug of professional agreement. "Hydraulic anomalies happen under pressure. The ocean is unpredictable."  
He didn't look convinced, but he let it drop, finishing his coffee and heading to his rack. He knew something defied logic down there, but he didn't have the vocabulary for it.  
But I did. As I walked back to my station, listening to the steady hum of the submarine's reactor, I thought of the heavy crystal doorknobs on Vineville Avenue. I thought of the footsteps circling the dining room table. The entity hadn't just tried to drown me in the ice; it had tried to crush me and four innocent men at the bottom of the Atlantic. It was a malicious, stalking evil that used the environment as its weapon.  
The retrieval had been flawless because the entity had already made its play and lost. It knew my armor held. It knew the Scripture was a wall it couldn't breach. But it also knew I had to come back to the surface eventually. It would be waiting.

I survived the submarine, but I never abandoned the water. My love for it was too deeply ingrained, tangled up with my arrogance and a restless, analytical mind that demanded to understand the mechanics of my own haunting. I became hypervigilant near the surf, the rivers, and the pools, but not entirely out of fear. It was out of a clinical, dangerous curiosity. I began trying to attach my intellect to combatting the entity. I needed to dissect it. What was its point? Was it actually trying to kill me, or was the terror itself the harvest? Was it feeding on my panic?  
One night in the St. Marys River.  
I was out there in the dead of night, cast netting for shrimp illegally. I had waded waist-deep into the slow, pitch-black current, my bare feet sinking deep into the viscous, immovable mud of the riverbed until I was practically anchored in place. I was baiting the shrimp with sachets of cheap cat food tied up in knee-high pantyhose, waiting for the surface to boil with silver flashes in the light of the lamp from a nearby dock.  
That’s when the temperature dropped.  
It didn’t happen all at once; it started at the soles of my trapped feet. A freezing, unnatural cold that gnawed at my skin. As the cold crept up my calves and thighs, the sting turned into a searing burn, like wading through crushed dry ice. Then, the current seemed to stop, and the water level began to rise.  
Instinct screamed at me to deploy the armor, to start reciting the Psalms. But my intellect, that fatal hubris that had always driven me to the edge, whispered something else: \*Wait. Let’s see what it can do.\*  
How far could this thing go? Could a localized, malicious spirit actually raise the water level of an entire river just to drown me where I stood? I stood there, shivering violently in the mud, watching the black water creep up my chest. I analyzed the impossibility of it. The physics breaking in real-time. By the time I realized the sheer, overwhelming power it possessed, by the time the freezing water reached my shoulders and the burning cold started to paralyze my diaphragm, I knew it absolutely could, and would, drown me.  
I fired off a Psalm. “Save me, O God; for the waters are come in unto my soul.”  
The verse acted like a concussive blast. The freezing sting shattered, the water level violently receded back to my waist, and the heavy presence vanished, leaving me gasping in the humid Georgia night.  
But as I dragged my net back to the bank, my mind was already spinning. The Psalm chased it away, but what if I used Ephesians? If David’s poetic pleas for deliverance worked, what about Paul’s explicit, militant instructions on spiritual warfare? “Put on the full armor of God, so that you can take your stand against the devil’s schemes.” Would that be a shield, or a sword?  
I stopped avoiding the entity. I started pursuing opportunities to test this abyssal demon. I wanted to map the boundaries of its power and the efficacy of my weapons.  
I took my gear down to Florida and dove a familiar spot: Red Reef in Boca Raton. It’s a beautiful, thriving reef, but it has a notorious ledge that suddenly drops off into the deep blue, plummeting well beyond the limits of recreational scuba diving. It’s a precipice looking down into the abyss. I purposely dove right to the absolute ragged edge of those limits, hovering over the drop-off, waiting.  
I wanted to see if it would mess with me.  
It always did.  
Sometimes it was the feeling of a heavy, icy hand gripping my fin, pulling me toward the dark water below. Sometimes my buoyancy compensator would inexplicably vent all its air, trying to sink me like a stone. Every time it struck, I tested my Scripture.  
I ran through the armor mechanically. I tried the Gospels, the epistles, the prophets. It all worked. The entity was allergic to the Word in any form. But then I started testing the delivery. I recited verses in English, then in Greek.  
Then, I tried Hebrew.  
The first time I formed the ancient, guttural syllables of the Torah in my mind, biting down on my regulator, the reaction was different. It wasn’t just a retreat; it was a violent repulsion. Reciting the verses in Hebrew felt physical, like I was emitting a shockwave into the saltwater. I could almost feel the entity flinch, driven back not just by the meaning of the words, but by the raw, ancient vibration of the original tongue. I felt immensely powerful. I had turned my intellect into a tactical advantage against a supernatural predator.  
But as I sat on the boat after that dive, staring out at the rolling expanse of the Atlantic, a new, unsettling hypothesis formed in my mind.  
I had proven that the entity followed me into the water, and I had proven that the Word of God was its ultimate kryptonite. But what happens when the two forces physically overlap?  
What about the Holy Land?  
If I bought a ticket to Israel, if I strapped a tank to my back and dove into the Sea of Galilee, the very water where Jesus walked, where He commanded the storm to be still with a single word, would the entity from Vineville Avenue dare to follow me into that water? Or is the darkness so ancient, so deeply rooted in the abyss, that it would be waiting for me even there, daring me to test my armor in the very place it was forged?  
I thought I could out-think it. I thought I could turn my spiritual warfare into a sterile science experiment, testing the boundaries of the abyss with ancient Hebrew and calculated exposure. But a man cannot live his entire life walking the razor’s edge of the deep. Eventually, the exhaustion sets in. I needed allies. I needed dry land.  
I traded the ocean for the elevation of the Blue Ridge Mountains. A forty-mile, four-day backpacking trip on the Appalachian Trail with my church group. We were a tight-knit fellowship, men who carried our heavy packs alongside heavy conversations about grace, accountability, and the tangible power of Scripture. My plan was simple: get miles away from the coast, find a quiet clearing beneath the canopy, and finally tell them the truth. I would confess the haunting, expose the entity that had stalked me from the YWCA pool to the nuclear missile tubes, and ask for their help to finally break its grip. After all, what threat could this wet, abyssal demon pose up here, high in the dirt and pine?  
I had forgotten about the rivers.  
This particular section of the trail is scarred by the water. There are six major river crossings. No bridges, no safety lines. Just rushing, frigid mountain runoff spilling over slick, moss-covered stones.  
The first three crossings were shallow, a mere nuisance of wet boots. But the fourth was a swollen, angry artery of the mountain. We had to unbuckle our hip belts, hoist our heavy packs over our heads, and wade into the rushing current waist-deep.  
I was in the middle of the pack, the icy water churning around my torso, when the air died.  
The bright, Appalachian summer afternoon instantly fractured. The ambient temperature of the valley plummeted. A thick, localized fog boiled off the surface of the river, smelling intensely of stagnant salt and old mud, the distinct, impossible stench of the deep ocean, here in the freshwater mountains. The water around my waist stopped rushing past me and began to swirl, tightening like a physical snare.  
Then, I heard my friend ahead of me scream.  
He didn't just slip. He was pulled. His pack pitched sideways as he was violently yanked beneath the churning water. The man behind me shouted, stumbling backward as if an invisible, heavy hand had shoved his chest. The entity wasn’t just targeting me anymore. It was lashing out at the proximity of the light, attacking the men who walked beside me.  
The air grew so thick and freezing it felt like breathing crushed glass. My friends were thrashing in the waist-deep current, fighting a physics-defying undertow that sought to drag them into the rocks.  
"Don't fight the water!" I roared over the deafening rush of the river.  “Put on the armor of God!”  
I didn't have to explain the haunting. The malice in the river was absolute and undeniable. They felt the freezing grip on their ankles; they saw the unnatural, localized dark hovering over the ford. These men knew spiritual warfare wasn't a metaphor. They were believers.  
Still holding our packs aloft, struggling against the unnatural gravity trying to drown us, we opened fire.  
We didn't use our intellects. We didn't use our strength. We used the Word. Voices raw with adrenaline and terror echoed through the ravine, overlapping and cutting through the roar of the water. We shouted the Psalms. We roared Paul’s letters to the Ephesians. English, Greek, fragments of Hebrew, a barrage of ancient, weaponized truth hurled at the malicious cold.  
“The Lord sits enthroned over the flood; the Lord is enthroned as King forever!”  
“For our struggle is not against flesh and blood, but against the rulers, against the authorities, against the cosmic powers over this present darkness!”  
The water began to thrash violently, practically boiling around our waists. The heavy, salt-stained fog thickened, then evaporated, then thickened again. The entity was cornered in the shallow rapids, bombarded by the collective, unyielding faith of a brotherhood.  
"Baptize me!" I yelled, dropping my pack onto a high boulder.  
My friends looked at me, understanding instantly. When I was fourteen, I was baptized to feed my ego, to put on a show in the spotlight. It was an empty dunk. But this, this was a desperate, total surrender.  
Two of the men fought their way through the freezing current and grabbed my shoulders. The water around us was resisting, fighting like a cornered animal, pulling at our legs.  
"I surrender it all!" I screamed into the fog. "I belong to Christ!"  
They plunged me backward into the freezing, violent river.  
The moment the water rushed over my face, the roaring chaos stopped. The absolute, deafening silence of the underwater world took over, but this time, there were no heavy hands. The cold I felt was just the freezing, purifying shock of the mountain runoff. I turned my heart completely to Jesus, not as a shield to fuel my arrogance, but as a Savior to rescue my soul.  
They pulled me up, gasping, into the mountain air.  
The fog was gone. The smell of the ocean vanished, replaced instantly by the crisp scent of pine and wet stone. The water around our waists was just water, cold, fast, and indifferent. The heavy, stalking presence that had followed me, possessed me, I think, since I was six years old was simply... gone. The space it had occupied in my periphery was suddenly hollow, a terrifyingly beautiful emptiness. I felt a rare, impossible grace. I strangely recalled finding a four-leaf clover at Gettysburg, plucking a tiny, green miracle from a field saturated with old ghosts and ancient death.  
We dragged ourselves to the far bank, shivering, exhausted, and fundamentally changed.  
I am safe now. When I get back to civilization, I have a plan. I am going to find a tattoo artist and I am going to carve the verses into my flesh. I will wear the Psalms on my arms and Ephesians on my ribs. If the entity ever returns, it will have to read the Word of God on my skin before it can touch my bones. I want to love the water again. I want to surf, to dive, to float without looking over my shoulder.  
And yet.  
There is a scientific reality I cannot entirely shake. Water is a closed system on this earth. The rains fall, the rivers rush down the mountains, they empty into the sea, and the sun draws the ocean back into the clouds to rain again. Every drop of water we drink, every wave we ride, has been here since the beginning of the world.  
The entity was cast out of the Blue Ridge runoff. It was swept away by the current, sent hurtling down the rocks, bleeding into the streams, and rushing inevitably back toward the fathomless, black ocean where it belongs.  
I sit on the riverbank, the armor of God echoing in my chest, watching the current carry the water away. I am finally free. But as a dark, heavy storm cloud begins to crest over the mountain ridge, bringing with it the scent of impending rain, I look up.  
I know the demon is gone. But I also know where the rain comes from.  
I tilt my head back, close my eyes, and wait for the first drop to fall.  
Let it pour.
