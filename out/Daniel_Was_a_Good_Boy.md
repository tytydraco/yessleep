In my dream, I heard the crying like it was right by my ear, impossibly loud and inside my head.

My eyes shot open.

For a few moments, I was confused as to where I was. After all, it was my first night in the new apartment, and I must have fallen asleep on the couch.

My laptop screen sat illuminated on the coffee table, asking if I was still watching Parks and Rec. It was 3:12 a.m., and the lights were still on.

I had moved the majority of my belongings out of my recently ex-girlfriend's house the day before and driven the U-Haul three hours here, to Richmond, Virginia, where I lay groggy and still clothed in my barely furnished new place.

My heart was racing, and I had sweat through my clothes as if the room were boiling hot, but it was actually cold. The half-eaten pizza box lay askew on the floor next to me, and I sat up on the couch to hit "Yes" on the screen, providing something else to listen to besides the pounding of my heartbeat that rang through my head and throbbed in my temples.

As the show's cold open began playing the familiar and comforting dialogue, I held my head in my hands and massaged my temples as if nursing a terrible hangover. It was an all-too-familiar feeling—but one I hadn't had in months. Not since I gave up drinking as a last-ditch effort to save my relationship with Erin.

I gathered myself and stood up, my back aching from the awkward angle I had slumped into during my slumber on the small couch. The couch we bought together and carried up the stairs of our place in Virginia Beach not one year ago. The couch she told me I HAD to take, as she never liked it in the first place and it reminded her of me.

She loved that couch when we bought it. We made love on it the night we brought it home.

As I stretched and arched my back to relieve the pain, I heard the crying once more, but fainter and coming from out in the hallway.

I blinked hard and shook my head, but the crying continued outside my door—a low, mournful whimpering that was too soft to be the sound I had heard in my dream.

I shuffled over to the front door and peered through the peephole, seeing nothing through the fisheye lens—just the dim yellow haze of the hallway light overhead. The chain latch on the door was in place, preventing more than a few inches of access to the hallway. I opened the door as far as the latch would allow and saw the origin of the noise—a crouching figure on the floor by my door. A woman, on her hands and knees, with her face in her hands, moaning and sniffling into her wrinkled palms as she rocked herself forward and back the way a child would when trying to calm themselves down.

"Hey, uhh, ma'am, are you OK?" I asked through the three-inch gap between the door and the frame.

Her moaning stopped at the sound of my voice, and she slowly raised her head to meet my eyes.

The figure on the ground was an older woman, I guessed in her 70s, who had her salt-and-pepper hair pulled back under a shawl and wore a black overcoat much too large for her tiny frame. Under it, she wore a plain plaid dress with a frayed hem and beige slippers. Her face was red and splotchy from crying, and her irises were the color of deep emerald green against the bloodshot whites of her eyes.

"No, I emm not okay," she answered and looked through the crack in the door the chain latch allowed.

"I mees heem. I mees my Dan-e-yal." She lowered her head back into her hands and continued to sob.

Her accent was thick, Ukrainian, maybe. The woman's hunched back rose and fell with her sobbing.

"Ma'am, are you hurt? Are you looking for someone? It's very late, and I just moved here—I don't know anyone else in the building. I don't know a Daniel."

She spoke her reply into her hands, still rocking in place on the floor.

"Dan-e-yal leaved here. Een this apartment. He was… my… booooooy."

She drew the word "boy" out into a heightened, longer moan as her crying resumed.

I was at a loss for how to help this woman and wished I had never opened the door and engaged her in a dialogue.

"Well, I just moved here, and I don't know a Daniel. Like I said, I'm sorry, but I can't help, ma'am… Uh, is there anyone I can call for you? Someone who can come help?"

"I only… had… my Dan-e-yal," she wept into her hands.

I stood on the other side of the door and looked at the other apartments that lined the hallway, all closed. Surely I couldn't be the only one to hear this woman crying by herself at 3 a.m. I suddenly felt like I was being pranked, or worse, set up.

"Well, I'm very sorry, I—I can call the police or get some help, but I don't—" I spoke through the gap.

"No—do not call police on me, please. I am sorry, I am so sorry, I vill leave." The woman made an attempt to compose herself and get up as she said this, but a shaky foot gave way, and she fell back pitifully in a heap on the floor.

*Jesus, man, for fuck's sake, help the poor old woman up,* I heard my conscience tell me.

"No, I wasn't calling the police on you. I just wanted to find some help for you to get where you need to be—" I unlatched the chain and opened the door wide.

"Please, let me help you up," I continued.

I stepped through the doorway and bent down to gently take hold of her arm, with a hand on her back. "Here, it's OK, let me help you up."

She groaned as she secured her feet underneath her and pushed her body upright as I helped her stand straight. She was 5 feet 1 inch tall and very frail. I felt shame for not helping this poor old woman the moment I saw her crying in a heap on the floor of the hallway. My father would say, "Boy, what in the hell's wrong with you, not helping a woman in need?"

"Thenk you, young man… thenk you. I em sorry to disturb you at this hour. I am still in mourning for my Dan-e-yal. He was a good boy, and it's not right what happened."

"OK, OK… um, who can I call for you to get you some help? I can walk you down to the apartment office," I said, knowing there was zero chance anyone would be working this time of night.

"I em OK, just hav-ing a haard time. Please, leave me be, and I'll be on my way."

"Ma'am, I'm sorry about your son. Can I get you anything?" I asked half-heartedly as the woman turned to shuffle off down the hallway.

The woman stopped and started to turn back.

"Perhaps… some water? I em verry thirs-ty," she said above a whisper.

Shit. Had to open my big mouth.

"Umm, yes, of course, I'll be right back," I said as my hand reached back for my doorknob. I didn't even have glasses unpacked to pour water into for myself, much less a helpless old lady. I walked back into my apartment, leaving the door cracked open to the hallway.

In the kitchenette, I rummaged through one of three large cardboard boxes I had hauled up the stairs a few hours ago, looking for any kind of receptacle I could manage to pour this woman a glass of water to get her the hell on her way. Why did I have to open the damn door, anyway? Erin wouldn't have opened the door. She would've told me to tell her to get the hell away and stop waking people up in the middle of the night.

Erin never appreciated how thoughtful and empathetic I was, anyway. She only thought about herself, and that's why it was never going to work out. I found a glass fraternity beer mug with change and driving range tokens inside, dumped the contents into the box, and ran it under the sink for a bit before filling it with tap water.

I rounded the counter and saw the door open wide, the woman centered in the doorframe with her head lowered, motionless.

"Um, ma'am, I have some water for you… let's get you some help… downstairs."

She stood still in the doorframe, her face still pointed at the floor. She seemed a bit taller than before, less hunched than she had been in the hallway.

The hallway light behind her flickered, silhouetting her in the frame as I slowed my approach, glass in hand.

"You are a… very kind… man," she spoke finally, her head still down and her body unmoving.

She was one foot inside the apartment, and my spine was tingling now with a new kind of unease—I was scared and unnerved by her posture and wanted this to be over as soon as possible.

"Here, have some water. You can keep the glass." I walked over to the woman and held out the mug in front of her.

"Dan-e-yal was a kind man, too."

Her voice was a dry rasp, and she didn't acknowledge the mug in front of her. The hairs on my neck stood straight up, and I felt a primal fear that burned behind my eyes and made my heart pump so loudly I was sure the woman could hear it.

"Yeah, I bet he was… kind. Umm, listen, I think it's time you're on your way. It's very late, and I have to work… early." I lowered the glass, and the woman took another shuffling step inside the apartment.

"Daniel was a good boy, always helping those in need…" Her accent was gone, and her voice sounded stronger, less muddled. "He would come to the door, too, if he heard someone in pain. He wanted to help an old lady… just like you."

Her head slowly arched upward, and the hallway light behind her flickered again. In the split second of darkness, her face was no longer that of a helpless old lady, but something else entirely—something with all the features of a face: eyes, nose, mouth, and teeth. But the eyes were now black and shiny. Her nose was a small curve, and her mouth had widened to the sides of her ears, hundreds of sharp, needle-like teeth arranged in rows behind her cracked and bleeding lips.

My mind reeled at the sight of what stood before me. I dropped the mug on the ground and screamed as the figure slid forward toward me, the pale and outstretched hand closing across my face with impossible strength.

My scream was cut short. I choked out my last breath against the claw that held my jaw shut and felt the hot breath of her open mouth close over my face before the world around me went black.
