I've had the same nightmare since I was 9 years old.

I'm 33 now. For 24 years, I've woken up from some variation of it, usually sweating, sometimes screaming, and occasionally with the strange conviction that I haven't actually woken up at all. 

It always begins in the woods.

I'm barefoot, walking through wet leaves and mud. The trees are packed so tightly together that I can barely make out the sky. Their branches hang heavy with moisture, and every so often a drop of water lands on my shoulders or runs down the back of my neck. The ground is soft beneath my feet, and the mud makes a sucking sound whenever I pull them free.

I never know where I am. I just know I'm supposed to keep moving.

There's always something behind me.

I never see it, and I never hear it, but I'm certain it's following me through the trees. The forest is otherwise completely silent. No insects, no birds, not even the wind moving through the canopy. Just the sound of my own footsteps and the persistent feeling that something is getting closer.

Eventually, I reach the top of a hill.

That's when I see it.

A hole in the mountainside.

It's enormous, with edges that are jagged in some places and impossibly smooth in others, as though something has bored through the mountain and melted the surrounding rock. It descends farther than I can see. Somewhere deep inside, a sickly purple light illuminates the walls.

I always stop at the edge, unable to bring myself closer.

Then I hear a telephone ringing somewhere inside the mountain and I wake up.

For most of my life, I assumed the dreams were some remnant of childhood, something my brain had held onto and replayed over the years. That was before my uncle died.

Walter left me his property in rural West Virginia. A few dozen acres, an old farmhouse, and a considerable amount of land I hadn't visited since I was a kid.

He'd served in the Air Force when he was younger and spent most of his life working with electrical and communications equipment. He was a quiet man who never married, never had children, and rarely discussed his military service. I spent several summers with him growing up, fishing in the creek, helping him split wood, and getting yelled at whenever I wandered too far into the woods.

I also remember a concrete structure built into the hillside behind his house. I always assumed it was an old storm shelter. Walter told me never to go inside, and I never questioned him.

The property had been sitting empty for several months when I finally went down to sort through his belongings. Most of the house was exactly as I remembered it: dusty furniture, old photographs, and tools hanging in the garage. Behind a set of shelves in the basement, however, I found a narrow door I didn't remember seeing before. The wood around its frame had begun to rot, and I managed to force it open.

A flight of concrete stairs descended into the darkness.

At the bottom was a small room filled with electrical cabinets, old wiring, and equipment that looked like it belonged in a museum. Metal racks lined the walls, and several reels of magnetic tape sat on a desk. There was also a black rotary telephone.

As I stepped into the room, it began to ring. I stood, staring at it. There was no visible connection to the wall, no power cable, nothing that suggested it should work. Eventually, I picked up the receiver.

"Hello?"

For several seconds, there was nothing. Then a man spoke.

"Is this the current owner of the property?"

"Who is this?"

"I'm calling from a secure line. We need to discuss the equipment at your location."

I glanced around the room.

"What equipment?"

"The installation beneath your property. Your uncle was assigned to Kestrel Station. He remained associated with it long after it was officially decommissioned."

One of the cabinets had a faded metal plate bolted to the front.

PROJECT NIGHTFALL - KESTREL STATION

"Project Nightfall?"

"An old research program. Your uncle was responsible for maintaining the communications equipment."

"Why?"

"To monitor an unusual transmission originating from that location."

"What's transmitting?"

"We don't know."

I asked why my uncle had been responsible for maintaining it. The man explained that Walter had signed an agreement requiring the equipment to remain operational for as long as the property remained privately owned. Whoever owned the land inherited that responsibility.

I asked what the equipment actually did.

"It monitors the signal and prevents additional connections from being established."

"Connections to what?"

"We don't know."

I laughed. "You're telling me there's something in my basement that you don't understand, and I'm supposed to maintain it?"

"We're telling you that your uncle understood the importance of keeping it operational."

There was a brief silence before he asked whether I'd experienced recurring dreams since childhood.

I didn't answer immediately. When he asked again, I told him about the woods, the hill, and the opening in the mountain. He listened without interrupting.

"How old were you when those dreams started?"

"Nine."

"Did you spend time on the property around that age?"

"Yes."

He took a breath.

"Then I would strongly recommend that you stay away from the installation. Don't disconnect anything. Don't alter the equipment."

I asked what would happen if I did.

He didn't answer.

The line went dead.

I spent the rest of the afternoon going through my uncle's belongings. In his bedroom, I found a filing cabinet containing old military documents. Most were yellowed and brittle, but one folder was labeled PROJECT NIGHTFALL - 1949.

Inside were photographs of the property before the farmhouse had been built. One showed the concrete installation under construction. Another showed several military personnel standing beside a helicopter.

A report described an aerial survey of the surrounding mountains. The crew had identified an opening in the mountainside that didn't appear on any previous geological survey. A recovery helicopter was dispatched to investigate.

It never returned.

There was no wreckage, no distress signal, and no confirmed explanation for its disappearance. The last recorded radio transmission was included in the report.

"We have visual confirmation of the opening. It appears to descend ... our instruments. There is something ... the light."

I read that sentence several times.

The installation hadn't been built to investigate the opening. It had been built around it.

I continued looking through the records and found several notebooks filled with Walter's handwriting. The early entries were organized and precise, mostly technical observations about signal strength and equipment maintenance. The later ones were different.

He described waking up in unfamiliar places, finding mud on his bedroom floor, and losing entire afternoons. One entry mentioned driving nearly two hundred miles before realizing he was heading toward the property. Another described waking up barefoot in the woods.

I recognized the handwriting, but the words didn't sound like my uncle.

I can feel it when I'm away from the station.

It knows when I am sleeping.

I have begun losing time.

On a page dated several years before his death, he'd written:

It is getting easier for it to move me.

I sat there for a long time, trying to reconcile the man I'd known with the person described in those notebooks.

Walter had spent decades studying whatever was beneath that mountain. The longer he was exposed to it, the worse his condition became. His notes suggested that he was losing control of his own thoughts and, eventually, his body.

The equipment wasn't designed to communicate with whatever was down there. It was designed to monitor its attempts to establish connections with people beyond the mountain.

Walter had been fighting it for most of his adult life.

That night, I slept in the farmhouse.

For the first time in years, the nightmare changed.

I was back in the woods, surrounded by the same wet trees and suffocating darkness. The feeling of being followed was stronger than ever, but when I reached the hill, I noticed someone standing below me.

The purple light illuminated the opening, brighter than I'd ever seen it. The figure was almost swallowed by the darkness between the trees, but I recognized the shape of its shoulders and the way it held its head.

It was Walter.

He wore the same brown jacket he'd been buried in. I tried to call out, but my mouth wouldn't move. He looked directly at me, then slowly raised his arm and pointed toward the opening.

Something shifted inside the mountain.

I woke up on the floor of the farmhouse basement.

My feet were covered in mud. I was standing at the bottom of the concrete stairs, directly in front of the installation door. I don't remember getting out of bed, walking downstairs, or opening it.

The rotary telephone was sitting on the desk.

It began to ring.

I backed away, but it continued until I reached the stairs. I ran upstairs, locked the basement door, and spent the remainder of the night sitting in my truck.

I left the property the following morning.

I haven't returned.

I don't know whether the military is still monitoring Kestrel Station or whether the equipment is still operational.

What I do know is that the dreams have returned.

They're no longer the same.

The woods, the hill, and the hole are still there, but I don't have to climb the hill anymore. I'm already standing at the bottom, close enough to feel the cold air coming out of the opening.

Last night, I saw the helicopter.

It was sitting among the trees, its metal frame twisted and covered in mud. The cockpit was empty, and the doors hung open. I could hear something moving inside the mountain, a low vibration that seemed to travel through the ground and into my bones.

Then I heard Walter behind me.

He told me to wake up.

I tried, but I couldn't move. His voice came again, closer this time, telling me that I needed to get away from the opening.

Then another voice spoke.

It was mine.

"There are so many of them up there."

I woke in my apartment, hundreds of miles from West Virginia. My front door was open, and there was a trail of dried mud leading from the hallway toward my bedroom. My car keys were missing.

I checked my phone and found a missed call from an unknown number at 3:17 a.m. There was a voicemail, mostly static, with a few seconds of silence before I heard my own voice.

"Don't let them turn it off."

I listened to it several times. I couldn't tell whether the message was meant for me, or whether it was something I'd said while I was asleep.

I haven't told anyone about it.

The dreams have become more frequent and the people in them have changed. I no longer see the helicopter crew or my uncle. Instead, I see people standing among the trees. Men and women I recognize from the apartment building, people I've passed in the hallway or exchanged a few words with in the elevator.

They stand facing the mountain, completely motionless.

Last night, one of them turned around.

It was the woman who lives across the hall from me.

She looked exhausted. Her clothes were soaked, and her bare feet were covered in the same dark mud I keep finding on my own.

She looked at me for a moment before speaking.

"How long have you been hearing it?"

I woke before I could answer.

This morning, I found a note taped to my apartment door. It was written in shaky handwriting, and I recognized the name at the bottom immediately.

It was from my neighbor.

All it said was:

I keep dreaming about a hole in the mountain. I think I've seen you there

I haven't answered her.

I don't know what to tell her. I don't know how she could possibly be having the same dreams or whether the equipment at Kestrel Station is still working.

But I've started to wonder if Walter was ever really trying to keep the thing beneath the mountain from reaching me.

Maybe he was trying to keep it from reaching everyone else.

And if that's true, I think I finally understand why he spent so much of his life alone.

He wasn't afraid of what might happen if he got too close to the mountain.

He was afraid of what would happen if it ever learned how to reach the rest of us.
