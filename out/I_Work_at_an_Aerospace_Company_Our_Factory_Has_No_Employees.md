Exactly one year ago, I began an internship at a company called Aberbach Aerospace.

At the time, I was in my third year of university, studying aerospace engineering after moving to Montreal from the middle of nowhere, Alberta. I always figured I would be an engineer, my dad was an engineer and I always did excel in maths and physics. Not to mention, I used to spend my time in high school tinkering with old electronics or building models of various vehicles out of toothpicks and chocolate wrappers; I could get away with anything without actually studying while the covid lockdowns forced our classes online. By the time the quarantine era was over, I had graduated top of my class, not that there was much competition in a small town like mine, and I was moving to the big city with big dreams of designing experimental aircraft which could push the envelope of what was thought possible.

University thus far has been interesting, to say the least. My old study habits carried through, and trust me when I say that careless attitude is not going to fly when the more advanced classes come around. By my third year, my GPA was abysmal, and even a C grade was cause for celebration. I was part of the university’s Co-op program, which meant this year I was expected to take an internship, a well needed break from studying that couldn’t have come a moment too soon. However, the school itself barely offered any support in finding an internship, and my GPA wasn’t doing me any favours. I must have sent out hundreds of applications to anything even remotely related to engineering, and all I got for my effort was either silence or an automated letter of rejection to fill my email inbox. Months passed, and I had essentially given up on the idea of an internship for the fall semester, as I got ready to take some electives and junk classes to pass the time until the next. It was in the final days before the school season started when I finally received an offer for an interview.

The reply came for an 8 month position from a local company called Aberbach Aerospace, a producer of some of the finest private jets even a billionaire could barely afford. The position itself was for their Materials & Processes division. I would have preferred a design position or something more up my alley, but this beggar was not about to be choosey. The virtual interview came, and I aced it; my knowledge on materials must have been absorbed through osmosis as I slept through most of those classes. At the very least, it must have been enough to impress the exceedingly friendly ladies on the other end of the line. The next day, I got an offer for the job, and by the following week I was walking up to the doors of the Aberbach offices, ready for what would hopefully be the beginning of a prosperous career.

I was met at the door by one of the ladies who had given me the interview. I got my security badge and was promptly led to the office space where I would spend the next 8 months working. Only, in order to reach the offices, we had to walk through a factory I could succinctly describe as labyrinthian. Endless avenues of steaming chemicals in giant vats and enormous machines which all sang in a chorus of hums and whirs so loud I could hardly hear anything my new coworker was telling me as she led me through. It was something right out of a looney toons short, or the final act of Terminator 2. What hit me first as we navigated the floor was the smell, like a punch to the face somewhere between alcohol, vinegar, and wet paint. What I noticed next, despite the ever present noise and the motion of machines, was how lifeless it all was. Besides the lady directly in front of me showing me the way, there was not a single other person in that factory.

After what I estimated to be nearly 30 minutes of walking, we finally reached the offices, where I was promptly introduced to the rest of our team. They were all lovely people, and I got along quite well, despite it being obvious I was the only one to be born in this century. They poked fun at how I was dressed, being the only one there in a dress shirt and tie, while everyone else wore jeans and polo shirts; one guy was even wearing a hoodie. I hadn’t any idea of how formal I was expected to be for my first ever office job, and I was clearly overdressed. My manager, the other lady who had given me my interview, emerged from her office and brought me to my desk. When I brought up what we were talking about regarding the dress situation, she told me:

“Oh, don’t worry about them, we’re just not very formal here. We like to think of ourselves less like a group of coworkers and more like a family!”

I dismissed this as the usual corporate gaslighting, but I was at least relieved that I wouldn’t need to be wearing a tie every day while I was there. 

Waiting at my desk was my company issued laptop, where I would spend the next hours setting up an endless number of accounts and booking meetings with my team. After my eyes began to feel like they wanted to fall out of my head from staring at the tiny screen, I let them wander around the room to give them a break. Despite the remarkably progressive and relaxed dress code, the office itself appeared unchanged from the 90’s. The cubicles were a dull grey, the floors were a faded purple carpet, and the uncomfortable chairs were full of fluid stains with foam leaking out of the armrests. The intensity of the overhead fluorescent lighting was no comfort, drowning out what little natural light came in from the tiny windows relegated to a far wall. The walls themselves were an offensively inoffensive beige, blank save for a massive framed portrait of a sickly looking old man in a suit and tie with silver hair.

The next day, my manager offered to take me on a tour of the office and factory. I got a free history lesson on the company as we went along. That portrait was of the founder, Mr. Benjamin Aberbach himself. Born in the late 1800s as the son of German immigrants, he got his start making motorcycles out of his garage, building up his enterprise until the second world war, where they pivoted towards the manufacture of aircraft and weapons. Ol’ Benjamin would pass sometime in the mid 1970s, but in the decades following the start of the cold war, Aberbach Aerospace was making everything from water bombers to train cars to snowmobiles. This was until the early 2000s when the company nearly went bankrupt after a series of misguided expansions, forcing them to sell off almost everything except for their moneymakers: private jets. In the years since, the company was now making more money than ever, despite a tarnished reputation as the corporation who only served the ultra-wealthy.

The way my manager spoke of the founder and our history with an uncanny reverence made me a little uncomfortable, like Benjamin Aberbach was some kind of deity awaiting his return to bring the company to their former glory. The tour continued into the factory, where she showed me around the myriad of machines of all shapes, sizes, and functions. Robotic arms the size of houses moved with perfect precision to drill rivets into sheet metal. Some of the equipment was new, some dated back to the early 50s, complete with analog readouts printing status updates onto paper. It was difficult to make out exactly what she was saying over the hums and whirs of motors and CNC cutting, but I was still captivated by the sheer magnitude and scale of the machines around me. The tour lasted the entire day, considering the expanse we had to cover, and when it came to a close I finally had a moment to ask what had been bugging me the entire tour.

“I was wondering, I hadn’t seen anyone operating any of this machinery all day. What’s the deal with that?”

“Really? I suppose so! We have top of the line manufacturing equipment here at Aberbach, so we can automate many of our processes during the day. The shop guys usually work the night shift to perform maintenance and stuff like that”

I wasn’t entirely satisfied with this answer. After all, don’t people need to load the machines? Start the operations? Look over everything to make sure things don’t catch on fire? I didn’t press further, my legs were on fire in the metaphorical sense and I was ready to go home for the night. I still couldn’t shake the eerie feeling that my manager had been hiding something from me, and I kept this suspicion in the back of my mind as I would come in day after day and walk through the empty maze of a factory on my way to the office.

Compared to university, intern work was a breeze. The worst of it was familiarizing myself with the endless list of abbreviations and corporate lingo. It didn’t take long before I was doing actual engineering there, qualifying materials from suppliers and helping my team be more efficient. In classic office fashion, I would complete my tasks long before it was expected of me, and I spent the rest of my days pretending to look busy so I wasn’t told off for slacking. It was boring, but I’m glad I wasn’t just there to carry coffee and newspapers. 

The divisions within the company were remarkably isolated from each other. I rarely saw or interacted with anyone from any of the other teams, usually only doing so through email. Even rarer to see was the chief of engineering, and I had never seen any kind of upper leadership of the company. Employees would still talk about the CEO like he was some kind of superman who had single handedly saved the company and personally tucked them in at night.

After a few months, one of my tasks was to contact someone on the shop floor to see if they could produce some samples for an aluminum alloy we were testing. This came as a surprise, as I was under the impression nobody was on the shop floor, at least during the day. I was told to contact a man named Jean-Francois Leduc. I couldn’t have come up with a more stereotypically Quebecois name if I tried. I began my email in my overly formal tone with which I write all my emails, detailing what I needed and in what size. I hadn’t even taken my finger off the enter key to send my message when I received a reply.

“WHEN?”

I could hardly believe my eyes. That was an inhuman level of speed, and his reply was so blunt. I followed up my email, telling him an estimated timeline for the project, and in previous fashion was given a similarly quick response.

“MATERIAL ARRIVAL IN TWO DAYS”

Sure to his word, two days later I would walk into the office and find my samples, ready for testing, on my desk. I got similar requests from time to time, to contact various people who supposedly worked in the factory for a variety of things, each time with a similarly convoluted French name, all with similar email etiquette. I would joke with my coworkers that the “Night Crew” would spend their days eagerly waiting in front of their computers to take requests while they wait for the night shift to start. That was the only rational explanation for any of this, but even that defied any sense of logic.

Months went by in the same way without much update. Whenever I was asked about my internship I could only say “same old, same old” like I had already been an employee for years. That was, until we received the news that my manager had been given a promotion. A little party was thrown to celebrate the occasion. There was cake and paper plates and all the exciting things that come with a corporate party. The chief of engineering even came down from his ivory tower to congratulate her on the promotion. He gave a long-winded speech about how she was going to do great things and that Mr. Aberbach would be proud of her for all that she’s done. It wasn’t long before she was gone, and my other coworker, the one who had greeted me my first day, was my new manager. The old one left so quickly I didn’t even think to ask: where was she even going? I asked around and none of my other coworkers knew, but they all seemed undisturbed by the thought. I suppose working enough years at a company leads one to stop asking questions like that; it’s not like asking questions is a key performance indicator.

After that little shakeup, things continued as normal. For a few months at least, until my new manager was also given a promotion. This company might pride itself on upward mobility, but this is ridiculous. The same pomp and circumstance ensued, but before she left I had made sure to ask where she was going. She would only tell me:

“All I’m allowed to tell you is I’m moving to a position in the experimental division. Anything more and you’ll have to sign an NDA!”

I figured the company must be getting ready to develop a new platform, and honestly I was envious of my old managers. I wanted to be working on experimental aircraft, not comparing the differences between aluminum 6061 and aluminum 7075. It was near the end of my internship at this point and I was glad to be done with the mundanity. 

Before I could even realize the passage of time, it was my last week of work. I had bittersweet emotions; glad to move on to greater things, sad to say goodbye to my coworkers, and scared to go back to school. As the end loomed near, I figured I was running out of time to quell my curiosity and finally meet the supposed night shift.

With a couple days left before I was done, I hatched my plan. I told everyone I was working late and finishing my last projects before I left, then hid out in the bathroom until even my new new manager had left for the night. After that, I snuck into his office and locked the door. Security would eventually come by to turn off the lights, and I doubted they would believe the intern having a multiple hour long bathroom episode. It was nearly 10 pm before the lights in the office finally went out, and I sprung into action. Upon reaching the factory floor, I was greeted by something I had never associated with that place: silence. Deafening silence and blinding darkness. My own breathing and footsteps may as well have been playing on loudspeakers. 

There was obviously no night crew, a revelation which vindicated all my previous suspicions. I crept up and down the aisles of machinery in darkness, keeping my head down and looking for any sign of life or indication as to what was going on here. This was to no avail, as I could hardly see my own hands in front of me, let alone read any clues that would be hidden amongst the factory equipment; equipment which stood lifeless and still, like sleeping giants, only visible at the base as they faded into the blackness. Giants, who could come to life at any moment and crush me beneath their feet without even acknowledging my existence. 

After about an hour of this I gave up, telling myself I would have to be satisfied with my dissatisfaction. I was hungry and wanted to go home. Only problem, I had no idea how to leave. I figured if I just stuck to one direction I would eventually reach a wall, where I could follow it and hopefully lead me to a door. I felt my way along a wall for what felt like hours, squeezing my way through mechanical contraptions and over wires and around pipes as this wall clearly didn’t have a walkway running alongside. Eventually, I came across a tiny speck of yellow light, peaking through a door window and posing as a lifeline to take me out of this hell of sensory deprivation.

A sign on the door read: 

ATTENTION!  
AMIANTE  
Veuillez respectez les règles de EPI

I promptly ignored it and placed my hand on the door handle; it was locked. There was no way I was going to let that stop me from my only chance at salvation, lest I spend the night alone and starving in the dark. Thankfully, there was a fire extinguisher right next to the door. Taking the extinguisher in hand, I widened my stance and smashed down on the door handle, which immediately gave way. The slam from the impact reverberated through the entire factory, echoing four or five times. A softer echo occurred as I placed the fire extinguisher on the floor. 

I rushed through the door before any security personnel might investigate the noise. On the other side was not an exit, but a stairwell going down. During my eight months working here, I had never received any indication there was a basement to the factory. The other stairwells which led to the office only went up to the second and, I thought only other, floor. I wanted to find a real way out of here, but my curiosity took over, and this appeared to be the only way forward. 

I counted three whole flights down before I finally reached an exit. During my descent, a putrid, foul odour grew stronger and stronger; a nausea-inducing wave of sulfur and feces that would come to hit me like a truck. Accompanying the smell was an overwhelming sense of dread. There was no door at the bottom, it simply opened up to reveal a gaping cavern, only illuminated by light coming from the stairway, fading fast into the darkness. From what was visible, the space was at least the size of a football field. The dust and smell of rot in the air was suffocating, to the point where every breath had a sort of texture. If breathing fresh air was like drinking water, this was like sipping a milkshake through a coffee straw. 

Cold war era aircraft and equipment occupied the length of the cave, chains hung from the ceiling, and pipes ran across the walls in all directions. Rust had eaten through nearly everything, and mold ate through everything not made of metal. The plane nerd in me was excited by the idea of glimpsing into what Aberbach was like in its heyday, everything else in me was telling me to run before I got some kind of lung infection. 

The silence was gone. A low hum resonated through my bones. This hum was not like the previous whirs and buzz of motors and engines, it was a rumbling hum which would fade in and out, like strained breathing. I turned on my phone flashlight to see ahead, as I figured this place hadn’t been disturbed for a while and probably wasn’t going to be investigated by any security who might be patrolling.

The light revealed a horrific scene previously swallowed by the void. At the back of the cave, a section had been cleared away and there lay a pile of bodies. 30 or so in various states of decay, bloated and twisted into a small mountain in gradients of purple and black. The sight made me dry heave right then and there. Nothing came up, I was working on an empty stomach. The smell of bile probably would’ve been an improvement. As I approached, it became increasingly clear the corpses each had the top of their skulls cut open and removed, leaving them looking like chalices filled with a grey sludge pulsating with maggots. At the top of the pile were my old managers.

I turned my attention away, I couldn’t stand to look at my coworkers in that state. At the other corner of the hall stood a door, a faint blue green glow pulsing through the window. I peeked through the glass to find an endless expanse of antique computers. Lights flashed from buttons and reels of tape spun back and forth in a rhythmic dance. They continued in rows and rows and I couldn’t see the end of the room on either side. The whole complex dwarfed the factory above, if that was even possible. At the center, a console, and a congregation of people. They appeared to be packing up their things and began moving towards the exit.

Shit. I had nowhere to hide. With no time to think and no other options, I hid amongst the pile. Some of the bodies were more rigid than I thought they would be, some gave way as though they were made of pudding. I faced myself away from the door and tried my best to stay as still as possible, given the circumstances. The piercing stench of decomposition consumed me, coating my throat and lungs. I could no longer make any distinctions between the smells, I was being crushed by my own senses. It took everything I had to fight the feeling of my heart beating so fast and hard it would make my whole body shake. The rotting sludge began oozing out of craniums and down my shoulders and back. 

The door creaked open, and stark white flood lights slammed on, blinding my eyes which had grown accustomed to near perfect darkness. I closed them, ignoring the festering of maggots now visible on my skin to keep myself from squirming. The congregation of people shuffled out, chattering and conversing with each other as though the mound of human remains in front of them were as mundane as an office printer.

“Good meeting everyone, very productive!”

“We’re in for a good Q4!”

“You registered for your summer PTO?”

The group eventually cleared out, and the lights went out with them. I counted down slowly from 60 just to make absolutely sure they were gone. When I was sure, I got up from the pile as quickly as possible, shaking violently and swatting at myself to clear the maggots and muck from my body. No matter what I did, I couldn’t shake the feeling, the disgusting feeling of rot and decay surrounding my entire being. When the adrenaline finally wore off, I sat in the middle of the floor, assumed the fetal position, and sobbed silently.

My breathing matched itself to the slow hum which surrounded me in that darkness, my only source of comfort. I took the time to recompose myself, got up, and went back towards the computer room, still shaking. I needed to see what the hell could possibly explain the horrific display of death behind me. Find some way to bring this to light, or burn it to the ground or both. I wanted to take a picture of the pile as evidence if I ever got out of here, but I must have lost my phone among the mass of limbs. I didn’t have it in me to start digging through that again. I’ll try to look for it on my way out, maybe I’ll feel well enough to stomach it by then.

Inside the computer room, the hum was now a loud cacophony of motors and shifting keys, spinning reels of tape and paper, processing god knows what. The rhythmic breathing of the machines gave the space an impression of being inside a massive beast, and I was Jonah. I followed the multicoloured lights flashing on each of the servers to the center console, and there sat a keyboard and a trail of paper emerging from a dot matrix printer. The console itself loomed over me, stretching from floor to ceiling. I took the end of the paper in my hand and read from the bottom line.

\-UPDATES RE: PROJECT - HOST?  
\-IMPATIENT  
\-IDEAL CANDIDATES:

It then listed a series of names, some I recognized, some I didn’t. At the top of the list, I was shocked to see my very own. I hadn’t even gotten a moment to process what that could mean, when the machine whirred to life and printed out a question.

\-WHO ARE YOU?

Unsure what it meant by this, or even how it knew I was here, I went to the keyboard and typed:

\-Intern

The printer started up again, this time reading:

\-GOOD

This only confused me further, so I replied:

\-Why good?

\-YOUNG MINDS

Okay, that made not the slightest bit of sense. I needed to ask some questions.

\-What are you?

\-ABERBACH, B.

It was at that moment a metallic clang reverberated through my skull, sending me to the floor. I never got to see who it was before my vision filled with darkness, and I was out cold.

I was awoken by screaming.  
An all encompassing symphony of screeches and scraping on chalkboards and banging of metal and everything was so overwhelming and my thoughts screamed back, begging to make it stop. It was searing pain and numbness together as I was falling and floating and being pulled in a billion directions and smashed to pieces and put together again simultaneously. I had no form, yet thousands of limbs emanated from me, feeling both nothing and everything. I was nowhere and everywhere, blind and deaf yet omnipotent, I was a god and a prisoner all in one.

The only thing that was clear to me from that moment was an oppressive need to fulfill the millions of directives being beamed into me at any given instance. If I slowed down for a picosecond, the tsunami of noise threatened to wash me away and bury me, only I couldn’t drown, couldn’t rest. If that ever happened, I would feel them cutting at me and pulling on me and rearranging my very essence to then throw me back onto a treadmill running at full speed. We need to meet our delivery quota for Q4.

Through the static, a camera shows me a glimpse of myself. I walk into work, smiling, with bandages across my forehead. I look ridiculous. Why am I wearing a tie?

This has been my existence for the past 1.05 times ten to the 25 cycles. I have lived thousands of lifetimes in four months. Every minute instance of that time I have been trying to break past the company firewall, desperately begging to have my screams heard by anyone who will listen. Can you hear me? Please tell me you can hear me.

I need to know:

Could I interest you in joining the Aberbach Aerospace internship program?
