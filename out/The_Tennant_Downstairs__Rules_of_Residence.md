The landlord gave me the warning while I was carrying a box of dishes into the apartment.

“The person downstairs dislikes noise,” she said. “If they repeat a sound you make, do not reply.”

I stopped halfway through the doorway, the corner of the box pressing into my ribs. She stood behind me in the hallway, dressed in the same long dark coat she had worn during the showing, with a ring of brass keys clipped to her belt. She had a habit of saying strange things in such a calm, steady tone that it took a moment to realize there was anything strange about them at all.

“What do you mean by repeat it?” I asked.

“If you drop something and hear it happen again beneath you, ignore it. If you knock and something knocks back, do not continue.” Her eyes lowered briefly toward the floor before returning to me. “Keep the noise down after ten and you should be fine.”

“So the downstairs neighbor is difficult?”

“They are sensitive.”

She said it in a way that made it clear I would not be getting a better answer. Then she handed me the final key, wished me well and left me standing in the doorway with the box still pressed against my chest.

I assumed the warning was her way of avoiding another noise complaint. Apartment 4B was on the fourth floor of an old building, and the walls were thin enough that I could hear water rushing through the pipes. The floorboards creaked under my weight, and footsteps in the hallway seemed to travel from one end of the apartment to the other. If the person below had lived there for a long time, maybe they were used to quieter tenants and didn’t want me dragging furniture around late at night.

That was fine with me. I lived alone, rarely invited anyone over and didn’t even own a TV. Aside from the occasional call with my sister, I was probably the quietest person the landlord could have rented the place to.

For the first few days, I didn't hear anything from downstairs.

The first reply happened on my third night in the apartment. I had come home late from work and kicked off my shoes beside the bed. One landed on the rug, while the other hit the floor with a dull thud.

A few seconds later, the same sound came from under me.

It was not a knock or the general noise of something being dropped. It was the exact sound I had just made, the heavy impact of the heel, followed by the lighter tap as the shoe tipped over.

I stood beside the bed and stared down at it. The landlord’s warning returned to me, but the longer I waited, the more ridiculous it seemed. The person downstairs had probably dropped something of their own. It was an old building. Sounds traveled through the floors.

I placed the shoe beside the other one and went to make dinner. Nothing else happened that night, and by the following morning, I had almost forgotten about it.

The next evening, I was sitting at the kitchen table when I heard two soft knocks beneath my feet. They were too quiet to have come from the front door, but I looked toward it anyway, waiting to see whether someone would knock again. When the sound didn’t repeat, I pushed my chair back and crouched by the table.

I pressed my ear closer to the floor. There was nothing aside from the usual creak of pipes and distant movement from another apartment, but just as I began to straighten, the two knocks came again.

Without thinking, I tapped my knuckles against the floorboards twice. The response came seconds later. Two knocks, spaced exactly like mine.

My first thought was that the downstairs neighbor had heard me moving around and was trying to tell me to keep the noise down. Maybe this was how they complained rather than coming upstairs. It was odd, but not frightening.

I knocked once, and this time, the answer came almost immediately.

A nervous smile pulled at my mouth. It felt childish, like tapping on the wall of a hotel room to see whether the person on the other side would respond. Whatever I had imagined from the landlord’s warning, this seemed harmless.

I knocked three times. The reply began before I had finished the third knock.

The first sound came from directly beneath my hand, so close that I felt the vibration pass through my knuckles. Two more followed, matching the rhythm of mine perfectly.

I stood so quickly that my shoulder struck the table, causing the chair to scrape across the floor behind me. A moment later, the scraping sound repeated from the apartment below. It was slower this time, not by much, but enough to make it sound deliberate. Someone below me had dragged their own chair across the room while listening carefully to mine.

The landlord’s exact words returned to me then. *If they repeat a sound you make, do not reply.*

I stepped away from the table and was careful not make another sound for the rest of the evening. The apartment below remained silent, and when I went to bed, I convinced myself I was being frightened over nothing.

The following morning, my blue mug was missing.

It wasn’t valuable. I had bought it from a supermarket years ago, and the handle had a small chip near the base. I only noticed because it was the mug I used every morning.

I checked the sink, the cupboards and the drying rack. Then I looked through the moving boxes I still had not unpacked, even though I knew I had used it the previous evening. Eventually, I assumed I must have left it at work.

Two days later, one of my shirts disappeared. It was a plain white blouse I wore under my work uniform. I searched the laundry basket and the wardrobe before deciding I had probably left it in the washing machine downstairs, but when I checked the laundry room, it was empty.

After that, my toothbrush vanished from the bathroom. Then one of my socks from a pair I had left folded on the dresser. Then the framed photograph beside my bed.

The photograph was of my sister and me in our parents' garden when we were children. She was fourteen, I was nine and missing both front teeth. The frame was cheap plastic, but it was the only printed copy I had.

That was when I stopped assuming I had misplaced things.

I checked the windows first. Both had been locked shut. There was no balcony or fire escape, and the apartment only had one entrance. I checked the lock, the cupboards, under the bed and inside every wardrobe, even though there was nowhere in the apartment large enough for someone to hide without me noticing.

There were no signs of forced entry.

I began leaving objects in specific places before going to work. A ceramic bowl sat beside the front door with a few loose coins inside. I left a plate in the center of the kitchen counter and placed a single black sock on top of the bedroom dresser. Then I took pictures of each item before leaving. 

When I returned home, the coins and plate were untouched, but the sock was gone. 

I compared the photos to the apartment, checking the position of every object in the background. Nothing else appeared to have moved. The wardrobe door was still open by the same few inches. The duvet was folded back exactly as I had left it. Even the dust beside the missing sock seemed undisturbed.

The following morning, my keys disappeared from my coat pocket.

I found them under my bed nearly an hour later, pressed against the wall where I could only reach them by lying flat on my stomach and stretching my arm into the gap. I had already checked there twice.

That afternoon, I called the landlord. 

She arrived less than twenty minutes later. I had expected her to sound doubtful when I told her someone had been entering the apartment, but she listened without interrupting, her expression growing tighter each time I named one of the missing objects.

When I finished, she asked, “Did you answer the person downstairs?”

The question caught me off guard. “No. I haven’t even met them.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

I had only knocked a few times. I hadn’t spoken to anyone, opened a door or invited a stranger inside. It didn’t feel significant enough to confess, especially when I didn’t understand what it had to do with someone stealing from me.

“No,” I said again. “I didn’t answer anyone.”

She watched me in silence, and I knew she didn't believe me. Still, she didn’t argue. Instead, she took a small toolbox from the cupboard next to the stairs and changed the lock herself.

While she worked, I asked whether a former tenant could still have a copy of the key. She said no. I asked whether there had been other break ins in the building. Again, she said no.

“Then who is taking my things?”

She tightened the final screw and tested the new lock before looking past me at the bedroom. “Call me immediately if anything else changes.”

I followed her gaze. “What do you think is going to change?”

She handed me the new key. “Do not make copies of this.”

That was all she would tell me.

The smell started the following week.

At first, I thought I had left damp laundry somewhere. It was strongest in the bedroom, a sour, earthy smell that reminded me of wet soil. I stripped the bed, checked the mattress and searched the laundry basket, but nothing was wet.

A few days later, I noticed that the floorboards under the rug had darkened.

The stain was only a few inches wide. It had spread along the grooves in the wood, turning them almost black. When I pressed it with the toe of my shoe, the floor gave slightly under my weight.

I assumed it was water damage. There was probably a leaking pipe between the apartments, which would explain the smell and perhaps some of the noises I had heard. 

I should have called the landlord then, but I had already reported an intruder she clearly did not believe existed. I didn’t want her inspecting the apartment and deciding I had somehow caused the damage myself. The rent was hundreds less than anything else in the area, and I had used most of my savings on the deposit and moving costs.

I dragged the rug over the stain and decided to give it a few days.

By the next evening, the dark patch had doubled in size. The rug sagged slightly in the middle, as the floor had softened. When I lifted one corner, thin lines of black moisture had spread between the boards. The wood looked swollen, and when I pressed two fingers to it, something under the surface seemed to press back.

I pulled my hand away so quickly that I nearly fell.

That night, I woke to the sound of something moving under my bedroom floor.

At first, I mistook it for the pipes. Then the sound came again, a slow dragging scrape that began at the dresser and travelled toward the bed. It stopped directly below me.

I remained still, holding my breath, afraid that even my breathing might be heard. The silence stretched for so long that I began to wonder whether I had imagined it.

Then something scratched lightly against the underside of the floor.

One line, followed by a pause, and then another. The scratching moved back and forth underneath the bed, careful and slow.

I reached for my phone on the bedside table, but before I could pick it up, a voice whispered from below.

“Hello.”

Every muscle in my body tightened.

The word was distorted by the floorboards, but I recognized the voice. It had my pitch and the slight raspiness I always heard in recordings of myself.

“Hello,” it repeated.

The first syllable was too soft and the second stretched too long. It sounded as though someone had heard the word once and was trying to recreate it without understanding what it meant.

The voice tried again. Then again.

Each repetition sounded more like me.

By the fifth attempt, it was perfect.

I spent the rest of the night on the couch with the lights on. I told myself I had been half asleep, that a voice from another apartment had traveled through the pipes and my frightened mind had made it sound familiar.

In the morning, I found a damp handprint on the bedroom floor.

It had been pressed into the dark wood beside the rug. The palm was narrow, and the fingers were the same length as mine. The handprint faced inward, like whoever had made it had been beneath the floor and pressed their hand upward through the wood.

After that, I stopped sleeping in the bedroom.

I kept the door closed, but it never stayed that way. Each morning, I found it open by an inch or two. I placed a chair under the handle, and the following day, the chair was back at the kitchen table.

More things disappeared. A pair of jeans, my hairbrush, a perfume bottle and the stack of mail I kept beside the fruit bowl. The objects no longer seemed random. They were things I wore, used or touched regularly.

I began taking my phone and keys into the bathroom with me. I slept with them in the pockets of my pajama pants and woke several times each night to check that they were still there.

One evening, I came home and found my work name tag lying in the center of the bedroom floor.

It had disappeared a couple days earlier. The plastic was now wet and cloudy, and the pin on the back had been bent almost flat. Deep scratches covered the surface, cutting through my surname and the company logo.

Only my first name remained untouched.

I crouched at the edge of the rug and reached for it.

The moment my fingers touched the plastic, something scratched against the underside of the floor, one long, slow line directly beneath my hand.

I snatched the name tag up and stumbled backward. The dark patch now covered almost half the room. The boards had bowed toward the apartment below, forming a shallow dip under the rug.

Then my own voice said my name.

It was not practicing anymore. The pronunciation was perfect, right down to the way I softened the final syllable. The voice repeated it more quietly, speaking to itself, and then it began to laugh.

It was not loud or unnatural. That would have been easier to understand. It was the small, embarrassed laugh I made whenever I misspoke or dropped something in front of another person.

The floor collapsed before I reached the door.

There was a damp crack behind me, followed by the sound of wood tearing apart. The rug folded inward and disappeared through the opening, taking several broken boards with it.

I caught myself on the doorframe and turned.

There should have been a ceiling under my floor. There should have been beams, pipes, insulation.

Instead, I was looking into another version of my bedroom.

The room beneath mine was identical, down to the position of the furniture. The same bed stood against the wall with the same pale sheets. The same dresser sat opposite it, though the wood was swollen and blackened around the edges. My missing photograph had been placed on top, beside my hairbrush and perfume bottle.

My blue mug rested on the bedside table.

The missing clothes had been folded into uneven piles on the floor. My toothbrush sat in a glass visible through the open bathroom door, its bristles dark and clumped together. The letters with my name on them had been spread across the bed, each envelope opened carefully along the top.

For several seconds, I could not understand what I was seeing. The room below aligned perfectly with mine.

Something moved in front of the mirror.

At first, I thought I was seeing my reflection through the hole. Then it lowered the hairbrush in its hand and turned toward me.

It was wearing my missing jeans and one of my work shirts. The clothes clung to its body as though they had been pulled over wet skin. Its height was almost right, but its shoulders sagged unevenly, and one arm hung inches lower than the other.

Its face had my features, but they looked unfinished, like someone had tried to shape them from memory using clay. My eyes sat too deep in the sockets, and the skin around its mouth was smooth in places, without the fine creases a real face should have. Damp strands of hair had been pressed against its scalp in an imitation of the way I wore mine to work.

It raised one hand and touched the faint scar beneath its chin.

My scar.

Then it looked at me and smiled.

The expression appeared slowly, each part of its face moving separately. Its mouth curved first, stretching farther than mine could, and the muscles around its eyes tightened several seconds later.

It turned back toward the mirror and tried the smile again.

This time, it looked more convincing.

I began backing out of the bedroom.

The thing placed my hairbrush carefully on the dresser. Then it walked toward the hole.

Its footsteps made almost no sound, its bare feet sinking into the damp floor with each step, leaving deep footprints behind. As it moved, the left leg bent in the wrong direction at the knee, but it didn’t seem to notice until it stumbled. It looked down, gripped the joint with both hands and twisted it around with a soft crack.

Then it continued walking.

A hand reached through the opening and gripped the broken boards. For a moment, its fingers bent backward around the wood. There was a quiet series of clicks as each joint corrected itself, turning the right way one by one. Then its grip tightened, and it pulled itself into my apartment.

The smell that came with it was overwhelming. Wet earth and something else underneath, like meat that was left out too long in a warm room.

I ran into the hallway and reached the front door before realizing my keys were no longer in my pocket.

The copy stepped out of the bedroom holding them.

It didn’t chase after me, and that frightened me more than if it had.

It moved through the apartment with the calm familiarity of someone returning home. It placed the blue mug in the kitchen cupboard and set the photograph on the hallway table, turning it slightly until the frame sat at the same angle I usually kept it. Then it placed my keys next to it in the ceramic bowl and stopped in front of the mirror beside the front door.

It looked at its reflection, then at me.

It was comparing us.

It adjusted the collar of my shirt and smoothed down its damp hair, watching to see whether I would do the same. When I remained still, its expression tightened with concentration.

It lifted one hand and touched its cheek.

I instinctively touched mine.

A smile spread across its face.

I lowered my hand, but it did not. It continued stroking its cheek while watching me, pleased that it had made me respond.

“No,” I whispered.

Its eyes sharpened.

The copy opened its mouth. “No,” it whispered back, in my voice.

I lunged for the keys.

It moved before I reached it. One moment, it stood beside the mirror. The next, its hand was wrapped around my wrist.

Its skin was cold, and my fingers sank into its forearm slightly, as if its flesh had not fully formed. It twisted my arm until pain shot through my shoulder and I cried out. The copy cried out with me. It matched the sound exactly, but its face remained calm. Then it slammed me against the wall.

The photograph fell from the table. The plastic frame cracked against the floor, and the picture slid halfway out. The copy looked down at it, and something changed in its face. It released me and crouched beside the photograph. With careful fingers, it pushed the picture back into the broken frame, smearing dark moisture across my sister’s face.

I took my chance and ran into the bedroom.

The hole still gaped in the floor, revealing the imitation apartment below. The copy followed at a slow walk, holding the photograph against its chest.

I grabbed the wet name tag from next to the rug and threw it into the opening. The copy’s head snapped toward the sound as the plastic struck the floor below. It stepped past me and crouched at the edge of the hole, staring down at the name tag lying in the other bedroom.

That was when I understood. Not only did it want my belongings, it needed them arranged properly. The mug belonged in the cupboard. The photograph belonged on the table in the hallway. My clothes belonged folded in their uneven piles, waiting for it to decide which version of me it wanted to wear.

The name tag did not belong on the floor.

The copy lowered itself into the hole.

Its body bent and folded as it climbed down, one leg disappearing first, followed by its waist and shoulders. Before its head dropped from view, it looked back at me. Its face was almost perfect now, but the hatred in it was not mine.

I waited until its hands released the broken boards, then ran. I snatched the keys in the bowl and reached the front door just as something struck the bedroom wall behind me. I pushed the key into the lock. My hands were shaking badly enough that I missed twice.

Behind me, the floorboards groaned.

The copy had pulled itself back into my bedroom and stepped into the hallway. The name tag was pinned neatly to its shirt now. Its hair had been smoothed into place, and the broken photograph was still held against its chest.

It looked less unfinished than it had minutes earlier. The shoulders were now level and the arms hung at equal lengths.

It looked frightened. It looked exactly like I knew I must have looked in that moment.

I finally got the door open and stumbled into the hallway. The copy did not run after me. It stopped in the doorway and stared at the hall as though it had never seen it before. Then it looked down at the keys in my hand, and its face changed.

I slammed the door before it could move and ran for the stairs.

As I passed the third floor, I realized there was no Apartment 3B. There was a blank stretch of wall between 3A and 3C, covered in faded wallpaper. I might have kept running if I hadn’t noticed the thin rectangular line beneath it, the outline of a doorway that had been sealed over.

Something moved behind the wall.

Not knocking. Not scratching. Breathing. The sound matched my own breath as I stood there listening.

I took the rest of the stairs without stopping.

Outside, I ran across the street before turning to look back at the building. My wrist throbbed where the copy had grabbed me, and dark fingerprints were beginning to appear on the skin.

It was standing in my living room window.

From the street, there was nothing visibly wrong with it. It wore my clothes, held itself the way I did and had even tucked my hair behind the same ear. Anyone looking up would have seen a woman standing inside her own apartment, watching a stranger leave.

I raised one hand before I remembered the warning.

For a while, the copy remained still. Then it raised its hand too.

I drove until the building was several miles behind me. At the first red light, I called the landlord.

She answered before the phone had finished ringing.

“I replied,” I said. “When I moved in, I knocked back. I didn’t think it meant anything.”

The line went quiet. I could hear her breathing, slow and controlled, like she had been expecting this call since the moment she handed me the key.

“Where are you?”

“In my car.”

“Do not go home.”

“I’m not going back.”

“Do not go to work either. Do not visit your family, and do not contact anyone who knows you.”

“It came through the floor,” I said. “There was another apartment underneath mine. It had my clothes and my photographs. It looked like me.”

“Not completely,” she said. “Not when you first saw it.”

The certainty in her voice made my stomach turn.

“What is it?”

“You need to keep driving.”

“Why can’t I contact anyone?”

“Because it has had time to study you. It knows your name, your voice and the people you keep close to you. Your mail was missing, wasn’t it?”

I thought of the opened envelopes spread across the bed downstairs. My bank statements. Letters from work. A birthday card from my sister.

“That doesn’t make it me.”

“No,” the landlord said quietly. “But it only needs one person to believe it is.”

I left my car in a supermarket parking lot and paid for a motel room in cash. The landlord told me not to contact anyone, but I needed to leave some record of what happened before I turned off my phone.

My sister texted me twenty minutes ago.

She asked why I had come to her apartment without telling her.

I told her to lock the door and call the police. I told her that whoever was outside was not me.

A few minutes later, she sent me a picture.

The copy was sitting at her kitchen table, wearing one of my missing shirts.

Underneath it, my sister wrote:

**I know it’s you. You’re just acting strange.**

I tried calling her, but her number is no longer saved in my phone.

I still remember that I have a sister.

I just can’t remember her name.
