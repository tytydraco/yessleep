I never believed my brother disappeared.  
I was wrong.



Five years ago, my younger brother, Arthur, disappeared. The police called it a runaway case because his bed was neatly made. But they ignored the details that kept me awake every night: his canvas shoes were still by the door, his jacket still hung in the closet, and his bedroom glass had been wiped completely clean. Not a single fingerprint.

It was my fault. I was the older brother; I was supposed to be watching him that night instead of sneaking out. Since his disappearance, I couldn't stomach my own reflection. Every bathroom vanity and darkened windowpane became a reminder of my failure. I covered every silvered surface in my apartment with heavy bedsheets, terrified of the guilt staring back at me.

Then the Crying Mirror murders began.

The victims were always young adults, seated before antique, full-length panes. Their eyelids were surgically pinned open with steel retractors, forcing them to stare at their own faces until cardiac arrest or blood loss took them. For three grueling years, I hunted the killer across the state, tracking a trail of mutilated bodies.

The breakthrough came with the third victim. Tucked inside her rigid, dead palm was a blood-smeared note. It didn’t just feature the killer's usual tagline (*"Mirrors never lie. People do."*). Scrawled at the bottom was a specific addition: *“He’s waiting for you, Danny.”*

*Danny.* Only one person had ever called me that.

A memory flared in my mind, sharp and biting. When we were kids, I’d frequently find Arthur sitting in the pitch-black bathroom, staring unblinkingly into the dark glass. *“If you look hard enough, Danny,”* he’d whispered, his voice tiny against the cold tiles, *“the boy on the other side stops copying you. He starts choosing.”*

Yesterday, the hunt led me and my partner, Detective Miller, to an abandoned Victorian brownstone. Rain hammered the roof while thunder rolled overhead like distant artillery.

"My daughter's graduating next week," Miller said quietly, adjusting his vest. "Promise me this is the last psychopath we chase."

I nodded, the metallic taste of dread heavy on my tongue.

We pushed the front door open. The air inside tasted of copper and stagnant rot, thick enough to coat the throat. Dust floated through our flashlight beams like drifting ash. Every wall was choked with hundreds of reflections—cracked, tarnished, and swallowing the light.

At the far end of the drawing room stood a massive Victorian frame. Seated before it was a young woman in a white silk dress, soaked crimson down the front. The silver retractors bit deep into her orbital bones, stretching the meat of her eyelids until the skin tore at the corners, weeping a mixture of yellowish fluid and dark blood. Someone had crudely stitched a jagged, bloody smile across her lips.

Resting in her lap was a tape recorder. My hands shook as I pressed play.

Twenty agonizing seconds of complete hissed silence. Then came a wet gasp, a choking breath, and a whisper: *“Detective… you’re late.”*

Before the audio could even register, a horrific, wet tearing sound erupted behind me.

I spun around. Miller dropped to his knees. A jagged blade had sheared completely through his trachea and carotid artery. A violent jet of hot, arterial spray painted my face. He locked his eyes onto mine, a fountain of bloody froth bubbling from his severed windpipe as his fingers clawed uselessly at the air. He collapsed forward with a heavy, final thud.

Standing over the corpse was a tall figure. He stepped into my flashlight beam and slowly peeled away a mask stitched from raw human skin. Chemical burns scarred half his face, but the other half was devastatingly familiar.

"Arthur…" The word was choked with denial. "No. No, you died five years ago. You’re a fucking ghost."

His cracked lips curled into a sickening smile. "I didn't disappear, Danny. I looked into the glass. It looked back. The flesh is just a vessel," he rasped, his voice vibrating with an underlying, metallic resonance. "The glass needs the agony. The screaming feeds the transition. I've worn this skin for five years, but it's rotting. It's time for an upgrade."

Before I could raise my pistol, Arthur rushed me with unnatural, terrifying speed. He slammed me into the hardwood floor, knocking the gun from my grip and driving the air from my lungs. Straddling my chest, he calmly opened a leather surgical case from his coat. Inside, rows of silver instruments gleamed.

"You always avoided reflections after I left," Arthur murmured softly, selecting a pair of cold steel retractors. "So tonight… you'll finally look."

He pinned my head to the floorboards. I thrashed, screaming until my throat tore, but his strength was inhuman.

Then came the metal.

The cold prongs slid beneath my upper lid, scraping against the delicate, hypersensitive surface of the eye. I shrieked as he clicked the ratchet mechanism. *Click.* The skin stretched past its breaking point. *Click.* The sound of muscle fibers separating echoed inside my skull. He did the same to the lower lid, pinning the raw, exposed flesh directly to the bone.

My eyes burned, instantly drying, the veins mapping across the white in angry, bursting crimson. I was entirely helpless, blinded by tears I couldn't blink away.

Arthur dragged me by the hair, leaving a trail of blood and sweat, and propped me up inches from the massive mirror.

"Look," he whispered in my ear.

I looked.

My reflection was entirely autonomous. It wasn't mimicking my agony; it stood perfectly still, watching me with a cold, terrifying serenity. Slowly, deliberately, the copy of me raised its hands, gripped its own jaw, and peeled the skin of its face back, revealing a shifting, chaotic abyss beneath.

The surface of the glass began to ripple like thick, black water. A hand, pale and freezing cold, pushed straight through the solid pane. Those icy fingers wrapped tightly around my wrist.

My reflection *pulled*.

The drawing room dissolved. For one impossible, agonizing second, I was dragged through the threshold into a terrifying expanse. I saw hundreds of victims trapped behind endless rows of glass, their eyes raw and lidless, screaming soundlessly as their fingers wore down to the white bone clawing at the silvered panes.

Every single one of them had my face.

The thing that wore my skin stepped fully out into the warm air of the waking world, while I was pulled backward into the freezing dark. Glass seemed to fill my mouth, my lungs, and my forced-open eyes.

The last thing I saw through the dark, rippling pane was Arthur kneeling beside the mirror. "Finally," he whispered. "The real detective is gone."



The official report said Detective Daniel Hayes vanished during the investigation. The Crying Mirror murders stopped that same night.

Six months later, Detective Hayes returned to work.

He looked the same. Sounded the same. But he was far more efficient now—cold, calculated, and hunting down cases with surgical precision. The entity had learned from past mistakes; it no longer left messy clues or targeted those who would be easily missed. It was perfecting the harvest. His wife remarked that he smiled more, though she couldn't shake a violent chill whenever he entered the room, or the fact that he never seemed to blink.

Last week, another body was found. The tape recorder beside the corpse played twenty seconds of silence. Then, a voice—my voice, stripped of all human warmth and dripping with sadistic pleasure—whispered into the microphone:

*“I found your wife, Danny. She thinks I have your eyes.”*
