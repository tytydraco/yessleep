The sun didn't bring any relief. It just traded the shadows of the night for a grey, bone-chilling morning mist that hung low over the corn stubble.

I didn't sleep another wink. I sat on the edge of the bed with the Remington across my knees until the light started creeping through the gaps in the blinds. Every time a floorboard creaked downstairs or the wind rattled the old aluminum siding, my stomach tightened into a knot.

When the first tractor started up somewhere down the township road, old man Miller heading out early to check his tile lines I finally stood up. My legs felt stiff, like lead.

"Stay in here with Wyatt," I told Sarah. Her face was pale, the dark circles under her eyes telling me she hadn't slept either. She just nodded, holding our boy close to her chest.

I popped the safety off the 870, unlocked the bedroom door, and walked down the stairs. The house was dead silent. I checked the front door and the deadbolt was still locked.

Then I walked into the kitchen and pushed the door open, stepping out onto the porch.

The morning air smelled like wet dirt and rotting leaves. At first glance, everything looked normal. The rusted hog pen was empty, the clothesline hung slack, and the north woodline looked quiet under a blanket of fog.

Then I turned and looked at the back wall of the house, right beneath our second story bedroom window.

The white vinyl siding was completely ruined.

Starting about four feet off the ground and tearing straight up toward the roofline were four deep, parallel gouges. The thick plastic siding wasn't just scratched, it was violently ripped open, exposing the splintered wood sheathing and pink fiberglass insulation underneath. Deep, ragged holes were punched into the wall every few feet where massive, thick nailed fingers had found purchase, using the exterior of my house like a vertical cliff face.

It hadn't jumped. It hadn't flown. It had climbed my house like a bear scaling a cedar tree, its long, backward bending hind legs and inhumanly strong hands digging right into the siding to pull its massive frame up to the second floor.

I stepped down off the porch into the muddy grass, my boots crunching softly on the frost. I walked closer to the wall, shining the morning light over the shredded vinyl.

That’s when I saw the prints in the flowerbed.

They weren't dog tracks. They were wide, splayed impressions left by something heavy four long toes ending in blunt, thick claws, with a deep heel strike that showed it walked on the balls of its feet like a man. And right in the center of the mud, directly beneath the window, was a smear of dark, foul smelling grease mixed with coarse black hairs stuck to the jagged edge of the torn siding.

I swallowed hard, the taste of copper and dry panic rising in the back of my throat.

This wasn't a stray dog. It wasn't a prank, and it wasn't some wild animal passing through looking for garbage. It had tested the house. It had climbed twenty feet straight up into the air just to look my family in the eyes while we slept, testing the glass to see if it would yield.

I walked back to the porch, grabbed my truck keys off the hook by the door, and drove straight into Norwalk. I didn't go to the hardware store this time. I drove past the grain elevator, past the auto parts store, and pulled up right in front of the local gun shop before the owner even unlocked the front gate.

When he flipped the open sign and unlocked the glass door, I walked inside and laid a thick wad of cash on the counter.

"I need buckshot," I said, my voice sounding flat and hollow in the quiet shop. "The heavy stuff. And a few boxes of slugs. As much as you've got."

The old clerk looked at the money, then up at my face. He didn't ask why I needed a small armory on a Tuesday morning. He just turned around, pulled several heavy green and red boxes off the shelf, and slid them across the glass.

"Huron County's been getting rough lately, huh?" he muttered, shaking his head.

"You have no idea," I told him, sweeping the boxes into my bag.

It’s noon now. I’m back at the farm. I’ve spent the last three hours nailing thick sheets of three-quarter-inch plywood over every single window on the second floor, screwing them down until the house looks like it's boarded up for a hurricane.

Sarah is upstairs packing a few bags for her and Wyatt. I told her we’re going to stay at her sister’s place in Cleveland for a few days, that we need a little vacation. I didn't tell her the truth. I didn't tell her that I’m not running away, I’m staying.

Because when I looked at those claw marks on the siding this morning, I saw something else in the torn wood. The scratches didn't go straight up. They curved inward, toward the window frame, like it was trying to pull the glass out.

The sun is high right now, but the days get short fast out here in the flatlands. By 5:00 PM, the shadows will stretch long across the cornfields, and the woods will start to go black.

I’ve got the 870 cleaned and loaded with slugs. I’ve got two boxes of shells sitting on the kitchen table. And out in the driveway, I left my truck running with the high beams pointed right at the back wall of the house.
