 Walking to school one morning, I saw a black dot in the sky. Walking back home that afternoon, the dot had become a black splotch. By dusk, it was clear that there was a black balloon-shaped object hovering over town. A *huge* balloon, like the kind folks filled with hot air to race around the world for three months.

It wasn’t till the next morning that it was light enough to see the guy dangling from the balloon-shaped thing. To be clear, the guy was dead. Visibly dead. Like, but for some jerky-looking skin, he was a skeleton.

The skeleton wore what looked like a military uniform, but no specific nation could be identified. By viewing the floating corpse through a telescope, my science teacher was able to make out lettering on the uniform. It took a trip to the library to figure out the writing was in Sumerian cuneiform.

Someone in my English class said it was “apropos” that the writing on a dead man’s uniform would be in a dead language. Whatever that means.

Of course most folks got freaked out by the dangling corpse. Our town’s police force, however meager that was, tried shooting down the parachute. The fire department got a huge net from somewhere, but the dead guy was just the slightest bit out of reach when they tried to catch him.

So, the corpse dangled there. Plainly visible from whatever part of town you were standing at the time. And here’s the weird thing: it always looked like the skeleton was *facing* you. Seriously, you could move around it in a perfect circle, and those dark, empty sockets would just follow you, with a lipless mouth set in a sneer. I don’t think anyone ever saw the back of him.

Right now you’re thinking “why didn’t the wind carry the body away from the city?” That’s the thing: the day the parachute appeared over town all the winds stopped. Like, just stopped. And our town was in a flat area of the country that always had at least a breeze blowing through.

Humans tend to tune out the sounds of the air, and nature in general. But when that noise is gone, things get eerie. You’ve seen that part in every other movie “It’s quiet, too quiet!” right before the monster or enemy army pops out and attacks the hero. Well, no monster or army popped out to attack us, but it always seemed like something was about to.

It wasn’t like the town went completely soundless. In fact, the noise human voices made seemed extra loud now.

But that doesn’t scratch the surface in describing what a lack of wind did to our town. Birds dropped out of the sky, unable to glide on thermals. Not a couple birds, thousands of them.

 I can tell you this much: the cats in my neighborhood had a feast for the next few days!

Along with the dead winds, the radios were out, too. Satellite signals just seemed to quit within three square miles of the city limits. No internet, no TV, no radio stations, no contact with the outside world. But only inside the town.

You could literally get behind the wheel and drive away from town, in any direction, and once you got past a certain point, the music on the car’s radio would start playing again.

Not coincidentally, it took three square miles outside city limits before the parachute and its grisly passenger were too far away to see.

Our sheriff went to the national guard, asking for help. No one believed his admittedly crazy story, and he got put in the drunk tank for a day, to boot. The federal government wasn’t coming to our rescue.

Our town was tiny to begin with, miles from any highway. The population only shrank further as people started moving away. Can’t say I blame them. Who wants to raise their family with an omen of death hanging over you at all times?

My family wasn’t so lucky. We weren’t among the poor that physically *couldn’t* leave because they legit had nowhere else to go.

Dad just point blank refused to load the family into the minivan and haul ass out of Corpseville. He said we had to stay, because of his job.

My dad wasn’t the mayor or police chief, or anything. He ran one of the two grocery stores in town. But the other grocer took his family and left right at the top of the balloon business, so we were de facto the only place to buy food.

“If we don’t stick around,” my dad told me, “People will starve.”

I couldn’t argue with him there. For my part, I didn’t mind the dead guy so much. Maybe the Republicans were right, and too many horror movies had rotted out my brain and made me a deviant, but I’d watch the parachute through my binoculars every evening after school. That’s how I know that it never moved from the spot where it first appeared. I measured the distances repeatedly. Nothing changed.

My little brother, who already loved ghoulish stuff, would fight me for the binoculars. He was the only person who openly talked about the black parachute, when everyone else tried to ignore it. Pretend like it didn’t exist. Not my brother, though. The phrase “So cool!” came up more than once in relation to the inexplicable cadaver in the sky.

Dad was like most people, ignored the parachute and going about his daily business. But my mom, who had always been kind of religious, was now becoming super Christian.

Despite having a monopoly on groceries, the family store was struggling to make ends meet. The only business that was *thriving* in our new reality was church business.

“The Halls of Glory have been shuttered,” the pastor said the single time mom had successfully dragged me and my brother to church. “We must open them. Open the Halls of Glory!”

The pews were absolutely packed that Wednesday. Pretty soon, they’d run out of space in the physical church building.

 They held revival meetings, out in the woods, where they’d chant the same thing, over and over again. “RELEASE THE HALLS OF GLORY! RELEASE THE HALLS OF GLORY!”  

My brother and I could only speculate what these “Halls of glory” were. The pearly gates of heaven? And what did it mean to “release” them?

To be honest, it sounded like a call for blood sacrifices, but I didn’t tell my brother that. Throughout the ordeal, I still wanted to maintain some of my kid brother’s innocence, y’know?

And he *was* innocent, despite his macabre hobbies. I think that’s why only a little kid could come up with the idea that my brother did.

“We’re going to what?” I’d asked.

“I want to see if we can make contact,” my brother said solemnly.

“Touching the dead guy?” I asked. “Not even the firefighters could do that. You saw the gigantic butterfly net.”

“Have you noticed the change in the air?” my brother asked.

I nodded. “Every day, it feels like it’s getting harder and harder to breath.”

“Well,” my brother grabbed the garden hose. “I’ve been doing some *ex-peer-eeee-mints*!” He pronounced the word with each individual syllable emphasized. “Experiment” had been on his most recent vocab test.

He got an A, incidentally. The whole town was acting like it was doomsday, but school life continued on as usual. Go figure.

My brother threw the hose in the air. You know that Indian rope trick? It was like that. The hose was suspended in the air, held up by nothing.

I swore, then apologized for swearing. “Just how high do you think that thing will go?” Maybe the issue with the firefighters is that the air hadn’t been thick enough at the time when they first tried moving the corpse.

That’s the only way I can describe it. The air was *thick*. No humid exactly, but walking around outside now felt like you were moving in slow motion.

The utility of garden hoses is that you can attach one to other, and then another, to get whatever length you want. We borrowed hoses from our neighbors to the left and right of us. Both had skipped town weeks before, so they didn’t mind.

At the end of our prodding device, I duct taped a bunch of feathers. Feathers just happened to be a material we had a lot of lying around. Apparently, the cats didn’t want to eat them.

“Nice touch,” my brother said.

“Thank you,” I responded.

Using my binoculars and measuring equipment, I pinpointed the spot directly under the parachute. The skeleton glared down at us. My brother gave him his best smile, in turn.

We started hauling up the hoses. “And if making contact works,” my brother said, “Then we can start thinking about actually moving Bernie.” That was his nickname for the dead guy.

“But then he’d be some other town’s problem,” I pointed out.

My brother shrugged, letting go of the hose. It stayed in the air, like it was a kite.

“Screw it,” I said. The air outside was so stale, it was only a matter of time before people started asphyxiating. *Something* had to be done. The town couldn’t take anymore. And besides, all we were doing was an experiment, after all.

It took effort, and my little brother didn’t help much. Not for lack of trying on his part, he just didn’t have the muscle strength for the task. But finally, we just *barely* had the feathers where dead guy’s bare feet were.

Trying to lighten the mood during our gory business, I said “Poke. Poke. Poke,” as we brushed the feathers against the corpse’s bare feet.  

My brother started giggled. “Stop it!”

“Poke!” I said one last time.

And then we weren’t the only ones laughing. Despite there being no change in the skull, it looked like the dead guy was smiling, instead of sneering. I looked into his dark sockets, and I could swear he looked into my eyes.

There was loud laughter. It didn’t seem to originate from the body, but from all around us! It was like thunder from the sky, and I’m a hundred percent certain that everyone who’d stayed in town for these last few miserable months heard it, even if they won’t admit to it.

Then, it started to rain. Heavy rain, which we hadn’t gotten in all this time. And with the rain came wind.

My brother’s makeshift rope dropped to the ground, the metal end clanging against asphalt. We left the feather hose on the ground, and ran for shelter.

When the rain finally stopped, some hours later, and we came back to pick up the contraption, the black parachute and its passenger were gone.

A little tickle had set our town back on the path to normalcy. Radios worked again. The TV played shows, instead of static. The revival broke up before the nascent cult had its chance to “release” its glorious halls, so mom lived.   

In time, most of the families who had left returned. The other grocer was back in business, but our dad didn’t mind.

My brother had some stories to tell, but nobody believed he’d actually been the one to save the town. I mean, who would believe you could lift a curse with just a couple feathers?

And I started writing this as soon as the internet came back on. I’ve drafted it over and over, making sure to get all the details right. I interviewed fellow residents, and consulted the journals I’d obsessively filled up out of boredom while the TV was out.

Look, I know most of you are going to think I made all this up. If I were lying, however, I’m sure I could have come up with a better climax than “We made the dead guy laugh, and he flew away in gratitude.”

 I get it, it’s absurd. But so was the black parachute appearing in the first place. So was everything we’d gone through, and probably now needed therapy for.

Do I have a theory on why what my brother and I did worked? Yeah. I think the entity, whether you call it a ghost or demon, had an intelligent agenda. It wasn’t a grim sign of the end times, like a lot of residents thought. It was watching us – watching all of us – to see what your average little American town would do if it slowly suffocated.   

Whether the last few months was its equivalent of a social experiment, or it was just screwing with us for fun, doesn’t really matter from the human perspective. I’ll say this, though. If a black parachute ever appears above your town, with a dead man dangling from its cord, maybe all the being wants is a little entertainment. Attaching a feather duster to a kite might be all you need to move things along.

That is, unless making it laugh only worked the one time.
