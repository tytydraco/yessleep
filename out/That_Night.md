It happened a little after my mother died. If I were to guess, about a month. My father had always been a stoic man, barely able to show much emotion. Quiet, contemplative, stern, but not uncaring or unlovable. Growing up, he was always there for us, in his own way. He was never exactly like other dads, the ones who’d find ways to prank their sons or just mess around with them. He’d read us bedtime stories, work through homework with us, plan surprise birthdays, and work long hours so we had a roof over our heads. Yet he was always very quiet, always a man who kept to himself. While he could share our problems with him, his answers always tended to be short and simple. 

My mother was the one who could get him out of his shell. Just being around her caused a subtle but noticeable change. The tightness in his face relaxed, his shoulders would slump, and he’d really smile. You can see it in the family pictures, his arm wrapped around my mother’s shoulders, head leaning against hers, their wedding rings prominently displayed, matching red gems gleaming against the gold.

He hasn’t smiled since her funeral, or if he has, I haven’t seen it. That’s how I knew he was broken. He wasn’t in his shell anymore. Nothing was. He still tried to take care of us, but his heart wasn’t in it. I can’t really hold it against him, though, can I? Mom’s death hit all of us hard. She was a constant, always there for us, always ready to gently tell us how we should handle a problem - not by telling us the answer, but by listening. She’d always be there, holding our heads as we cried, whispering how everything would be alright, that we needn’t be afraid of the dark or the thunder or the lightning.

It’s hard to put into words the time after the funeral. It’s all so vague, like poorly drawn dreams in a fog, if that makes any sense. I know they’re there, but it’s nearly impossible to recall them clearly. I was only a kid, processing this tragedy, and I could never see her again for as long as I lived. And after I die…

That night, however, will always be fresh.

Sorry for the tangent; it’s difficult for me not to get sidetracked talking about this. The night in question was at a cabin somewhere outside of Saginaw, surrounded by trees and cornfields. Dad rented it for us, somewhere we could get away and just live in our grief. I don’t know how my dad found it.

Pulling into the driveway, I wasn’t impressed by it. It was a simple, one-story brown building, a bungalow, I guess you could say. There was a TV, a kitchen with an empty fridge, two bedrooms, a bathroom, a few books lying around, and a spotty wifi network. Dad got us settled in pretty quickly. There wasn’t much for my brother and I to do aside from watch the TV, but that was nice. We channel-hopped a bit until we found a show we liked, and just sat side by side.

The next day, we played outside. Tag should be played with more people, but just the two of us was fine. He chased me along the grass, then I’d chase him, and then we’d both collapse from exhaustion, feeling like we’d just thrown a weight off our shoulders. When we came back inside, dad was sitting at the kitchen table, two paper plates before him, PB&J spilling out of the sandwiches.

“Hey, kids,” he said, his voice heavy, “have fun outside?”  
Obviously, we had, but I didn’t know how to answer him. He barely resembled my father, the man I knew so well throughout the years, a man I loved. His eyes were scarlet red, his shoulders were slumped, and he seemed to have shrunk, barely able to tower over us.

My brother nodded, and we took our places at the table, eating our sandwiches.

“You know,” my dad said, “your mother always did love PB&J. She was the one who convinced me to try when we were kids.” His lips quivered. “Yeah… I didn’t like PB&J. See, I was… trying to be different.” The chuckle that crawled out of his lips rattled through the kitchen. “Thought if I was different, I’d be special.” He shook his head slowly. “Your mother, she wouldn’t have it. She was one of my only friends growing up, and when she started pushing for me to try PB&J, I put up a bit of a fight. A bit. Eventually, she wore me down, and, well…” He gestured to our sandwiches. “Now I know how to make PB&J.”

He stood up and walked away, almost sluggish. When his door clicked shut behind him, I glanced at my brother.

“Maybe he already ate,” he murmured, then took another bite.

The day after that was when he changed. I can pinpoint the exact moment it happened. We were lounging on the couch, reading while the TV spoke with a cartoon’s voice. None of us were paying it any attention. Well, when my father finished one book, he would set it down, look through the cupboards, and start on that one. It was when he plucked down a black leather journal. He opened it to the first page and froze. It was bizarre. He stood there, staring into the open page, and I saw color returning to his face. Snapping the book shut, he walked back to his room. He only came out to make us lunch and dinner.

The fourth day was strange. My dad went into town to buy some supplies, leaving my brother and me to watch ourselves. He made sure the doors were locked and that we couldn’t go outside. Said he’d be quick, barely ten minutes. The closest town was at least thirty minutes away, probably more. It took him a lot longer than thirty. When he finally did come back home, he was carrying two grocery bags. I assumed, obviously, he’d bought food, even though our fridge wasn’t in danger of running out.

He didn’t stop in the kitchen. He walked right past my brother and me, straight to his room, without even glancing at us. Just before the door closed, I caught a glimpse of what was in the bags. Clothes. Women’s clothing. All of them a stark white. When the door clicked shut, I turned to my brother. He didn’t seem to have noticed anything. So we just watched the TV and put ourselves to bed.

The fifth day was when things got a little weird. No; that’s underselling it.

I remember walking up to my dad’s voice. A little flutter crossed my heart, think that maybe, he’d finally snapped out of it. Then I heard the words he was saying.

“...break the veil, shred it to pieces…”

I sat up, frowning, head cocked to the side, trying to listen through the wall.

“...let no spell bind you, let no curse befall you, be it of man, God, or demon. Let all who see you love you…”

His voice faltered, and then I heard him sob for the first time. It was a single, low, guttural pop, but hearing it made my heart twitch. It hurt. Hearing such a big, strong man weep like that changes you, especially when it’s your own father.

“Let you return to me.”

A long silence came afterward. I listened, but my father didn’t speak. When I looked over at the other bed, my brother was sitting up as well, staring at the wall. The look on his face must have been the same as mine.

“What was that?” He said it quietly, and while it seemed a question, a single note of certainty betrayed him. I had that same inkling.

The rest of the day was almost normal, if not for my father’s attitude. His shoulders weren’t as slumped as they usually had been; he walked a little faster, and his attitude had brightened. There was still no smile on his face, yet when I looked into it, I almost caught the promise of one. I didn’t feel relieved, however. Neither did my brother. Watching happiness return to our father should have been a cause for celebration, but the look in his eyes was bewildering, intense, desperate. Whatever had gotten into him wasn’t what had gone out.

The sixth day was when it happened, or rather, the sixth night. That day should have felt normal, but as my brother and I sat through it, we didn’t smile. We didn’t laugh. We didn’t even look at our father. He kept mumbling under his breath, and while I couldn’t make out all of it, what I did make out was very, very familiar to me. I’d already heard it yesterday.

After sunset, Dad put us to bed. My brother asked him for a story before he left. When my father turned around, there was a vacant, confused look on his face.

“Not right now,” he said, “tomorrow, we’ll read you a story.”

Then he shut the door.

“Who’s we?” I asked in the darkness.

“Who do you think?” my brother said.

I’m not sure how long it was before the door slammed open. Sitting up in bed, I looked outside. A towering, broad figure was striding through the shadows and moonlight. Under one arm was a roll of fabric. Blankets. Blankets wrapped around… what?

I watched as the figure strode into the trees, then kept going, shrinking away.

“Where’s he going?” my brother asked hesitantly. I turned around and slung back under the covers, pulling them up to my chin.

The next thing I knew, there was pounding at the window, and then my father’s voice was screaming. “Get up! Get up right now, kids, GET UP!”

My eyes snapped open, breath skipping in my throat, hearing his fist bang against the window. I couldn’t move; my body wouldn’t let me.

“Both of you, get up now, open that window! NOW, NOW!”

“Dad?!” My brother had obeyed, head sticking out into the fresh night air. “Dad, what is it? What’s wrong?”

I was looking at my brother when our dad’s massive hands reached under his armpits and yanked him out. My brother gasped, limbs flailing, knocking against the frame. Then he was gone in a flash.

My dad’s head came through next. “Get up,” he said, panting heavily, “and get over here, now! We’re wasting time!”

I did as he told me. I was trying to climb out when he grabbed me, yanking me outside, mumbling something under his breath. He plopped me on my feet in the cold dew-covered grass, panting, voice ragged and scratchy. I looked up at him, trembling myself, a little kid in my pajamas.

“Go get in the car,” my dad said firmly, “both of you. I’ll get the keys, but go!”

My brother was already sprinting, but I couldn’t take my eyes off his pale face, white as the moonlight, without a speck of color. He had glanced over his shoulder, staring off into the woods. I looked but saw only a pitch-darkness gripping the tree trunks.

“What the hell are you still doing here?” my dad hissed, his face suddenly inches from mine. “I told you to get to the car!”

I stumbled back from him, then turned and sprinted away. The car already had one occupant, sitting in the back, head lowered. I stumbled in beside him, and his arms wrapped around me, tight and warm.

“What’s wrong with Dad?” I asked him.

He shook his head, scared. “I think… maybe he’s gone insane.”

I tried to think of something to say when I saw it. Just for an instant, gliding between the trees. A white shape, tall, thin, barely distinguishable yet unmistakable. I leaned forward, trying to see it again, but there was only the night’s shadows.

The door was yanked open as my dad slammed into the driver’s seat. He jammed the key into the ignition, twisted it, and the engine sputtered like a dying toad. “C’mon, goddamn, c’mon!” He slammed his hand against the dashboard, loud as a gunshot.

“Dad, what’s going on?” my brother said, quivering.

If my dad heard him, he didn’t show it. He twisted the keys again, and the engine wheezed to life, headlights spilling over the ground.

Something passed into them. Pale, tall, body so dry the skin clung to bones, a white dress hanging on them. Black tendrils of hair coiled around its face, its mouth stretched open, wet tar flowing over the lips, and the eyes… they were gone.

It raised its arms out on either side of its body, and a long, hungry wail erupted from its throat. Then it flew at the car.

My brother started screaming. I began weeping, my chest heaving with every sob. My dad’s face went pale. For a split second, I knew I was going to die. As a kid, death always sounds so distant and faraway, but it’s not. It’s always right around the corner, scythe held high, waiting for a moment like this.

My dad shifted the car into gear, all but crushed the accelerator, and yanked the steering wheel to the side, barely missing its outstretched hands. Its head turned to follow, the skin breaking apart around its neck as bones violently tore themselves out. The taillights dyed it red a hazy red - then the darkness swallowed it.

We didn’t go back for our stuff until at least a day had passed, and when we did, found the cabin in a state. The walls were clawed to pieces, the TV had been smashed, the windows shattered, furniture ripped apart, and every belonging we’d left behind was covered in black tar, from our shirts to our shoes. Dad was able to convince the owners that some punk kids had broken in, but it was still a hassle arguing about the damages.

The police would interview my dad later in the week, concerning an incident at the cemetery. I’m still not privy to all the details, but we did have to get a new headstone for mom. Sometime after that, I saw my dad standing outside one night before a bonfire. Something flew from his hand onto it, making a black rectangle. The next day, I retrieved that black leather book from the cold ashes, undamaged, and gave it to dad. I don’t know what he did with it.

We have never spoken of that night. Not to anyone. Not even each other. We can’t. No one would believe us. No one would believe why the rate of missing persons around Saginaw has gone up since then. And if they did, what would they say? How can they tell us it’s okay when I was so close I saw the tension lines in its palms? And, worst of all, the golden ring on its finger, that red gem catching the light?
