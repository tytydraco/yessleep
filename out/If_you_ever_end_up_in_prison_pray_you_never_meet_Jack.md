The Executioner   
I never would have gone through with it if someone had told me I’d end up doing life for a robbery. I look back with sorrow on mistakes I can’t undo. I’ve been behind bars so many times I’ve lost count—petty theft, bar fights, and now a murder I didn’t commit. I can still see it vividly.  
My longtime best friend—I’ll call him Scrap, because that’s what he did: rob people for the tiniest scraps he could find—and I had been committing robberies for a while. One night it didn’t go as planned. We were breaking into a man’s house, rummaging through his things, when I dropped a vase. Despite having done this many times, I still dropped it. We heard nothing out of the ordinary, so we kept searching for anything of value. Eventually we reached the master bedroom. I stood behind Scrap as he eased the door open.  
That’s when I heard the loud whack. Scrap got hit. In an instant, he pulled his gun and shot the man. Our lives were over.  
After we left the house, the vase I’d dropped had my blood on it. I guess it cut me when it broke. We took my car. Scrap left the gun in it. They booked me, searched the car, and sent me to prison for twenty to life. Scrap got five years. They extradited him to another prison, which was for the best—because it turned out he lied. He told them I was the one who shot the man, that I had plotted the robbery. I had no way to prove him wrong, so I took the heavier charge.  
I was eating in the prison cafeteria when I overheard two prisoners talking about an executioner the warden was bringing in. Executioner, I thought. How ridiculous. Even though capital punishment is still legal in California, it’s highly frowned upon. You’d have to be a sick son of a bitch for Californians to root for your death.  
Then the alarms rang. Mealtime was over. We walked back toward our cells. In the distance, I noticed two guards and the warden walking with someone I couldn’t quite make out. I stopped in my tracks and stared. As my eyes focused I saw a man who looked about seven feet tall, wearing a brown trench coat, black turtleneck, black fedora, black slacks, and dress shoes. He glanced my way. A sense of uneasiness flooded the air around me. Just as it peaked, a guard yelled at me to get to my cell.  
I’ll never forget that feeling—impending doom.  
I stayed in my cell mostly after that, replaying the murder over and over, thinking about how my closest friend had lied about me, until I finally fell asleep.  
In the morning a guard told me to leave my cell. That was strange; we were usually allowed to stay inside if we wanted. My case was a shut one. Maybe they’d found evidence proving it wasn’t me, but that was a hope I’d already given up on months ago. I asked what this was about. Reluctantly he said everyone was meeting the warden’s new guest.  
Why? Even if the guest was the new executioner, why were we all seeing him? Shouldn’t that be a matter for the death-row inmates? Maybe it was meant to instill fear. He was a big guy, after all.  
The dim hallway lights faded as blinding courtyard light poured in. When my eyes adjusted I saw the warden, nearly every guard the prison had, and the man the prisoners called the Executioner. He was even more menacing up close. His face was pale—handsome, but devoid of life. His eyes were hollow yet piercing, his lips thin. His frame was massive. Those eyes looked predatory: tiny pupils set in light-yellow irises that seemed to scan everyone. I told myself I must be imagining it.  
The warden interrupted my thoughts. “This right here is Jack. He’ll be staying with us the next few weeks. Make sure you treat our guest kindly.”  
Odd words for a prison—a place devoid of hospitality. I always kept to myself for that very reason. My first week inside I’d been jumped and bruised badly. Nothing broken, thankfully. Sometimes I wondered if isolation wouldn’t have been better.  
The warden announced the meeting was over. Jack never said a word. Maybe he was just shy.  
It was time to rest again. Some days and weeks blurred together in the boring routine. Other days the routine became almost unbearable. I never understood how some prisoners counted each day; that alone seemed maddening. But what difference did it make if you went mad in prison? The worst they could do was send you to a psych ward and medicate you until the days truly became blurs. I knew the real worst was solitary. The idea of being away from this hell—from these demons wearing human skin—sounded almost peaceful, yet I knew solitary brought its own kind of hell.  
I finally drifted off. I don’t usually remember my dreams; most nights are just black. This time there were snapshots: my brother with a cap gun, my grandmother driving away, police officers at my grandfather’s door, and then a woman’s face poking out of a rolled-up Persian carpet. Not the way you’d expect if a body were stuffed inside. Her head emerged from the tasseled side, looking straight at me, smiling. Not a grotesque smile—more like a model from a commercial—but the uneasiness was intense. Her eyes looked black in the foggy distance, long hair flowing down.  
I woke gasping.  
And there he was.  
Jack stood staring at me, face contorted, eyes fully widened and almost bulging, smile stretched unnaturally wide across his face as if it hurt, long teeth bared. The moment he noticed me watching, he dropped the expression, made his face appear normal again, and walked away laughing—a low, guttural, almost disembodied sound that offered no reassurance. The laugh only deepened the fear.  
Was the warden simply trying to scare us? Some kind of sick joke?  
I spent hours tossing and turning, jolting awake to check if he was still watching. Eventually exhaustion took me. When I woke it was already morning, and a violent scream jolted me upright.  
The shrieks echoed through the halls. It wasn’t a yell. It was a scream so haunting it could only belong to someone making a last desperate attempt to flee. Maybe a prisoner in psychosis. Maybe someone being stabbed. Or something worse.  
Then the screams formed words: “No, no, no! I don’t want to go back there! Don’t take me back there with that thing! Please just shoot me, shock me, whatever you have to—just don’t take me back there with Jack!”  
Through the open space of the cell block, I saw, one level above, a man strapped to a stretcher being wheeled out by two guards. I recognized him—the prolific serial killer the papers had labeled the Long Beach Butcher. How could someone of his status be so terrified of death, the very thing he had inflicted on so many others?  
He had said Jack’s name. Why was he so afraid?  
An hour later the cells opened. I went down for the same bland breakfast we were served every day. Sometimes I wanted to complain about how bad the food was, but then I remembered what we were. Prisoners. I shouldn’t have been serving twenty years, but they didn’t know that, and I would have done time here eventually anyway.  
That’s when I heard it—the most blood-curdling screams yet. Shrieks of pure torment, the kind of sounds a boar might make while an alligator bit its leg in half. The screams of the execution taking place.  
Maybe something had gone wrong. People sometimes survived the electric chair or botched lethal injections. Or a firing squad. But we were indoors. Whatever the reason, the sound made the already unappetizing food inedible. Dread settled deep in my stomach—the same dread I’d felt the day I heard my life sentence. And the root of it was Jack.  
After that day I avoided looking at him. Whenever I woke from nightmares—or woke at all—I forced my eyes shut. For the first time in my life, I truly feared for it. The fear wouldn’t leave, but I kept moving. I had no choice.  
I walked the yard to clear my head, heading toward the library. Prisoners on good behavior got certain privileges, and I was one of them. Near the phone booths, I noticed no one was around. Unusual, but not unheard of. A guard with a heart tattoo on his neck was lingering nearby, watching the area. I decided to pretend I wanted to make a call.  
“The telephones haven’t been working for about a week now,” he said.  
“Ah, okay. No problem.”  
I walked on, thinking: Hasn’t Jack been here about a week? I brushed the thought aside as wild and went into the library to read something that might ease the boredom.  
Back in my cell I found my cellmate, John. I asked if he knew anything about the phones.  
His eyes went wide. “You think it’s weird too? So I’m not crazy.”  
“Yeah. Only reason I noticed was that guard hanging around them.”  
“It’s worse than that,” John said. “I take it you don’t get many visitors. I call my daughter and wife every other day and see them once a week. The guards told me the visiting rooms were being remodeled. When I asked about the phones they said the tower was down. But you know what really sealed it for me that they were lying to my face.”  
“What?”  
“I snuck a phone in two days ago. I’ve got bars.”  
His face was completely serious—the look of a man who had been keeping a secret so he wouldn’t sound crazy, and I was the first person he’d trusted with it. I nodded. It sounded conspiratorial, yet I couldn’t help believing him.  
As I lay on my bunk, eyes unfocused, I saw him. Jack was on the far side of the prison, little more than a blur at that distance, but he was looking straight at our cell. He couldn’t have heard us—we were too far. A moment later he walked away. I sighed in relief.  
John kept talking, but his words blurred. My mind stayed locked on what I’d just seen.  
I stayed in bed the rest of the day, too scared to leave. Eventually the sun set. The thin strip of light through the barred window faded. I tried to sleep, tried to ignore the feeling of being watched. Just as I started to drift, a whisper came from somewhere near:  
“I know you saw me.”  
My eyes snapped open. In the top-left corner of the cell door bars, a face stared straight at me—eyes wide, smile fully bared, features contorted. I screamed. The face vanished.  
John yelled, “What the fuck, dude? What’s wrong?”  
I stared at the empty spot. “Sorry. Nightmare.”  
But I knew what I’d seen. Not only had I seen him, but the position was impossible. He would have had to hang down from the floor above, and even then his neck would have needed to be completely snapped and twisted to look at me that way.  
I’ve seen terrible things in this prison and outside it. Nothing had ever cost me sleep like this. I didn’t sleep that night, or the next. Only exhaustion finally claimed me on the third night.  
I woke in time for lunch, having overslept. After the past few days, I almost missed the dull routine. I almost missed the days when the worst that could happen was being killed by another prisoner. I hoped Jack would leave soon, but my gut said he would stay longer. There were several death-row inmates here—some in solitary, others walking among us on good behavior.  
My stomach rumbled. Time for more prison slop.  
As I entered the cafeteria a fight broke out. One prisoner stabbed another in the neck. I looked away before the image could scar me further; I’d already seen one murder I never wanted to see again. When I looked back, my eyes locked on the attacker’s face and neck—and the heart tattoo.  
My eyes widened.  
The fight escalated. The warden’s voice came over the speakers ordering the guards in. Several of them pointed guns at the brawlers and the violence stopped. Then the warden appeared at the front of the hallway with Jack beside him.  
“You guys messed up,” the warden said. “You need to be punished. Jack—clean this place up.”  
He walked out and closed the doors behind him.  
Jack moved quietly into the cafeteria, straight to where the fight had happened. One prisoner joked, “What’re you gonna do?”  
Jack raised his hand and brought it down in a single swipe. The prisoner’s upper torso flew across the room. Legs and an arm collapsed to the floor.  
Every face in the room went pale.  
Then Jack bared his teeth, widened his eyes, and contorted his face exactly as he had outside my cell at night. This time his pupils were so dilated his eyes looked like two black pools.  
One prisoner started running. We all did.  
I looked back and saw him rip several more men apart as if they were nothing. I reached the end of the hall and waited. A guard stood frozen in horror, still believing he wasn’t one of Jack’s targets. Jack walked toward him slowly. The guard tensed, raised his gun, and fired multiple rounds. It made no difference. Jack tore the man’s leg off, then crushed his head, and stood up as if nothing had happened.  
Terror became pure dread. No human could take automatic fire like that and keep moving.  
Jack looked down the hall toward me. His hat was gone. Half his head was blown away. Then the truly impossible thing happened: the wound sealed itself shut.  
He darted toward us so fast the sound lagged. Prisoners were torn apart left and right. I ran down the stairs, down another hall, into the guards’ office cubicles, expecting him right behind me. When I turned he wasn’t there—occupied with others for the moment.  
Why were there no guards nearby? They must have fled when they saw the cameras, or known in advance and evacuated. I slipped into the camera room and barricaded the door.  
On the feeds I watched Jack tear into locked cells, ripping steel like paper. Blood sprayed. Screams penetrated the metal door. Three guards in full combat armor with riot shields and shotguns surrounded him and opened fire, blowing chunks of flesh away, even taking an arm. Jack swiped one so hard the armor became a coffin. He bit through another’s neck. He picked up the last and slammed him into the floor hard enough to crack it. Then the missing arm grew back. He stood fully healed, hair coated in blood, shirt destroyed, body glistening red.  
He looked directly into the camera with that same demented smile, bloodshot eyes, and dilated pupils. He knew exactly where I was.  
Another guard fired. I used the moment. There was a large vent in the room and a set of keys on the desk. I unscrewed the bolts with shaking hands, popped the vent open, crawled inside, and replaced the cover behind me, praying he wouldn’t notice.  
I crawled until I reached the solitary wing and saw one of the death-row prisoners in his cell. A loud thud sounded. Jack ripped the padded steel door off. I scrambled forward. The prisoner screamed, then went silent. Jack grabbed the outside of the vent and tore it away. Sweat slicked my palms.  
Please no. Please don’t.  
His head poked through the opening and stared at me. My heart stopped. Then he pulled back. He was too large to fit.  
I crawled faster. Behind me he repeated the process—running between cells, ripping doors, killing, tearing open the vent just inches from my heels.  
Ahead I saw the visiting area: a large empty space of cubicles and glass, doors leading to the main entrance. My chance.  
I kicked the vent cover until it gave way, crawled out, and bolted for the exit. Jack was right behind me. A huge crash sounded as the wall exploded outward. He stood in the rubble. I ran. He chased. Every part of me screamed that this was the end.  
I reached the door and shoved it open just as he swiped. The blow launched me through. My legs broke on impact. I lay there watching him walk slowly toward me.  
This was it.  
My whole life flashed: wanting to be an artist as a kid, whatever that meant; never seeing my brother again; realizing that even after the life sentence I had still had more life left than I’d understood.  
I was ready.  
Then I realized Jack had stopped. He stood just behind the threshold, staring at me from inside the doorway. My eyes grew heavy. I passed out.  
I woke to the sound of beeping and crying. My eyes focused on my brother and grandmother at my bedside. Somehow, despite everything, I had made it.  
Months later, after I had healed a little, they filled me in. The public was told a few prisoners had taken over the prison and shot it up. My old friend—the one who had turned on me—eventually confessed to the original crime. I was transferred to a different prison to serve only five years.  
I can’t use my legs anymore, but that ended up benefiting me here. I stay in the medical wing.  
I was getting ready to nap when a nurse came in and said there was a group meeting. She started wheeling me out. The warden of this new prison stood at the front of the room.  
“Hello, everyone. Next week there’s going to be a new executioner coming to visit the prison. Make sure you treat him with hospitality.”
