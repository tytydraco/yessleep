I stack shelves at a cash and carry on an industrial estate in south Birmingham. Not saying which one. I'm 27, I live with my mum in Erdington and I've worked there three years. I don't do drugs. I have a few pints at the weekend and that's it. Never had anything wrong with my head, never been on any meds, and last week I went to the GP and she said my blood pressure's fine and I should sleep more.

I know everyone who posts here starts like this. I used to read this sub on my break and laugh at people swearing they weren't mental. Yeah.

I tried telling my mum. She gave me the look she gives the woman next door who talks to saints, and I stopped halfway. So I'm writing it here. It's going to be long and probably all over the place, I wrote it on my phone over three nights and there's stuff I can't remember the order of. Sorry.

First, a favour: if you've worked at a cash and carry in south Birmingham and you knew a Maureen on till 4, about 58, white hair pinned back with a clip, who sometimes brought her grandson Tyler in, message me. Anything.

It started on a Monday. Alarm went at 4:50 like always, I reheated yesterday's coffee and left quietly because Mum was still asleep. My phone was on 11% because the charger only works if you bend the cable a certain way and I went to sleep without checking. That doesn't matter at all, I only remember because I spent the whole day scared it'd die.

I got the Cross City line from Erdington, rammed. For anyone not from Brum, it's the train that takes half the city to work, and nobody looks at anybody. Everyone's got headphones in.

When it started getting light I saw the stripe. Pink, dead straight, from one side of the horizon to the other, and the sun wasn't even up yet. Bright pink. Nothing like the dirty orange you normally get.

The whole carriage looked. I'd never seen that, people taking their headphones out to look out the window. A bloke at the back said:

"What the fuck is that?"

Nobody answered. Some kid started filming. Later I looked for that video on Twitter, TikTok, everywhere, and found nothing, but I don't really know what I expected to find. There was an old woman by the doors holding a rosary who didn't look out the window once.

Got to work at 6:30. Clocked in, hi-vis, box cutter, pallet truck. My aisle's 4, rice, tinned beans and cooking oil. Gary, the supervisor, walked past saying we had targets today. He says that every Monday and nobody's ever known what the targets are.

Kyle worked my aisle with me. He'd owed me fifty quid since Christmas and every time I asked he said the same thing:

"Friday, mate, Friday."

That day he came over before I'd even opened my mouth.

"You seen the sky?"

"Yeah."

"Pink, man."

"Probably pollution."

"Pollution doesn't go pink, Ryan."

At lunch the canteen telly had the news on and they showed the same stripe over Tokyo, Lagos, Buenos Aires. They interviewed a scientist who said he didn't know what it was. The woman from the bakery counter crossed herself. Loads of people went out to the car park to look and Gary went after them telling them to get back in.

Got home at twenty past four. Mum already knew, she'd seen it on the telly that morning.

On Tuesday the pink had taken over most of the sky. On Wednesday it went orange at the edges and my uncle sent a voice note to the family WhatsApp saying it was a Chinese weapon. My auntie replied with a Bible verse, my cousin sent a screenshot of some website saying it was a solar storm, and the three of them argued till midnight. Mum left the group. Mum has never left the group.

On Thursday there were no birds. There's a tree outside our house that's always full of starlings, they start screaming at five, and that day there wasn't one. I stood there looking at the tree for a bit before I went to the station.

Gary got everyone together by the loading bay and said the store was staying open and anyone who didn't turn up would get docked a day. Half the team looked like they hadn't slept and came in anyway. Me too.

On Friday the sky came up red and the sun was a dark little dot you could look straight at. The churches on our road opened at five. There's a bloke who preaches outside the Co-op every Saturday with a speaker strapped to a shopping trolley, everyone takes the piss out of him, and on Friday there were people kneeling on the pavement listening.

In the afternoon the store filled up with people who don't normally shop there, loading trolleys with water, pasta, candles and batteries. There was a fight over rice. I saw a bloke get punched over a pack of water, fall and smack his head on the floor, and security just watched. I watched too, to be honest. Someone took him to the bench by the entrance after and I don't know what happened to him.

On Saturday the internet went down. On Sunday I sat on the sofa with Mum listening to a battery radio she's had since the 90s, and she told me about my dad, which she never does. That he left on a Tuesday and left the radio behind. I didn't know the radio was his. It's got nothing to do with the rest of this, but that's what happened on Sunday.

On Monday the sky started making a noise like metal dragging, far off, high up. The whole store would stop to listen. Kyle asked if it was some government test and I said the government hasn't got a sky to test, and he laughed but not really.

The next day I went to work same as always, because what else was I going to do.

Tuesday, 11:40. I'd just looked at the clock over the tills.

I felt it in my chest before I heard it. The oil bottles shook on the shelf. Then came the sound, one note, low, coming from everywhere. The front windows blew out. Every car alarm in the car park went off at once and stopped at once. It lasted about thirty seconds. Some people's ears bled. A girl went down by the baskets and didn't get up.

The lights went off. Someone screamed that it was the end and everyone ran, me included, and I ended up in the stockroom behind a pallet of beans. There's a little tilt window in the stockroom that looks out on the car park.

The tarmac had cracked and white light was coming out of the cracks. And there was something in the sky, coming down. I'm not going to be able to describe it properly. It had wheels around it, one inside another, and the wheels had eyes. It had more than one face. I couldn't tell how big it was, every time I looked it seemed different.

Later, at home, I looked it up and found something like it in Ezekiel, creatures with wheels full of eyes. Some people call them cherubim, others ophanim. I don't know if that's what it was. It's the closest thing I found.

It landed in the car park and spoke. The voice was everyone at once. I heard my mum in there.

"TIME IS UP. EACH ONE WILL BE WEIGHED."

The back of my right hand started burning and a number came up, red, under the skin. 12.

The doors locked themselves. We tried the loading bay and it wouldn't open. People tried smashing the glass that was left and it wouldn't break. Gary climbed up on a pallet with the megaphone they use for offers and told everyone to stay calm and go back to their positions. His number was 94, I saw it when he lifted his hand, and he saw me see it and put his hand in his pocket.

I don't know exactly when the second trumpet was, about two in the afternoon. It came with a smell of sulphur and sewage. The cold room door opened by itself, there was a hot hole in the floor in there, and these thin grey things came out with mouths full of teeth. I'm not going to describe them. One of them grabbed a woman in the drinks aisle and pulled her arm off. I didn't know her, I think she was a customer.

I found Kyle on the floor between two shelving units and dragged him into the stockroom. We stayed in a little cubby behind the pallets. His number was 31.

Then the light ones came in. I call them angels because I don't know what else to call them. They didn't have faces. They were like columns of light with wings, and the air around them wobbled with heat. One went up to an old man in a flat cap by the butcher's counter and he turned to ash. Another lifted a little girl out of a trolley, looked at her hand and put her down on the floor. She sat there crying and nobody picked her up.

I'd like to say I understood the rule, but I still don't. In there everyone had a theory. Brenda said it was sins adding up, like it says in the Bible. The bakery lot thought it was about how you treated people. One bloke swore it was debt, actual bank debt. I don't know what I think. Gary had 94 and he was just a supervisor who was a pain in the arse. There was a girl on the tills with seventy-something who I never saw be nasty to anyone. Mine went up when I wasn't doing anything. Sometimes it went down a bit and I didn't know why. Kyle swore his went down after he prayed, and then it went back up for no reason.

What everyone took from it, right or wrong, was that high numbers went to the hole and low numbers went to the light. Then someone shouted that we had to hand over the heavy ones.

They got Gary first. He tried to run for the loading bay but he had a bad knee. The one who picked up the metal bar was Neil from stock control, a skinny bloke with glasses I'd never heard raise his voice. He said sorry with every hit. I'm not going to tell all of it. They dragged Gary to the cold room and one of those things pulled him in.

Behind the beans I was thinking about all the times he docked someone a day for being late because the train was cancelled. I'm not proud of it. But I thought it.

Neil's number had been low, 8 or 9, and after that it went to thirty-something. He sat on the floor and stared at his hand for ages.

After that time went strange.

I don't know how many days it was. Four or five, I think. Outside it was always the same light and my phone died the first night. Kyle counted by how many times we slept, but nobody really slept.

People formed groups. Darren from the butcher's counter took over the meat section and made people pay for food. The bakery lot kept the oven. Some of them dressed in white from the clothing aisle and said they were the chosen ones. Their leader was Big Steve, ex-security, with 67 on his hand, one of the highest numbers in the store. Nobody had the nerve to remind him.

At one point one of the things from the cold room stood at the end of the cleaning aisle saying it'd knock points off for anyone who brought it someone. I don't know if that was true. Some people believed it. Kerry from till 6 took her husband, Lee, and her number went down, or she said it did, I hadn't seen it before. She spent the rest of the time walking round the store not talking to anyone.

There was a cat as well. A black cat that lived round the loading bay, the delivery lads used to feed it, and on the second or third day it turned up in the stockroom. Kyle wanted to check its paw for a number. There wasn't one. Then it disappeared and I never saw it again. I don't know why I'm telling you this. I thought about that cat a lot.

It was around that day that Maureen turned up in the stockroom with Tyler hanging off her cardigan.

"Ryan, help me hide him."

I knew Maureen to say morning to. She'd been on till 4 since before I started. I'd seen Tyler a couple of times, sitting on the little stool behind her till playing games on her phone. Eleven.

I made room in the cubby. His number was 40. I asked what the hell an eleven-year-old could have done to have 40 and she said she didn't know. She said his mum had died not long ago. Nothing else.

There was another trumpet that night, or what we called night. The floor shook. The things came out of the cold room in a line and one of them grabbed Big Steve by the back of the neck. The ones in white took the white off after that.

Kyle started going strange. He killed a bloke who tried to take our water, I saw that. Later he told me he'd killed two more, and I don't know if that was true. His number was seventy-something. One night he asked me, if he turned into an animal, to stop him. I said he was talking rubbish. He asked again and I said fine.

Then there were more trumpets. I lost count. Kyle said it had been the fifth, Maureen thought it was the sixth. Something started falling on the metal roof, little dots of light, and the sky was black.

Then the voice spoke again.

"ONLY ONE WILL BE WEIGHED BEFORE ME."

That was it. Nobody knew what it meant. Darren decided it meant the last one left alive and said it out loud, and once he'd said it, it was like everyone had understood the same thing. I keep wondering if that's what the voice meant or if it was just Darren.

He went first. He got Ron from the stockroom, 61, thirty years at the place. Ron put his hands up and said he had a little grandson, that the boy lived with him. Darren didn't even answer.

Then he went for Jamie from the bakery, who tried to run down aisle 7 and slipped. And then Chloe from till 3, who'd started a month before and still had the badge with her interview photo on it. She was sitting on the floor by the tills and I don't think she even saw him coming.

The one who stopped Darren was Brenda the cleaner, 63. She threw bleach in his face and hit him with the mop until he didn't get up. Then she stood there pushing the blood towards the drain with the same mop, humming a hymn, until Neil came up behind her, said sorry and hit her with the bar.

After that I can't tell it in order. There was a big fight in the drinks aisle. I didn't leave the stockroom, I heard all of it from there. When it was over we counted and there were seventeen people standing, and after that nobody counted.

Kerry was leaning against the dairy fridges. When Neil walked past she went up to him and asked him to get it over with. He said sorry, and she said it wasn't her he needed to say sorry to. He did it.

There was a couple, Josh from the cake counter and Amy from till 5, who told the whole store they weren't going to fight. They stood in the middle of aisle 9 holding hands. For a while nobody touched them. Then Neil did.

My number kept going up the whole time. I didn't kill anyone in that part. I was in the stockroom holding the cubby door shut.

More time passed, I don't know how much. In the cubby we barely talked any more.

Kyle came back to the stockroom one of those nights talking about Tyler. He said the thing from the cold room had told him the boy was worth forty, that if he took the boy he'd go down to sixty. He was talking fast and looking at the pallet Maureen was hiding behind with Tyler.

I told him to shut up. He said he was only saying.

He stood there a while, with his knife in his hand, looking at the floor.

"You remember what I asked you?" he said.

"Yeah."

"Well then."

"Kyle, you're knackered. Sit down for a bit."

"I just looked at the kid, Ryan. I looked at him and all I saw was a number. Didn't see a kid, didn't see Maureen, nothing. I saw forty."

"Sit down."

"Can't. If I sit down I'll get up and go over there."

He dropped the knife on the floor and kicked it away, over by the pallets. Then he stood there looking at it, and I could see he was measuring the distance.

"Do it now, mate. While I still want you to."

I didn't move for a while. He waited. Then I went over to the pallets and picked his knife up off the floor. It was still dirty. I went back and did it.

"Am I still me?"

"Yeah."

"Alright."

He didn't say anything else.

Tyler saw. I didn't get him out of there in time.

Some people walked into the hole in the cold room on their own. Others disappeared and I don't know how. I never saw Darren again... no, Darren was already dead. Sorry. I mix people up sometimes.

At the end it was me, Maureen, Tyler, Neil, and Connor from fruit and veg, who was 24 and followed Neil around because he was scared of being on his own.

Neil came to the stockroom door with the bar. He said he didn't want to. Maureen said if it had to be someone it should be her, and asked me to keep Tyler's hair out of his eyes, he doesn't like it. Connor held her arms and Neil did it.

I went for Neil. I don't remember it properly. I remember he was stronger and that at the end he wasn't moving.

Connor went down on his knees saying he'd only held her. Tyler picked a pipe up off the floor. I shouted and I didn't hold him back. I was right next to him.

After that me and him sat on a stack of oil boxes. I don't know how long. His number had gone up. I didn't look at mine.

The roof opened.

I've tried to write this part about five times and deleted it. There was one last sound, a light coming down that I can't describe, and I fell to my knees. The light said my name. It said I was the last.

A man came up out of the hole in the cold room. Bald, sweaty work shirt, suit jacket over his arm, smoking. Normal. He talked to the light like they'd known each other a long time.

"Here we are again, then."

The light didn't answer.

The number on Tyler's hand went away. I watched it go. The light took him. I asked why, and the answer was something about children not counting. I don't remember the exact words and I'm not going to make them up. He looked back.

"Ryan..."

And he went.

The man crouched in front of me and said the list had to close, and it closed with me. I asked where the others had gone.

"Some that way, some this way."

I asked what decided it. He took a kitchen scale out of his jacket, the little spring kind, told me to step on it, and the needle went all the way round. He put it away and didn't say anything about it.

I asked for someone at least to remember Kyle, Maureen, Tyler, the people who'd died in there. The light said it couldn't do that.

Then the man explained. I was going to wake up on Monday again, at 4:50, like none of this had happened. The store would be fine and nobody would remember anything. Just me.

"And I'm meant to cope with that?"

"You will. Badly, but you will."

After that I don't remember anything until my phone went off.

I woke up to the alarm. 4:50. Monday.

I sat on the edge of the bed for ages looking at my hand. Nothing there. I cried quietly so Mum wouldn't hear. Then I got the pen I use for date labels and wrote on my hand: Kyle, Maureen, Tyler.

I went to work. The store was fine. Neil said morning to me by stock control. Gary walked past with his clipboard and asked what that was written on my hand. I said it was people's names. He told me to wash it off before the manager saw, I said I wasn't going to, and he shrugged.

I asked the lad on till 2 about Maureen and he said there wasn't any Maureen. I asked Big Steve, who was on the door, about Kyle, and he said nobody by that name had ever worked my aisle. I told the woman in HR it was for a birthday collection and looked at the clock-in records for the last three years. None of them.

On the lunchtime news they said there'd been a freak hailstorm over north Birmingham early Sunday morning. I don't know if that's connected.

The week after I went to the GP, that's when she said the thing about my blood pressure. I asked to be referred to someone to talk to as well. She said it sounded like stress and the waiting list is about four months.

A few days later I saw the woman with the rosary on the train, by the same doors. I sat next to her with my hand on my knee, names facing up. She looked at it for a while and then opened her hand. There was a list in blue biro, about fifteen names, some written over others.

She didn't say much. That the first time she was a kid, at the Cadbury factory in Bournville where her mum worked. That there was another one in a church in '98. I asked if it would happen again and she said next time I should try not to be left on my own. She got off a few stops before mine, and I'm not saying which.

That was all eleven days ago. I still go to work. I write the names every morning before I clock in and by the afternoon they've half faded. I don't know what to do with what she told me.
