I taught grown people to read for twenty-two months, in a school that had been closed since nineteen seventy-six. County literacy program, nineteen eighty-nine. Two evenings a week, one student at a time. The letter from the district said the building was available to us Monday through Thursday. We used Tuesdays and Thursdays. I never asked what Mondays were for. You don't ask a lot of questions when somebody hands you a building for free.

I was twenty-nine. I'd done two years of college and stopped, and the county would hire you to teach without the degree if you'd logged classroom hours and somebody inside would speak for you. That's how it worked there. Hours and a name. The program was hours. Four dollars and seventy-five cents of them, but hours, and after two years of it Marjorie Dunn was going to write the letter.

The north corridor was the only part of the building with power on. Six rooms, and we used one. The fire door at that end didn't latch and never had, and if you let it shut behind you it locked, so if you wanted to go out to your car between students you taped the strike. There was a ridge of old paint on the plate you could feel under your thumb when you pressed the tape flat. And I ended every session the same way. I'd read a page out loud, slow, and they'd follow along with a finger. You hear it right, then you see it, then you have it.

Second week of September I went out to the lot for my bag and came back and the door was taped. I stood there with my roll in my hand. I'd taped it, I must have taped it, except I hadn't, because that was a different tape. Wider. Cloth, not the clear kind I bought at the drugstore. I peeled it off and put mine on and went back in and did the session. Wendell was working through the grocery circular that week, so he'd know what was on sale before he got to the register.

There were four of them. Alma Kessler was sixty-one and had run a farm's books thirty years by memory, asking her husband to read the columns out to her. Ronnie was twenty-two and had come up through the school I was teaching in, which neither of us ever mentioned. A woman named Patrice came eleven times and stopped. And Wendell Boye, fifty-eight, who welded. Wendell could look at a job and tell you what it would cost and how long, to the hour, and he couldn't read the word danger on a drum. He'd been guessing at drums forty years off the shape of the letters and he was tired of guessing.

The program had one rule and it was the only reason any of them walked in. Nobody is told. Not the district, not by name. Not the custodian. Not my mother, who asked every Tuesday how it had gone and got the same three words back. You have to understand what it costs a grown man to sit down across from a woman half his age and sound out danger. They came because I could promise nobody would ever know they had. That promise was the program.

I decided it was the custodian. There had to be one, somebody ran that boiler enough to keep the pipes from splitting, and Mondays were probably his. I never met him. Never saw a mop, or a light on where I hadn't put one, or a truck in the lot. I told myself he came Mondays and I went on taping over his tape for twenty-two months without once stopping to think about why a man with a key to the building would need to tape a door.

I got as far as the phone twice. If you call the district they send somebody out. Somebody out means a report, a report means a roster, and a roster means four names in a county file in the courthouse where Alma's daughter-in-law worked the tax desk. They'd have stopped coming. Not one of them would have said why. They'd have just stopped, the way Patrice stopped, and the program would be a room with a light on and no hours in it. I hung up both times.

October and November and most of December, nothing. The door was taped some nights and not others and I quit keeping track of which. It got comfortable. Winter in that building was the best I ever worked. The radiators knocked, Alma brought pecan bars in a tin, Ronnie got through a whole paragraph without stopping and cried about it and then didn't come for two weeks and then came back.

February. I was reading to Wendell out of a driver's manual, because he'd decided he was going to get a license at fifty-eight, and I stopped in the middle of a sentence to cough. And down the corridor something moved. Not a settle. Not a pipe. A chair, on tile, shifting about an inch, the sound a chair makes when the person sitting in it changes position. Four rooms down, past two closed doors. Wendell looked up and I said it was the building, and I found my place, and I finished the page slower than I've ever read anything.

The third of March. Alma, seven o'clock. We'd been on a page about how a check clears. I read it through and closed the book and we sat there a second the way you do, and I'd started leaving a second there. I'd got into the habit of leaving a second there without ever deciding to. And somebody down the corridor read the next line. Out loud. Slow. Sounding it out the way I'd been teaching them to, a false start, a stop, and then the whole word at once and a little too loud, the way it comes when you finally get it. He had been doing the exercise. That's what I understood, standing there with my hand still flat on the cover of that book. Somebody had been sitting out there through every session I'd run since September, following along, and he'd got far enough to take a run at the next line himself. Alma didn't hear any of it. She was sixty-one and deaf on the left. I walked her out to her car and drove home and sat in my own driveway until the engine ticked out.

I cancelled the twenty-fifth. Strep. I called everybody, and Wendell's number was his sister's and she wasn't home. He came anyway. He'd have let himself in through the fire door the way the rest of us did. They found him the next morning at the bottom of the north stairwell, a part of that building I had never once been in. The county said he fell. He was fifty-eight, he had a bad ankle off a scaffold, and there was no light down there. That's what the county said and I have never had one thing to put against it. The program closed in June.

I went back the one time, in July, to strip the tape off that door and hand the keys in. It came up in a single piece. Underneath it, the ridge I'd been pressing flat with my thumb for twenty-two months was not paint. It was adhesive. Layers of it, hard and yellowed, gone down into the plate, older than the program and older than me. And the top edge of my own tape had been cut across, square, with a blade, and laid back down.

He couldn't come in. That's the whole of it. He was a man who could get into a closed building and could not walk into a lit room and say out loud that he couldn't read, so he took the corridor, and he'd been taking it a long while before there was anybody in there to listen to. I don't know who he was. I don't know whether he was in the building on the twenty-fifth of March. I don't know if he ever got it. I taught thirty-one years after that and never once took an evening class. Not for the money and not as a favour. Whatever needed covering after four o'clock, somebody else covered it.
