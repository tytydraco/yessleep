The penny trick worked for three days.

Every evening before the fog rolled off the bayou, I placed thirteen pennies on every window ledge, the top of the porch stairs, and right outside the front door.

And every night at 2:15 AM, the heavy, wet splashing would start beneath the stilts. The creature would haul its gaunt, eight foot frame up onto the planks, stop dead at the threshold, and begin its agonizing ritual.

One. Two. Three...

I would sit on the other side of the deadbolt with my shotgun resting across my knees, listening to the rhythmic clicking of metal against wood. The beast would count to thirteen, confuse itself, sweep the pile back together, and start over. It was trapped in that loop until the first pale grey of dawn forced it back into the black swamp water.

By night four, I got arrogant. I thought I had solved the monster.

Yesterday evening, exhaustion finally caught up with me. I’d been surviving on two hours of broken sleep a night, jumpy at every creak of the floorboards. As the sun began to dip behind the moss draped trees, I rushed through my routine, laying out the copper coins along the porch by flashlight.

My hands were shaking. My eyes were burning. In my haste, I made a fatal mistake.

On the pile right in front of the front door, I miscounted. I didn't lay down thirteen pennies.

I laid down twelve.

I went inside, locked the heavy steel deadbolts, pulled the window shutters tight, and collapsed onto my cot.

I woke up at 2:14 AM to the sound of heavy iron chains rattling against the porch stilts.

Then came the footsteps. Thud. Thud. Thud. The floorboards groaned under the immense weight as the creature approached the threshold.

I sat up, gripping the stock of my shotgun, waiting for the familiar, agonizing whimpering to begin. Waiting for the slow, mechanical counting to keep it frozen until morning.

Outside, the clicking started.

One.

Two.

Three.

Four...

My breathing slowed. Even in my groggy state, I felt a wave of relief wash over me.

Ten.

Eleven.

Twelve.

Silence.

The counting stopped. There was no sound of coins being swept back into a pile. No confused, animalistic whine.

Just a long, suffocating silence that stretched on for ten agonizing seconds.

Then came a sound that made my blood run colder than the bayou water. A wet, ragged exhalation right against the wood of my front door.

It reached the end of the pile. Without the thirteenth coin to break its logic and force a restart, the spell was broken.

The heavy brass door handle slowly turned.

The deadbolt held, but the wooden frame screamed under sudden, crushing pressure. Then came the sound of yellowed claws scraping against the exterior wood slow, deliberate, like a man testing the strength of a crate before tearing it open.

Panicking, I raised the shotgun and fired straight through the heavy oak door.

The deafening blast filled the tiny cabin with gunsmoke and ringing ears. Outside, the creature let out an ear splitting screech half hound howl, half screaming man and crashed off the edge of the porch into the black water below.

I spent the rest of the night pressed into the corner of the room, gun aimed at the shattered hole in my door, praying for the sun.

When dawn finally broke, I crept out onto the porch. The twelve pennies were gone, crushed into flat disks of metal or embedded deep into the wood. But right beside the splintered doorframe, smeared in dark, foul smelling grease, was something else.

A small, jagged slash mark in the door jamb. And sticking out of the wood was a single, long grey hair... and a scrap of torn fabric.

I didn't stay to clean up. I grabbed my keys, ran to my truck, and drove straight into town to the parish chapel. I found Father Thomas sitting in the quiet nave, wiping down the altar.

When I told him about the twelve coins, he didn't call me crazy. He just closed his eyes, crossed himself, and sighed with a heavy, ancient sorrow.

"You don't understand what the Rougarou is, son," he whispered, his voice trembling. "It isn't just a beast from the swamp. It's a soul trapped in a curse of blood and broken vows. It doesn't want to kill you. It wants to be freed. And if it draws your blood before its seven years are up, the curse passes to you."

He looked me dead in the eye.

"If it bypassed the coins, it knows you're vulnerable now. It knows you're tired."

I drove back to the cabin before sunset. I spent three hours re-checking every single pile of pennies with a magnifying glass. Thirteen on the porch stairs. Thirteen on the window sills. Thirteen at the door. I counted them three times over to be sure.

I locked the doors. I turned off the lights. I sat in the center of the dark living room with my back against the brick chimney, shotgun ready.

It’s 2:30 AM now.

The porch is completely silent. There is no splashing in the swamp below. No footsteps on the cypress planks. No counting outside.

Instead, I heard a faint, wet click from the back of the house.

The lock on the floor hatch, the old root cellar beneath the kitchen that leads directly down into the crawlspace under the stilts just slid open.

Right now, from the darkness of my own kitchen, I can hear the heavy, ragged sound of wet breathing. And beneath it, a muffled, weeping voice, softly counting to itself in Cajun French.

Un... deux... trois...

I'm sitting here in the dark, staring at the kitchen doorway. And I just realized... I forgot to put pennies on the cellar hatch.
