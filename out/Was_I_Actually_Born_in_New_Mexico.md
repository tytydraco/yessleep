Part 1 - [I was never allowed to hear a recording of myself growing up.](https://www.reddit.com/r/nosleep/comments/1v83139/i_was_never_allowed_to_hear_a_recording_of_myself/)  
  
  
It was almost noon before I realized I hadn’t eaten.

I had been sitting at the kitchen table since I woke up, doing nothing. Every few minutes, my father’s voice came back to me.

Don’t bring her into this.

The way he said it bothered me more than the words themselves. Then him hanging up before I could ask what he meant. In twenty-four years, I had never heard him sound like that. I opened his contact on my phone and held my thumb over the call button until the screen dimmed.

Then I locked it and placed it face down.

I wasn’t ready.

My roommate had already left for work. He hadn’t asked whether I was okay before leaving, which was probably for the best. I wouldn’t have known how to answer.

I made a peanut butter sandwich so I could say I had eaten something. Halfway through it, I did what I had been doing most of the day. I opened the voice memo app and pressed record.

“Testing.”

I played it back.

The old man answered.

Low. Rough. Worn out.

That voice came from my phone in the middle of my kitchen, with sunlight coming through the blinds and half a sandwich sitting beside me.

I don’t know what I expected. Maybe I thought it would correct itself overnight. Maybe I would finally hear the slightly nasal voice my roommate claimed I had always had, and I could blame everything else on stress.

I deleted the recording and told myself it would be the last one.

It wasn’t.

It has never been the last one.

I check every day now. Sometimes more than once. It has become like pressing a sore tooth with your tongue. I know what I’ll find, but I keep checking. The voice is always there.

If it couldn’t tell me anything, maybe my past could.

I opened an email account I had used in middle school. I only kept it because several old logins were still attached to it. I scrolled past school newsletters, password resets, and expired coupons. I kept going until I reached the years when I was still in speech therapy.

There were dozens of appointment reminders.

Jill’s by the Bay Speech and Language. Your appointment is confirmed for 3:00 p.m. Please arrive ten minutes early.

Most were signed by the same person.

Rachel.

I hadn’t thought about her in more than a decade, but the name brought back an entire room.

A narrow office with one window facing the parking lot. Flashcards with cartoon animals. A plastic mirror on the desk. Rachel asking me to slow down, watch the shape of her mouth, and try the sound again.

She had worked with me for years.

If anyone knew what I had sounded like as a child, it was her.

I copied the clinic number into my phone.

Then I stared at it.

Some part of me already understood that I might regret calling. I wish I could say I made a decision, but really, I sat there too long and my thumb eventually pressed the screen.

A recorded menu answered.

Press one for new patients. Press two for current patients. Press three for billing. Press four for all other inquiries.

I pressed four.

After several minutes, an older woman picked up.

“Jill’s by the Bay. How can I help you?”

“Hi. This might sound strange, but does a therapist named Rachel still work there? She would have been there around twelve or fifteen years ago.”

“Do you have a last name?”

“I don’t. I was a kid.”

“One moment.”

I heard typing.

It continued for long enough that I checked the call timer.

Finally, she said, “I’m sorry. I don’t see anyone named Rachel in our staff records.”

“You mean she left?”

“I mean I don’t see anyone by that name who has ever worked here.”

I looked at the email open on my laptop.

Rachel’s name sat beneath the clinic address and phone number.

“I have appointment reminders from your office,” I said. “They’re signed by her.”

“Could Rachel have been a nickname?”

“I don’t think so.”

“Can you check whether I was a patient?”

“I can try. What’s your full name and date of birth?”

I gave them to her.

More typing.

Then silence.

This pause felt different.

“Yes,” she said. “I found a transferred file.”

Relief moved through me so quickly that I almost laughed.

Then she continued.

“It says you were originally registered in New Mexico. Is that correct?”

I thought I had misheard her.

“New Mexico?”

“Yes.”

“No. I grew up in Maryland.”

“All I can tell you is what the file says.”

“I’ve never lived in New Mexico.”

“Then the information may have been entered incorrectly during the transfer.”

“Transferred from where?”

“I’m sorry, I don’t have access to that.”

“Does it list Rachel?”

“No. There’s no provider name attached.”

I told her I would call back when I had more information, though I had no idea what that meant.

Then I ended the call.

I sat there with my phone in one hand and one of Rachel’s emails open in front of me. That was when I saw the pattern. 

The video had changed after I watched it.

Rachel had existed until someone checked.

My childhood had happened in Maryland until a record said otherwise.

Every time I looked at my life twice, it came back different.

The voice was the same.

I had heard myself one way for my entire life. Then I checked. Something else answered.

I left the apartment and drove to a park near my building. I sat on a bench and watched people walk their dogs while I went over the same facts. 

The video had changed.

The clinic had no record of Rachel.

My patient file said New Mexico.

I had no evidence that stayed the same long enough to show anyone.

There was only one place left to search.

My father’s house.

The house where I remembered growing up.

The Fourth of July was that weekend. My father and I had spent almost every Fourth together since I moved out. I typed a message before I could reconsider.

Hey, it’s almost the Fourth. Mind if I come down for the weekend?

He replied within an hour.

Yeah, feel free. Bring some fireworks.

Nothing about our call.

No anger. No questions.

Just my father sounding like my father. That frightened me more than if he had refused. He hadn’t forgotten what happened. A person does not say Don’t bring her into this like that and forget it the next morning. He had decided to pretend it never happened.

The week passed in pieces. I went to class, took notes, and absorbed almost nothing.

On Saturday, I drove across the Pennsylvania line to buy fireworks. The better ones are cheaper there, and everyone around here knows it. I spent too much money on a box of mortars I wasn’t sure we would use.

Then I drove south. The fireworks rattled in the trunk while the GPS guided me along a route I had taken dozens of times.

I kept feeling as though I was about to do something wrong.

I was visiting my father for the Fourth. That was all.

Except it wasn’t.

By the time I reached Maryland, I had convinced myself I was prepared for whatever I found.

Standing in his driveway an hour later, I knew I wasn’t.

He opened the door before I finished knocking.

“Hey, there he is. Traffic bad?”

He sounded normal.

Completely normal.

That bothered me too.

His voice was warm and familiar. I would have known it anywhere.

Then I caught myself wondering whether that was actually true.

What would happen if I recorded him?

Would the phone play back the voice I had known my whole life?

Would something else come out?

I thought about recording him several times that night.

I never did.

I was afraid of both answers.

We had a good evening.

That was the worst part.

He made ribs because it was a special occasion. We drank beer in the backyard and lit fireworks as the sky got dark. For a few hours, it felt like one of the weekends I used to look forward to.

Maybe it was real. Maybe he was performing the whole thing because he knew I had questions. I couldn’t tell anymore.

Around eleven, he handed me a blanket and said the guest room was ready.

The guest room was my old bedroom.

He went to bed.

I didn’t.

I stood in the hallway until the light beneath his door went out. Then I waited until his breathing settled into the heavy rhythm I remembered from when I was a kid sneaking downstairs. I pulled the cord for the attic ladder.

The hinges groaned as I lowered it.

I climbed slowly, stopping whenever the wood creaked beneath me.

The attic looked exactly as it always had.

Exposed insulation. Cobwebs. Stacks of cardboard boxes filled with things nobody wanted enough to use and nobody could bring themselves to throw away.

I searched using the flashlight on my phone.

Recipe books. An old stand mixer. A microwave with rust around the door. Christmas decorations. Boxes of clothes. No camcorder. No tapes. No baby videos. There was not a single recording of me anywhere in the house.

Three weeks earlier, I would have considered that normal. My family simply wasn’t the kind that recorded things.

Now it felt deliberate.

Then I found the notebook.

It was at the bottom of a box filled with my mother’s cookbooks. Her name was written inside the front cover. I recognized the handwriting immediately. Most of the notebook was ordinary.

Recipes. Grocery lists. Notes from the library where she worked. Complaints about coworkers. A kind sentence about my father that I won’t repeat because it was private and because reading it hurt more than I expected.

It was the notebook of a normal woman living a normal life.

Except for several pages.

Between recipes and shopping lists were lines written in a language I couldn’t recognize.

At least, I thought it was a language.

The letters looped and crossed in confident strokes. There were spaces between groups of symbols like words, and repeated shapes that looked like they followed some kind of grammar.

My mother had written them in the same neat hand as everything else.

I tried whispering a few of them aloud.

Nothing I said sounded right.

I photographed every page I could find.

I still check the pictures daily.

So far, they haven’t changed.

On the inside of the back cover was a phone number.

Beneath it, my mother had written one word.

Personal.

I should have waited until morning.

It was after midnight. I was crouched in my father’s attic with insulation dust on my clothes.

I should have copied the number and left.

But I had spent a week watching pieces of my life disappear whenever I checked them again.

I didn’t want to give the number time to become something else.

I called.

It rang twice.

An older man answered.

“Veno’s Pizzeria. What can I do for you?”

For a moment, I couldn’t speak.

“Hello?” he said.

I tried to ask where he was located, but the words came out badly enough that he asked me to repeat myself.

“Sorry,” I said. “Wrong number.”

I hung up.

A pizzeria.

My mother had written the number in the back of her notebook and labeled it personal.

I searched the name immediately, shielding my phone screen with one hand as though the light might travel through the ceiling and wake my father. There was only one Veno’s Pizzeria.

It was in New Mexico.

I don’t remember deciding to leave the attic.

I remember my hands slipping on the ladder rungs. I remember fumbling with the folding mechanism twice before it locked. I remember trying to push the ladder back into the ceiling without letting it slam.

Then I turned around.

My father was standing at the end of the hallway.

He didn’t yell.

He looked at me.

Then he looked at the attic cord, still swinging behind my shoulder.

I watched him understand.

The ribs. The fireworks. His question about traffic. The whole evening changed in his face.

“What were you doing up there?”

It didn’t sound like a question.

“Dad, I can explain.”

He stayed where he was.

“I was looking for Mom’s number,” I said. “I know you have it somewhere. I just want to call her. I want to talk to her.”

His jaw moved before he spoke, as though the words were stuck behind his teeth.

Then his face twisted.

“Get out.”

“Dad—”

“Now.”

He pushed past me and entered the guest room.

Drawers opened and slammed. A few seconds later, he came out carrying my bag, my jacket, and my phone charger, still half wrapped around his fist.

He walked to the front door and threw everything into the yard.

I followed him.

“Please, just tell me what’s going on.”

He grabbed my arm.

Hard.

His hand was shaking.

Then I saw that he was crying.

“Making fun of a dead woman,” he said. His voice cracked on the word dead. “Is that what this is?”

I stopped moving.

“The call last week, and now this. What do you want from me? What are you trying to dig up?”

“Dad, what do you mean?”

“I thought if I acted like everything was fine, it would be.”

He wiped at his face with the heel of his hand.

“And now you’re in the attic, going through her things.”

“You said she left.”

His expression changed.

“What?”

“Mom. You said she left us.”

He stared at me.

“Dad, she left. She moved away. You told me she didn’t want us to contact her.”

He shoved me onto the porch.

It wasn’t gentle, though I barely felt it.

The last thing I saw before he shut the door was his face, wet with tears, his breathing uneven in the same way it used to be when he fought with my mother and thought I was asleep down the hall.

The lock clicked.

The porch light stayed off.

My belongings were scattered across the grass.

I stood among them and tried to understand what he had said.

Dead woman.

My mother had left. That was the story. That was what I remembered.

She packed a suitcase. She stood near the front door. My father wouldn’t look at her. She told me she loved me and that someday I would understand.

I remembered watching her car pull out of the driveway. I remembered waving.

Except when I tried to picture the car, I couldn’t remember its color.

I couldn’t remember what she was wearing.

I couldn’t remember whether she had spoken in her own voice.

My father had called her a dead woman.

Not missing.

Not gone.

Dead.

According to him, she had always been dead.
