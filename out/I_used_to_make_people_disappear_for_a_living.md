Not literally, no, but stage magic. Claustrophobic work with thrilling escapes, bodies sawn in half, occasionally doing a back-and-forth with the audience, you know, the usual stuff. I prayed to Houdini when the ropes got too tight. 

After around three years of hard work, a show opened up in a relatively mid-sized theater the next city over. Hell, I say, money, and I end up taking the one job I never should have. Or, at least, one of the two.  

To make a long story short, some schmuck decided that my heckling was too personal, and took me up on a lighthearted offer to meet him after the show. He got in my face, yelling slurs and the like, and after a very long performance on a very hot stage, I wasn’t having any of it, I suppose. I saw red. Something took over me, and I just…pushed. I pushed the guy really hard. Didn’t know I had the strength, to be honest. 

He fell backwards and, predictably, cracked his skull on the pavement right outside the venue. It’s a sound I still think about when it’s quiet. Of course, a sizable crowd of people watched this confrontation, a few of them recording it rather than stepping in to help. There was no case in my favor at that point. I was arrested that night, for the first time in my life, and spent the night in a confined cell with nothing to do but read a Bible one of the officers offered me. 

The prosecutor made it sound like I tried to kill the guy. The judge, thankfully, didn’t quite agree, and I was charged with simple misdemeanor assault. Fine. Whatever. It was nothing I thought would ruin my life at the time. At least there was no probation attached. 

The theater I worked for let me go the next day. Predictable. Anything to protect their public image.

Word travelled surprisingly fast, though, thanks to the local papers’ sirens section and a few offbeat remarks by the radio host, Mr. Shamber. Gigs dried up just as quickly for me. I tried everywhere around town. Bake shops, sales positions, warehouses, call centers…anything with a pulse and a good stream of cash. 

Living in the area that I was, my money was evaporating fast. First my unemployment, then my savings, then my dignity. I was forced to move tens of hours away, back to my childhood home, to square one. I listened to my mother, day after day, pestering me about finding work in the rural community we lived in. I told her again and again that I was looking. Months passed. No offers in sight. Not even a callback. 

I heard the way their voices changed at dinner. I saw how the fridge had been growing more and more empty the longer I stayed there. A silent resentment boiled. I knew something had to change fast. 

So I swallowed my pride. Decided to apply for the worst job I could think of. That just so happened to be an overnight stocker at the Walmart, twenty minutes away from our house. I knew the hours would suck, the pay would be low, and the position was only temporary. But maybe it would help me get my foot in the door somewhere else. After all, it’s easier to find a job when you’re already employed. 

So yeah. I applied. And I waited. And I waited. They took forever to get back to me, a grand total of four weeks when they initially said I would get an offer within one. Part of this was the automated background check, I’m sure.

You can be offered a job and then have that shit immediately rescinded. What a low blow that would be. The possibility caved my stomach for those long weeks.

Instead of the rejection I was expecting, though…they called me back. Welcomed me aboard. Relief hit me like a tidal wave of cool, cool ocean. I would begin my orientation Monday morning. I showed up fifteen minutes early, just to be safe.

A manager with the name tag “Ronald” came out to greet me. We’d met before, of course, but this time he gave me a firm handshake and a warm hand on my shoulder. He joked with me about how nervous I looked, and told me not to worry about anything besides doing what I’m told. 

I was given a tour of the back rooms of the facility, where all the magic happened. Standard stuff, nothing too revolutionary to warrant heavy description. A giant trash compactor, palettes stacked high, forklifts, the like. We went to the freezer section last. 

“How do you do with cold temperatures?” he asked. “You’ll be in and out of here a lot.”

“I can manage,” I said, lying through my teeth. 

He showed me how to prop the door open with a crate, telling me how the handle tends to stick on the inside. With that lesson out of the way, I was asked to start hauling palettes. Simple enough. None of the ones in the freezer looked heavier than fifty pounds. All it would take was a little leg work. 

A few minutes passed, maybe an hour. The pile inside the freezer slowly melted away, most of its contents having been moved to the front of house or the deli fridge. I’d worked up a sweat, despite the temperature, and was relieved when I walked back there to see maybe two-or-three more palettes to go. 

But then the door shut behind me. That little black crate I was using was gone, snatched up by unseen hands. I tried the handle, but it had already stuck shut. I banged and screamed for help. My sweat began to freeze to my cheeks. My ears grew numb.

  
I did the one thing I know how to do best, and I started working the handle's mechanisms with a spare hair pin, trying desperately to pry it open.

The vents grew colder, now spewing forth a white cloud that enveloped me. 

I don’t know if it was the cold or the fog, but a few minutes in, and I was feeling drunk. I couldn’t stand up straight. I slumped against the wall. I passed out, thinking that this was a stupid way to die.

Later, I woke. Not in a hospital, or in a freezer, but standing, with my arms tied tight behind my back by itchy twine. My side burned. Looking down, I saw a neatly-stitched wound running along it, blood oozing from within. Something important was missing. Like the way you know a tooth is gone before your tongue finds the gap, right? I just hoped it wasn’t anything important.

Looking up, I saw them; Ronald and his three assistant managers. One of them was chewing on some fresh, fleshy mass. I only grew more nauseated.

They had been dressed in clear raincoats and yellow rubber gloves, slaughterhouse employees that showed up to the wrong store. Ronald gave me an empty smile. His crazed eyes didn’t give me the same courtesy. Neither did the large knife in his left hand. 

Ronald said that criminals "like me" often see establishments "like his" as "easy pickings." You show up to work for a few years, wait for society to forget what you did, and get off Scott-free. 

He talked for a long time, about responsibility, about maintenance, about cleaning up parts of the criminal justice system that he deemed as “half-measures.”

Then he started talking about his daughter. Ronald couldn't get through the whole story without his face reddening and his buddies giving him complementary pats on the back.

He asked me if I knew how long the perpetrator got. When I shook my head, he told me. Eight years. Eight years for what he put that man's child through.

Now, I hardly understood what any of this had to do with me, but here I was. Tied to a post and begging for my life.

Ronald wouldn’t listen to me. He explained that even if my crime was a mistake, it was proof that I had the capacity for evil. He told me that I had no idea what I had done to that man, the long-term effects of his injury, the medical costs...the long-short of it was that I was somehow an irredeemable monster for defending myself.

The only good I'd be, now, is giving back to society in a non-violent way. That being, getting shoved through a processor and turned into deli meat. Ronald wasn't subtle about the details.

While the idiot went on and on, I worked the ropes around my wrists in the way I’d practiced a thousand times on stage. Cheap knots and cheaper cord. They really expected my grogginess to hold me down on its own. 

One loop slips, then another. I kept my shoulders hunched, and my expression purposefully fearful. Maybe that last part wasn’t entirely up to my own control. When the last binding gave, I drove myself forward and shoulder-checked the prattling Ronald. He didn’t see it coming, knocked over into his compatriots, the knife going into one of their throats. 

I didn’t stay to watch the aftermath, no, I bolted. Through a maze of plastic curtains and cardboard, I ran like hell until I hit the exit doors and sprawled into the cold night. Blood trailed behind me, my fresh wound having ruptured during the flight. I held onto it, groaning, calling for help. This cycle continued for around a mile and a half before my adrenaline ran out, my blood loss caught up with me, and I collapsed on the sidewalk. 

Then, I was in a hospital bed, with my scar sutured shut and a lot of questions on my mind. A nurse explained to me that a homeless man had found me and dragged me to the emergency room early in the morning. She told me that my kidney was missing, and asked what I had gotten myself into. 

I told her what had happened. I don’t think she believed me, but her expression seemed to soften, probably out of pity. 

The police took a statement. They told me again and again that they checked the store and found nothing suspicious. My case was ruled as a mugging, with the homeless man who saved me as the number one suspect. My own explanation was passed off as nothing more than delirium from my blood loss. 

The investigation went nowhere. God, of course it did.

I’m scared. I don’t want to live in this city anymore. I feared for my life after reporting them, and now it’s even worse that nothing has come of it. Worse yet, an innocent man is going to go to prison over this, and his life is most certainly going to be ruined if it hasn’t been shit enough already. 

My parents’ place is no longer an option. I have to get moving, anywhere but here. But it’s tough to get anywhere in this country without resources.

I'm going to need a job first.
