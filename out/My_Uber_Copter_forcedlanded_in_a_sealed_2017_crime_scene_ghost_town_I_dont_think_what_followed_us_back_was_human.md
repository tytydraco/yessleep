I haven’t stepped foot in an Uber, a plane, or any rideshare since this happened a few months ago. I still sleep with the lights on, and every time I hear a low, rhythmic thumping in the distance, my chest tightens up. I’m writing this because I need to get it out there, and honestly, to warn anyone who thinks booking a luxury flight is completely foolproof.

I needed to travel between two major cities about 500km apart. I decided to ball out and try the "Uber Copter" service. By pure chance, nobody else booked my specific time slot. I ended up scoring a massive, multi-million dollar helicopter entirely to myself. Just me, a pilot who looked like he hadn't slept in days, and a completely transparent glass cabin floor that made me feel like I was suspended over thin air. 

For the first thirty minutes, it was an absolute rush. We crossed over a massive, illuminated metropolis that looked like an RGB circuit at night. But as we left the neon grid of the "RGB city" behind, entering a massive, pitch-black void of rural terrain, the vibe completely died. 

The cockpit suddenly erupted in a chorus of high-pitched, panicked warning beeps. 

I leaned forward, looking over the pilot's shoulder. The digital instrument panel was flashing a violent crimson. The fuel gauge read \*\*5/100\*\*. Five percent. The pilot’s posture instantly went rigid. He didn't say a word to me. He just backed off the throttle, his knuckles turning white on the controls as he fought to enter a glide speed, desperately searching the absolute blackness below for a place to put us down. 

Through the transparent glass floor, I watched the empty, dark earth rush up to meet us. My stomach was in my throat. Out of nowhere, the silhouette of a dead runway appeared. The pilot slammed the chopper down in a brutal, bone-rattling emergency landing. 

The second our skids hit the tarmac, he reached up and hit the Master Kill Switch. The engine sputtered out, the warning lights died, and the entire aircraft went pitch black. The sudden silence was suffocating. 

\*"This airport is abandoned,"\* the pilot whispered, his voice trembling slightly. He didn't look back at me; he just stared out the front windshield into the dark. \*"It’s not safe. If I start running, you run right with me. Understand?"\*

We stepped out of the cabin into a humid, heavy night air, immediately swarmed by thick clouds of mosquitoes. As my eyes adjusted, the true horror of where we had landed began to set in. This wasn't just an old airfield—it was a time capsule of something terrible. 

The main access road leading away from the tarmac was completely blocked by rusted metal barricades. Flapping in the hot wind were rows of decaying, faded yellow \*\*crime scene stickers and police tape\*\*. Near the edge of a crumbling hangar, a weathered, water-damaged poster hung sideways. I shined my phone light on it and felt a cold sweat break out. The entire facility had been legally sealed shut by federal authorities in 2017 after a violent incident, and the surrounding village had been completely evacuated. It had been left to rot for nearly a decade. 

The road out was swallowed by massive,  potholes and overgrown weeds. It looked less like a road and more like a maze. 

The pilot’s hand-held radio suddenly chirped with static. The air traffic dispatch told him the ground rescue vehicle would take at least 30 to 45 minutes because they couldn't navigate the broken asphalt in the dark. 

Right as the radio went silent, the pilot froze. He stopped breathing. 

\*"If you hear a human voice, gunshots, or any scraping noises, run to that specific corner over there,"\* he muttered, pointing a shaking finger toward a blind spot between two collapsing sheet-metal hangars. \*"That corner is a blind spot from the runway."\*

I killed my phone screen immediately. We stood there in the absolute dark, listening to the wind howl through the hollow, empty buildings. Then, I saw them. 

Way down at the far end of the dead runway, two glowing red eyes cut through the blackness. They weren't vehicle lights. They were low to the ground, perfectly still, staring directly at us. The air felt ice-cold. My mind scrambled, trying to convince myself it was just a wild animal, but the way those eyes sat perfectly level, completely unblinking in a place abandoned because of "paranormal activity," made my blood run cold. 

I crouched low behind the helicopter skids, gripping my bags, ready to bolt to the blind spot. The shadow slowly detached itself from the dark and stepped into a faint patch of moonlight. It was a stray dog—but it was abnormally large, emaciated, and silent. It didn't bark. It just slunk past us, watching us with those reflective eyes, before melting back into the shadows of the crime scene hangar. 

A few minutes later, a local police cruiser rolled through the outer perimeter on a routine check. I thought we were saved. But when the officers rolled down the window, they didn't offer us a ride. They looked genuinely terrified to even have their engine idling near the tarmac. They told us they were completely alone on this stretch of the highway, warned us that the area was highly unstable, and told us to leave the property immediately before driving off into the night. 

They left us completely exposed. 

Just as the paranoia was peaking, and I was convinced the shadows around the hangars were starting to move, a heavy, rhythmic thumping echoed from the clouds. 

Our rescue didn't come by land. Uber's emergency dispatch had sent a \*\*secondary helicopter\*\* to pull us out. The blinding searchlight cut through the fog, illuminating the decaying 2017 crime scene tape and the empty, haunted buildings around us. 

We kept low under the deafening, violent downwash of the rotors, bolted across the cracked tarmac, and scrambled inside the new cabin. As we lifted off, looking down through the glass floor one last time, I swear I could see shapes standing on the edge of the runway, watching us rise back into the sky. 

I left the original pilot a massive "hamburger tip" on the app because I had literally eaten his lunch out of pure, panicked survival instinct while we were still in the air. I made it back to my hometown 100km away in one piece, but a part of me never left that airfield.....
