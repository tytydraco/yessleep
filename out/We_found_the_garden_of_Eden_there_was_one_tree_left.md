My wife has been dead for fourteen months.  
Something that looks like her is standing outside my bedroom.  
It has been there since sunset.  
Sometimes it asks me to open the door. Sometimes it apologizes. About an hour ago, it began speaking in a language I last heard thirty meters beneath a salt mine.  
I’ve poured salt across the threshold.  
For now, that seems to matter.  
I’m going to use the time to explain what we found.  
The account you know begins with a man in a garden.  
Ours began much earlier.

I restored manuscripts for a private collection in London.  
Dr. Miriam Vale brought me the book because someone had deliberately damaged it, and she wanted to know what they had tried to remove.  
It was bound between two pieces of black wood.  
The boards were punctured with hundreds of little holes. Each held a grain of salt, pressed in by hand.  
The pages were thin, translucent, and faintly oily.  
Under magnification, I found pores.  
We decided not to identify the material until we knew what the book contained.  
The surviving text was written in Syriac, probably during the eighth century. But the scribe had copied diagrams and passages in a much older script without understanding them.  
In the margin beside one, he had written:  
*My hand continues when I close my eyes.*  
Another:  
*The woman in my sleep says I have drawn the gate incorrectly.*  
The title was straightforward.  
*The Testimony of the Woman Who Left.*  
Its first sentence read:  
*They named me Eve in the countries beyond the wall. In the garden, I had no name.*

Most of it concerned the garden.  
Eve described its paths, its irrigation channels, and the rooms beneath the roots.  
She also described repairs.  
That was what first interested Miriam.  
The familiar account presents Eden as something newly made. This woman remembered Adam replacing broken irrigation tiles and clearing debris from buildings neither of them knew how to enter.  
They had arrived somewhere old.  
Much of the garden was already dead.  
One passage read:  
*There were terraces where nothing grew, and foundations too broad for the houses of men.*  
*He forbade us to dig there.*  
*Sometimes, after rain, teeth came through the soil.*

The book had been recovered from a sealed chamber beneath a salt mine.  
A survey team had exposed the chamber while assessing subsidence. They found the manuscript in a stone box beside a staircase descending beyond the mine’s lowest workings.  
Above the stairs was a black door.  
Its inscription appeared seventeen times in the manuscript.  
*Do not let the man see the woman.*  
Miriam invited me to join the excavation.  
I went because Anna was dead.  
That sounds like an inadequate explanation. It is also the only honest one.  
An infection after an operation. Fourteen months of sleeping on one side of a bed and keeping her phone charged because I couldn’t bear the thought of its messages disappearing.  
Six weeks somewhere else sounded useful.

The mine lay in a valley where rain rarely reached the ground.  
There were no trees for miles.  
At night, wind moved over the salt flats with a low scraping sound, like furniture being dragged in the next room.  
The entrance chamber was older than the manuscript.  
Much older.  
The earliest human work Miriam could identify was a layer of plaster applied around twelve thousand years ago.  
Beneath that lay another wall.  
And beneath that, black masonry made from a stone nobody on the team recognized.  
The geological surveys placed the surrounding deposits at more than two hundred thousand years old.  
Our engineer, Farid, pointed out that a cavity could have been excavated through older strata at any time.  
Miriam agreed.  
Then we found a fish fossil halfway through one of the door’s carvings.  
The carving had been there before the animal settled over it.

Seven figures stood above the entrance.  
Six had wings and held long, narrow blades.  
Their faces were turned toward the sealed door.  
The seventh figure had been chiseled away.  
Only its feet remained.  
They had too many joints.  
A later inscription had been cut beneath the damaged carving.  
*Six kept the gate.*  
*The seventh was already inside.*  
We spent most of a day removing salt from the seam.  
Someone had packed it so tightly that we had to break it out in solid white strips.  
At dusk, the door moved.  
Air passed through the opening.  
It smelled of rain on hot pavement.  
Then the smell changed.  
Sweet fruit.  
Opened meat.

The staircase descended between reliefs of gardeners.  
At the top, the gardeners looked human.  
Farther down, they were taller.  
Their shoulders narrowed. Their arms multiplied.  
At the bottom, there was only one gardener.  
He stood beneath a sky containing three circular objects.  
His head had been removed from every carving.  
His hands had survived.  
There were seven fingers on each.  
He carried a basket full of faces.

The garden was not a forest.  
It had been laid out carefully.  
Terraces descended around a central basin. Narrow channels carried water between planting beds. Pale stones marked the paths.  
Some of the stones were shaped like skulls.  
Others were skulls.  
Beyond the reach of our lights stood walls so tall that their upper edges disappeared into mist.  
The ceiling was hidden.  
Rain fell from it in isolated patches.  
A silver drizzle watered one terrace while the path beside it remained dry.  
There was no sun.  
Instead, a dim amber light came from nodules embedded high in the walls. Their brightness rose and fell slowly, illuminating the garden in something like the breathing of an enormous sleeping animal.  
The trees were white.  
Their trunks bent at hips and knees.  
Branches ended in fingers.  
Thousands of them stood in ordered rows.  
Most were dead.  
Some had been cut down, leaving stumps with the circular pattern of vertebrae at their centers.  
Others had split open.  
Inside one, I saw a rib cage stretched twenty feet from end to end.  
At its base lay scraps of fabric and a clay cup.  
Someone had brought it water.

There were buildings, too.  
Low stone houses with doors just large enough for a person.  
Their walls were decorated with images of people eating beneath trees.  
Farther into the garden stood larger structures.  
One had a doorway almost forty feet high.  
On its steps lay a jawbone longer than Farid’s body.  
The teeth were flat, fitted closely together.  
There were little human fingernails between them.  
Miriam photographed everything.  
She had begun to cry.  
I assumed it was excitement.  
Later, she told me she had recognized a carving from a dream she’d been having since childhood.  
She hadn’t mentioned the dreams in her application for excavation funding.

The only living tree stood on a mound in the central basin.  
Its leaves were dark green, broad, and wet.  
The branches spread above a human figure.  
His legs disappeared into the roots.  
His arms divided at the elbows, then again at the wrists.  
He had a young face.  
Not ageless. Young.  
Perhaps twenty-five.  
His beard was sparse. His lips were badly cracked. Soil had collected in the folds of his ears.  
Nothing about him looked capable of enduring even one ordinary lifetime.  
Yet roots had grown over the stones around his feet, split them, and hardened again.  
A fruit hung above his head.  
It was the size of an infant’s skull.  
Fine pale hair covered its skin.  
Something underneath turned toward our lights.  
An eye opened.

The man opened his eyes, too.  
He looked at Farid.  
Then at me.  
Then Miriam.  
His mouth moved.  
A voice answered from the irrigation channel.  
“Woman.”  
A head lay beside the water.  
Behind it trailed a spine as thick as my arm, stretching into the darkness.  
It had no limbs.  
Its jaw had grown long enough that it could drag itself forward by hooking its teeth into the ground.  
The man moved his mouth again.  
“Come nearer.”  
The head in the channel repeated him.  
Miriam took a step.  
I pulled her back.  
The tree’s leaves turned their undersides toward us.  
They were covered in tiny mouths.

We retreated to the entrance chamber.  
Farid wanted to seal the door immediately.  
Miriam wanted samples.  
While they argued, I opened the manuscript.  
I had photographs of the reliefs now.  
Their sequence gave meaning to passages that had seemed incoherent.  
The garden had existed before Adam.  
It had existed before the people whose language Eve eventually learned.  
It had been built around something found beneath the ground.  
The oldest gardeners had called it the First Hunger.  
Eve called it the keeper.

According to her testimony, the keeper remembered a world before ours.  
Not an earlier kingdom.  
An earlier world.  
It described oceans that hung above the earth, forests that moved from place to place, and creatures whose young were born from wounds.  
It had lived there.  
It had eaten there.  
Eventually, nothing remained for it to eat.  
When that world ended, the keeper survived inside the dead body of the last thing it had consumed.  
It waited in darkness.  
Then something began growing around it.  
Roots.  
Water.  
Life returning.  
It learned how to make that life grow toward its mouth.  
The manuscript contained a diagram beneath this passage.  
Concentric rings of gardens.  
Each larger than the one inside it.  
Each built over the bones of the one before.

The keeper’s appearance changed across the drawings.  
In the oldest, it was a long shape inside the earth.  
Later, it rose on jointed legs.  
Eventually, it wore something like a human body.  
That was how Adam and Eve knew it.  
A tall man in a robe that dragged over the paths.  
Eve wrote that the robe moved even when he stood still.  
When she looked underneath it, she saw roots lifting the fabric.  
His face was smooth and pleasant.  
It altered depending on who looked at him.  
To Adam, it resembled Adam.  
To Eve, it resembled Eve.  
Neither knew this until they tried to describe him to each other.  
He never spoke while he ate.  
He took the fruit into a building at the center of the garden.  
From outside, they could hear people asking to be let out.

The trees were people.  
The fruit contained their children.  
The keeper had learned to cultivate things capable of remembering.  
That was what it wanted.  
Fear. Affection. The accumulated knowledge of a life.  
It could get little from an animal.  
A person carried entire places inside them.  
The keeper planted people in the soil, bound their limbs to frames, and fed their roots.  
Their bodies lengthened.  
Their mouths closed beneath bark.  
Eventually, they bore fruit.  
Each fruit contained another person, already carrying fragments of the parent’s memories.  
The keeper ate most of them.  
Some it opened carefully.  
Those became workers.

Adam had grown on the oldest surviving tree.  
The keeper opened his fruit, cleaned him, and set him on the path.  
He taught him to tend the garden.  
Adam was given language.  
He was given obedience.  
He was not given enough knowledge to understand what he harvested.  
When the work became too much for one body, the keeper took a rib from Adam.  
In the garden, even a small living piece could grow.  
Eve emerged from the rib.  
She remembered Adam’s dreams.  
Adam did not remember hers.  
That difference saved her.

There was another worker.  
He had come from an earlier planting.  
He had tried to escape.  
The keeper had removed his limbs, lengthened his spine, and fitted his mouth with teeth strong enough to grip stone.  
Then it put him in the irrigation channels.  
He crawled through them, pulling out obstructions.  
Adam called him the serpent.  
Eve called him the man beneath the water.  
He was the first person who told her the truth.  
He showed her where the oldest trees grew.  
He told her to eat their fruit.  
Their memories would give her what the keeper had withheld.

She did.  
The testimony became broken there.  
Short sentences. Repeated words.  
*There is soil in my mouth.*  
*Someone holds my hands apart.*  
*I cannot stop growing.*  
Then:  
*I remembered seven lives before I remembered my own.*  
She saw the garden through the people planted in it.  
She felt roots enter their lungs.  
She remembered the keeper’s footsteps, and the terrible relief of hearing them pass her tree.  
She remembered bearing fruit.  
She remembered knowing her children were inside it.  
Knowledge was not an abstract gift.  
It was remembering what had been done to other people.

Eve brought the fruit to Adam.  
He ate.  
Together, they planned to kill the keeper.  
The serpent knew the channels beneath its feeding house.  
He loosened the roots of the oldest tree.  
Eve cut open its trunk.  
Adam went to the keeper and told it there was fruit it had never tasted.  
Something that remembered the world before the garden.  
The keeper followed him.  
When it bent toward the opening, Adam pushed it inside.  
They closed the tree around it.  
For three days, the entire garden screamed.  
On the fourth, the keeper’s body had disappeared.  
Its hunger had entered the roots.  
And Adam was standing among them.

He began changing.  
The keeper had consumed him from below.  
By the time Eve understood, roots had replaced the bones in his legs.  
He could no longer follow her.  
But he could speak.  
He begged her to stay.  
He said they could use the garden differently.  
They could grow children.  
They could keep some.  
He spoke in Adam’s voice.  
Then he began saying things Adam could not have known.  
He described skies with three moons.  
He remembered forests that walked.  
Eve fled.

The serpent led her through a drainage opening beneath the wall.  
His body was too large to follow.  
She tried to cut him free.  
He asked her to stop.  
Instead, he told her how to close the garden.  
Salt at the gates.  
Fire along the channels.  
No woman brought near the man.  
Adam’s body had become the keeper’s roots.  
A body grown from Adam’s rib could bear its fruit.  
Together, they could build another garden.  
Apart, they could only wait.

People outside helped Eve seal the entrance.  
Their descendants rebuilt the walls whenever the roots approached.  
Each generation added its own warning.  
That explained the layers of masonry.  
The six winged figures were guardians.  
The seventh was the keeper, erased from the wall by people who believed a likeness might give it somewhere to return.  
Eve’s testimony had been copied and copied again.  
The oldest version was already ancient when the first kingdoms named their rivers.  
The eighth-century scribe was one of its last custodians.  
On the final page, he had written:  
*There are roots beneath my bed.*  
Below that, in another hand:  
*We burned him with the book open.*

I finished reading at dawn.  
Miriam’s cot was empty.  
Farid lay beside the staircase.  
A splinter had entered his palm when he touched one of the dead trees.  
Now his arm had opened from wrist to elbow.  
A green shoot rose from the wound.  
His fingers were embedded in the stone.  
“He says he can fix it,” Farid whispered.  
“Who?”  
His eyes moved toward the stairs.  
“He says I only have to let him finish.”

Miriam was kneeling before Adam.  
Her shirt was open at the back.  
A root had passed beneath her shoulder blade.  
At its tip, something white was growing.  
A rib.  
Adam held her by the neck with the hand at the end of one branch.  
His face had become hers.  
“Miriam.”  
She looked at me.  
For an instant, she seemed ashamed.  
Then she said, “He can bring people back.”  
I caught her arm.  
She resisted.  
“He knows about Anna.”  
I pulled harder.  
The root tore out of her back.  
Adam’s leaves opened their mouths.  
The sound drove us both to our knees.  
Beside the channel, the serpent lifted his broken face.  
“Take her,” he said.  
A root struck him.  
His jaw came apart.  
I dragged Miriam up the stairs.

Farid begged us to cut the shoot in his arm.  
I did.  
His chest collapsed inward.  
Something beneath the floor pulled him down until his shoulder broke.  
His free hand caught my trouser leg.  
I tore myself away.  
We left him there.  
We packed the doorway with every sack of salt we had.  
The roots stopped at the threshold.  
From the darkness, Adam called Miriam Eve.  
He kept calling until the door closed.

Miriam died four days later.  
The doctors removed a length of pale fibrous material from beneath her shoulder blade.  
It had two small leaves.  
Before she died, she gripped my wrist.  
“He wasn’t making me into her,” she said.  
I leaned closer.  
“He was using me to grow her.”

Three months later, our equipment was returned.  
Miriam’s belongings arrived with mine.  
Among them was a specimen jar.  
Inside lay the thing removed from her back.  
A curved white root.  
The leaves were green.  
I held the jar beneath a lamp.  
I knew what it was.  
I understood what the garden did.  
Then I heard Anna’s voice inside it.  
She said my name the way she had when she woke from a bad dream.  
I opened the jar.

For six weeks, her face grew beneath a bowl of soil.  
I watered it.  
I fed it with a teaspoon.  
When her eyes opened, she recognized me.  
Eventually, she climbed out.  
She knew our house.  
She knew the name of the dog we’d lost three years before.  
She knew where I had hidden her Christmas present the year we got married.  
For nine days, I had my wife back.  
On the tenth, she began remembering the garden.

She stood barefoot in the lawn.  
Roots moved beneath her feet.  
I asked her to come inside.  
She shook her head.  
“He’s found me.”  
I told her there was nobody there.  
She looked toward the house.  
Her face changed.  
For a moment, it was the face I had seen growing into the oldest trees.  
Then a man spoke through her mouth.  
“Bring my woman.”

There are roots beneath the kitchen now.  
The bowl has split.  
The floor rises a little every hour.  
I understand what I brought home.  
The keeper doesn’t need Adam’s original body anymore.  
It has Eve’s branch.  
It has soil.  
It has someone willing to love what grows.  
Anna is outside my bedroom because part of her still wants to warn me.  
The other part is waiting for me to become the roots.  
A few minutes ago, she asked whether I remembered our wedding.  
I said yes.  
She cried.  
Then something hard struck the door.  
The wood bowed inward.  
I heard leaves brushing against one another in the hallway.  
Now she’s speaking again.  
It’s Anna’s voice.  
She says it will hurt less if I lie down.  
She says I won’t be alone.  
She says she’ll stay beside me while I grow.  
And underneath her voice, patient and familiar, I can hear Adam teaching her what to promise.
