A little over a month ago, I went hiking with my dad on the Appalachian Trail. This isn’t abnormal for us; I often go hiking with my friends and family, and my dad does as well, with our dog Layla. This time we had no dog, just a nice father-daughter hike to end the summer before I start my sophomore year of college. 

The AT cuts through our town in rural Pennsylvania, so it wasn’t a long way from home, but the isolation of the thick forest makes you feel far away from the mundane routines of life. Often, I find it peaceful; however, this experience makes me hesitant to have a solo hiking trip in the future. 

It was day three of a relaxing thru-hike with my dad. He is an older man with various health issues, so we tend to take it easy on backpacking trips. It's better to take it easy and enjoy your time rather than stick to a schedule and injure yourself, he says. I share the same motto for life, and when he says this, I mourn the edibles I forgot in my bedroom, hidden in a raggedy shoebox. Whatever, I enjoy nature regardless.   
	  
I have never felt uneasy when hiking. If I lived in a perfect world, I would be completely off-grid, and nobody would bother me. Maybe some dogs and a flourishing garden, complete with a pond or lake to swim in as I please. With this mindset, I follow my dad, enjoying the beautiful rocky views that the AT provides for me. Emerald mountains surround us as we silently breathe in the thick summer air, almost suffocating. The sun will be setting soon, and the pink sky that's peeking over the mountains entrances me. I can feel a bead of sweat travel down my back between my pack and me, tickling and bringing my attention back to reality. I realize my dad is breathing heavily, and I suggest we sit   
down and rest. He shuts down my idea proudly and states there is a trail shelter about a half mile further down the path, where we can eat and relax for the night. Food isn’t something I considered for a while, and I realize now that it is a great idea.   
	  
We finally reach the shelter and drop our packs in a pile of pine needles. Not wanting to join them on the ground and get poked in the ass, we ask an older man, about 65, if we can join him at a moss-covered picnic table nearby. He seemed to be alone and the only other person at the site, so it was best to break the ice. 

He was a very worn-in man, with a very light fuzz of white hair on his head, almost like a baby bird, and a green button-down tucked into blue jeans to cover his thin, brittle frame. Without hesitation, he welcomed us to sit next to him with a beckoning hand. Most people on the trails are pretty kind; it's hard to be sour when you’re surrounded by the beauty of nature. However, this man is the only person we have seen in three days. This isn’t necessarily alarming, but pretty uncommon for a popular trail in the summer.   
	  
 My dad introduces us as he sits next to him, and I sit behind them, us all facing the same direction towards the shelter on site. He says his name is Grant, shakes both our hands, and asks us where we are from, common small talk that is in no way unusual, especially for an older man. My dad takes the lead with the talking, and I sit and listen, enjoying the cool breeze under the canopy as it chills the sweat on my neck. 

The comforting environment is interrupted when Grant interrupts my dad bluntly with an intense, confused look and a quiet tone, almost inaudible as I sit directly behind them:

*“Wasn’t there a girl with you?”*

I sit there, confused. Was he talking about me? I’m right behind them. The last time we spoke was less than five minutes ago. I furrow my brow and look at my dad, confused and unsure if I should speak. He does the same, but looks at me, then back at Grant. He smiles, almost sympathetically at the man, and says:

*“Yes, this is my daughter, Claire. We are hiking the AT as a pair before she heads back to college.”*

He looks back, almost surprised that I am there, and I smile. I feel that I am still wearing a mask of confusion on my face, my smile appearing disingenuous. He smiles back:

*“Oh… hello, Clara,”* he says with a smile. I can see a bit of decay on his teeth, much like the moss on our picnic table. 

*“Oh, I’m sorry, it’s Claire,”* I say, continuing my smile. 

His smile drops. Not suddenly, but slowly, his lips are a closing curtain on the moss-like decay on his upper teeth. I begin to feel uncomfortable, looking at my dad for some kind of backup. He looks at me as if I have done something wrong, correcting an older man. When I look back, his eye contact is no longer with me, but past me. Unsure if he is distracted by something scenic or an animal, perhaps, I turn to look. Nothing. I look back, and he is back to his welcoming grin, eye contact perfectly with mine. 

*“I’m sorry, I’m hard of hearing these days,”* he says with a hoarse laugh. I smell his breath. I try not to emote and offend him more than I already have, but it smells horrible. Usually, an old man's breath smells like stale spit and old coffee, but this smells sour. Almost sweet, like rot. I chalk it up to the distinctive decay in his mouth. I smile back and keep to myself for the rest of the conversation, afraid to disturb the peace more. 

Another half hour passes, and the forest is beginning to dim. We haven’t eaten yet; my dad is still being polite in conversation, and I am growing impatient. I stand up and begin to set up our hammocks, trying to start a transition to separate us from Grant and hopefully introduce us to dinner. Grant notices and is receptive immediately, almost too suddenly, as he stands up quickly, unusual for an older man. 

*“Well, I better set up camp in the shelter before it's too late. Takes some time for an older fella like me. Enjoy your night!”*

My dad reciprocates the well-wishing and joins me in setting up. He is an even-tempered man, but his movements seemed like he was upset. 

“What's wrong*?” I say hesitantly. He’s rarely angry, especially during our hikes.* 

*“You were very rude to Grant,”* he snaps. *“He is obviously lonely, and his memory is not doing well. You shouldn’t have corrected him.”*

I stand with my headlamp in my hand, pausing to think. I watch him pull our camp stove out of his pack and toss it on the hard ground, making me wince.  

*“I’m sorry, Dad, I didn’t realize,” I* said, unsure of what else to say. He rarely scolded me, so I felt pathetic. He doesn’t respond, and a few more seconds pass, dragging with the tension between us. I follow up: 

*“I’m going to apologize while there is still daylight left”* I say, hesitant on if I even want to. My dad turns to me, relief on his face. 

*“Thank you. I’ll get dinner ready.”*

I was always raised to respect everyone and everything, including nature, so apologizing felt right. However, I dreaded speaking with Grant alone. I rode on the high of thinking I was doing the right thing and started walking towards the shelter. If you don’t know, shelters on the trail are three-walled buildings with a slanted roof, with one wall missing as the opening. The opening was the furthest away, with the back of the shelter facing our camp. The only way I could tell Grant was awake was the glowing light of what I assumed was his lantern, and the scratching and scraping of him setting up his camp. 

I was just about to round the corner to the shelter when the rustling stopped. I knocked on the side before I revealed myself, making sure to make myself known to avoid a possible heart attack from Grant. 

*“Hey Grant, do you have a minute?”*

*“Sure, come on in, little lady!”* he said with the same welcoming tone that he used at the picnic table. I’m surprised he remembered me. 

I turned into the shelter and saw him, standing in the middle of the structure, with the same smile as earlier. I was grateful there were no hard feelings from earlier. I began my apology:

*“I didn’t mean to rush you out of our site earlier, we are grateful you chatted with us. Feel free to join us for breakfast tomorrow if you’re still around.”* 

He held his smile and thanked me, saying it was nice to see young people being so kind. I smiled and listened; however, I began noticing something strange as he continued his comments. Grant had no gear. 

The light that I had seen was built into the shelter, one bulb with a single lousy chain hanging to click it on. The only thing that accompanied him was a few sticks and other foliage laid on top of irregular lines scratched underneath. How was he out here with no gear? No food? We are miles from a parking lot in either direction. Given that pointing out his mistakes was met with uncomfortable interactions, I ignored this. I figured I would tell my dad, and he would talk to him if he thought Grant needed extra help. 

After he was done talking, I wished him a good night and headed back to my dad, who I saw about 40 feet away, crouching above our camp stove with a field bowl of stew heating up on top. Stew isn’t typical for the summer; however, for packing purposes, we found it more practical. 

*“How did it go?”* He asked. He seemed pleased that I took this initiative. 

*“It went well. I invited him to breakfast tomorrow if he’s still at camp,”* I said optimistically, thinking of how I was going to bring up his lack of equipment without sounding rude or judgemental. 

*“Hey, Dad,”* I started 

*“Yep,”* he said without looking up, splitting up our dinner. 

*“Grant didn’t have any gear with him, like at all.”* I finally said, ripping off the band-aid. 

My dad paused what he was doing and faced me, brow furrowed in concern. *“What do you mean?”* 

*“Like he had nothing, no pack or food that I could see. No sleeping bag either, and the light is from the shelter itself”*

My dad crunched his mouth to the side, as if thinking intently, staring at the browned pine needles below us. 

*“That is odd,”* he said, pausing, still staring. “*Older guys can be weird like that, though, they live by their means and don’t bother with the extra comfort items.”*

He seemed satisfied with that answer and continued plating our food, handing one to me. If my dad wasn’t worried, neither was I. I grabbed the field bowl from him and sat on my pack. We chatted about school and my upcoming classes as we ate, finishing our food at a rapid pace. After we were satisfied, we rinsed out our field bowls and put the empty MRE-style packages of stew in our Ziploc garbage bag. It was dark by now, and with both of us being blanketed with exhaustion, we crawled into our respective hammocks and fell asleep promptly. 

I woke up a few hours later to a splitting bang. Unsure if I had dreamed it, I propped myself up on one arm and stared into the darkness as if somehow that would help me hear better. Another bang echoed through the clearing. There are no guns allowed on the AT, so I was unsure what could cause a noise so powerful. I whispered to my dad, cautious not to make a lot of noise until we located the source. 

*BANG!*

*BANG! BANG!*

It was coming from the shelter. My first thought was that something had happened to Grant, and he had nothing with him to protect himself. After I hadn’t heard the last blast in at least 20 seconds, I turned towards the shelter to see if the lone lightbulb was on. It was. 

Standing in the yellow glow just outside of the shelter was Grant, facing me, with a stillness that seemed inhuman. He had something long and skinny in his hand, no taller than his hip. It wasn’t straight, but bumpy. I couldn’t make out any emotion; it was too dark for that and too far away to tell anyway. 

I stayed propped up and stared back with a similar stillness, paralyzed and unsure what I was seeing. Before I could react or say anything, he turned and went back into the shelter. 

*BANG BANG BANG BANG…..BANG BANG*

I figured there was no point in being quiet, so I hopped out of my hammock as soon as I was sure he was behind the cover of the shelter and woke up my dad, who makes anywhere he sleeps a tomb. 

*“Dad, get up!”* I pat his chest, unsure if shaking him would do the trick. *“Something is up with Grant”*

My dad sniffs sharply and blinks the film of sleep off of his face, looking at me like I’ve truly lost it. When I was younger, I had such bad anxiety that I begged my parents to let me sleep on their floor until age 14, so they tend to brush me off when I have a bad feeling about something. 

*“Claire, what is going on?”* He gurgled, exhaustion in his voice. *“Are you good?”*

*“I think Grant isn’t okay, Dad. There are noises coming from the shelter, and he’s moving around”*

He closed his eyes and sighed, tilting his head to the side. *“He probably had to get up to go to the bathroom, Claire. There aren't many places to go in the shelter, you have to leave it to pee,”* he said sarcastically.

*“I know that, that’s not what I’m tal-”*

*BANG BANG BANG BANG…….taptaptaptaptaptap….BANG..*

The sounds echoed again, deep and metallic, like he was hitting the wall of the shelter itself. I squeezed my dad’s arm as a reflex and stared at him as his eyes pierced into the dark to listen, just as I did before in my hammock. After a few moments of silence, I saw the light of the shelter turn off without a sound. My dad and I both froze, not expecting the lightbulb to turn off with Grant being clearly awake. 

Just as I was about to get my phone out of my pack, I saw a light. Not the shelter light, but a small one, a headlamp or a smaller flashlight. The beam went from inside the shelter and began circling it. Based on the height, I’m willing to bet it was a headlamp. It didn’t look up or down, it stayed parallel to the ground for about seven laps around the small structure, at a dragging pace. 

After he was done with his laps, the light moved towards the treeline and stopped for at least two minutes, unwavering. Grant was wearing this headlamp and was staring directly into the trees. 

*“Grant is freaking me out, man,”* my dad said quietly. 

Just as he said that, the beam snapped directly at us. There is absolutely no way he could hear us. Earlier, he said he was hard of hearing, and he was at least 50 meters away. Luckily, the beam wasn't strong enough for him to spot us, but we froze anyway, like deer caught in the crosshairs right before the trigger pull. 

After what felt like an hour but was probably closer to ten seconds, Grant walked back to the shelter and seemed to click the light on. 

*“Pack your stuff, kid, we’re going somewhere else,”* Dad said quietly. I didn’t hesitate to untie my hammock and put it in my stuff sack. I tried the best I could in the dark, there was absolutely no way we were using our headlamps and making it easier to be spotted. As we were packing, the banging continued. Over time, it became much more irregular and rapid, impossible to predict a pattern. It sounded angry and confused, and at this point, so was I. 

*BANGBANG…BANG…taptaptaptaptap…BANG BANG BANGBANGtaptaptapBANG….*

Once my dad had finished packing, we threw our packs on and decided to head a little further down the trail for the night, assuming Grant would stay at the site. The only issue was that the entrance to the trail we needed started a few feet from the side of the shelter, right where Grant was. 

We kept our headlights in our hands, intending to keep them off until we reached a more secure point on the trail, away from the old stranger. We started slowly, the crunch of our boots matching the bursts of blows so that we lowered the risk of being heard. 

It was working well, considering the unpredictable nature of the noise, however, any noise that had been happening, and that we anticipated would keep happening, stopped when we were both 10 paces from the entrance of the trail, hugging the back of the shelter. We froze, unsure of what to do. We didn’t hear anything coming from inside, and I mean anything. No bangs, no taps, no scratches, no breaths. Silence. It was like nobody was in there, the only indicator was the light still glowing. Or perhaps someone was in there, listening with the same silent intensity we were. 

After about three minutes of standing still, my dad decided to keep pushing while keeping his steps light. I had no choice but to follow him. I could feel my throat tighten, accompanied by the whooshing of my rapid heartbeat in my ears, almost shushing me. We made it to the entrance of the trail in silence and began pushing on, eager to leave. 

I didn’t want to look behind us once we started, but I had this overwhelming need to, like being addicted to a substance, or looking at a car crash. When I did, the light to the shelter was off, and almost on cue, the banging returned, more rapid and angrier than before. It sounded like the man was desperate to get to the other side. 

*BANGBANGBANGBANGBANGBANGBANG!!*

The noise shoved my dad and me further, like when you chase a dog with a vacuum and it runs pathetically with their tail between their legs. We stayed walking briskly until Grant was out of earshot, even with my dad’s poor health. It took us about 15 minutes before we turned our lights on, and an hour before we stopped to rest. We ended up on the top of a lookout in a beautiful clearing, the moonlight bathing the rolling mountains of PA in a blue hue. 

We sat to get a drink and decided we were far enough away from the shelter to get some more rest. This was the first time we talked since packing up our hammocks at the site, and it broke the tension, finally feeling like our trip again. 

I decided that given the nature of the clearing, I wasn’t going to set up my hammock, just use my stuff sack as a poor excuse for a pillow, too exhausted to care. My dad did the same, and we lay opposite each other as we tried to drift to sleep after the nightmare we just experienced.

In the morning, all was well. No sign of Grant, all our limbs attached, and a beautiful view of the mountainside, intertwined with the golden beams of the sun. 

I still don’t know what to make of this encounter. I was hoping someone had some insight or had any information on a man named Grant in the area. Long shot, I know, but I figured it was worth it.
