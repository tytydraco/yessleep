It was almost three in the morning when my GPS rerouted me onto a single-lane temporary road due to a sudden closure on the A4 highway just past Dresden. After an exhausting shift, I just wanted to get home. On both sides, head-high concrete barriers cut off any view of the surrounding countryside. No shoulder, no emergency lane, no streetlights. Just the narrow corridor of asphalt ahead and the loud, rhythmic humming of my tires over the milled surface.

I was driving barely twenty-five miles per hour through the heavy drizzle. After about a mile inside this narrow concrete trench, I saw the first flash of light. Headlights. They were coming straight toward me in my single lane—at insane speed.  
A wrong-way driver.

In this concrete corridor, there was absolutely zero space to dodge. My heart pounded in my throat as I hit the hazard lights and slammed on the brakes with all my strength. The other car—a battered black Golf—also hit the brakes hard. With screeching tires, the smell of burning rubber, and a shower of bright sparks, it scraped along the left concrete wall and came to a halt barely six feet from my front bumper.

I sat frozen, hands gripping the steering wheel until my knuckles turned white. The Golf's headlights completely blinded me. Angry and pumped with adrenaline, I honked three times and flashed my high beams so the driver would back out.  
In the bright flash of light, I saw the driver. A man in his early forties, his hands covering his face, his head pressed trembling against the steering wheel. He wasn't flashing back out of rage or drunk confusion. He was frantically pulling the high beam lever back second after second while screaming something through the rain-slicked glass. He wasn't pointing at me. He was pointing past my driver's door with pure terror—behind me, into the pitch-black darkness behind my car.

He wasn't flashing his lights to annoy me. He was illuminating the space behind my vehicle.  
I looked into my rearview mirror. Through the rhythmic, strobing flash of his high beams, I saw it: about twenty yards behind my trunk stood a tall, unnaturally contorted figure in the reflective orange suit of a road worker. But under the hard hat, there was no human face—just a smooth, pitch-black void. With every flash of light, the thing jerked closer in unnatural, erratic movements. Despite my closed windows and the idling engine, I heard the sharp, metallic scraping of a heavy shovel being dragged across the asphalt.

The man in the Golf couldn't take the terror anymore. He slammed his car into reverse, floored the gas pedal, and shot backward away from me—deeper into the construction zone. But on the wet, milled concrete, he lost control. The rear spun out, hit the angled end of the concrete barrier like a ramp, and launched the car with full force over the wall and down into the steep ditch.

The deafening crunch of crushing metal echoed through the quiet night air. The lane ahead of me was clear. Down in the dark ditch, I caught a glimpse of the driver crawling out of the wreckage and sprinting screaming into the thick woods.  
Behind me, the metallic scraping on the road was getting closer.

I shifted into first gear, stomped on the gas, and accelerated forward, desperate to put as much distance as possible between myself and the thing behind me. The speedometer climbed to forty, fifty, sixty.

Only when my own high beams cut through the pitch-black darkness two hundred yards ahead did I realize with icy claustrophobia why the wrong-way driver had turned around in the first place:  
The temporary lane was a death trap.

Fifty yards ahead of me, the asphalt ended abruptly in thin air. The road dropped straight down at a long-demolished highway bridge—a hundred-foot abyss into the pitch-black gorge below.  
I slammed on the brakes with both feet, tires screeching violently against the wet concrete. The car ground to a desperate halt barely thirty feet from the sheer edge.

I was shaking uncontrollably, the engine stalling in the silence. I couldn't go forward. I restarted the engine and shifted into reverse to back up carefully.  
But as I lifted my foot off the brake pedal, the red glow of my taillights reflected off two bright orange stripes standing directly against my rear window.  
And then something heavy and metallic slammed onto my roof.
