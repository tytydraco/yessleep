I need to write this down while I still know which version of myself is doing the writing.

It started with a sentence.

Not a big one. Six words, sitting at the bottom of a notebook page I know I filled out completely the night before. My handwriting, my pen, the same lazy slant I've had since sixth grade. But I don't remember writing it, and I've gone back to that page so many times since that I could recite it in my sleep, which honestly might be the whole problem.

*I have to find where he wakes up.*

I told myself I was just tired. We were moving that week — new house, same city, boxes everywhere, my mom doing that thing where she turns into a clipboard with legs. I told myself I'd written it half-asleep, the way I once texted a whole paragraph about raccoons to a friend and had zero memory of it by morning. Brains do weird things at 2 a.m. That's not a haunting. That's a Tuesday.

Except the sentence didn't feel like something *I* wrote. It felt like something I was being *told.*

I want to be upfront about something before I keep going, because it matters for literally everything that happens after this: there's a girl at my school named Lily. I have never spoken to her. Not once, not "excuse me" in a hallway, nothing. I know the small, embarrassing things you know about someone you like from across a quad — she gets iced matcha, she plays piano, she has a little curved scar over her left eyebrow, she chews the end of her straw when she's thinking. That's it. That's the whole file I have on her. We have never had a conversation.

I'm telling you this because on the day everything went wrong, she looked at me across the courtyard like she already loved me. And that look is the reason I can't tell you, anymore, which version of my life actually happened.

The night we moved into the new place, I saw something in my window that I've been trying to explain away ever since. I was sitting on my bedroom floor, unpacking books, when something flashed outside. I looked up. Nothing there — just my own reflection in the dark glass, boxes stacked behind me, tired face, normal.

Then the reflection didn't match.

For half a second — less than half a second, really, just long enough to leave a mark — the boy in the glass wasn't sitting like I was sitting. He was standing. Directly behind me. And he was smiling the way you smile when you already know how something ends.

I spun around so fast I hit a box with my elbow.

Empty room. Open door. Hallway light spilling in normal and yellow. My hoodie hanging off the closet door like a slouched-over person, which is a stupid thing to notice your brain flagging as a threat, but it did.

I turned back to the window. Just me. Pale, wide-eyed, alone.

I pulled the blinds down and didn't look at glass again for the rest of the night, which, looking back, was a promise I would not manage to keep, because a few hours later I stood in front of the bathroom mirror to brush my teeth, and for one full second, my reflection's mouth kept moving after mine had stopped.

I didn't tell my parents any of this. What would I even say? *Hey, I think the version of me in reflective surfaces is running about a second and a half behind schedule.* You don't say that out loud. You just start avoiding mirrors and telling yourself you're tired, which is what I did, right up until the sky caught fire.

It happened close to midnight. No storm, no warning — just a low hum outside that made the crickets stop mid-chirp, all at once, like someone had hit mute on the whole hillside. Then white light came through the blinds so bright it didn't look like light anymore, it looked like the *idea* of light, something with weight and heat behind it. My whole family ended up crammed into the windowless bathroom while the house shook around us like it was trying to shrug us off. The mirror over the sink cracked down the middle. I remember the taste of copper in my mouth that had nothing to do with blood. I remember hearing what sounded like a crowd of people whispering a name from somewhere very far away, and some sick, cold part of me was sure the name was mine.

Then everything went white, and I stopped being anywhere at all.

When I woke up, I'd lost almost two days.

That's the part I need you to sit with, because it's the part that still doesn't add up no matter how many times I run it. My parents told me I'd been unconscious on our new bathroom floor the whole time — that my dad carried me out himself, that I never left the house. But I remember waking up somewhere else entirely. I remember fluorescent lights underground, a subway platform, a phone with no signal lighting up anyway to show me a text from a number that didn't exist five seconds later: *Not this way either.* I remember running up a staircase with strangers while the whole city stood in the street staring at a white-hot shape tearing the clouds apart above us. I remember being on the phone with a girl who told me, in a voice going thin with static, *don't look at it* — and I remember that girl was Lily. Not stranger-Lily. Girlfriend-Lily. A Lily who knew my nickname, who drew tiny footballs on her cheek in blue pen, who once told me *there you are* like she'd been looking for me my whole life.

Both of those nights happened to me. I was in a bathroom shielding my little sister. I was underground, alone, terrified, hearing my girlfriend's voice die out over a dropped call. I have both memories with the same weight, the same texture, the same smell of dust and ozone. I didn't dream one and live the other. I lived them both, and I don't know how that sentence can be true, and I don't know how to stop it from being true either.

Here's where it gets worse.

I opened my eyes in a hospital bed to the sound of a heart monitor and my mother crying my name — my real name, the one on my birth certificate. My whole family was there. My friends were there. Everyone I actually know was standing around that bed looking at me like I'd come back from somewhere they couldn't follow.

And then a shadow moved behind the curtain, and a girl I have never spoken to in my life stepped into the room like she'd been sitting outside it for two straight days.

Lily.

She looked at me and her whole face broke open with relief, the kind of relief you only feel for someone you love, not someone you've admired quietly from forty feet away for a year. She reached for my hand like she'd done it a thousand times and then hesitated, like some part of her already knew something was wrong with the way I was looking back at her.

"Do you know me?" she asked. Her voice was shaking.

I told her the only true thing I had. We go to school together.

Her face went white in a way that had nothing to do with the fluorescent lights.

"No," she said. "It's me."

And then, so softly it barely counted as sound, she said a name.

Not my name. Not the name on the chart at the end of my bed, not the name my mother had been sobbing into my hair for two days.

She called me a name I had never told a single person in that hospital room. A name I only knew from the *other* memory — the one with the football field and the subway station and the meteor that maybe, somehow, fell twice.

The monitor next to my bed sped up before I even understood why my pulse had spiked.

I don't know which version of me she was talking to. I don't know which set of memories is the lie. What I know is this: right after she said that name, I turned to my friends — the same friends I've had since middle school, the ones standing at the foot of my bed with red eyes and torn-up faces — and I asked them where Lily was. Not "who is this girl." *Where is she.* Like I already had a whole relationship's worth of her stored somewhere behind my ribs and just needed someone to hand it back to me.

They looked at each other the way people look at each other when they're deciding who has to be the one to say it.

One of my friends — the one who never lies to me, not even a little — said, very quietly, that I had never spoken a single word to that girl in my life.

But Lily was still standing right there. Still holding my hand like it belonged to her. Still crying like she'd almost lost something she'd built a whole future around.

The heart monitor kept climbing. Nobody in that room moved to explain it. Nobody could.

I keep going back, in my head, to that sentence in my notebook, the one I never remember writing.

*I have to find where he wakes up.*

I used to think it was talking about some character I hadn't invented yet. Now I think it was talking about me. I think there are two of me, and only one of us is supposed to open his eyes at the end of this, and the meteor — whatever that thing actually was — didn't crash into the city.

I think it crashed into the space between us.

I don't know which one of us is writing this post right now.

I don't know if the next time I blink, I'll still be the one holding the pen.
