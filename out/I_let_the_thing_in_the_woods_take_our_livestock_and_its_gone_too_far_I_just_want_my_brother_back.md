I always wanted to be free of everything. Always thought about jumping on a train and leaving it all behind. I dreamed of running away to university, with my own life and ability to do whatever I chose. But the time for that has passed now, and a cost more heavy than a familial blood debt binds me now.

Being born and raised on a farm comes with a lot of inherent responsibilities day in, day out, gruelling work that would make my bones leaden and my mind light, the fatigue would do funny things to my head. While trudging along the long grass, heavy buckets in both hands, the fields would seemingly warp and expand and contract in a vignette of darkness until it blurred into a never-ending, suffocating tunnel wreathed by mundanity and wasted potential. A constant flood of responsibility and work, the kind of menial tasks that eat up every inch of your time until what remains of your days are scraps of free time that are marred and chewed up by exhaustion. Besides, there had never really been any such thing as free time. Being free of the farmwork meant you were free to do any other number of things that needed doing, and there was always something that needed doing. Time was never free as I felt the cost deep within my tender bones.   
The concept of cost was very immediately relevant to a small family spread so thin. Priority was given to the most valuable assets, our cows, whose milk represented our biggest and most reliable stream of income- they were valuable, the horses, which were known as some of the finest in the west country, valuable. Chickens, sheep, everything was valued and treated as an essential cog in a machine, without which life ceased to function. This line of thinking was iron clad until it came to me. 

When it came to their character, my parents were as starkly contrasting as the black and white of a sheepdog’s coat and about as pleasant as a mangy one. My mum was perpetually silent, though her blanched, empty eyes spoke volumes, a pale tracing paper woman whose bleak life story of resignation was so transparently evident you could see the whole narrative with one look. She was only ever sometimes seen and her voice seldom heard. It didn't need to be, my father was the voice, a sharp, severe man whose leathery skin and patchy, whose greying hair was as withering as his character. A permanent blearyness wrought his eyes, which were always half lidded with exhaustion. Something I had always considered fitting as he always only seemed to notice half of what was going on around him, Only ever fixated on the half he deemed worth the time, leaving the other without a thought. Specifically the part that involved me. I trailed about the house like a ghost, and like a ghost to a fraudulent seer, only seemed to exist when it was convenient to them.

As time went on my father became pallid and sallow-skinned at the behest of some wasting illness. He could no longer get around with the efficiency he once had. The well-oiled machine of the farm began to falter as he could do less and less to manage the livestock, weeds began to swallow the farmhouse and its many coops.  
I was the disappointing return on an investment that my parents had made as a sort of feeble attempt at a saving grace.   
Grace was apparently not what my parents had exhibited when they came to realize I was not the baby boy they had counted on, but it was what they went with when the fearful nurse had presented them with the birth certificate. My own name a reminder of how I spited them. They looked at me as though they’d be cheated, swindled. Not the future strong young lad they had needed, but me, the parasitic little mite in the mangy coat.

Despite this, they pursued their expectations. I was to carry on the workload in much the same way without being handed the warmth and care they had reserved for their anticipated son. I remember thinking that it was good for them that they had held onto that so tightly and kept it so safe, because thirteen years later my brother was born.

It was like they were two entirely different people. The ecstatic smiles they wore made the slack, perpetually pained faces I had always known into something entirely unfamiliar.  
It was as though someone had taken two haunted ragdolls and cursed them in reverse, unpossessing them of malice, resulting in two worn, but pleasant representations of people, objects of comfort for their new child.   
My heart hammered against its osteoporosis cage, spurred by a feeling I mistook for fear but would come to be recalled as the first moment of hope I had felt.  
Heart wedged in my throat, I managed to take a half step. The warm smile I was attempting died in its conception as I found myself frozen in the stare of my father. His eyes wide and alive with a fixed animosity I had never before seen from his weary eyes. I learned almost instantly that those new people were reserved entirely for my new baby brother.

With my parents enamoured in the care of their child the weight of the work clamped down on me like a pillory. Under the assault of the early morning sun, my arms bound down with the wooden restraints of the brimming egg baskets, I pulled myself into the threshold of the sheltered kitchen. I was immediately immersed in a dreadful cacophony of wailing, the shrill scream of the kettle sounded above the shrill chorus of my baby brother's shrieks as he beat the kitchen table with his fists. My skull added to the dreadful cacophony, pounding along with a dizzying drumbeat and my nerves cried out like guitar strings under a hacksaw.  
*“Please just”* It was more of a whisper than a word, buried deep under the noise of the kitchen. My father turned to me slowly and suddenly everything became white noise.  


“Please just? Please just what Grace?” The plastic baby bottle he held hit the table with a crack. My brother began to wail louder, though my father’s tone stayed low and without inflection. “Because I swear to God. If I end up going out there and I find things not done while you’re in here with the bloody gall to complain.”  


“I didn’t say anything” I managed, regretting my salvage attempt immediately. “I was just about to-“  
There was a sickening crunch as one of the baskets hit the floor. I almost followed as my world lurched sideways with panic. My baby brother’s wailing ceased, replaced by a joyful babble as he gave a short, blithe wave at the basket and its contents that he had knocked to the floor. I stared at the crumpled heap and thought vacantly about how visceral the mess of ruptured shell and weeping fluid looked. I wondered how dissimilar my remains would look as I listened to the heavy ticking of the old clock that counted in the liminal silence before the inevitable threshold of consequence.

By the fourth day without food it felt as though my body had resorted to consuming itself. The leeching tendrils of anaemia had dug deep, now bereft of even the small amount of nutrients that typically supplicated it like one would feed a familiar parasite.  
I stopped to retch. With nothing to expel I gagged senselessly on my own tongue, insides squirming as though they wanted to extricate themselves from the rest of the body, the way an leper might bang furiously at the gates of a leper colony.  
I lay my body down on the porch, bones and muscles racked and faltering, but in the dead, early hours of the morning I felt the deep desire to gouge out some time wherein, just for a moment, I could exist free and without expectation. It feels cruel. As though on some cosmic level it was punishment, that the first instance I took something for myself set off the chain of events that took everything from me.

  
It was small and simple, merely sitting on the porch of our house, buried in a textbook that I had found in the old attic. It was something about stars and such, It was wonderful. I remember thinking with an unfamiliar surge of hope that maybe this would be the thing I’d study when I went to university. Against the utter silence of the dawning hours, I was broken from my daze by a soft, almost imperceptible sound. A sound I realized was the gentle creaking of the tall wooden fence that stood just behind the chicken coops. My gaze fixed on the orange-furred, slinking shape atop it. Panic surged in my chest and I told myself that I needed to run and tell my father immediately. Strangely, this instinct suddenly floundered as my mind calmed and presented a different perspective. One supported by the very reasonable argument that I’d have no way of knowing about the fox if I was in bed as I was supposed to be. This apt reasoning served to blanket the unacknowledged spite of the decision. Alone and shrouded by the dark of the late night, I allowed myself a little smile of satisfaction. I leant forward slowly for a better view. I watched as the fox slipped in front of the coop and fixed the chickens with a calculated intent. My book hit the ground with a clatter. I reeled back, hands still frozen in their raised position as the smile faded slowly from my face. 

  
The fox stood hunched before the door of the coop, Its gums stretched like wet taffy as its jaws jutted outward to the sound of popping cartilage, too many teeth gathered around a black tongue that looked marred and eaten with some form of sickness. It froze at the sound of the impact, as entirely motionless as my own terror-stricken nervous system. Mouth still impossibly agape, the thing that looked like a fox turned slowly to meet me, the feeling in my chest rose further but this time it came with the sickening taste of bile and was kept down only by the steel vice of my fearfully clamped jaw. After what felt like an eternity, the thing lumbered forward into the coop. Through the wired windows I watched its elongated maw snap down like a mouse trap. Once, twice, its neck bulged and shifted, the chain of vertebrae surfacing against its mottled skin as its throat swelled before popping back into place. Once it had finished its body bore no sign of the two animals it had consecutively consumed and it backed out of the coop in one swift motion, disappearing once again over the fence. I stood trembling, at a loss for any explanation for what I had witnessed. It was just a fox. But foxes don’t look like that, they aren’t able to do that. *No,* that was, in fact, a very sick, diseased fox. I felt the anxious tension leave me as I rolled this explanation around my head, where it gained momentum like a snowball and grew into the perfectly satisfactory notion that the thing was just an incredibly sick looking animal. Nothing more. In fact, this could even be good news, maybe whatever was wrong with it was contagious and would speed up the process.   
Its flesh sunk and bulged, cracking wetly like thickly plastered wallpaper over a cavern of damp rot. *Whatever it has must be pretty severe*, I remember thinking. Even then I didn’t believe it, but the idea served my interests just fine.

When our seven hundred kilogram cow went missing the following week it shook the foundations of this belief. But I held steadfast, choosing instead to focus myself with mentally crossing the task of milking off my job list from here on out.  
My parents hadn’t exactly allocated me food again but they no longer actively prevented me from taking it. As our livestock steadily depleted I squirreled away as much as was possible, day by day, without raising suspicion. I told myself that I was preparing to run, to break the cycle of revilement and mundane suffering. I would scrape together what strength I could and flee, leaving everything I was obliged to.   
And then they would see just what they took for granted.

Egg collecting became increasingly easier. From twenty, to eighteen, sixteen, fourteen and so on, it took the chickens in twos. I counted them down with each step as I walked the span of the chicken run. My twice daily circuit of the chicken run shortened every day, the angry red band the basket pressed into my arm lessened with the reduced weight.   
This blessedly meant that, little by little, I had time on my hands. I spent it as out of the line of sight of my parents as I could. This was not always achieved by distance. Often I favoured the roof, the fields all of a sudden starting to seem a little less suffocating as I viewed them through the hopeful lens of this place becoming past tense within my life. I would abandon here, leaving it to become no more than a bad memory within the much broader scope of my existence.  
Despite this, the roof had its downsides- more than just the literal harsh drop down if I misplaced my footing, but the regrettable fact that, just one barrier above my baby brother’s room, I was many times beset by all manner of wailing, incessant whining and- worst of all, the calm, measured voices of my parents in response, placating their sole child with low, singsong tones that I would never have recognised.   
Sometimes this worked, pacifying their precious pride and joy for a time. When it didn't, as it often didn't, he would persist to wailing, screaming, piercing my nerve endings with his high pitch as effectively as taking a hammer and nail to them.  
This was the arrangement on the night I let it happen. It wasn't my fault. I didn't *do* anything. Nothing I couldn't be forgiven for, committed no sin I couldn't be empathised with for.  
I lay against the slants of the roof, hard clay tiles bore into the aching notches of my crooked spine. I counted the pinprick shapes of livestock roaming the fields, twenty sheep, three horses, zero cows, eight chickens. One fox.

  
I smiled as I watched a thin shape emerge as reliable as clockwork from the tree line. Turning my gaze towards the sky, I trusted the creature to fulfil its nightly favour. The late evening sun was completing its descent down into the dark well of the horizon, turning the animal figures on the sloping field to dark silhouettes, creating a shadowplay puppet theatre against the dusky backdrop and staining the sky with an oily, iridescent sheen. Watching the bleeding rainbow I felt a sense of serenity that I had never before been overcome by. Just for a moment I felt my anxiety, my pain, trickle away to numbness, away and over an invisible apex that felt as far away as the horizon. This moment felt more precious to me than anything I'd ever had, and it was mine. The first thing that felt like it fully belonged to me. I wished I could bottle this feeling so it could never leave me as I knew it would. 

  
Then came the wailing. I couldn't even have this. This little moment. He would always take everything, have everything I wasn't given the slightest morsel of, most of all our parents' affection, reserved and dedicated as it was for him. I bit down on the fleshy insides of my cheeks and tasted the warm ooze of blood, I curled onto my side and pain rushed back to every facet of my body like someone had turned a valve. My back screamed with pain, the muscle felt as if taken to with an electric hot knife and prised away as you would honeycomb from a frame. With my hands pressed to the sides of my head it took me a long moment to register the shape of the garbled words that had burst from my throat with the force of a ruptured artery.   
I went rigid, bathed in fear and white hot agony as I listened for the consequence of my outburst. Nothing else punctuated the continued wails of my sibling. *How could they possibly sleep through this?* When no recompense raised itself I was overcome with an adrenaline fuelled sense of boldness. I shouted again,   
“Let me be!” I heaved. My lungs burned. My voice dropped to a whisper, volume seized by a racking cough. “I don’t ever want to see you again, I don't ever want to hear you again, just give me bliss. That’s all I want.” The *drip, drip* of tears against the tiles was entirely inaudible against the noise, the salt rendering my eyes raw and painful to keep open. I climbed slowly and agonisingly down the side of the house and back through the already open window of the hallway.  
The wails ceased not long after. I turned over in my bed and was consumed by a ravenous sleep.

I awoke the next morning to a silent house. I moved hazily, untangling the bedcovers from my aching legs, my mind felt as though coated with a thick blanket of fog, no doubt just the wake of an uncomfortable dream that would be rendered completely forgotten by the time I made it downstairs to breakfast.  
I stood in the threshold of the kitchen, the blazing white sun lit up the kitchen table like a milky floodlight, exposing nothing but the utter vacantness of the space. A distant part of me wondered inanely if I had passed away in my sleep and I had been left in a pallid purgatory of the only existence I had ever known. A soft, almost inaudible ticking met my ears. 8:34am. Far past my allotted time for many of my daily tasks. My heart thumped and I swiped an empty egg basket from the counter.

I passed by the sheep and counted all twenty pressed tightly into a single squirming cloud. The three horses shied in the crook of the fence. I arrived at the chicken coop.  
Two, four, six, eight. Eight chickens.   
All present and accounted for. A whistling whisper crept through the fields as though the tall grass itself was trying to muffle a secret held by the land. The gnarled limbs of an outstretched tree creaked as it cradled a choir of wrens. The birds trilled, a chorus- shrill and abrupt, almost a cry, that reverberated in my mind,   
I spun on my heel.   
*Not quite all.* 

When I reached the top of the stairs I found everything in a state as though it had been handled with careless haste. The deep gauge parallel to the doorhandle made plain that the door had been thrown wide, my parents' bedcovers obscured their floor, pillows lay strewn across the carpet, but the room bore no sign of where they could have gone.  
Beyond the threshold of my brother's room lay nothing but an empty crib, rocking slightly, caught in the breeze that swept the curtains of the open window.  
I moved hesitantly towards the window and drew back the gossamer fabric. A car pulled up, tyres crunching on the gravel road, sun glistening off its fluorescent Battenburg markings. I watched as my mother and father stepped out from the back of the police car, faces pointed to the ground as they turned, their backs to the window, and were joined by the officer who lingered for a moment to exchange a few, short words and patted my father gently on his shoulder. With this, he left my parents standing rigidly by the side of the road. When they finally raised their heads and saw me standing before the window of their absent child’s bedroom I was at the mercy of a look which did not bare hatred. It didn't need to. Hatred was fast and simple, it burned out in explosive actions and engulfed sense. There was a measured, patient look in their eyes, whatever lay behind their placid expressions would manifest at some date and had ample time to fester.   
I ran. 

I screamed. I cried out for the thing to give me back my brother. That I hadn't meant it.   
Curled and weeping against the forest floor, my shouts turned to choking gurgles as I pressed my face to the dirt and sobbed.

It was dark when the train pulled into the station at the clearing of the forest. I don't remember boarding it. The cold metal of the handrail burned against the sunburnt flesh of my neck, I pressed against it, the pain kept me lucid until the train pulled into the final station. 

I could never say that I left that place behind. Even as I stepped onto the platform at Marylebone station I couldn't manage to convince myself that I was free of it.   
After several years of walking the streets of London I knew that it would never release its grip on my psyche. I felt as though it was waiting for me. I knew something was.

It was early morning when I stepped from the same train carriage five years later.   
Even in the stark light of day the clearing looked exactly as it had the day I left.   
When I arrived at the farm everything was still. The barnhouses were covered in graffiti and withering, entirely bereft of any sort of livestock. The house was in an identical state, hollowed out and occupied only by woodworm. I hope my parents moved away, that they sold their livestock off to some other farm, but I don't have much hope anymore.

It's all quiet now. The silence hangs as heavy as the toll I paid for it.   
I can’t bring myself away from this place, saddled with the debt that I took on in exchange. Drowning in a sunk cost so deep there is no longer any hope of resurfacing.  
Was he still alive somewhere beyond my reach? Or had his life been claimed with the rest of him? The questions marred my mind. Bid me no sleep, no rest.   
I feel there is only one form of salvation for me. To wait for the thing to return and decide that everything I had given wasn’t enough, and offer myself in recompense.
