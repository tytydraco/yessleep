I’ll keep this short because my phone is at 31% and I’m still standing on the scale.

Last post, I stepped onto Kiosk 4 at 3:20 so it would stop asking. The front doors opened and I heard keys.

It was a guy in a Hartwell’s vest. Fifties, gray in his mustache, lanyard like mine. He walked in like he’d done it a thousand times, went to the podium, and pulled my notebook out from under it. I was six feet away. He didn’t look at me.

He read the whole thing standing up. At the end he said, “Oh, you’re on four.” Then he said, “You’re standing on it.”

I said yes.

He said most people run.

I asked who he was. He said Walt, he’s the day guy, he comes in at ten to six. I checked my phone and it said 3:26. The clock over customer service said 5:50. I can’t explain that. I wrote both numbers on a receipt.

I asked what he does when he gets here. He said he taps the card, it says “Please continue,” and he goes and clocks in. I asked what happens to the person. He said he’s never seen it. Twenty-two years. He has a shelf of notebooks at home, all different handwriting, and he can’t place a single name in any of them. He keeps them anyway. He threw one out once and said he had a bad week after.

I asked if he’d ever thought about not tapping it. He said no. It never asked him to do anything else.

Then he took his card off his lanyard and turned it over. It said 01. I turned mine over. 04. I’ve had that card four months and never once looked at the back.

I said there are six kiosks. He said yeah. He’d always figured two and three were spares.

He said “huh” and didn’t say anything for a while.

I told him I wasn’t going to let him tap it. He said it doesn’t work that way. The card doesn’t do anything on its own, it just tells the machine somebody’s there to say yes. If nobody says yes, the machine keeps asking.

I asked what happens if I just stay on the scale.

“Then it reads right,” he said. “It doesn’t care who’s on it. It cares that the number’s what it expects.”

I looked at the screen. The red was gone, and under the weight there was a line of small gray print I’d never seen in four months of looking at that screen.

EXPECTED: 172.0

The scale said 172.0.

So I did the math right there. If I stay on, it’s happy. If I get off, it asks. If it asks and nobody taps, the lights go and the doors start breathing. If somebody taps, I’m gone and I’m the only one who wouldn’t know about it.

I told Walt that meant I can’t leave. He didn’t argue. He said he was going to go clock in and that I should holler if I needed anything. I think he meant it.

At 6:02 Gina came in. Day cashier, braids. Or somebody. I wrote Gina in the notebook and drew a box around it, because I’m not sure anymore. She said good morning to Walt and walked right past Kiosk 4 without turning her head.

At 6:40 the first customer came to the self-checkouts, a woman with a basket. Kiosk 4 was the only one with its light on, so she used it. She scanned a dozen eggs and a box of cereal and set them in the bagging area. On the platform I’m standing on.

The screen went red.

“Unexpected item in the bagging area.”

The number in the corner said 181.4.

Eggs are about a pound and a half. Cereal is maybe a pound. I’m 172.0. That’s 175.5, not 181.4. I did it twice in my head and then I did it again on the receipt.

The woman said “Oh, come on,” to the screen and lifted the eggs off. She lifted the cereal off. She set both on the floor next to her basket.

The number said 178.4.

Nothing on the scale but me. And whatever’s the other 6.4.

I looked down. My shoes. The rubber mat. The little yellow footprints someone stuck on the platform years ago. Nothing touching me, nothing against my leg. I didn’t move. I haven’t moved.

The woman looked around for an attendant. She looked at the podium, which is empty, and then straight through me to the back of the store.

The gray line under the number just changed.

EXPECTED: 178.4

The doors opened by themselves. Nobody came in.
