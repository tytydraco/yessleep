Back in the early 70s, I attended an art class run by a very interesting fellow by the name of Jacques. I never caught his last name, but that’s not important. What is important is that out of his small class of four, I am the only one who didn't end up being *turned into an art piece*.

I had just moved to Boston to begin my undergraduate studies, and was looking for a way to fit in. Although my field of study was in no way, shape, or form related to the arts, I was intrigued by a campus flyer I saw during my first week. A gentleman who referred to himself as Jacques was teaching a “class” on fine art. He was not a faculty member, and he appeared too old to be a student, but I gave it no thought. I wanted in.

When I arrived for the first meeting, I found myself alone in a classroom with Jacques. He was a quizzical looking man, and his thin-framed glasses and large front teeth brought to mind a beaver who decided to become an architect. He approached me, and the sleeves of his wrinkled white shirt slid back as he raised his hands slowly towards the ceiling.

“Oh, how wonderful,” he said, in a voice like a child’s. “The others are in here.”

He kept one arm extended above his head as he used the other to point towards a small door at the edge of the room. As we entered, I noticed three other students positioned behind canvases, and they all turned around to face us. The room was massive, and it was filled with junk and sewing equipment, likely a long-forgotten storage space.

“This is the art room,” said Jacques, looking at me, as though for approval.

I nodded and approached one of the many empty easels, next to a red-haired woman who had caught my eye.

“What have you decided to paint?,” I inquired.

“I’m thinking something *odd*,” she replied.

I settled on painting one of the dusty looms in the corner of the room. Something about the strings and the cobwebs being intertwined spoke to me. The red-haired woman, who told me her name was Gabriella, painted a portrait of a cow in a dress that I truly quite liked.

“Very good, everyone,” said Jacques, as he approached the two of us.

“Quite the contrast here, it seems,” said Jacques. “I see a happy swine next to a sad, old loom.”

The way he said it rubbed me the wrong way, but I ignored it. As he approached the two other students to compliment their work, I took note of the crucifix necklace he was wearing, and left.

A week passed before the club met once again, and nobody new had joined. In fact, the flyers around campus had been removed. 

I stood next to Gabriella again, who seemed to be taking a liking to me. We had spent time together after the first art class and had studied for our actual classes together a couple times since.

As Jacques entered the room, Gabriella squeezed my hand. Hard. As Jacques passed the two of us, I looked at him, and saw the crucifix on his necklace was upside down. The other two students took notice as well, and we all traded glances before Jacques turned around to face us all.

We completed his exercise, something about cathedral paintings, and left. Gabriella and I caught up with the two other students who had been there, and we stopped them. They were also unnerved by Jacques’ necklace, so I proposed we meet that night in the art room to figure out what to do about it. We set a time, and went our separate ways.

I arrived at our little meeting a bit later than I expected, and my three compatriots were already in the art room.

“He’s a witch,” said a man.

“I think he would technically be considered a warlock,” said the other man, matter-of-factly. “Besides, that’s ludicrous.”  
  
“I agree. It is ludicrous,” said Gabriella. “Besides, Salem’s nowhere near here.”

I laughed as I entered the room, and the others turned to me. In my hands, I held two buckets, one filled with various paints and the other with hand tools.

“I apologize,” I said. “I became sidetracked.”

The others forgave me, and I went into my spiel.

“I say we create something that forces him to leave,” I said. “Something so awful and grotesque that he never comes back here.”

I smiled a sly smile, while Gabriella giggled and the two men looked at each other and nodded.

“Now, I’ve already prepared a bit of a surprise for everyone, so I need you all to close your eyes,” I said.

The three closed their eyes, and I removed the hatchet from the toolbucket, slowly walking up to the man who I presumed to be the strongest in the room, and buried it into his neck. He began to gurgle as the man next to him opened his eyes, and right as he did, I plunged the letter opener I had been keeping in my pocket into his throat, the blade entering through the gap in his Adam’s apple and out the back of his neck. 

I then lunged towards Gabriella, who had begun to run towards the door in a beautifully futile attempt to escape. I tackled her into an easel, and she smashed the blank canvas that fell off over my head. I tore the fabric and broke it in half as she screamed and lunged for the doorknob. I picked up the easel and rammed the top of it into her back, impaling her. I grabbed its legs and her small frame and tilted her up to face the ceiling, her body slowly sliding down until it came to a rest where the easel’s legs intersected. Blood poured from her stomach as she choked on more blood, a beautiful pool forming under the easel as it ran down her body. The death rattle of one of the men blended with the choking in perfect harmony, and as the soundtrack played, I began my work disassembling the three.  
   
I removed their legs, discarding them. I nailed their upper halves with their arms outstretched to pieces of wood I had salvaged from one of the looms. After nailing the torsos to the wood, I moved three easels from the makeshift art room into the classroom it was connected to. I placed the two smaller easels on either side of a larger one. I mounted the two male torsos upside down on the smaller ones, whilst I mounted Gabriella’s right side up to the larger one, placing Jacques’ inverted crucifix around her neck.

In the end, I believe the scene truly rivalled the great crucifixion paintings. Though in paintings, you cannot smell the death, touch it, or taste it. I could.

While this was not the first effigy I had created, I will say that it was my most difficult, both in terms of creating the work of art itself, as well as orchestrating the deception required to lure my canvases and cover my tracks.  
   
Framing the odd bastard was the easy part, as he was quite dense, but at all the other colleges, I had found each art professor’s on-campus residence within days. Seeing as Jacques wasn’t a faculty member, I had to trail him back to his filthy tenement to alter his necklace, then later steal his necklace, and then again stage a scene while he wasn’t there. It was in his kitchen that the police found the pentagram I had painted on his floor, along with the murder weapons, and a handwritten confession I’d penned for him. The trial was fast, and he was hanged.

Now, you have likely inferred that I spun a flam about the decade this took place, and you’d be correct. I didn’t tell you which 70s, after all. But even though that was a lie, you certainly would’ve heard about something this gruesome regardless of when it took place, right? Maybe the 1872 Massachusetts Crucifix Murders would have their own Wikipedia page by now, had it not been for the Great Boston Fire of 1872 that happened a few months later, which destroyed any and all trace of them ever occurring.

The entity I summoned the first time I did this offered me eternal life in exchange for more effigies. My end of the bargain? Four effigies per annum. While I used to gladly make more, it has become tedious to the point that now I only do the bare minimum of one person per effigy, four times a year. 

I don’t exactly pass for a student anymore, which has forced me to abandon my traditional way of doing things. Eternal life has not equated to eternal youthfulness. Plus, modern investigative techniques mean I must hide my work. Through trial and error, though, I’ve found that it is much easier now than it was before to source my canvases.

See, I had to travel many hours or days on foot or by carriage to myriad college towns, only to spend weeks getting acquainted with people whilst posing as a student. Now, with a few simple keystrokes, I can do the same thing in minutes to people of all ages, and then just drive to their homes.

Of course, I’ve had to check your profiles and online presences to see who’s an art fan, which takes a bit more time, but it’s still very convenient. I’m very particular about that. Only people who appreciate art can *become* art.

Now that this has been up for a little while, I’ve had plenty of time to vet the candidates for my third quarter effigy, and I think I’ve found the perfect subject.

See you soon.
