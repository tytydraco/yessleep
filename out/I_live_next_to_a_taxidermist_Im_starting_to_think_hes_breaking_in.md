It started with objects in my house moving on their own: a plate on the table instead of the counter, furniture shifted a few inches to the left or right. I mostly wrote it off to forgetfulness, to accidents, to my cat Chirps knocking stuff around while I was at work. In retrospect, it started around the time that *he* moved in, even if I didn’t connect the dots back then.

Victor Crutov was an old coot who had supposedly inherited a small fortune and was now holing up somewhere to waste it away until he died. It was just the two of us on my floor of the apartment, and somehow he wound up in the room next door to mine. I’d met my fair share of weirdos while living at that particular complex, but this guy was on a whole different level.

His apartment was filled from floor to ceiling with taxidermy, real weird pieces. I’m just not taking deer heads and stuffed birds, but horrible combinations of animals. Skunks with the limbs of a deer, snakes sewn to have the heads of cattle, everything swapped and stretched and twisted out of proportion. It seemed to be his thing, replacing bits of one thing with bits of another.

The whole room was always dead silent, filled with the stares of hundreds of glassy eyes.   
Needless to say, I tried to avoid the place at all costs, and started taking extra care to keep Chirps from going outside my apartment. I didn’t want him getting scooped up and stitched into something nasty.

Even with my avoidance, it’s not like I *never* bumped into Crutov. If anything, he seemed to seek me out wherever he could. He’d find me in the shared laundry room or the mail drop. There were a few times when I swear he was waiting in the lobby for me to return from work.

We would always have polite, awkward, creepy conversations. He loved to talk about his taxidermy, what he called “the work”. He made it very clear that this apartment complex was going to be the site of his greatest creation, the “last big project” of his.

The man was crazy, but I tried to brush him off as much as I could. I’d dealt with weirdos before, and Crutov seemed like just another case of insanity. 

What really got me was the snipping.

Crutov had a very particular pair of scissors he would always use, tiny ones that looked made for kids, but were wicked sharp. Every time he would clip them together, they would make a loud “snip” that would echo through the thin plasterboard walls of my apartment. 

The man was always working, and so into the late hours of the night I would hear those scissors, snip, snip, sniping away at whatever poor creature he had on his table. I tried earplugs, I tried white noise, nothing seemed to help. Eventually exhaustion would catch up and I’d drift off, but even then I wasn’t freed. I would hear those clippers in my dreams, I swear. 

My days weren’t much better. I was stuck at a horrible, soul-sucking office job. One of my coworkers told me I looked different, somehow. Another told me I look like shit, straight up. Everyone at a place like that looks terrible, but because everyone looks terrible, you have to look *really* bad to stand out.

I will admit, I kind of noticed it in the mirror at times. I looked off, in a way that was hard to place. I reasoned that it was probably the lack of sleep. Like most things, I just brushed it off. 

That was, until Chirps went missing.

I searched my entire apartment top to bottom. I moved everything I owned from one side of the room to the other, looking for Chirps everywhere, even trying to find a hole that he might have escaped through. I stood in the hallway shaking his bag of treats for hours on end.

I never found him. More worryingly, during the search, I realized that my spare key was missing as well.

I always left the spare in the drawer in my kitchen. I never, ever moved it for any reason. It would still be there, unless it was moved. Or, unless it was stolen.

I immediately called my landlord to ask who else might have access to my apartment. The missing cat was a shock, but the key made it a legitimate threat to safety. Someone would have to be able to get into my apartment to even take the spare in the first place, I complained. 

He assured me that nobody but himself had the master key. I was just about to hang up when he said,

“I mean, it’s *possible* that some of those keys are a bit interchangeable. The locks for each floor are based on the same mold with small adjustments, so hypothetically, if the keys got worn out enough,” he said, thinking, “someone could maybe jimmy it into the wrong door? But you’re alone on that floor, right, besides that one old guy?”

After another pause:

“And are you sure you haven't just lost the key? You sound a bit off right now.”

I didn’t even justify this with a response. I sat there, fuming in my living room. Slowly, as the anger wore down, I became aware of the noise that had been going on this whole time, echoing through my walls. Snip. Snip. Snip.

I charged into the hallway and banged on Crutov’s door, hard. I knew he was in there, and I wasn't going to take no for an answer. I banged again, and the snipping stopped. Crutov opened the door. He looked weirdly happy to see me. 

“Yes?” he said, dragging out the s longer than any normal person would. 

“You’ve been breaking into my room, haven't you? You took my fucking cat.”

He grinned. His smile curled at the edges, almost Grinch-like. 

“I’m a bit taken aback that you’d accuse me of such a thing!” he exclaimed, “why you you think that?”

I stopped a bit in my tracks. Why did I believe it was him? In retrospect, I was not thinking clearly. Things had been fuzzy since the man moved in next door, since I’d stopped getting good nights of sleep. I had no real evidence besides the maybe tip about the spare key, and it wasn’t like anything of value was missing. Chirps could, theoretically, have just run away somehow.

I told him as much.

“Well, I can put your mind at ease, then. I’ll give you my key, and you can see if it works in your lock,” he said, before stepping back, "come in, why don’t you?”

Anyone who has ever watched a slasher is likely screaming at me to not do it, and I thought the same thing. But before I’d rushed out of my apartment, I’d had the foresight to grab a small, but very sharp cutting knife from my kitchen drawer. It pressed heavily against my back pocket. Plus, I made a mental note to keep Crutov in sight at all times.

His apartment was as bizarre as I remembered it. Mounted directly above the doorframe was a taxidermied eagle, wings spread wide. The piece included a large bass, too, but instead of being clutched in the talons of the bird it was bursting from its stomach, Alien style. The eagle's face was one of shock and pain.

Worse still was the poor thing lying on the table in the center of the room. It must have been his new project, and was likely once a labrador, its fur matted with red blood. It was covered in all kinds of strange incisions, cuts that wouldn’t be how I stuffed a family pet. The skin hung in flaps along its face and spine, revealing scarily polished bone. I would say it stared at me, but the eyes couldn’t even do that. They were just empty sockets. 

The scissors gleamed beside the carcass. 

Crutov caught me looking.

“Oh, don’t mind that,” he said, “just a little side project to keep the fingers warmed while I work on the masterpiece. It’s amazing how it’ll all stitch together when I’m done.”

I felt a little nauseous, and only mustered a “sure.”

“No, truly,” he said, pulling the skin back together and mimicking a stitch, “the stitching method is a family secret. You'll never know it was even cut.”

“Look, I hate to be rude,” I said, already backing towards the door, “but if you’ve got something to show me, do it now. Otherwise, I’m leaving. And changing the locks.”

Crutov looked a bit put out, but only for a moment, before turning to step into a small side room.

Unlike my apartment, I realized, Crutov’s had not one, but two rooms branching from the central living space. I could see him in his bedroom, rifling through his drawers. I couldn’t see Chirps anywhere, even as I tried to quietly peek into a few of his drawers. He was still fumbling for that key when I got to the door for the other room. 

It was locked.

“Oh here it is,” Crutov said, returning to the living room with a key in his hand, “my copy of the key. Try it in your lock and see if it fits. That should put your mind at ease.”

I backed out into the hallway, making sure to keep my eyes on the man. Sure enough, Crutov’s key wasn’t worn enough to work in my lock, not even close.

When I stepped back into his apartment he was cutting at the poor dog again. The snips seemed to echo around the entire space. Snip. Snip. Snip.

“Sorry, man,” I told him, going to hand the keys back, “I haven’t been feeling myself lately. If you see my cat, let me know.”

Crutov didn’t even look up from his work. By now, his arms were deep into the carcass, matted with blood. He was cutting away at something inside it, pulling apart organs and tissue and bone. Snip. Snip. Snip.

“You know,” he said, “Sometimes a piece gets damaged during the whole process. I’m not perfect, I mess up cuts. You’d be amazed at how interchangeable pieces can be.”

He laughs out loud. I can’t manage the same.

“Sometimes, I’ll finish with a project,” he says, as if telling the punchline of a joke, “and half the animal will be spare parts. Spare legs, spare fur, spare eyes.”

Snip. Snip. Snip.

I realized I didn’t want to be there anymore. The guy was weird, and the whole place was creeping me out. I felt the bulge of the knife in my back pocket, and placed a hand on it. Glass eyes stared at me from all angles, crying.

“Yeah, thanks for the offer, but I think I’m going to go,” I said, placing his key down on the nearest table and backpedalling.

I didn’t quite hear what he said as he slammed the door, but it sounded to me as though he responded:

“It wasn’t an offer.”

I didn't sleep well at all that night. I was thinking about Chirps, about where he could possibly be, about my spare key, and worst of all, about Crutov himself. The snipping snuck through my walls, as loud as ever, and I could just picture him grinning as those tiny scissors separated sinew from bone, skin from meat, eye from socket. Snipping away until nothing was left.

As I turned things over in my head, I became fixated on that one locked room in Crutov’s apartment. Why would he have it locked off, when he already had a lock on his main door? What could he be hiding in there? And as the snipping continued and Chirps never returned, I grew more and more convinced that that’s where the man next door was hiding him. 

I formulated a plan. I would call off work and wait for Crutov to leave his apartment. The man was obsessive, but he still had to eat, to wash his things. While he was gone, I would see if my key, a worn piece of junk that only worked half the time, worked on his door. Then there would be the problem of getting into the locked room, but I’d figure that out when the time came. I’d kick it down if necessary.

I eventually drifted off into sleep during the late hours of the night, the exhaustion of the whole situation punctuated still by the snip, snip, snip of those scissors. They seemed to measure the seconds, cutting time itself into maddening pieces. 

I had a dream that night, a vivid one. I was trying to climb the stairs to my apartment, only little bits of me kept falling off. First it was my earlobe, then my pinky finger. My toes and thumbs were next, followed by bigger chunks. My whole thigh broke free, separated at the hip by a neat line. I began to tumble, to fall apart, to roll down the steps piece by piece, eventually just a head and eyeballs.

And at the bottom were the scissors, giant and snipping, always snipping, no matter the hour of the day, and as my feet and hands and forearms rolled into them they were diced into fine bits, smaller and smaller pieces, snipped and snipped until nothing remained at all. My head rolled, bouncing closer and closer, and the snips grew louder and louder and louder, snip, snip snip snip, until they were right in my ear.

I shot awake. The scissors had been right next to my ear, I was sure of it. I had heard them in that half awake way, cutting, not just as echoes through the walls but right next to me, in my room.

I flicked on the lights. It was early morning, and the sun hadn’t risen enough to light anything beyond vague outlines. All in all, I had probably slept less than three hours. I shook my head to clear it, as much as I was able to.

I realized it was too quiet. I couldn’t hear the snipping from the next room over.

I did a check of my whole room, then a double check. Nothing was out of place, and I was sure of that. I figured if Crutov wasn’t cutting, he must be either asleep or out somewhere. Either way, I had my opportunity. I *had* to see what was in that locked room. 

I grabbed my key, and my knife again. Both felt strange in my palm, different in some way that I couldn’t quite place.

I crept out into the hallway. My key slid into the lock in Crutov’s door with ease. I turned it, but the lock wouldn’t budge. I fiddled the key a bit more, and still no luck. Every small motion of the key made loud, metallic grinding noises. I was about to give up when, out of impulse, I turned the door handle.

The door silently swung open. It had been unlocked this whole time. 

I drew the knife from my back pocket, on edge. This was getting too weird.

All around me I could see the eyes and strange heads of the taxidermy, staring in the dim light of morning that was starting to crawl through the window. I was just barely able to see and move throughout the space. I inched forward, stepping as gently as I could across the floor.

The dog had been removed. In its place stood only its four legs, standing upright and posed as if running. I wondered, for a second, what it was fleeing. 

The second door was unlocked too.

I should have known, then, that something was off, that I was somewhere deeply wrong. I should have turned. In a way, I knew that this was the threshold of something, but I was pulled forward anyway.

The room was dim enough that I had to flick on the flashlight of my phone. A quick glance around revealed nothing special, just rows and rows of tiny drawers, each labelled carefully in neat handwriting. I didn't see anywhere that would have been big enough to hide my cat, that was for sure, but I knew Crutov wouldn’t be above cutting it to pieces.

I reached for the nearest drawer and read the label. *May 29, 5:12 A.M*.

It was dated less than a month ago. I slid it open.

Inside sat a single human finger, still in perfect condition, still bloodied at the stump. 

I jumped back, knocking into the shelves behind me. My eyes flicked to other drawers. *May 15, 4:29 A.M. April 8, 5:51 A.M.* They were all recent. They were all dated after Crutov moved in next door.

I opened another. It was a tiny chunk of flesh, one that I eventually figured to be an earlobe. Another that seemed to be a tongue. 

My heart began to pound. I kept opening drawers. *April 23, 3:45 A.M.* A thick chunk of flesh that I couldn’t identify. *June 2, 4:43 A.M.* A part of a toe.

I spun behind myself, and realized the other wall of cabinets was dated for the future. *July 30. August 9.* These ones didn’t have times, not yet, but there were blank spaces next to each and every one. I slid one of the drawers open and inside was a thumb, looking perfectly intact and normal. Another revealed an eyeball, which I prodded with my finger. It wasn’t glass.

Something was eating at the back of my mind, something about the times. I continued to open drawers. There was a big one, August 28. Written underneath was: the finishing touches.

I slid it open, and at first I didn’t realize what it was. It looked like a crumpled sheet of paper, or a discarded piece of clothing. As I turned it over, it clicked. It was a face. 

It was *my* face. 

I stood there, a perfect replica of my face in my hands. *August 28.* No time written just yet.

And then it hit me. I had gotten little sleep since Crutov moved in, but all of the times on all of the drawers I had opened were times when I would have been sound asleep. 

I rushed back to the drawer with the finger, and after bracing myself, I pulled it free. I held it up next to mine. The two were almost a perfect match, down to childhood scars. 

And in the dim light of my phone flashlight, I noticed something for the first time, something I hadn’t seen before. A thin, almost imperceptible scar along the base of my pointer finger, as if somebody had stitched it back on.

That, or stitched something close enough.

There was a banging in the hall that made me jump. It sounded like it was coming from the door to my apartment.

I heard Crutov’s voice.

“I found your cat!” he said, then after a moment, “come out so I can get him back to you!”

There was something off in the man's voice, more off than normal. It sounded even more enthusiastic than normal.

I realized in that single, terrifying moment that I’d left the door to Crutov’s apartment open. He knew I was in here.

I clenched the knife in my hand and bolted for the door. A frame moved to intercept me, and I swung. I heard the solid thunk of the connection, and Crutov’s face was there before me, wide-eyed. Something that was once a cat fell from his hand.

And even then, as blood started to pool from his lips, he was grinning.

“I’m glad you finally appreciate the work,” he said.

I turned and ran.

I haven't visited the apartment in days. My work keeps calling me, but I don’t answer. I’m holed up in a cheap motel room, miles and miles from where I once lived, typing this out so that if something happens to me, you’ll know where to look.

Even now, when I look in the mirror, I can’t totally be sure of any of it. Did my nose always look that way? When I trip in the hallway, is it tiredness? Or has my right leg always been that long? Have my fingers always fit that way across my keyboard, around my phone, across my face? 

I want to go to the police, but they’ll never believe me, and I stabbed Crutov in broad daylight. I have been sleeping terribly. I’m sure it’s just the stress, that’s what I tell myself at least, but every night, as I slip off into the depths of my nightmare-ridden sleep, I still hear it.

The snip, snip, snipping of those scissors.
