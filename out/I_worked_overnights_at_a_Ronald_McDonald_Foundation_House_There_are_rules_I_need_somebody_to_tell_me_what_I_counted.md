Okay. I've started this post about nine times and deleted it about nine times so I'm just going to type and not look back, and if the commas end up in the wrong places that's between me and God.

I worked overnights at a Ronald McDonald Foundation House for eleven months. I'm not telling you which one. There's better than three hundred of them scattered across the country and here's the thing nobody tells you, they are all the same House. Same carpet the color of weak coffee. Same kitchen with the industrial fridge full of donated Go-Gurt. Same bulletin board with the same thank-you cards from the same families. And out in the lobby, same bench, same clown parked on it in his yellow jumpsuit, one arm stretched along the backrest like a guy at the drive-in waiting for his date to lean in.

You know what a House is, probably. If you don't, count your blessings, and I mean that. Families stay there for free while their kid is across the street in the children's hospital getting the kind of treatment that has a wing named after it. It is a good place. I need you to hold onto that because I'm about to tell you some things and I don't want you thinking I'm saying it's a bad place. The pancakes on Sunday are real. The moms crying in the laundry room at 1 AM are real. The little girl who called me Mister Night was real.

The rules are real too.

They don't give you the rules at orientation. Orientation is the handbook, which is forty-two pages of fire exits and peanut allergies and a laminated flowchart about what to do if a family's insurance lapses. The rules come after. After everybody else has gone home and it's just you and the House Manager and the sound the building makes when the HVAC kicks on, which I always thought sounded like somebody upstairs sighing.

My House Manager was Dolores. Nineteen years at the House. Dolores smiled at everything, at the UPS guy, at the burnt coffee, at the Blue Jays losing. She did not smile when she slid the card across the desk at me.  
It's an index card. Laminated, the kind of lamination that's gone yellow and lifted at the corners. I still have it. I shouldn't, I know I shouldn't, but I do and I'm typing it out word for word:

**OVERNIGHT COORDINATOR — RESIDENTIAL GUIDELINES (SUPPLEMENTAL)**

Lobby lights stay on. 

If they go out do not go to the breaker. Wait. They come back.

Do not sit on the bench. Do not let a parent sit on the bench. Children may sit on the bench.

If a child is on the bench after 2:00 AM, do not speak to the child. Note the time in the red binder. Return to the desk.

If a child asks you where their parents are, walk them to Room 7. Do not tell them there is no Room 7.

The morning headcount must match the evening headcount. If it does not match, write the evening number. Do not recount.

He is a friend to all children. Remember he is only a friend to children.

I laughed. 

Of course I laughed, you'd have laughed, it's a laminated index card about a fiberglass clown. 

Dolores waited me out the way you wait out a kid who's got the giggles in church. Then she said, "Most nights it's nothing. Follow the card on the nights it isn't." And she put on her coat and went home and left me there with the clown and the sighing.

Here's what overnights are like. You do headcount at 10 and again at 6. You restock the juice boxes. You sit behind a desk with a little black-and-white monitor showing you the lobby and you read paperbacks, I went through most of the Dark Tower that year, which in hindsight, whoo boy. And the clown sits on his bench with his arm along the back and his big red shoes pointing at the front doors, and the lights hum, and nothing happens.

For a month, nothing happened.

Then one night in the second month I looked up from my book and the shoes were pointing at me.

Now. I want to be careful here, because I know exactly how this reads and I'd be rolling my eyes too. It's a statue. Fiberglass, hollow, bolted to a bench that's bolted to the floor. I went and checked the bolts, on my hands and knees on that coffee-colored carpet at three in the morning like an idiot. Bolts were fine. Bench hadn't moved an inch. The arm was still along the backrest. The face was still the face, that big painted grin that's supposed to say *I'm your pal* and in a dark lobby says something else entirely. Only the shoes. Thirty-some nights I'd looked at the soles of those shoes and now I was looking at the toes.

I wrote it in the red binder because I didnt know where else to write it.

I should explain the red binder. 

It lives in the bottom drawer of the desk and it's the binder from Rule 3 and it is almost empty. Almost. The entries go back to 1996 in a dozen different hands, ballpoint and pencil and one in purple gel pen, and nearly every one is the same: a time, and the word *bench.* 2:50 bench. 3:10 bench. A few have a kid's first name after. And one, from October of 2004, says:

*3:05 — bench — asked about Room 7 — walked her — came back alone — that is fine.*

That is fine.

Somebody sat where I was sitting, fifteen years before me, and wrote *that is fine* to themselves at three in the morning, and I knew exactly why they did it. You write a thing down and it stops being a thing that happened to you. It becomes a thing you are reporting. You get to be the reporter instead of the guy it happened to.  
I've done it since. I'm doing it right now.
