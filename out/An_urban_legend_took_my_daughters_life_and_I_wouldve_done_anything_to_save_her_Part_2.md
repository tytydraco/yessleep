Willow and I rode the rest of the way back to her house in silence.

I rolled to the curb in front of her house.  The place was dark, except for a garish fluorescent light in the kitchen.  Willow just stared up at it.

“It’s my fault,” she finally said.  “I should’ve stopped Nina from playing.”

“It wasn’t your fault,” I quickly answered.  “It was my responsibility to be there for her.  And I wasn’t.”

Willow took a few deep breaths, still trying to calm her nerves, then started chewing on the ends of her hair.

“I want you to take care, Willow.  Okay?”

I think this made her feel better.  That someone cared enough to be concerned.

“I want you to be careful, Mr. H.  Okay?”

“Don’t worry about me.  I’m going to find the gun.  I’m going to destroy it, I promise.”

After a long moment, Willow told me she wanted to believe me.  She said she'd try hard to believe me.  She said she genuinely thought that if anyone could do that, it would be me.

And then she said she’d pray for me.

I thanked her.

She smiled, which was a little forced but nice to see, then climbed out of the car.

Later that night, after two hours of Mario Kart 8 with Max, I tucked him into bed and jumped back online.

I quickly disappeared down a rabbit hole.  Theories and stories about the gun and the game made me dizzy.

Every search eventually pointed me toward the dark web.  Around midnight, I finally gave in and signed on to Tor, opening an even deeper rabbit hole, one seemingly without a bottom.

Hours later, the sun came up without me even noticing.  I paused long enough to get Max off to school, then returned to the computer.

More hours slipped by.

It wasn’t the first chatroom I’d found devoted to the game – there were others – but I finally stumbled into a vast room whose members seemed almost pornographic in their thirst for the gun.

The participants were faceless fanatics sequestered behind their computer screens, longing to play a game that offered a trapdoor out of the placidity of their lives and into our violent past.

Being here, “listening” to these conversations made me uncomfortable.  But I stayed, hoping someone – anyone, for God’s sake – had answers.

The buzz of my phone snapped me out of my trance.  It wasn’t the first time it had gone off, but it was the first time I’d paid attention.

It was Max’s school calling.  Again.

I was still blurry-eyed when I arrived.

Standing in Principal Gutiérrez’s office with Max’s math teacher, Ms. Archer, felt disorienting after too many hours on the dark web.

They had already explained the situation.  Now I was seeing the evidence.

Max was supposed to be taking notes on fractions, but instead, he’d been drawing a picture of a gun.

I knew that gun intimately.  It was the Lefaucheux revolver.

My first thought, if I’m being honest, was that I was impressed.  Max’s detailed shading had brought the gun vividly to life.

And it wasn’t the only sketch.  I flipped through Max’s notebook.  There were dozens of identical drawings.

“You can understand our concern,” Principal Gutiérrez finally offered.

I could.

“It’s rather disturbing,” Ms. Archer added.  “Wouldn’t you agree?”

“Disturbing?” I shot back.  “Are you implying my son is mentally disturbed for merely drawing a picture of a gun?”

Ms. Archer backpedaled.  She wasn’t giving a psychological evaluation.  She wasn’t qualified to.

I waved away her apology.  I had made my point.  Defended my son.

But the truth was, I had no explanation as to why Max had been drawing pictures of a gun.

Even more unsettling: Why had Max been drawing pictures of the Lefaucheux?

“Have you been on the internet?” I asked Max when we got home, sounding more like an accusation than a question.

“No,” Max said.

I wanted to believe him, but I was struggling.

“Then how do you know what a Lefaucheux revolver looks like?”

Max just shrugged.

“Look, I’m not mad.  I’m sorry if I sound mad.  I’m not.  But we’ve talked about you being on my computer without my permission.”

“I wasn’t.”

It was frustrating, but I was trying not to show it.

“I just want to understand, Max.”

“I wasn’t on your computer.  I wasn’t on the internet.  I’ve never seen the gun before.”

“Then how is it possible you drew a picture of it?”

Max shrugged again.  “It just sort of popped into my head, and I started drawing it, and I couldn’t stop.”

This didn’t make sense.  There had to be an explanation.  A logical one.

I was about to dispute things again when Max asked whether the gun was haunted.

“Where’d you hear something like that?”

Max couldn’t remember.

Whatever control I thought I still had on things was slipping away, and suddenly I knew one thing for certain: I had to protect my son.  I had to get Max as far away from all of this as I could until I found a way to rid our lives of this gun – and this game.

I suggested Max stay with his aunts for a few days, even though I hadn’t checked with Lauren and Kim.  I knew they’d say yes.

We were almost out the door when Max discovered his favorite T-shirt missing.  He started to panic.

I recognized how close Max was to the edge.  His anxiety was quickly escalating.  He was becoming animated – the way he does when he’s overwhelmed – his body rocking, his hands aggressively gesturing.

“Can’t you live without it for a few days, buddy?” I asked as gently as I could.

Max made it clear he couldn’t.  He wouldn’t survive without it.

“Have you checked your dirty-laundry basket?”

Max bolted up the stairs to check, hopeful.

I waited by the front door, even more hopeful.

When he didn’t return after a few minutes, I yelled up to him but got no answer.

I let another minute pass.

I needed to get Max to Lauren and Kim’s.  I needed to get back to my search.  Finding the gun was the only way to put this all behind us.

But Max still hadn’t returned.

I called to him again, then headed upstairs.

I entered Max’s bedroom, already talking.  “Max, your aunts are waiting for us.”

He wasn’t there.

A noise from the closet drew me across the room.  I nudged the door open.

Max was sitting on the floor with his back to me.

“You find it?”

I could see that he had.  He was wearing his favorite T-shirt, Astro Boy emblazoned across the back.

I stepped closer.  “Let’s go now, buddy.”

Then glanced over Max’s shoulder.

The Lefaucheux revolver sat cradled in his lap.

*What the--?*

I froze.

“Where’d you get that?”

Why did I sound angry?  I wasn’t angry.  I was *terrified*.

“Max, tell me.”

“I found it.  In my laundry basket.  Under my Astro Boy T-shirt.”

“Give it to me, okay?  It’s alright, just give it to me.”

Max lifted the gun out of his lap.  I snatched it from him.

“Don’t worry, buddy.  I’ll take care of it.”  I tried to sound casual, which wasn’t easy.  “Come on.  Your aunts are waiting for you.”

How the hell did the gun get into my house?

Into my son’s laundry basket?

I stuck the gun in my office safe, closed the door, and spun the dial, locking it.  Only then did I start breathing again.

Lauren and Kim were thrilled to see Max, smothering him with hugs.

With my son safely in their arms, I was eager to get back home.  I’d already begun thinking of ways to destroy the gun.  I would drill holes in a metal garbage can, fill it with wood, douse it with gasoline, and stoke the hottest fire I could.

I stepped into my office.  The room was cluttered as always, but I saw it immediately.

The door to the safe was swung open.

How was that possible?  I was the only person who knew the combination.

Even before I reached it, I could see the gun was gone.  My passport was there.  An envelope of cash.  My wedding ring.

But no Lefaucheux revolver.

I quickly called Lauren, still staring into the safe.

Still searching my brain for a logical explanation.

Maybe it was time for me to stop trying to make sense of things.  Maybe it was time to accept the anarchy.

Lauren answered.  I skipped hello – “Let me talk to Max.”

She had left him in his room unpacking, but he wasn’t there.

She checked the TV room.

She checked with Kim, who was making Max’s favorite – cheese enchiladas.

She peered into the backyard.

No Max.

Panic took hold.

I was in my car before I even knew what I was doing.  My sister’s house was less than ten minutes away, but time had slowed to a taunting crawl.

Then a memory forced its way into my mind.  Max at Lauren and Kim’s house, unpacking his knapsack.  Discovering the Lefaucheux among his things.

The scene played in my head as if I’d been standing in the doorway watching.

But I hadn’t been. 

I was still trying to push the uninvited memory out of my mind when I rushed through the front door, hoping Max would be there.

Lauren and Kim’s faces confirmed what I already knew.

My son was missing.

We all sprinted to the woods at the end of the block – Max’s favorite playground – and spread out without discussing it, each heading in a different direction.

Daylight was fading, draping the woods in shadows.

I kept calling Max’s name, but my voice caught in my throat, strangled by guilt.

Was all of this my fault?  I hadn’t been the parent I wanted to be lately.  My grief and anger over losing my wife had made sure of that.

Did the gun know that?

Did it understand how vulnerable my family was?

Was that why it had shown up in our lives?  Why it wouldn’t leave us alone now?

I pushed the thought away.  The questions were absurd.

I reached the old trestle and finally saw Max standing on the path a few yards ahead.

The Lefaucheux revolver was in his hand.

He was staring at it, transfixed.

I started toward him, then froze.

His thumb was resting on the hammer.

“Max, no!”

Maybe I should’ve rushed him.  Tackled him.  Ripped the gun from his hand.

But I didn’t.

I stopped just out of reach, unable to move.

“Max, drop the gun.”

His eyes never left the revolver.

I may as well not have existed.  Only the gun did.

“Look at me, Max.  Please!”

He didn’t blink.

Slowly, he placed his left thumb over his right.

My stomach dropped.

“Max!”

The hammer eased back.
