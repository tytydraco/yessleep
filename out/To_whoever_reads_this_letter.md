Or to the insignificant universe, time and space in the solar system that they call life, I would like to clear my chest to be free from the bonds of my subconscious.

Time has faded away like a leaf falling from its tree, slow but certain, carrying with it the knowledge that it will eventually come to rest upon the ground. The years of my pilgrimage are sixty too many, perhaps, for an undeserving creature like me.



I will not bore you with the entirety of my journey, but when everything began to shift into the depths of darkness, twenty-nine years became the days of my descent.



I had gotten out of a difficult marriage. She took everything I had, and with what little remained, I moved to another city to begin again, to start from genesis. But starting over is easier said than done. Jobs were scarce, and desperation has a way of humbling a man. I took the kind of work no one else wanted. It was not honorable, nor was it something I had ever imagined myself doing. But I was desperate.



I became a gravedigger.



In the beginning, it was difficult seeing the dead, smelling the rot of flesh, cleaning the graves, and handling what remained of people who had once laughed, loved, and breathed as I did. With time, however, I became accustomed to it. My main duties were digging graves and preparing corpses for reduction once enough time had passed. When the year was up, families would come to the cemetery and wait while we opened the casket to determine whether the remains were ready.



If they were not, we would tell them, “It is not ready. We will have to wait another year.” It became routine. The dead stopped frightening me. Then my coworkers introduced me to something else.



Bad influences have a way of making evil seem ordinary. They stole jewelry from the newly buried rings, necklaces, gold teeth, anything of value that could be taken after the ceremonies had concluded and the families had gone home. At first, I wanted no part of it. Then desperation, once again, proved stronger than conscience.



I became one of them.



I thought that was the worst thing I would ever do.



I was wrong.



There was a phrase I kept hearing among the gravediggers: “Two for one.”



I never asked what it meant. I was afraid that asking would make me look ignorant, and perhaps some part of me already knew I did not want the answer. If only I had asked.



It happened one afternoon while we were preparing a grave for a scheduled reduction. The family had come to the cemetery and waited nearby while we examined the casket. We opened it and found that the corpse was ready.



But there was something else inside.



Another body.



I remember staring at it, unable to understand what I was seeing. I looked at my coworker, and he looked back at me. Neither of us said a word. He closed the casket, turned to the family, and calmly told them, “It is not ready. We will have to wait another year.”



They thanked us and left.



We waited until the end of the day. When the cemetery was empty, we returned, removed the second body, and put it through the reduction process.



That was when my coworker explained what “two for one” meant.



It did not happen often. Most graves held what they were supposed to hold. Most caskets contained a single person, buried by a family who believed they were laying their loved one to rest. But sometimes very rarely there was another body. A second body that did not belong there.



Delinquents had discovered that a cemetery could be the perfect place to hide an atrocity. Somewhere no one would think to look, where another body could remain buried among the dead until time erased whatever evidence remained.



And when we found one, we became part of the process.



I asked my coworker the only question that seemed to matter: “Why don't we go to the police?”



He looked at me for a long time. Then he told me that someone had already tried. Someone had gone to the police and told them everything, but somehow the blame for the murder had fallen upon him. He was accused of being involved and was never able to prove otherwise. That was when I understood what we had become.



Not murderers. Not killers.



Accessories.



Every time we discovered one of those bodies, we were destroying evidence evidence of a crime that might otherwise have been solved, evidence that could have put a murderer behind bars.



And we destroyed it.



Not every grave. Not every casket. Only sometimes.



But once is enough to change a man's life.



For thirty years, I have carried this burden. Thirty years of knowing that beneath the earth, among the dead, there were secrets that were never meant to be found. Thirty years of watching families mourn while knowing there were things they would never be told. Thirty years of helping monsters bury their sins.



And there were too many of us.



Far too many.



Now I am sixty years old.



And I have finally decided that the dead deserve to speak.

Old age should burn and rave at close of day;



Rage, rage against the dying of the light".
