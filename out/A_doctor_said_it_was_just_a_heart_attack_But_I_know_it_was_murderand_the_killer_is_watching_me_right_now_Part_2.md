I built a device to make myself disappear, because the man who killed Daniel Rogers had already found me once.

The basement was cold, the kind of cold that gets into your fingers and stays there. A single bulb hung above my workbench, swinging a little every time the old furnace kicked on. Upstairs, the house was asleep. Down here, I had been awake for three nights straight.

It had been one week since Daniel died. One week since a library screen went black, then came back with four words burned into my head: *Welcome to our world, Neophyte Genius.*

In front of me sat a small black box, not much bigger than a matchbox. Wires ran out of one end, soldered into a tangle of resistors and a tiny chip I had ordered under four fake names, sent to four empty addresses, picked up late at night over four separate trips. This was not a flash drive. This was armor.

*Never again,* I thought. *Never again will someone trace me back to a library chair, or a school router, or my own bedroom.*

The plan was simple. Once plugged into any computer, the box would rotate my IP address, fake my hardware, and rewrite my whole digital trail every single second. A moving target. A ghost that left nothing behind.

I called it the Mask.

Footsteps came down the stairs, light and uneven on the fourth step, the one that always groans. Shelly. My hand moved before I even thought about it — one key combination and the terminal window was gone, replaced by a half-written school essay. By the time the door opened, I looked like a kid doing homework next to some spare parts.

She dropped onto the old stool by the bench, wrapped in an oversized hoodie.

"You've been lying on your bed for the last two hours, staring at the ceiling," I said, not looking up. "And you just wiped dust off Dad's photo frame."

"Okay, that's actually creepy now," she said, with no real heat behind it. "How do you do that without even looking at me properly?"

"I looked at you for less than a second. That's enough."

"Why were you crying?" I asked.

"I wasn't crying. Just thinking about Dad again. Couldn't sleep, saw your light on, came down."

"It happens to me too," I said.

We sat there for a moment with nothing but the fan humming between us.

"What is this thing?" she asked, nodding at the wires. "You've been down here for three days. Mom thinks you turned into a basement gremlin."

"It's a shield."

"Against what? Vampires?"

"Something like that." A small smile. "School project. Robotics club."

That was not entirely true, and not entirely a lie either. She squinted at the tangle of wires, clearly not buying it, but let it go.

"Did you know it's already been a two days  since Mr. Rogers died?" she said. "Maria's uncle."

She said it lightly, the way you mention the weather changing. She had no idea what those words did to me.

I kept my face still. On the outside, a small nod. On the inside, the basement disappeared, and I was back in the library, tracing a virus backward through server after server, the progress bar climbing. Eighty percent. Ninety. Then the screen goes black, and comes back with those same four words. Whoever killed Daniel had reached through the network and physically destroyed a machine in under three seconds, just to send me a message. Every scrap of proof I had gathered died with that terminal.

The only reason that attack hit a library computer and not my own bedroom was pure luck.

"Scott?" Shelly's voice pulled me back. "You went somewhere again. That weird quiet look."

"Sorry. Just thinking. It's sad. About Mr. Rogers."

Mom's voice came down the stairs a minute later, tired but warm. "Are you two down here in the dark again? Scott, it's almost midnight."

"Just a school project, Mom," I said, smooth. "Five more minutes. I'm almost done."

She studied me the way mothers do when they suspect there's more but are too tired to dig for it. "Five minutes. And eat something tomorrow. You've been skipping breakfast."

Shelly stood to follow her up, then paused at the door. "I'm glad you're down here, even if it's weird gremlin stuff. Less lonely knowing someone else is awake thinking about sad things."

"Goodnight, Shelly."

"Night, weirdo."

The door clicked shut. I sat alone with the small black device in my hands. *It's already been a week.* Her words kept landing like a quiet hammer against the calm I wore just for her.

*He knows there's a hacker out there who got close,* I thought. *He does not get to know who.*

I plugged the box into the old computer and hit enter on the final command.

Instead of a quick setup, the screen filled with a gray bar. **FLASHING FIRMWARE. DO NOT DISCONNECT.** Beside it, a number blinked to life.

*Estimated time remaining: 19 hours 42 minutes.*

There was nothing to do but wait. I switched off the lamp and went upstairs, careful to skip the fourth step. Below me, the machine kept working in the dark, building a ghost no one would see coming.

The next night, alone in my room, I plugged the Mask in and watched the numbers climb. MAC address rewritten. IP rotating, one new address every second. For the first time in a week, something in my chest loosened.

I dropped into the library's server logs, hunting for one date, one hour. The night the screen went black. Rows of timestamps filled the monitor. Most of it was nothing.

Then the screen went black again.

My heart slammed once, hard. Not again. Then I noticed the power light. Still on. The fan is still spinning. This was not a hardware kill.

A command prompt bloomed in the center of the dead screen, framed in thin green lines. Letters crawled across it, one at a time.

*Welcome, Neophyte Genius. It looks like you played a big game to escape from me. But I will find you anyway, boy.*

I typed back. *And I will find you too.*

*You are more skilled than the others. I have competed against big-time hackers before, but no one could ever catch me.*

*But I did catch you,* I typed.

*Yes, you did. But you cannot defeat me. I challenge you. I am going to launch a DDoS attack on you. Keep yourself online if you can. We are hackers, not murderers.*

*Okay.*

I hit enter and sat back.

It started as a low hum. Then it became a scream. My laptop's fan spun into a higher gear, buzzing against the desk. My network monitor lit up red, numbers climbing so fast they blurred. Latency jumped from twelve milliseconds to over four thousand in two seconds flat.

Sweat gathered along my hairline. I did not panic. I watched.

Under the noise, a pattern started to show. The requests came in tight bursts, exactly twelve milliseconds apart, too precise for ordinary hacked laptops. I pulled the packet signature apart and found the same footprint every time — thousands of hijacked smart devices, doorbell cameras, routers, thermostats, all firing on the same fixed clock because they weren't built to do anything smarter than that.

Weak design. And weak design meant a weakness I could use.

I fired up a gateway shifter, moving my own connection the instant it caught the botnet's pulse. The red numbers stuttered, then climbed slower. Not enough. I stacked a mirror script on top, bouncing a slice of the junk traffic back at the botnet's own relay nodes, letting it choke on its own garbage.

For three full seconds, everything on my screen turned solid red. **CRITICAL LOAD.** My hand froze over the keyboard.

Then, slowly, the numbers began to fall.

The fan's scream dropped to a hum. My latency crawled back toward twelve. I had held it.

The chat window blinked back to life. One line crawled across it.

*See you in the next challenge, Neophyte Genius.*

The window folded in on itself and vanished before I could reply, no trace left behind. Except this time, nothing had been destroyed. This time, I was still online.

He had not attacked to break me. He attacked to test me, the way a bully throws one small punch on the first day just to see if the new kid flinches. I had not flinched. Somewhere out there, hidden behind a hundred masks of his own, he was already thinking about his next move.

The war had just begun.

That night I sat with my eyes closed and tried everything I knew. Hardware fingerprints. Power grid spikes. A hidden exploit through his own chat client. Every idea fell apart the same way, one after another, like he had already thought of each move before I made it.

This isn't tracking a person, I thought. This is chasing a shadow that already knows every trick I know.

The next day blurred past me at school, my mind stuck on the same locked door. That evening, Shelly showed up with her boyfriend Ethan. We ate, we played a video game split-screen on the television, and somewhere in the middle of it, I noticed something small. Ethan wasn't just lucky. He adjusted to me half a second before I moved, cutting me off at the exact right spot instead of chasing blind. That kind of timing comes from real hours logged, not beginner's luck.

I filed it away and said nothing.

Later, Shelly put on a movie, and Ethan pulled a folded worksheet out of his jacket. "Our teacher made us type this up. Something feels off about it. Mind checking it?"

I looked down at the page.

There was a strange little space sitting right before every comma, every period. *The war began in 1939 , and it changed everything .* A gap that should not be there.

I had seen that exact gap before.

The room disappeared behind my eyes. Ethan's worksheet floated in the dark in front of me, and beside it, the killer's own words from three nights of chat logs.

*Yes , you can.*

*I have competed against big-time hackers before , but no one could ever catch me.*

*See you in the next challenge , Neophyte Genius.*

I lined the two strings up side by side.

They matched. Not close. Exactly. Down to the same misplaced space, every single time.

Ethan was the hacker. Ethan had killed Daniel Rogers. And right now, he was sitting on my couch, three feet away, his arm resting behind my sister's shoulder.

My face didn't move. My breathing stayed even. I needed one thing first.

"Shelly," I said, keeping my voice normal, "can you go grab the popcorn from the kitchen?"

"Sure," she said, stretching. "Butter or caramel?"

"Butter's fine."

Her footsteps faded down the hallway. Now it was just the two of us, the television throwing flickering light across both our faces.

I opened the hidden portal on my phone, the same encrypted channel he had used on me . I typed three words.

*I found you.*

I hit send.

A sharp buzz rattled inside Ethan's jacket pocket. He didn't notice right away, still nodding at the folded paper in my hand. "Did you find any mistakes in the homework?"

I just watched him.

He reached into his pocket, almost absently, the way anyone checks a phone out of habit. He looked down at the screen.

His face changed. The easy, half-distracted look dropped away, replaced by something tighter. His eyes narrowed at the four words sitting on his lock screen.

*I found you.*

That was the exact moment I turned my own phone around and held it up, the same portal open on it, the same four words, sent, delivered, timestamped to the second.

His eyes moved from his own phone to mine. He read it again, side by side this time, and I watched the exact moment it landed. No confusion left in his face. Just recognition.

His smile was gone completely now. What replaced it wasn't panic. It was something colder, something calm and knowing, the kind of look that had no business being on a sixteen-year-old boy.

I stared back at him and let him see exactly the same thing on my own face.

Both masks were off.

"There she is," Shelly said, walking back in with a bowl of popcorn against her hip. "Butter, extra salt, just how you like it."

She dropped down between us, already laughing about something from the movie, completely unaware of anything at all.

I sat frozen on one side. Ethan sat frozen on the other. Our eyes stayed locked across the space above her head, over the sound of her laughing, over the flickering light from the screen.

Neither one of us moved.

Neither one of us said a word.
