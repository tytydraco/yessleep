There are nineteen paces between the fire door and the nurses' station. I have started walking them backwards.

I'm a nurse on a locked psychiatric ward, and I've made that walk maybe twenty thousand times.

The first night I turned around in the middle of it, something behind me stopped walking at the same moment I did. That was months ago. The footsteps lasted about three weeks. The mistake I made because of them took me months to understand.

Three quick steps, then nothing. Two more, then nothing. I turned and the corridor was empty, and the sound stopped while I was still turning.

I stood long enough to feel stupid, then went back to the desk. It started again before I sat down.

I blamed the pipes. Sound from the heating system travels through stone in ways that make distance useless. I've explained worse with less.

The pipes don't care which way I'm facing.

That was what I couldn't let go of. Not the sound. The fact that it had an opinion about my back.

There's a ward story I ignored for seven years. Patients say there are children in the corridor at night. That's all of it, or at least that was all anybody ever repeated.

Seven people had said it across eleven years, about one every couple of years. I've worked nights for seven years, so I was here for four of the seven. The first three happened before I existed on this ward, which is the only reason I took the rest seriously enough to look.

I didn't go looking for children. I went looking for the leak.

People on a ward hear each other. A nurse repeats something in the kitchen. Somebody asks a leading question without hearing herself do it. A patient hears another patient through an open door, and the answer comes back a week later sounding independent. Seven reports spread across eleven years is exactly what contamination can look like.

It's boring, which is why I expected it to be right.

It's also why I never asked any of them. Four of the seven were my patients, and the question that would settle this is the same question that ruins the answer. You cannot ask somebody whether they saw children in the corridor without telling them that children in the corridor is a thing people see here.

I expected it to take an afternoon.

Irene is the reason I could look at all. She was on nights with me that week, and when I said the paper observation logs from before the ward went digital were gone, she said they weren't. They were down in the basement, boxed up, because nobody had ever gotten around to scanning them. She said it the way you say a thing that's never mattered, then went to check the doors.

The records office is two floors down and smells like toner. I went on a Tuesday with a written request signed by our nursing coordinator, officially to trace a long-standing nighttime noise complaint through the old ward records. I'm not losing my job over a ward story. For the same reason, I've changed anything here that would identify the building, and Irene isn't her name.

The old logs are bound ledgers, one line per check, initials in the margin, with a narrow notes column for anything that couldn't be reduced to asleep, awake, calm, agitated.

I found four of the reports in the first two hours. Four different hands. Not one of those nurses ever documented a second one.

More importantly, none of those patients had ever been on the ward at the same time.

That killed the easiest route.

I kept going. The fifth was in another box, the sixth eighteen months later. By the third report, none of the staff from the first were still on the ward. I hadn't started yet.

Contamination needs a route. The obvious routes kept disappearing.

Then I found the detail nobody had bothered preserving when the reports turned into a ward story.

Every one of the seven patients said the children were facing the nurses' station.

Not looking into bedrooms.

Not looking at each other.

Toward the station.

The notes also recorded where each patient said the children were standing. Usually no more than a few words: outside six, near five, by the fourth door.

The first six went like this.

Room six.

Room five.

Room five.

Room four.

Room four.

Room three.

I wrote them out in date order and stared at the column until I saw it.

They didn't move every time.

But when they did, they were one room closer to the station.

The first six were enough to make the corridor feel different.

The seventh should have made the pattern easier to dismiss.

It didn't.

I came within about four seconds of going home without finding it.

There was nothing in the year I expected it in. I checked twice, put the lid back on the box, and stood up because I'd been down there four hours and my shift started in ninety minutes.

Then I sat down and opened the year before, because ledgers get filed by whoever happens to be holding them.

It was one folder past where I'd stopped.

Room six.

The seventh was the most recent, and it put them back at the far end, where the first report had.

Somebody wrote the wrong number. Maybe the patient pointed badly. Maybe the nurse wrote the patient's room instead of the place she was indicating. I can't check; the nurse left the following spring.

So it's six points that make a line and a seventh that doesn't belong.

I'm telling you about the seventh because leaving it out is how you end up believing your own arithmetic.

There was another reason I didn't trust the line.

The ward had been reconfigured between the third report and the fourth, shortly before I started working there. Two bedrooms were altered, one doorway moved, part of the corridor wall was rebuilt, and the room numbers shifted with it.

You can still see where one of the old beds stood. In the vinyl floor of what is now room five, there are four old bolt holes. You can put your fingers into them.

I found the old floor plan in the same records room and mapped the first three reports onto it, then mapped the later ones onto the current layout.

The room numbers changed.

The positions didn't.

What had been called room five on the old plan wasn't the same doorway we call room five now. Once I stopped following the numbers and followed the physical locations instead, the first six reports still advanced toward the station.

The renovation hadn't broken the line. It had hidden it.

I checked the sight lines in daylight. Every report came from a patient in a bedroom along that corridor. From those beds, patients could see where the children were said to be standing. From just outside the station, the doorways line up so you can see every bed at once. Move three meters either way and you lose most of them.

The architecture gives the station a clear view of the bedrooms.

It also gives the bedrooms a clear view back.

The other thing in each note is a date, and I didn't look closely at those until the week after.

Our rotating schedule is printed on a laminated sheet by the door, curled at one corner where people have peeled it back to check the month underneath, and it doesn't line up neatly with the calendar.

Night staff work a fixed rotation. Other nurses occupied my slot before I started here.

There's supposed to be an additional nurse on weekend nights. That shift has gone uncovered more weekends than not for years, and nobody has ever fixed it.

I went back through the old schedules and matched them against the dates.

All six reports that make the line happened on weekend nights when the nurse assigned to the rotation slot I occupy now was on duty and the extra shift was uncovered.

Three of those six nights, I was the one working that slot.

I don't remember any of them, and there is nothing to remember. A patient said something to somebody at three in the morning, a nurse wrote nine words in a notes column, and I was twelve meters away doing something else.

That combination comes up more than once a year. Most of those nights produce nothing.

But every one of the six happened on one of those nights.

Seven reports in eleven years is a count of witnesses, not a count of movements. Somebody had to be awake to see it, and somebody else had to bother writing it down.

The schedule isn't an alarm clock for whatever is in that corridor. It only decides when there are fewer people there to interrupt it. The rest depends on somebody lying awake in a bed with nothing to look at but the hall.

The seventh doesn't fit that either. Wrong location, wrong night.

I did try to do something about the footsteps. I reported the noise, not the rest of it, and wrote one line with no interpretation: recurring impact noise, corridor, night hours, source not identified.

The reply came four days later. The pipes were already a known issue, buried in an eleven-page maintenance backlog behind a boiler valve that had been waiting since before the reconfiguration.

Nobody did anything wrong.

That was a correct answer to the question I asked.

I'd asked about a noise.

You can see all nineteen paces from either end. That's what the layout is for. There's nowhere along that stretch for a person to stand without being visible from the station. The only thing that moves down there is the light outside room six, which has flickered since before I came and probably won't be fixed by anyone I'll ever meet.

By then, the footsteps had been following me for about three weeks.

The night after the reply came back, something took hold of the back of my collar while I was walking toward the station around three-thirty in the morning.

The fabric at the base of my neck went tight and stayed that way for as long as it takes to say a word, like somebody behind me had stepped on the hem of a coat I wasn't wearing.

Not a pull.

Held, and then not.

I turned around in less than a second. The corridor was empty for its whole length. Every door was still exactly as I'd left it.

My collar was standing up at the back when I reached for it. I put it down with my own hand, and my hand wasn't steady.

For the first time in seven years on that ward, I was afraid to turn my back on an empty corridor.

I stayed facing it until I heard Irene moving at the station behind me.

That happened halfway along the nineteen, not at any of the doorways in the reports, and it happened while the six-report line was still three rooms away from the station.

My column doesn't explain it. I've stopped pretending it does.

Irene gave me the rest of it four nights later without knowing she was doing it.

We were at the station at two in the morning when she opened a Kinder Bueno, took one of the two bars for herself, put a paper napkin beside my keyboard, and placed the other bar on it for me.

She was telling me about her nephew's birthday when she mentioned a game. One child faces the wall and counts while the others move behind her, and when she turns around, everybody freezes. Anyone caught moving goes back to the start.

I asked what happens if nobody turns around.

"That's not a rule," she said. "That's just children running at you."

She laughed, looked at my face, and stopped.

I didn't tell her why. I said I had a headache and went to do rounds.

Her bar was gone when I got back. Mine was where she'd left it.

Here's what I knew by then, and what took me another week to put into a sentence.

The footsteps stop when I look and start again when I turn away.

The reports don't show movement. They show positions.

But over eleven years, the first six still ended three rooms closer to the station.

That's not a series of sightings. That's a distance closing.

They've been playing a game.

Nobody thought to ask them the score.

My job is walking away from things with my back turned. Nineteen out, nineteen back, twelve times a night, and every trip means giving that corridor another stretch of time when I'm not looking.

So on Thursday I stopped at the station end of the nineteen, and what I did next wasn't reasoning so much as an attempt to turn something random into something I could control.

If Irene was right, the turn was the only move I understood. Anyone caught moving goes back to the start. I already knew turning stopped the footsteps, and I told myself that stopping them was the same thing as catching them.

Until then, I hadn't understood that I'd already been playing. I'd only been walking through the corridor and turning when something made me look.

Maybe the reset only counted if somebody took the wall.

Face the wall.

Give them time to move.

Turn.

Send them back.

Ten seconds of my back for a reset. That was the trade I thought I was making.

I know how that sounds, but something had held me by the collar about two weeks earlier, and once that happens you stop being able to choose which ideas you're allowed to act on.

I faced the wall beside the station glass, where the line runs out, and counted to ten under my breath at 4:10 in the morning, with my face close enough to the paint to see the roller marks.

I heard nothing behind me. Not the pipes. Not a hinge. Not a shoe on vinyl.

At ten, I turned around as fast as I could.

Nothing was there.

I did it again. Ten more seconds facing the wall, ten more seconds of nothing I could hear. Then I turned.

Empty corridor.

I should have stopped there. Instead, I decided that nothing happening was the result I wanted.

I did it twice more over the next few weeks. Same count, same silence, same empty corridor when I turned.

For a while, I let myself call that evidence.

There hasn't been another report since that first Thursday. Seven reports in eleven years meant I knew a few quiet months proved nothing, but I wanted the silence to mean something.

Then I looked at the laminated sheet again.

Since that Thursday, my rotation slot hasn't landed on a weekend at all. The next time it does is still several months away, and I can't know yet whether the extra shift will be covered.

So the silence tells me nothing. It's exactly what I would have seen if I'd done nothing at all.

And I've had all that time to look at what I did at that wall.

The mistake was treating an empty corridor after the turn as a reset.

An empty corridor was what I'd always seen after turning. All I'd ever proved was that turning stopped the footsteps.

I had decided stopped meant caught.

It doesn't. Nothing said it meant sent back.

So every second I spent facing that wall belonged to them.

I gave them ten uninterrupted seconds. Then another ten. Then I came back and gave them twenty more.

I thought I was buying resets.

I don't know that I bought a single one.

I know what I paid.

And in that game, whoever faces the wall is the finish line.

The others aren't running away from her.

"That's just children running at you."

I put myself at that wall four times and counted for them.

I haven't faced it since I understood that.

I kept the seventh in my notes when leaving it out would have made the other six cleaner. I don't get to start leaving things out now.

Nineteen paces.

I walk them backwards whenever I can. When I can't, I keep the corridor in view for as long as the job allows me to.

That's the part I haven't solved.

Patients need doors opened for them. Medications have to be prepared. Somebody falls. Somebody vomits.

You cannot work a psychiatric night shift without turning your back on something.

And every time I do, I count the seconds without meaning to.

I've had plenty of time to arrange a shift swap for that weekend and I haven't asked anybody, and the reason isn't only that the explanation ends with somebody sitting me down.

Last week I looked up from the desk and Irene was coming back from the far end.

She was facing the fire door the whole way. Her toes were going down before her heels.

She did the full nineteen without turning around once, sat down, and asked me whether the pharmacy order had gone through.

I said it had.

I haven't asked her and she hasn't told me. I don't know how long she's been doing it, or whether she started before I did.

If I'm not there, somebody else is.

I'm thirty-five. I've got thirty-two years left before I [can retire](https://www.reddit.com/u/FireDoor_RN/s/jo15HGlY48).
