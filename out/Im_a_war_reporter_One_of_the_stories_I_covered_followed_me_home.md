My CV describes me as a "photojournalist with more than two decades of experience in major conflict zones", "HEFAT certified" and a "member and occasional consultant for Reporters Without Borders (RSF)".

What all that means is, I've been in the shit. I've photographed mass prison graves in Syria, cartel killing fields in Mexico, and Islamic State battles in Mali. I've embedded with Ukrainian soldiers in Donetsk, Indian soldiers in Kashmir, Congolese soldiers in Kivu--you name the "global hotspot", I've probably reported from it.

It's not an easy job. There's a lot of death and destruction, obviously. You see things that should only occur in nightmares right in front of you, in loud, screaming color. At first you feel like it's whittling away parts of your soul, but you learn to harden your heart, do your job, and move on to the next project.

Some jobs are harder to leave behind than others.

During my first assignment in Afghanistan, the American military unit I was attached to responded to a Taliban attack on a police station in Herat. Men were staggering out, bloodied from head to toe, stumbling and disoriented. One, I remember, walked out calmly carrying a briefcase and his own arm.

The unit sergeant laughed at me as I puked my guts out all over the street, my camera catching some of the sick.

A few years later, in 2006, I was in Lebanon. The Israelis and Hezbollah had been trading fire over the border for a couple of weeks. My team of three had been in the town of Kfar Roummane for most of the day, reporting on refugees continuing to pour out of the south for the relative safety of the capital.

We'd figured we could do some human interest stories the next day, so instead of heading back to Beirut for the night just to turn around and come back to the area in the morning, we decided to sleep in the car.

During the night, the Israelis started bombing. Once the first explosion shook us awake, we grabbed our gear and raced out of the truck, hunkering down in a nearby ditch filled with fetid water, helmets clutched to our heads, flak jackets flung over our backs. The smoke and the dust and the screams of frightened people created an atmosphere of pure chaos. But this wasn't my first rodeo.

I grabbed my camera and headed towards a plume of smoke rising a few blocks away, my colleagues racing after me. We soon came to the ruins of a house, where clouds of dust and smoke rose in a choking haze. Struggling to walk through the rubble was a man who was carrying something in his arms.

"Sa'idni," I heard him say in a raspy voice, his eyes wide and white in the darkness as he held up what appeared to be a woman--or what was left of her. She was covered in white dust, as was he. She was missing both legs and had been eviscerated. "Sa'idni." *Help me.*

I reached out to help him, but professional instinct made me pull back. I made sure my flash was on and took some snaps of him from the front, then did a dozen or so in profile. The woman's spiraling curls, hanging loose and blowing in the wind, were a grisly, beautiful echo of her entrails, which reached nearly to the ground.

"Sa'idni."

At his repeated plea, I shook my head, as if banishing a haze. I slung my camera around my neck and reached out a hand to help him to the street. I kept my gaze straight ahead as I guided him by the elbow through the dark streets and handed him over to a civil defense crew. I kept my gaze straight ahead, even as I heard the woman's quiet moans settle into a gut-wrenching silence.

That one messed me up a bit, to be honest, but I was able to get back on track after a short sabbatical. I felt better being back in the field, like I was where I was supposed to be.

Earlier this year, I was in eastern Chad near the Darfur region, speaking to refugees about reported paramilitary atrocities taking place across the border. As we drove, we encountered hundreds of people, many of them clearly close to starvation, their faces gaunt and their eyes bulging. Mothers hugged quiet babies to their chests as fathers carried all their families' worldly belongings on their backs. They tried to flag us down, their stick-thin arms waving desperately, but we didn't slow.

We weren't supposed to venture too far into Sudan. The army and the rival paramilitary didn't take kindly to Western media so we would do mad dashes across the border, photograph and film what we could, speak to whoever would speak to us, then head back.

One day, we were heading back to the border when we saw something strange by the side of the road. I tapped on our driver's shoulder and asked him to stop, which he did, with great reluctance, telling me we needed to be back across the border before dark.

I squinted at the figure on the road, then hopped down and approached it, as my colleague and the driver shouted at me to get my ass back in the truck. As I got closer, I saw the figure's back move up and down in rough pants. Was it a stray dog? We were far from any village. Maybe a small jackal?

It was a child. His back was bowed, his forehead on the ground, as if he was too tired to pick it up again. Every single rib was visible under his skin, the knobs of his spine a grotesque contrast to the bulging, distended stomach underneath. He could have been anywhere from 5 to 12--it was hard to tell with severely malnourished children.

He was shaking, fine tremors racing along his limbs, but even that seemed too much for him. He turned his head at my approach and as I crouched down, he stared at me with enormous eyes.

His breathing slowed as he realized I wasn't going to hurt him, and then, painfully slowly and with a whimper of effort, he reached out a small, shriveled hand towards me, his dry lips moving wordlessly.

I stepped back.

After snapping a series of photos of his face and his gesture, I stood and got a few more shots from a different angle. The light was great, almost the golden hour. I looked around to see if any carrion birds were around for background. It would be so cool if I could recreate that "Vulture and the Little Girl" photo from the Ethiopian famine in the '90s. Yes, there were two in a nearby tree, their hideous black heads lowered between their stooped shoulders. Could I coax them down with something? I frowned at the horizon and glanced at the truck. The team probably wouldn't like that.

My blood thrumming with triumph over a job well done, I turned away from the dying child towards the truck, my focus already on the tiny screen of my camera. It would have been great to get the boy's last breaths on film but we really did need to be getting back. I knew exactly what I'd title the photo: "Starving Boy".

It came out beautifully. The despair, the pain, the boy's haunting eyes pulled the viewer in immediately. The black-and-white print was my favorite. It was a brilliant photo, if I do say so myself. I really felt like that one would get me a Pulitzer.

But no. I was a finalist but lost to a shot of a bunch of Palestinians storming an aid truck in Gaza.

No one gives a shit about dead Africans--I should have remembered that.

Still, it did all right. It ended up in the coverage of a few news outlets on the horrors of the Sudan war, the image bracketed by ads for Ozempic and Chili's. When I first showed it to my boss, he was thrilled. He suggested trying to do a follow-up photo story on the kid, and asked me if we'd been able to find his family. I shrugged and told him we'd left him there on the road, that we'd had to get back to the other side of the border. He stared at me, his eyes wide, mouth slightly open.

I've been back stateside for a few months now. My boss seems to think I need a break. Maybe I do. I haven't been sleeping much. There are dreams but I can't quite make sense of them.

In the dreams, I am walking through my neighborhood in Portland. The streets are dark but the moon is high. No lights are on in the houses I pass. It's hot and I'm thirsty, so thirsty I don't even have spit in my mouth anymore. There is an acid churning in my stomach, as if its contents are about to bleed through the walls of my gut and spill out onto the ground.

When I wake up, I still feel that thirst, I still feel that pain in my stomach. The feeling lasts most of the day, even after I drink, even after I eat.

I change my diet, stop drinking, commit to an exercise routine. I visit the doctor for a test for ulcers and GERD and diabetes and whatever else Google told me the symptoms could be caused by. I consider therapy, but I don't think there's anything wrong with my upstairs. I ask the doctor for sleeping pills, and he gives me a prescription.

Still, the dreams come.

*I am walking in the daytime, now. I have left the neighborhood behind. I am looking for someone, but I don't know who. The panic of being alone shoots through me, battling with the pain in my stomach and the dryness of my throat for my brain's attention. There was no one around me when I woke up, I think to myself. And now everyone is gone and I don't know where they are. The night was hot but the day is twice as uncomfortable, the sun beating down on my head. I think that maybe I can catch up with them if I keep walking this way.*

I wake up and chug the liter of water I have at my bedside and then almost immediately shoot out of bed, running to the bathroom to puke it back up.

This is getting unbearable. Back to the doctor, more tests, some meds to help with the reflux, another type of sleep aid. I try to sleep in the daytime, but the dreams still come. I stare at my ceiling, terrified to close my eyes. I sit around doing nothing all day, exhausted, and I walk in my dreams all night.

I can't go on like this.

*I have walked so far, I don't recognize where I am anymore. I often come with my mother to get water from the well outside the village, walking six hours round trip, but I am far, far beyond the well now. I am desperate to see her, I am desperate to see my brothers and sisters, my aunts. I don't know why they left me behind. I am alone and so afraid. I am so hungry and so tired. I try to cry but I have no tears left. I sit down by the side of the road.*

*I have been sleeping, I think, the sun hot on my back. I hear something, a truck. I think it is the soldiers and I am shaking with fear over what they will do to me. They came months ago and took all our animals and burned the fields around the village. They did bad things to my mother and my two sisters. They killed my father and my uncles.*

*Boots land in the dirt and walk quickly towards me on the road and I am so frightened and I am breathing fast. But the person who crouches down to look at me is a strange, strange creature. Her skin is white like milk, her hair the color of sand. She has blue in her eyes and she has a kind smile. She looks so strange, so different from anyone I have ever seen, that I think that she is a spirit here to help me find my way. I reach out to her.*

*But she moves away, and there is a strange clicking noise from a black box she holds to her face. I reach out, though it is difficult for me and takes more strength than I have. Please, I try to tell her. Please help me.*

*Soon, she climbs back onto her truck and leaves. I am alone again.*

*I am alone and night is coming. I am alone and I want to cry out for my mother, why did she leave me, but my throat is dry and I cannot get up and I hear the animals and spirits in the grass moving around me and I am afraid.*

I wake with a start and automatically reach for the water on my nightstand, but I find I can't turn over.

There is something on my chest.

As my vision adjusts to the blackness, I stare into two large, dark eyes, only inches from my own. A weight is bearing down on my chest and I am finding it difficult to breathe. There is a sour smell, like unwashed skin and stale urine and dry mouth.

The boy stares at me, unblinking, his dry, cracked lips parted. His small, claw-like hands have dug themselves into my shoulders, pinning me down with an incredible strength. I cannot move, my terror so complete that I feel like my heart will explode.

Slowly, deliberately, the boy lifts his hands to my face. Gentle as a caress, he puts his fingers in my mouth and begins to wrench my jaw apart. I can feel every part of this, every snap of sinew, the crack of my jaw, and I scream and I scream and I scream but no sound comes out.

The boy, his eyes never leaving mine, puts his hand into my mouth and begins to push. Past my teeth, past my tongue, into my throat. I am choking, I am trying to scream but he pushes downward, downward, downward.

"No one is coming to help you," he tells me solemnly, pushing his hand farther down my throat. "No one is coming to help you."

His voice is my own.
