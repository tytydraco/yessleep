Nicole and I wanted a kid the second we got married.



When she finally got pregnant, we did everything right. We bought a small house just outside the city, painted the nursery blue, and spent three solid weekends trying to fix up the squeaky wooden stairs. Nicole ended up giving up on the sanding, laughing with sawdust smeared across her cheek, telling me the ghost of the old staircase could haunt the kid for all she cared as long as he finally went to sleep.



Every room had some trace of the baby coming, and Nicole spent half her day on the phone with her parents talking about names, cribs, and schedules. She was obsessive about the baby's socks, folding them into tiny, perfect squares that looked like postage stamps.



Life was shifting fast.



Then came her family's baggage—literally.



The doll wasn't a toy. It was a heavy, crudely carved wooden thing held together by rusted iron pins and string, with patches of real human hair glued to its scalp. Its joints clicked with a dry, sharp snap whenever it moved.



Every single night, her mother would pull out a low wooden table, loop the twine over her gnarled fingers, and make the thing twitch in front of us.



"You can't skip this," her mother told me once, her grip on my forearm hard enough to leave a mark. "You have to promise."



I didn't care enough to argue. Keeping the peace meant nodding along, especially since they helped us with the down payment.



Our due date was set for December. Then came yesterday.



Her mother was up at dawn, making coffee and trimming the hydrangeas. I was in the kitchen when I heard a heavy, hollow thud from the hallway.



I bolted out. She’d pitched forward and tumbled all the way down the stairs.



I dropped to my knees. Nicole had driven to the corner store to mail out some birth announcement cards. I grabbed her mother's hand, feeling completely useless against the pool of blood spreading under her head. She was already gone. 



I grabbed my keys, drove to the hospital, and sat in the waiting room numbly while my phone buzzed with texts from Nicole asking if I wanted anything from the store—specifically a ridiculous craving for pickled jalapeños and saltines.



The doctor gave me the news, and I sat in the hospital bathroom, washed the blood off my hands, and stared at my reflection. Then I called Margaret, her sister.



When I pulled into the driveway, Nicole was standing by the front window, holding a little jar of saltines. She froze when she saw my face, but the second I stepped through the door, she started talking a mile a minute about how she’d finally settled on a middle name.



Except she wasn't blinking. And her voice sounded weirdly flat, like she was talking into an empty tin can.



My phone rang. Margaret was crying on the other end.



"How did mom die?" she sobbed.



"She fell down the stairs," I whispered. "I was right in the kitchen."



"Does Nicole know?"



"No."



"Good. Keep it that way. If Nicole finds out right now, the stress could trigger early labor. Mom was her entire world."



"I'll handle it," I said.



I hung up and looked at Nicole. She was wearing a wide, sweet smile, but tonight it felt empty.



"Who was that?" she asked.



"Just your sister. Your parents decided to take a trip up to the cabin."



I turned off the lights and sat in the quiet. Even with her gone, the house felt wrong.



Worse, it was concentrated upstairs. In the nursery, right beside the tiny drawer of postage-stamp socks.



The little wooden stage sat right on the dresser. The spare puppets hung limply from the coat rack by their tangled twine.



Later, I tucked Nicole into bed and walked back down the hall.



This was the first night we weren't putting on the show. And as I stood there in the dark, I heard it: a faint, rhythmic click-clack coming from the empty floorboards of the nursery upstairs.



It wasn't mice. Tick. Tap-tick. Click. Someone sitting cross-legged on the floor just above my head.



I crept back to the kitchen, grabbed a heavy cast-iron skillet off the stove, and headed back to the stairs. The wood groaned under my socks.



When I reached the top, the hallway was pitch black. The door to the star-painted nursery was cracked open an inch.



The floor outside wasn't dusty. It was coated in a fine, yellowish sawdust.



I pushed the door open.



The room smelled intensely of linseed oil, old mothballs, and hot copper. The spare puppets weren't hanging on the coat rack anymore. They were gone.



Sitting right in the center of the floor, facing away from me toward the blank wall, was the mother’s personal puppet. Its wooden strings stretched straight up into the dark of the ceiling, pulled taut by nothing I could see.



The clicking stopped.



Down the hall, our bedroom door creaked open.



Nicole stepped out. She was wearing her oversized sleep shirt, her swollen belly pressing out against the fabric. Her arms hung stiffly at her sides, her fingers twitching.



"Nicole?" I whispered, dropping the skillet. It hit the floor with a dull thud. "What are you doing?"



She didn't blink. Her eyes were completely blank.



"She forgot to finish," Nicole whispered, her voice dropping into a register that sounded too deep, too heavy for her.



"Babe... your mom went to the cabin."



Nicole turned her head. Too far. I heard something snap-click in her neck.



"Mom doesn't need to pull the strings anymore," she said softly, looking down at her stomach. "She left them inside me."



"Hey, let's get back to bed, Nic," I said, hating how small my voice sounded. "You're sleepwalking."



"The actors don't leave the stage," she shot back, her voice dry. "Mother missed her cue."



"She fell," I snapped. "It was an accident."



"Nothing in this house is an accident," Nicole whispered. She lifted her right hand, her fingers twitching against the air like she was wrapping invisible twine. "When the sun goes down and the strings go slack, the weight pulls everything under. She told you that. Why didn't you keep your promise?"



"It was a stupid game, Nic."



"It wasn't a game," she said, tilting her head. "It was the only thing holding the roof up."



She turned and walked back toward the bedroom.



I stood in the dark hallway. Instead of grabbing the keys immediately, I walked past our bedroom and went straight into her father’s old study at the end of the hall.



I dropped to my knees by his oak desk and ran my fingers along the baseboard until I found the loose panel. I yanked it open.



The space behind the wall wasn't insulation. It was packed with thousands of meters of tangled, dried catgut and rusted wire, all vibrating faintly as they led straight upward into the rafters.



Inside the cavity sat a single leather-bound notebook.



I flipped it open under the light of my phone. The pages were covered in frantic handwriting, old property deeds, and rough sketches of jointed wooden frames. Near the back, the ink grew manic, scrawled across a water-stained page:



*...primary custodian... failure of the loop... if the sequence breaks... God help us, the window drops to seven days.*



I threw the book down and bolted back toward our bedroom.



"Nicole!" I yelled.



The door was wide open. The room was freezing.



Nicole was sitting on the edge of the mattress, her sleep shirt pulled up. In the dim amber glow from the hall, I could see the skin of her abdomen rippling. Something heavy was shifting beneath the surface.



And standing right behind her on the bed was the mother's grand puppet.



Its wooden feet were planted deep into the mattress, its painted face tilted down toward Nicole's stomach. Long strands of human hair brushed against her neck. From its shoulders, dozens of fine strings stretched straight up into the ceiling.



"Get away from her!" I screamed.



I lunged across the room, grabbing the puppet by its chest and ripping it off the bed. It felt heavy and hot. It fought back with a vicious snap, its wooden jaw clicking shut inches from my thumb, drawing a line of blood.



I hurled it against the wall. It shattered.



Nicole slumped sideways onto the pillows, gasping for air, her skin slick with sweat.



"Arthur?" she choked out, her voice sounding like her own again. "What was that? It hurts."



"We're leaving," I said, hauling her up. "I don't care about the bags. We're getting in the car."



I snatched her phone, grabbed my keys, and half-carried her out into the hallway. Every board groaned beneath our feet.



We made it to the top landing.



And then the front door downstairs slammed shut on its own.



Down in the entryway, right at the base of the stairs, a tangled pile of the smaller wooden puppets—dozens of jointed limbs and matted hair—shifted and clicked together. From the center of the heap, the grand puppet pulled itself up, its broken limbs snapping back into place.



It raised its arm, pointing a carved finger straight up at us.



"Arthur," Nicole whispered, her fingers digging into my arm. "The strings... they're still up there."



Above our heads, hidden behind the plaster of the ceiling, something began to pull.



"Back," I muttered, pulling her toward the guest room. "We can go out through the backyard window."



"The glass is painted shut."



"We'll break it."



We shoved our way into the guest room, slamming the door and throwing our weight against it. Dozens of small wooden feet were marching in a frantic circle right outside.



I rushed to the window, grabbed a brass jewelry box, and smashed it straight into the glass. It gave way with a loud crash, showering the flowerbed outside with sharp shards.



"Go," I said, hoisting Nicole up and pushing her through. "Just drop into the grass."



She scrambled through, coughing on the cold air, and tumbled onto the lawn.



I turned to follow when the guest room door slowly began to push inward. The wood around the latch splintered. Standing in the threshold was a tangled silhouette of hanging twine and jointed frames, its fingers twitching at its sides, pulling invisible lines that ran up through the doorframe and into the ceiling.



I didn't wait. I vaulted over the sill, diving headfirst through the broken window just as a heavy crash echoed behind me.



I hit the dirt hard, rolling over the hydrangeas.



"Arthur!" Nicole screamed from near the driveway.



I scrambled to my feet and ran toward her. She was huddled by the passenger side of our sedan, crying in the moonlight.



"Get in," I gasped, fumbling with the keys.



I threw her into the passenger seat, ran around to the driver's side, and yanked the door open. Before climbing in, I looked back at the house.



Every single window on the second floor was wide open.



Standing in each frame, leaning out over the sill with their jointed wooden arms hanging limp, were dozens of small puppets, their painted white faces all turned toward the driveway.



I slammed my door shut, threw the gear into reverse, and tore out of the gravel driveway.



We made it as far as the toll booth on the highway south of the city before I pulled over to the shoulder, my hands shaking so violently I couldn't turn the engine off. The dashboard clock glowed: 3:15 A.M.



Nicole was quiet beside me, staring out the windshield at the dark line of the mountains.



"Are you okay?" I asked, my voice cracking.



She didn't answer right away. She slowly looked down at her lap, her fingers resting against the heavy swell of her shirt.



"The pain stopped," she whispered.



"We're going straight to the hospital downtown," I said, putting the car in drive. "We'll find a doctor who can explain this. We can go anywhere."



Nicole gave a small, tired nod, leaning her head back against the headrest.



"Okay," she murmured softly. "Let's go."



I hit the gas, merging back onto the highway as the city lights faded behind us. But as I kept my eyes fixed on the dark road ahead, I felt it beneath my right foot—a rhythmic tap-tick translating straight through the accelerator pedal.



It wasn't coming from the floorboards. It was coming from the sole of my shoe.



I looked down, my breath catching in my throat. My own leg was moving in rigid jerks, pressing the gas down in exact intervals. I tried to lift my foot, but the muscles wouldn't obey. My fingers locked around the steering wheel, tightening against the leather.



Beside me, Nicole didn't look at the road. She was staring straight ahead, her hands resting calmly in her lap, her fingers gently pulling invisible twine against the air.



*“You have to promise,”* her mother’s voice echoed in my head.



I pressed the gas pedal down, and the car sped faster into the dark, and I realized with absolute, freezing terror that I hadn't broken anything at all.



I was just the one driving the stage home.
