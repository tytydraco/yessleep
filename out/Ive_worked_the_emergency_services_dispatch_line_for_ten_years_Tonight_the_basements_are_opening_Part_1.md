I spent ten years working the emergency services dispatch line here in Oregon and across the United States. My family and I moved out here looking for a quiet life, far from the metropolises and the hustle and bustle of the big cities.



Ten years of listening to the worst of humanity: the suffocating screams during wildfire season, the muffled sobs of domestic violence on Christmas Eve, the clinical desperation of a cardiac arrest, and the millions of people reaching out for help in their agony.



Over time, I learned to disconnect my soul just to survive the shift. You become numb to the tone of tragedy because tragedy always has a pattern, a logical ending, an ambulance that arrives or a body that gets picked up a few hours later. It’s part of the daily routine.



But never, in a whole decade, had I received a call like the one tonight. I pressed my headset tighter as the incoming line connected:



"Emergency services, what is your emergency?"   


"I’m trapped in a basement."



There’s static in the background, but it’s not the ordinary static from lines jammed by a thunderstorm. It sounds like a low hum, both mechanical and organic, as if a gigantic, wet engine were starting up right against my ear.



"Sir, what is your exact location right now? Give me your address so I can send a patrol car, please."



 "I don't know... I checked my phone before going down the hatch, it said my house in Gresham, or at least I remember it did. But the stairs... the stairs don't go up anymore."



"What do you mean they don't go up? Is the door jammed?" 



"There is no door. There's just an empty frame looking out, but what you see out there isn't my backyard. It's a gray, endless plain, and there's something standing under that so-called light... looking down. It hasn't stopped looking at me." 



"Who is looking, sir? Is there an intruder on your property? Have you ingested any substances?"



"No… Fuck no! This is my property, but it's in every house with a basement in this damn neighborhood. And it's talking to all of us at the same time, through the heating ducts. I know I sound like a fucking lunatic, but you have to believe me! It says it's time to close up the house and that we have to go out and see it."



The red light on line 4 was blinking with unusual insistence, looking like a Christmas tree with all its lights stuck on at once. 



In my ten years of service, I’ve learned to read the consoles before hearing the screams. I know when a call is a drill, when it’s a prank by drunk teenagers at a college party, and when it’s a reality anomaly. This didn't fit into any known category. 



The man’s tone of voice wasn't one of acute panic, but of icy resignation, as if his brain were barely processing a physical impossibility that defied explanation.



Seventeen minutes passed before the second call came in on my console. In that span, I tried to trace the origin of the first line. 



The computer system gave me a parity error I had never seen before: the signal was bouncing between three different cell towers in Portland, but the fixed coordinates pointed to a spot five hundred meters below sea level, two kilometers away from any registered residential structure.



"Emergency services, emergency line." 



"Help, please. My son went down to get his sister's toys and he hasn't come back up in ten minutes. We've tried everything and all we hear are his footsteps. I went to look for him and..." 



"Ma'am, stay calm, stay on the line. What do you see right now?" 



"The basement is deeper than it should be. Our house is single-story with a crawl space under the kitchen, but... I walked down five floors of damp concrete and I can still hear the echo of my own footsteps sinking into something soft. I don't know how to explain it." 



"Is anyone else in the house with you? Your husband or a neighbor?" 



"I don't mean the house. The bottom of this staircase connects to the neighbor's next door. And to the one three streets over. It’s one single, interconnected underground."



 "Ma'am, listen to me very carefully. Get out of there right now. Go back up."



 "I can't."



 "Why can't you?" 



"Because the stairs aren't concrete anymore. They're made of dry, damp flesh. It smells like rot down here... and they just took a step up."



The call cut off with a sharp click in my headset.



I took off my headphones for a second to rub my eyes. Cold sweat was running down the back of my neck, a thick sweat that strangely smelled like damp earth and rust. I looked toward the adjacent cubicles. 



My coworkers looked the same, frowning, answering chain calls with a strange rigidity. The silence in the ops room was deathly, broken only by the mechanical clicking of the terminals.



It was 03:14 when the state police frequencies began to cross over in my headset in an anomalous way. 



The system no longer distinguished between counties; calls were pouring into a regional loop where local barriers seemed to have dissolved, feeding directly into my terminal.



"Emergency department, what is your zip code?"



"It's three in the morning and the radio frequencies of the neighboring counties are jammed. This is operator 88 from the regional central."



"Officer, Oregon here. We have a massive report of structural anomalies in residential basements. Are you getting them too?"



 "Not just in residential ones. Service tunnels, old fallout shelters... everything is collapsing inward. I've had forty calls in a row with the exact same report." 

"What are they reporting, officer?" 



"Basements opening downward. And the sound... a synchronized breathing coming from the pipes in houses where supposedly no one is home. And when we answer from the backup center... nobody speaks, but you can hear the sound of shoes splashing in stagnant water beneath the living room."



The shift supervisor, a veteran with burn scars on his hands, stood in front of my screen without saying a word. He just stared at my monitor, where the heat map of active calls no longer showed scattered points, but a continuous black stain devouring the county.



"Turn off the main monitor," he told me in a hoarse voice, without taking his eyes off the screen.   


"I can't turn it off, boss. The system isn't responding." 



"I'm not talking about the system. I'm talking about your personal computer screen. There's a light blinking in the bottom-left corner and it has been for three minutes."



I looked down. In the department's internal messaging software, where only operators connected to the internal network can write, there was a direct message addressed to my terminal.   


It had no username. It just showed a series of numbers corresponding to the exact address of my own house in the suburbs of Salem, accompanied by a single phrase: *“Ignore them all. I’ve already reached the landing.”*



The next two hours turned into a sterile exercise in endurance. We weren't sending patrols anymore. 



They  weren't enough units left, nor anywhere left to send them. Half the staff had abandoned the building to go check on their families' houses, even though we all knew, with that silent terror that paralyzes the gut, that no basement in the state kept the bottom it had yesterday.



I was left alone in my section at 06:30 in the morning. I tried calling my wife and daughter. Neither answered.



That was when my personal cell phone rang. A private number. I stared at it for a full ten seconds before accepting the call. I didn't want to hear it, but my fingers moved on their own, driven by that damned dispatcher discipline that forces you to answer.



"Emergency services," I whispered, my voice cracking. "What is your emergency?"



On the other end, there was no static. All I could hear was the creaking of old wood, the sound of bare feet slowly walking up a very steep staircase, and a deep, hollow breathing coming directly from the hallway outside my own office.



"I’ve reached the landing, son," said a voice that sounded exactly like my own, but spoke from the bottom of a well. "Now it's your turn to go down and close the door."



The call is still active right now.



I can hear heavy footsteps crossing the front door of the dispatch center, even though I know perfectly well that I am completely alone on this floor. 



Or at least, that’s what I keep trying to tell myself as the ceiling lights begin to flicker and go out one by one, leaving me in the dark. I can already smell the damp flesh and wet dirt.



If anyone is reading this, don't go down. Don't look at the basements. The footsteps have just resumed their march toward me.
