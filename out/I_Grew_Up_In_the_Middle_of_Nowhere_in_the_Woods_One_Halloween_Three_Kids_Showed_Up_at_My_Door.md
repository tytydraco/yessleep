I had always wanted to go trick or treating. Unfortunately, my living circumstances prevented it. Until the age of eleven, I lived with my Father in a remote cabin in the woods. He'd spent most of his twenties constructing it, and his dream of living like a frontiersman was ultimately what led to the destruction of his marriage, shortly after I was born. That was eleven years before this Halloween memory, yet the cabin remained unfinished. It still had exposed insulation and piping, and was surrounded by rusting scrap and old car parts, half-hidden in a sea of bluejoint. My Father could never finish a project before something else stole his attention, something that would undoubtedly never see completion. There was an unused pizza oven, a half-collapsed smoke shed and a classic car, a 1971 Plymouth Barracuda, that was never restored as planned. A spruce tree had begun to grow through its center. 

Our only working vehicle was dad's truck, which was parked under a tin roof shed about a half mile from the cabin, right where the dirt track that led to the main road began. A ten minute drive took you down the steep track, followed by a twenty minute drive along the road to town. A hassle, but doable for any dedicated trick or treater. My Father never saw it this way, however, and no matter how much I begged and pleaded with him every October 31st, he'd never take me into town. Regardless of the season, a trip into town was rare. I was homeschooled, if in name only. Mostly, it consisted of Father leaving me in front of a text book, or popping an informational VHS on while he went off to “work”. Work for him was tinkering around the house. For a few months at this point, he was mostly working down in the basement, which I was never allowed in due to it being “too dangerous” for me down there. Whenever I asked him what it was he was working on, I'd only ever get vague mumblings about “preparing for things to come”. 

While I couldn't go trick or treating, I certainly made the most of decorating. Father would let me decorate the house however I wanted, and bring me back a steady flow of pumpkins to carve whenever he went out to town. Once, when I was nine, he brought me with him, gave me a fifty dollar note and let me loose in one of those pop-up Halloween stores. I bought masks and props, fake cobwebs and orange lights. Fifty dollars stretched a lot further in the mid nineties. Even though I knew we'd be the only two to see them, I always made an effort transforming the cabin into a haunted house. Every Halloween night, Father would bring back a horror film on VHS, and watch it with me while we drank hot chocolate, his with a splash of whiskey in it. Those were the best nights of my life. 

But Halloween 1996 was different. Father was so busy with his work in the basement that he left me on my own to watch the film. This Halloween, it was 1981's Hell Night. A good movie, but watching it without a Father just wasn't the same. At least I had half-a-dozen lit pumpkins surrounding the old TV to keep me company. I was about halfway through the film when a one in a blue moon event occurred - the doorbell rang. Confused, excited and slightly scared, I paused the film, stepped over our old dog, Soldier, and walked to the front door. Opening it, I was rewarded with a sight I had longed for since I was young. Three trick or treaters stood on our porch. 

Two boys and a girl. One dressed as a witch, the others as a skeleton and Frankenstein's Monster. Their masks, once cheap and plastic, had been repainted and done up with paper mache additions - a hooked nose, a dislocated jawbone and a thick, overhanging forehead. The costumes reminded me of those from the early 70s, the ones I saw in Father's old super 8 home video of Halloween when he was my age. I looked at the trio with a warm, festive feeling in my heart. Before I could speak, they each raised their arms in perfect synchronicity, holding out their goodie bags. I understood the silent plea, but realized  that since we'd never had a trick or treater up here before, we didn't keep any candy on-hand. 

“Wait right there!” I said, before enthusiastically turning and rushing into the kitchen. 

The room was a mess of pots and pans, with a sink like a skip and ramshackled cupboards. The electric light buzzed with the strain of being switched on as I began to rummage through the cupboards. Soon enough, I found a family sized bag of chews that I'd been grazing on the day before. The light blew a sigh of relief when I turned it off, then turned on my heel and ran back to the front door. It swung open, revealing… an empty porch. The three trick or treaters were gone before I could even dish out their candy. Confused, I looked around, but caught no sight of them. Then I stepped out onto the porch, walking down the rickety steps and onto the forest's floor. There was no trace of the three kids, not even a print in the mud. I ended up walking a lap of the cabin, shaking the bag of candy like I was trying to entice a timid dog. Still, I never saw another sign of them. 

“What's going on?” 

My Father's words made me jump. I turned and saw him at the front door, the light from within making him look like a silhouette. He cleaned one hand with an old rag and started to walk down the steps towards me. 

“We got trick or treaters, but I don't know where they went,” I explained. 

“Trick or treaters?” He said, confused, “couldn't be. No one would make the effort to come up here, especially not some kids. Hell, I don't think most people in town know there's a house up this way.” 

“But… but…” I muttered, still searching the treeline for the three children. 

“Come on now,” Father said, patting me on the back, “come back inside and I'll fix you up some of my famous hot chocolate. Then, we can finish watching that scary movie together. How does that sound?”

“Great!” I exclaimed.

Father laughed and tousled my hair, and I followed him inside. Just before he closed the door behind us, he looked over his shoulder. I watched his eyes, now squinted and stern, scan the forest around us. Eventually he dragged his gaze away, and closed the door. 

The last half of the evening was pure, seasonal magic. I had a wonderful time with my dad, and once the movie was over, he got me to sit down with him by the fire as he told me an old ghost story. Then, he educated me on the history of Halloween, all the way back to its roots as the Gaelic festival of Oíche Shamhna. Before too long, my favorite  night of the year was over. I got ready of bed, and Father led me to my room, which was directly across from his. He tucked me into bed as he usually did when he wasn't working and told me “goodnight”. He lingered a little longer sitting on my bedside, however.

“Son, there's something I have to tell you,” he said, looking absently out of my bedroom window. 

His face was the picture of internal struggle. He chewed his bottom lip and scrunched his brow. I was waiting for his revelation, but it never came. Eventually, he turned to me and smiled. 

“Maybe another time,” he said and leaned down, kissing me on the forehead.

He stood then and walked to my door, closing it firmly behind him as he flicked the light off. I was left in the darkness of my small room, with only a sliver of moonlight to illuminate the old, second-hand toys and torn posters that lined its walls. I rarely wanted Halloween to end, but after tonight, I was happy to drift off to sleep and awake in November. I suppose I did, in a sense, when a noise woke me up at three in the morning. I lay dazed in bed, not sure if I was still asleep or not. In a hypnagogic stupor, I sat up and swung my legs out of bed. I rubbed my eyes and, for a moment, thought it was morning. When the light never came, I soon realized  it was the dead of night. I was about to crawl back under my covers when I heard it again. 

*bang* *bang* *bang*

I was startled into wakefulness. I went from sitting to standing in my dark room, but couldn't quite broach the last task of courage and venture out into the hallway. I stood there for a long time, heart beating rapidly and breath catching in my throat. 

*bang* *bang* *bang*

The same noise that had moments ago paralysed me now spurred me into action. I cleared my room in two steps, and flung my bedroom door open. Then, finally, I saw the source of the noise. 

The cabin is small. One half is my room and Father's, the other is the kitchen and living room. The bathroom was an outside compost toilet, something I dreaded in the harsh winters. Opening out from the front door was the dead center of the house, from which the four rooms stemmed off from. Now, there was a second door. Father had added an extension onto the back of the cabin near the start of the year, and had begun digging out and laying the foundations for a basement. He told me his plan was to turn the living room into a dining room, and move the TV downstairs. The thing that most caught my attention was Father mumbling about how he'd like to build an indoors toilet down there as well. It was difficult to get a clear picture from him though. It seemed like his plan for the basement changed a little every time I asked.

It was the door to the soon-to-be basement that was now surrounded by three children. The trick or treating trio stood in front of it, taking turns to pound on the pine wood. I gawked wordlessly at the sight, unable to vocalise the questions that were whizzing through my mind. Then, with the same synchronicity they showed earlier, they all turned and looked at me. I slammed my door shut so quickly I was surprised it didn't splinter the frame. I rushed to the bed, stubbing my toe as I went, and dived under the covers. Pulling the duvet tight around me, I realized  I was shaking. My teeth clenched as I heard the door to my room creak open. It wasn't necessarily the presence of the children that had frightened me so much, but the glow that seemed to emanate from them. When I realized  I could see the basement door through their figures, that's when the terror set in. 

I screwed my eyes shut as tightly as I could, trying to wake up from this horror movie as the steps grew nearer. I wanted to shout out to my dad, for him to come and sort this all out. But any attempt caught in my throat. Even a simple whimper was too much for my terrified mind to muster. Then, what I'd been dreading the most came. A hand planted itself on my side, nudging me through the blanket. It wasn't the rough grasp I imagined, however. Instead, I was gently rocked as if they were goading me out from under the sheets. 

“Hunter,” a soft, almost static smothered voice said my name. 

“Hunter, please,” it said again, “talk to us.”

I tapped into my last reserves of courage. Slowly but surely, I pulled the sheets back. The three children had surrounded me, on either side and at the foot of my bed. Their presence was enough to illuminate the room. 

“Hunter, please listen.” I realized  the light, monotone voice drifted from all three of them. 

“Wh… what do you want? What's going on?” I still don't know if I actually spoke those words, or if I just thought them. Either way, they responded. 

“We are the same,” they spoke, “we are all his victims.”

“I don't understand,” I blurted. 

“That man is not your Father. He has never created life. Only ended it.”

All I could do was stare. The shadowless forms of the three costumed kids around me seemed to close in. The eye holes on their masks showed only darkness. Even then, I knew their eyes were locked on me. 

“Everything you know is a lie,” they continued their revelations, “your Father is a horrible man. He killed us. He killed your mother. He killed many others. One day, he will kill himself. Leave. Leave now before he kills you.” 

I shook my head. It was all I could do to make sense of the situation. I scrunched my eyes shut and rubbed them furiously. When I opened them, the children were still there. I tried again and again, only giving up when I knew they weren't going anywhere. 

“Leave this place, leave this place, LEAVE THIS PLACE,” their warning turned from a monotonous drone into a screaming command. I pressed my hands over my ears, beginning to scream in kind. Their voices seemed to echo around my head, rather than the room. I couldn't understand why they were saying all these horrible things. All I wanted was for this to be over. I thought I'd wake up by now, but that never came. It was a long while before I slept again after that night. Before their pleading voices grew so loud that the glass on my bedside would shatter, the door to my room flung open. Father. He quickly ran to my side, barreling through the form of the young Frankenstein. 

Like smoke billowing from a chimney fire, their visages suddenly became warped, stretching into  nightmare streaks of blackness. Hurricane screams echoed around the entire cabin, enough to draw blood from my ears. Their masks and costumes disappeared into nothingness as they became dark forms of crackling static, their distended faces curling in agony and carrying the appearance of a pumpkin roughly carved with an axe. In an instant, they were gone, leaving nothing but the smell of dirt and children's tears behind. Father rushed to me, and wrapped me in his arms. He let me wet his shoulder as I wailed in fear. The hand that gently brushed my hair was shaking, I noticed. For once, my Father's stoic persona had vanished. For a long while, he cradled me in his arms. It was impossible not to feel the love he had for me, but something was different now. All the while my brain rattled with what the three… ghosts, if you could call them that, had told me. Questions about my mother grew, but then wasn't the time to ask. Instead, it was the time to cower, and to fear, and to while away the last hours of moonlight on Halloween night afraid of what lived in the shadows. 

It was that following November that Father sat me down and told me what really happened to my mother. She wasn't, as I had always thought, living in Australia. The cards I received twice a year, on birthdays and Christmases, were really written by my Father. My mother died when I was a baby. My Father desperately wanted me to be raised in the wilderness, like my ancestors were, he'd always say. My mother was less enthusiastic, but was brushed up in his dream. She was left caring for her first child in a tiny trailer while her husband worked all day building the cabin. She quickly grew to resent the whole situation. One day, she decided to leave. My Father barely managed to goad me from her arms before she got in the car and drove off. Watching her disappear down that dirt trail was the last time he ever saw her. She was in an accident that evening, and died on the way to the hospital. My Father always felt responsible for her death, and had never forgiven himself. This, as he put it, was the truth. 

This is what I believed for the rest of my life. At 17, I moved out for college. The standard of the community college I attended matched the standard of education I received throughout my childhood, as you can imagine. I drifted further and further away from my Father, geographically and in our relationship. I hadn't spoken to him in almost a year when I got the phone call yesterday. He had died. I still don't know the exact date. He was found by the man delivering his monthly supply order. By that point, he was already quite decomposed. The body was practically stuck to the old chair they found him in, and they had to winch the whiskey bottle from his dead man's grasp. I was on the next flight over, and have been spending the time since in the home town that I barely know. The cabin was even more run down now than I might have guessed. There was no use sorting through his stuff up there. It's not like the family of raccoons that now dwelt on the grounds would help. So I piled all the boxes I could find onto my truck and drove back to my Airbnb. 

Old tools, schematics, journals, photo albums, nuts and bolts. These were the contents of most of the boxes. I got to arranging them into piles of stuff to keep, to donate, and to throw away. There was more on the throw away pile than I care to admit. After an afternoon of sifting through junk, I thought I'd start on the stuff that would matter, namely the albums. But I barely knew my family, and I realized  after skimming through three of the albums that I was just looking at strangers. That is until one photo fell from the margins. It was, for once, a photo I recognised. Whenever I'd gone into Father's room, I saw it on his bedside cabinet. It was a photo of my mother, with who I assumed was her brother. It had faded quite a bit now, and I realized  from the cropped recipe on the back that it had been cut from a newspaper. I remembered other photos of my mother I'd seen, but only vaguely. Before I searched for them though, there was something I wanted to try. 

You see, the internet is a strange thing. Power previously relegated to a shadowy department of the leading nations’ intelligence agencies is now in the hands of anyone currently holding a cell phone. I am among that cohort. All it took was a photo. I took a picture of the newspaper cutting and uploaded it to Google's reverse image search. The first result was on an archive site. I clicked it, and was shown a photograph - a digital scan of that exact photo I held in my hands. I was sure of it. Below was the headline of the connected newspaper article. It read, simply, “Local woman found murdered, infant son declared missing”.
