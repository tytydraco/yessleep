The slow, lazy wafting of the fan was swirling up a fine blizzard of dust motes in the sliver of sun poking into the room.  Everything was tacky and tacked together, twisted in my sheets as I was on what felt like was going to be a warm, humid day.  I was sobbing.  I don’t know why I was sobbing, and the bewilderment of this waking moment was immediately sobering.  Almost as soon as I realized, the tears dried up like they’d never been there at all and the thin sheen of sweat across my body chilled, prickling my skin.  Everything was okay.

 \---

 Fitful sleep meant for a fitful day.  The past two weeks had been a kind of hell, and though everything was okay in its own way, it wasn’t and there’d be more to come. I know – that makes perfect sense.  Or it will maybe soon.  *Hopefully*, for both our sakes’, because even knowing what I already know – it doesn’t make any sense to me.  But I guess there’s a lot to cover before we get to the pressing matter at hand and of the past two weeks.

Several years ago, I moved to an apartment on the outskirts of a state forest.  The forest was no stranger, but it had never been my backyard before – and the proximity and constant companionship of the trees made for a lot of interesting stories, like [my early run-in with a terrified would-be burglar who tried to get a stab or two in on his way out](https://www.reddit.com/r/nosleep/comments/il82ya/i_live_alone_on_the_outskirts_of_a_state_park_but/).  Looking back, that’s probably a good place to start.  It was the first in a lot of inexplicable events that, as time went on, started a saga that ebbed and flowed and became entangled in itself in ways I’m still piecing together. 

Following the night of the burglar and the creature officially documented as a rogue bear, there was a period of... not much of anything, or at least things that were infrequent enough that I told myself it was nothing.  Well, there was life – work, school, the expected things.  Long hours spent alone and caught up in the minutia of all that those things entailed.  And in between, simple observations that made little sense at the time.  Like that deer that would carefully look both ways to cross the highway cutting through the forest, walking steadily on its back legs as I sat quietly, undetected and watching.  Or the multiple occasions I’d been on a walk and would stumble upon a group of small animals gathered in circles – rabbits, squirrels, once even a mama opossum and her babies later at night.  Things like that, tossed in amongst the drudgery of existing in the modern world.

Once, at work about two years after the burglar ordeal, I tried to bring up that  deer incident – or one instance of it, because it had happened several times over that first year in the apartment.  I’m not sure why, but I thought it was the same deer each time.  I can’t quite recall the exact way the conversation got there, but somehow some idle chat with co-workers in the break between an all-day training turned into the sharing of strange experiences.  Yasmine swore she heard a woman screaming while out on a hike, but the verdict on that waivered between mountain lion (and the area’s long-running debate on whether there were a population of them in the region or not) and whether or not Yasmine was crazy and had simply been hearing things.  In the quiet moments after Carl suggested that and Yasmine went quiet, feeling put off, I mentioned that I’d seen a deer walking on its back legs.

“I mean, they do that sometimes,” Carl said.  “Have you seen the videos of two bucks rearing up against each other? They do their little back-leg waddle before butting heads.”

“I was always told when you see deer acting weird it’s that brainrot thing they can get,” Matt cut in.  “You didn’t get close to it, did you?” 

And before I could answer, Yasmine had bounced back, excited, “Or it’s some sort of zombie virus.  Like that show with that brain-control mushroom – have you guys seen season two yet?”  And I’d hummed and nodded my head, content to let it go as the conversation tracked in a different direction, but wondering if the explanation was that simple.  As small a thing as it seems, I’d seen things like that – little happenstances easy to brush off or explain away on its own – happen a lot, and dismissive explanations like those never seemed to sit quite right in my head as the truth.

Later that day, as the training presentation wrapped up and I prepared to head back to my office to take stock of what would need tackled the next work day to catch up, I bumped into another coworker.  Peter was quiet and I hadn’t had much interaction with him up until that point.  We worked in entirely different departments, and when we did interact it was for practical purposes.  He was our company’s sole IT guy, and except for when you had an issue or there was some equipment update that needed handled, you really didn’t see much of him.  He seemed content to hide away in his little office-slash-server room tucked away in the back hall for the most part, but he’d been there for the company-wide training.

“So you caught them acting like people, huh?” he said after a moment of quick apologies for bumping into each other and another awkward moment of quiet.  I must’ve had some sort of look on my face because he continued, “The deer you were talking about earlier.”

I took a half second to think about how I didn’t remember him being present for that conversation, then thought that was the perfect way to describe it – the deer walking, all the animal behaviors I’d humanized in various instances throughout my life to varying degrees.  Like the deer, but also like how a cat I’d had once who would sit on his behind, back legs stretched ahead or dangling off the edge of the table like a person would.

 “Yeah, they were acting like people.  Just non-human people.”

He just kind of nodded and after a moment continued through the door I’d been trying to pass in the opposite direction. 

A week later on Thursday, he was in my office – the company had bought everyone updated headsets that were being set up – and he brought it up again.  His tone was conversational and light, but I kind of got the feeling that he was very serious.

“Your  deer story - strange what nature can do, right?”

I thought back to the unconnected observations in the last few years.  I thought back to the various odd events throughout the rest of my life and wondered if these events were new or if I was just now noticing because of some unseen or unknown change. 

“Yeah, strange is... yeah.  Have you-?”

“You live on the edge of the park, right? West of the north entrance?” Peter was focused on some wire or another behind my desk phone and continued without really acknowledging what I said, nodding to himself.  “My place is up by the west entrance near the RV campground.”

“Oh, I didn’t know that.  I thought I was the only one from work way out there.”

He stood up straight, finished with his work.  He put his hands on his hips very seriously and gauged me for a second, then asked, “You ever get up to the trail past the old boat dock? The one that climbs up the hill?”  I imagined the map of the park in my head, trying to place where the old boat dock was – I didn’t fish or have a boat and, embarrassing as it is to admit, can’t swim – but I think I’d been to that trail once or twice. It was coming back to me – the initial climb on the trail had been pretty steep, I recalled.  Peter waited, his pose casual but his hands squeezing onto his hips tight and stiff. 

I nodded, said, “Pretty sure,” and he continued to look at me for several beats before his arms dropped to his sides.  I heard Yasmine exit her office to the left of mine, but I was more focused on the strange tension in Peter’s posture and the room.

“It’s a good trail.  Great views about 7pm on Saturdays around this time of year.”  Then Peter grabbed his little tote bag of supplies and left, taking a right out of my door and down the hall.  Yasmine slinked in through my door from the left just as I heard the door at the end of the hall click open and shut.  Her intrusion shifted my focus, not giving me any time to process the conversation, my mind a little wrapped up in Peter’s casual tone and pose but underlying... whatever that was.

“Oh, Tori, if that wasn’t the cutest thing!” Yasmine said, a mix of patronizing and conspiratorial (or maybe perfectly pleasant – I’m bad at peopling, sometimes, despite my job being all about people.) 

Intelligently, I responded, “Huh?” and a grin cracked across Yasmine’s face as she stepped further into my office.

“Peter trying to ask you out, all awkward like that.  I bet he thought he was being slick.”

Yasmine had a tendency toward a little gossip and drama at work.  I kind of always thought it was because her role was so dull – Yasmine was the department’s quality assurance person, and so her day consisted of reviewing program reports and documentation and monitoring grant adherence.  Most of her time was spent hunkered down with some sort of 50-page document or another, so when she could find a reason to slip away from her desk for a few minutes for something a little more interesting, she did. At least that’s what I’ve worked out in my head.

“I don’t know if that’s what that was.”

“He asked you to meet him at a romantic hilltop in the park on Saturday night,” Yasmine said flatly, then continued in a peppier voice, “Well, he didn’t actually ask... as a question.  But still! He gave you a specific time, place, and date and then shuffled off awkwardly back to his dungeon. Are you going to go?”

Yasmine’s phone started to ring – she always kept the ring volume at full blast, or else it “just became background noise to the words” when she was deep into generating and analyzing some report – and she began to back away toward the door. “I gotta take this, but think about it!  He just went about it a little weird, but you’re also – well, it’s cute, is all.  You should go.”

Unsure if the “weird” comment applied to both Peter and I was intended as a slight, I just watched her go.  I didn’t think her idea about it being a date was correct – but she was right.  Mentioning a specific spot and time was... something.  Maybe not a date, but something.  And I thought about what I would be doing this Saturday at 7pm for the rest of the day and the next.

 

 

The end of September brought a crisper evening, the humidity of August long gone.  When I arrived at the trailhead, the old boat launch along the western border of the park, I exited my car a few minutes before 7 and paused at the red sedan parked several spots down.   Peter wasn’t present, neither in his car or at the clearing in the trees to the right where the wooden sign, carved and painted letters reading, “Skip Run Trail,” sat announcing its start.

 The trail started off at a fairly steep incline, hugging the left border of this little section of the lake.  The incline would eventually even out, continuing to curve around this little cove, before it reached the lookout spot jutting out over the water that Peter had indicated.  I couldn’t make it out from here through the trees that clustered together between the right of the trailhead and the water’s edge, but I remembered that when you stood there, you could look out at the entire little cove.  The trail then continued down and drew you away from the water.

“I’m sure work does background checks,” I said to myself, mostly joking, as I began the more arduous initial climb of the trail.  “I’m not going to get murdered.”  It took less than 5 minutes to reach the lookout spot, so it wouldn’t be long before  I found out.

I’m not very fit, so while I didn’t exactly have to do any vertical climbing, by the time I reached where the ground evened out about a thousand feet ahead and 50 feet above water level, I was feeling a burning in my legs and was unreasonably out of breath.  It had been a while since I did any challenging walks, but I felt unreasonably worn out and a little self-judgmental for it.  I really needed to stop smoking, I guess. 

Peter came into view right away, leaned against a wooden post in the wood-and-rope fence that, in my opinion, didn’t seem to add any particular safety to standing at the edge of the cliff.  He was facing out toward the water and turned to look over his shoulder as I approached.  I looked at my phone – 7:10.  Must have been moving slower than I thought, and briefly felt bad about myself again – I had no reason to be so unfit, given the easy access to physical activity I had here at the park. 

“Sorry for being late.  Didn’t think getting up the hill would take that much out of me,” I said in way of greeting, not sure what my tone should be – and for a second worried that this really had been a date and the lateness was especially bad form.

“No worries,” he said, turning fully to face me.  “Didn’t think you’d come at all.  I realized I hadn’t been real clear on Thursday.”  I approached the fence cautiously, watching my footing in the already-fading light.  Sometimes I forget how quickly it gets dark out here once October rolls in, and I wanted to watch my footing.  I wasn’t scared of heights, necessarily, but just very, very aware of how uncoordinated I could be.

“Watch your step.  Some of these posts don’t seem too stable anymore,” Peter said.  “And don’t look directly down.  Or at least I don’t.  50 feet ain’t nothing.”  When he turned back to the post, I noticed that it was less that he was leaning on it and more like holding onto it for dear life.

I stood in silence with him for a moment, figuring I’d wait for him to get whatever this was started – he’s the one who invited me, after all.  I looked out at the water.  This overlook curved a bit, pinching this spot of the lake inward so that the old boat dock sat in an oval-shaped cove. The larger body of the lake to the left, the old boat launch to the right down below, and dense trees lining the other side of the water with a narrow 4 or 5 feet beach at the edge.  

It really was a beautiful spot, but it made sense why this boat dock was abandoned – this one had been built a long, long time ago, when it was just locals trying to launch little rowboats out onto the lake.  Once the land had been designated a state park and began drawing in larger boats too big for the small dock and its little chokepoint, a larger marina had been built.  People didn’t really use this spot for fishing for whatever reason, from what I’d gathered, and over time the only draw it had was for those wanting to hike or teenagers looking to mess around in a mostly undisturbed spot.

\- ah, that might be why Yasmine thought this was a date.

“This is a good thinking spot,” he eventually said.  “Used to come up here all the time on the weekends.  Then I worked a non-office job for a bit on a weekend shift and couldn’t for a while.”

“I’ve been up here myself a couple of times,” I offered.

Peter shifted from foot to foot, tried to loosen his grip on the wooden post, but then his balance seemed to waiver and he held on tight again.  He might have been afraid of heights. 

“I started getting into the habit again about five or six years ago,” he continued.  “Usually don’t get much farther than this, but the view is real good.”  He nodded his head out toward the water, and I looked back out myself – in the fading light, fireflies had come out.  It struck me as odd, because I don’t remember them staying out so late in the year.  I tried to recall the last time I ever noticed any out past the end of August, but the whole cove seemed lit up with them.  The more I watched, the more appeared – the whole cove blinked and glittered with their lights, flickering amongst the trees and swooping out above the water.

“You really gotta take deep, slow breaths to appreciate it,” he said, eyes transfixed out at the water and the trees across the way.  I was perplexed by the whole meeting – if this wasn’t a date, then what was it? – and in an effort to understand, just followed suit with his own steady breathing.

As I started to be more conscious of my own, drawing in slow, deep breaths, I watched the fireflies.  The chaotic, disorganized blinking that lit up the cove began to still and slow, as if each firefly was now just hovering in place, each a constant pinprick of light.  As I took in a deep breath, their lights seemed to brighten, and when I exhaled their lights faded.  When I took another breath in, the cove once more lit up, and on my next shaky exhale, their lights faded with it.

“The forest is breathing, too,” Peter said quietly.
