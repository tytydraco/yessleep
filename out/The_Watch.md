It was the summer of 2014. I was a junior studying anthropology at the University of Pennsylvania, and I had always been obsessed with collecting remnants of the past. Anything forgotten or lost called to me—notes sealed in glass bottles, antique toys, discarded relics of long-forgotten lives. My roommate and best friend, Matt, used to call me an urban archaeologist, though he secretly loved the thrill of the hunt just as much as I did.

On a humid Thursday morning, metal detector in hand and Matt trailing behind with a canvas bag and garden trowel, we set out to expand my collection. We tried our luck in our backyard, but found nothing but rusty nails. We tried a nearby public park—still nothing. Frustrated, we sat on the curb outside our apartment complex, staring across the street at a sprawling, overgrown grassy field.

“You know what used to be over there, right?” Matt asked, wiping sweat from his forehead. He had heard the stories from the older neighbors. Long before the field was abandoned to wild weeds, an old late-19th-century asylum had stood on that exact patch of land. In 1892, a horrific gasoline explosion ripped through the building, reducing the structure to ash and taking everyone trapped inside down with it. Since that tragic night, the land had remained an untouched stretch of wild grass—an undisturbed grave of secrets.

A surge of excitement rushed through us both. We crossed the street and stepped onto the field. The morning air was suffocatingly humid, thick with the scent of damp earth and crushed clover. I flicked on my detector and began sweeping the coil in slow, rhythmic arcs while Matt kept watch. For nearly an hour, there was only the dull hum of static—until we reached the center of the field, right near a lone, gnarled oak tree.

The machine suddenly let out a shrill, piercing squeal that made us both jump.

“Right here,” I whispered, taking a step back as the detector screamed continuously over a small patch of ground near the tree's thickest root.

Matt dropped to his knees, plunging the trowel into the turf. He cut a square out of the thick, tangled sod, peeling back the grass like a carpet. Immediately, the soil beneath looked wrong—it wasn't the rich brown dirt of the surrounding field, but a dark, soot-heavy black, speckled with tiny specks of white charcoal. As Matt dug deeper, a faint, lingering tang of burnt wood and chemicals drifted up from the pit, impossibly preserved underground for over a century.

Four inches down, the tip of the trowel struck something solid with a sharp, metallic *CLINK*.

“We’ve got something,” Matt said, his voice tightening with anticipation.

Abandoning the metal blade, we both got down on our hands and knees, using our bare fingers to claw through the cold, damp earth. My fingertips brushed against something dense and metallic wrapped tightly in a brittle, blackened mass. I pried the object loose from the soil, my heart hammering against my ribs.

It was a heavy, palm-sized bundle wrapped in a pouch of charred leather. As I lifted it, the brittle leather crumbled away into flakes of black dust between my fingers, revealing the object inside.

I brushed away the remaining grime from the surface. It was an exquisite pocket watch made of heavy, tarnished silver, intricately engraved with twisting vines along the rim. Strangely, despite being buried under four inches of damp, scorched earth for over a century, the glass casing was completely uncracked and pristine. The face was pristine white, and the hands were frozen at **3:14**.

I flipped it over. My thumb traced deep, jagged lettering etched into the back casing: *Dr. Elijah Vance, Ward 4*.

“That’s creepy as hell, man,” Matt muttered, leaning back on his heels with a nervous laugh, looking down at the dark, ash-filled hole we’d just dug. “Looks like the doctor didn't make it out.”

A chill of triumph ran down my spine. I shoved the watch into my pocket, and we hurried back to our apartment, eager to clean and catalog our greatest find yet.

That evening, as twilight bled into pitch darkness, Matt sat on the couch in our living room while I sat at my desk with a bottle of isopropyl alcohol, carefully polishing the silver casing. Wanting to see if the internal mechanism still functioned, I gently turned the small winding crown.

A faint, metallic *click* echoed through the apartment.

Instantly, a heavy, rhythmic noise filled the room.

*Tick. Tock. Tick. Tock.*

I froze. Matt stood up from the couch, his face instantly losing all color. “Anthony… tell me you’re making that sound.”

The ticking wasn't just coming from the small watch in my hands—it vibrated through the floorboards and reverberated off the walls, as if our entire apartment had suddenly become the hollow body of a giant clock. I stared down at the face in horror. The second hand was moving, but it was spinning wildly **counterclockwise**.

The temperature in the room plunged instantly. My breath caught in my throat, blossoming into thick clouds of white vapor alongside Matt’s. The sweet scent of our apartment was instantly choked out by the heavy, suffocating stench of sulfur, smoke, and burning gasoline—the exact same scent from the dirt under the oak tree, but a thousand times stronger.

*Tick. Tock. Tick. Tock.*

Panicked, I tried to drop the watch, but my fingers refused to open. The cold silver felt fused to my palm, radiating a bitter, deadening frost that rushed up my forearm. Matt lunged forward to help me, but he stopped dead in his tracks, paralyzed with fear.

From the darkest corner of the room, a low, wet rasping sound emerged—like someone drawing an agonizing breath through lungs filled with hot ash.

We watched in terror as the shadows stretched and twisted, coalescing into a towering silhouette between us. Two hollow, burning embers flickered where eyes should have been, set within a shadowy face stretched in a silent scream. Hallucinatory echoes flooded the room—iron doors slamming shut, frantic hands pounding on burning stone walls, shrieks of absolute terror drowning in flames.

*“You shouldn't have wound it, Anthony,”* a voice rasped, echoing inside my skull, cold and sharp as scraping metal.

The hands on the watch spun faster and faster, tearing backward through time. The apartment walls began to warp and dissolve, revealing scorched wooden beams, glowing embers, and rusted iron bars. The heat of an invisible inferno surged against our skin.

With a desperate, primal scream, I marshaled every ounce of strength I had left and hurled the watch against the far wall.

It struck with a sharp shatter of glass—and the frantic ticking abruptly ceased.

Silence slammed back into the room. The freezing cold vanished, replaced once more by the warm summer breeze drifting through the open window. The scorched beams dissolved back into our normal walls. Panting heavily, Matt collapsed against the couch, staring wide-eyed at the spot on the wall where the watch had hit.

I scrambled across the floor to the baseboard.

There was nothing. No silver casing, no broken glass, no trace of dirt.

“Is it… is it gone?” Matt whispered, his voice trembling uncontrollably.

I clambered back onto my feet, clutching my chest, trying to convince myself it was all just a nightmare brought on by heat exhaustion. I closed my eyes and let out a long, shuddering breath.

Then, from directly beneath our floorboards—right under my bed—came the soft, rhythmic sound:

*Tick. Tock. Tick. Tock.*
