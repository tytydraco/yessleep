It was the summer of 1997, the year we moved into that rotting trailer park on the edge of nowhere. The place smelled of damp earth and old metal. My older sister Nikki and I were the only kids for miles. Our parents were always gone—working double shifts or disappearing for days at a time. Nikki spent her nights in the next town over, leaving me alone with nothing but the hum of the refrigerator and the soft click of Spot’s nails on the linoleum.  
Most evenings I took him walking along the cracked roads between the trailers. On my twelfth birthday they gave me a yellow Diamondback BMX. It was the brightest thing in that gray place. I rode it until the sun bled out of the sky.  
One late afternoon I noticed the trail.  
It started just behind our trailer, a thin scar of dirt and dead leaves slipping between the trees. It looked forgotten. Overgrown. Wrong. The air around it felt heavier, like the woods were holding their breath. I should have turned around. Instead I went inside, clipped the leash on Spot, and told myself it was just an old deer path.  
The deeper we walked, the quieter everything became. No birds. No insects. Only the soft crunch of our footsteps and the low, uneasy whine in Spot’s throat. Then I heard it—my own name, spoken in my own voice, drifting from somewhere ahead.  
“Derrick…”  
Spot’s body went rigid. Every hair along his spine stood up. The voice came again, closer this time, almost playful. Something inside me wanted to answer it. Something colder told me to run.  
We ran.  
We didn’t stop until the trailer door slammed shut behind us. I locked every window, every latch, every useless little lock. We crawled under my bed and stayed there until the shaking stopped. When we finally climbed out, the dark felt different—thicker, like it was listening.  
The next morning I woke to a soft scraping at the glass. Slow. Deliberate. When I looked, the yard was empty. Spot pressed against my leg, trembling. We went back to the trail anyway. Curiosity is a stupid, stubborn thing when you’re twelve.  
We hadn’t walked five minutes when the trees seemed to lean in.  
He was standing in the middle of the path.  
Tall. Impossibly thin. The mustard-yellow suit hung off him in filthy folds, stained with mud and something dark that had dried to the color of old blood. His face was the worst part—raw and shiny, as if the skin had been scraped away and never given the chance to heal. When he smiled, the red flesh pulled tight over sharp teeth.  
He said my name in my voice.  
“Derrick… Derrick…”  
Then he laughed. The sound didn’t belong in a human throat.  
We ran until our lungs burned. My parents were home when we burst through the door. I tried to tell them. The words came out broken and frantic. They looked at each other the way adults do when they decide a child is lying. “Go to your room, Derrick. Enough of the stories.”  
That night I understood something cold and final: no one was going to believe me. Not unless I brought back proof.  
I left Spot inside. I took the little camera and walked back into the trees alone. The trail felt narrower. The air tasted like copper. I recorded everything—the dead leaves, the motionless branches, the way the light seemed to die a few feet ahead of me. For a long time there was nothing.  
Then the laugh came from behind me. Low. Wet.  
I turned.  
He was closer than he should have been. Blood glistened around his mouth this time, fresh and dark. His eyes—if they were eyes—never blinked. He didn’t chase me when I screamed and dropped the camera. He simply stood there in the middle of the path, head tilted, and spoke in the exact pitch and rhythm of my own voice.  
“Derrick… come back.”  
I never went looking for the camera. A week later Spot ran into those same woods and never came home. I stood on the porch and watched him disappear between the trees. I didn’t call after him. I didn’t follow.  
Some trails don’t lead anywhere you’re supposed to go.  
And some things that wear your voice never really leave.
