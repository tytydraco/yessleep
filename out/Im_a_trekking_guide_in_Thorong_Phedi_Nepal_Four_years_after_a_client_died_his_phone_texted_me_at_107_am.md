It was seven minutes past one in the morning when my phone lit up.

Thorong Phedi. Four thousand four hundred and fifty metres, in the Annapurna mountains of Nepal. In that lodge there is no signal, except in one spot: the corner of the dining room, beside the window, if you hold your phone against the glass.

I was lying in my sleeping bag. Awake. I am always awake in that lodge. And my phone, lying on my chest, found a single bar of signal on its own. One message. From a contact I had never deleted. “Joris V.”

Joris Vermeer died four years ago.

Five words. “Dawa. I am still here.”

My name is Dawa Gurung. I’m from Ghandruk, a village on the southern side of the Annapurnas. I’ve guided for nineteen years. The Circuit, Base Camp, Mustang. I have taken hundreds of people over Thorong La, the pass at five thousand four hundred metres, and I have brought them all back. Except one.

Joris was Dutch. Sixty-one. A retired schoolteacher from Utrecht. Slow on the uphill, first to laugh. He bought momos for the porters out of his own pocket and never let me thank him. His wife had died two years before. He had a daughter in Rotterdam, whom he mentioned only as “my girl, the lawyer.” I never learned her name. He told me walking was the only thing that made the silence in his house bearable. I understood that more than I admitted.

October, four years ago. We reached Phedi the afternoon the weather turned. By dark it was a white roar. The lodge was full. Thirty trekkers, porters sleeping on the kitchen floor. And the owner did what every owner does in a high storm: he bolted the front door, top and bottom, and told everyone, nobody goes out. A door left open in a storm can kill a whole lodge. I agreed. It’s the rule.

Joris was in the bunk across from mine, breathing hard. Altitude. Not dangerous yet. I told him to drink water and sleep. He squeezed my arm. “Thank you, Dawa.” That was the last thing he said to me.

Around one in the morning, I was awake. I heard the bolts slide. Someone going to the latrine, I thought. Then the bolts slid back again. A porter, tidy, doing his job. I thought nothing of it.

Then, a few minutes later, I heard a voice outside. Thin, under the wind. Like someone calling my name. I sat up. I switched on my headlamp. I pointed it at the window. And I told myself it was the wind.

I switched off the light. I lay back down. I did not get up. Because getting up meant unbolting the door, and unbolting the door meant breaking the rule, and the wind, I told myself, can sound like anything.

In the morning, Joris’s bunk was empty. We found him at eight o’clock, four hundred metres down the trail. Sitting on a low stone wall, facing the lodge. Hat on. Hands in his lap. Exposure, the police said. A man confused by altitude, who wandered out and could not get back.

They asked if I’d heard anything. I said I’d slept through the night. My cousin’s husband, the owner, said the same for his household. It is very easy to believe a guide.

I kept guiding. It is the only thing I know how to do.

Four years later, same October, same lodge. A new group of four. One of them, a quiet Dutch woman named Anneke de Wit, early thirties, had asked for me by name. I took it as a compliment. I should have asked why. I should have asked her a lot of things.

Which brings me back to seven minutes past one, and a message from a dead man. Then another. “Look at the window.” And another. “Come outside.”

I’m not proud of what I did next. I crossed that dining room in my socks, and I woke Anneke by the shoulder. “Who is doing this? Who has his phone?”

She was already awake. Fully dressed. Boots laced. As if she’d been waiting. “I have his phone, Dawa. He was my father. I took my husband’s name, so you never knew. It was in his coat. The police returned it to us. It took me three years to charge the old battery and open the files.” “And I kept his number alive. Every month. I scheduled that message for tonight, for seven past one.”

“Why?”

“Because that’s when he started recording. Come outside.”

Minus twelve. A sky so full of stars it looked spilled. She walked me four hundred metres down the trail, in the dark, in the wind that never stopped, to a low stone wall. I knew that wall. I’d seen it every night in my sleep. She sat. She patted the stone beside her.

I sat. And I looked back up at the lodge.

From there, the dining room window was the warmest thing in the world. A small square of yellow. And through it, if you look closely, the shapes of sleeping people. A headlamp, switched on. Switched off.

“He saw that,” she said. “Listen.”

She pressed play. And there, in the dark, on that wall, a dead man’s voice came out of a phone.

“Dawa. I only went out for the latrine. Someone bolted the door behind me. They thought I was already in. I’m not going to shout. Everyone is sleeping, and they’re sick, and the door is a rule for a reason.”

“I can see your light. You turned it on. You heard me. I know you heard me. That’s all right.”

“It’s strange. I’m not cold anymore. I’m going to sit here, where you can see me, in case you look out again. Don’t be sorry. You are a good guide.”

“One day, Dawa, open the door for someone else.”

The recording ran on. Five hours. Breathing, and wind, and every few minutes, very gently, a man saying my name, into a night that would not answer.

Anneke switched it off. I couldn’t speak. I knew that sound, the bolts sliding back into place. A tidy porter, doing his job. I had lain there and thought nothing of it.

“I’m not going to the police,” she said. “You broke no law. You followed the rule.” “I wanted you to sit where he sat. And I wanted you to know someone heard him.”

I have thought about it every night since. People ask whether I’d have opened the door if he had knocked. Of course. A thousand times, yes. But that’s not the part that destroys me.

It’s that he never knocked. He had a whole fist. He had five hours. And he sat on that wall, in the snow, respecting my sleep.

I still guide. In every lodge, I sleep beside the door. I never bolt it. The owners hate me for it. And some nights, around one in the morning, I hear something outside the wind.

I get up. Every time.

Because one night, it won’t be the wind.
