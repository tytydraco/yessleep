The devastation of Fairview, Tennessee started with the disappearance of Molly Cooper. Although, I suppose it started long before that, but not as far as the good people of Fairview knew. In the summer of ‘09, twelve-year-old Molly Cooper walked into the proverbial woods behind the trailer park just off the 62 and never returned. It pains me to say that the investigation that followed was lackadaisical at best, careless at worst. It was no secret that Molly wanted to be anywhere but Fairview and everyone could attest to evidence of that fact. The choppy hair, the thick eyeliner, the baggy clothes… she was the princess of Midwestern fairytales, wrestling out of her evil stepmother’s grip to go explore the world and find a prince. Everyone just assumed her pumpkin carriage came in the form of a 16-wheeler and her prince was a 40-year-old in a motel. We should have looked harder. *I* should have looked harder.

Around a year before her disappearance, Molly stumbled through a storm into the police station. The doors clanged open around two in the morning, startling both myself and the chief, who I suspected had been dozing in his office. Rainwater plopped from the edges of her jacket and hair to the floor around her, giving the worn police seal its first wash in ages. The amount of water puddling at her feet was a testament to the strength of the storm raging outside, considering she’d probably only walked the half mile between the station and the trailer park. Chief Stevens threw his office door open in a hurry, brandishing his pistol in a sad effort to come off as menacing. ‘Oh,’ he said, almost disappointed he had to holster the weapon. ‘It’s you.’ Molly, who’d been given a fright in light of the chief’s approach, rightfully so I might add, just stared back at him in response. 

Chief shook his head and made his way back into his office. 

“I’m calling your father!” he called out. Her eyes widened at this and they swiveled to me as if realizing my presence for the first time. 

“Hey, Chief,” I called out. “Hold on.” I approached, hands proffered in a show of peace. 

“Why are you here, Molly?” No response. “I can’t help you unless you tell me what’s going on.” Still nothing. At this point, I was closing the last ten feet between us. Chief poked his head out, phone cradled between his shoulder and his head. 

“Molly, are you lost? Do you need us to drive you home?” She shivered, though from the cold or the suggestion, I couldn’t tell. She never gave us a word. She eventually bowed her head as Chief put her in the car, in the back, I’m sure to teach her some kind of lesson, and drove the half mile back to the trailer park. I could never shake the tears that had welled up in her eyes, the way she’d rubbed her palms nervously. She’d wanted to say something, I’m sure of it. People stumble into police stations for two reasons: they’re running from something, or running to help. I think for Molly, it was both. 

She never visited us again. I wondered, when I’d watch the school bus rumble by on its morning pickup run, whether she would, but she didn’t. And pretty soon, the station returned to its drab routine, investigating the supposed haunting of Miss Laney every two weeks and chasing down Jason Newell, renowned Fairview bike thief. We rounded out our nights at Binny’s every Thursday while the rookies were on shift, Chief, me, and the guys. Mr. Cooper was one of them and as much as Chief wanted to believe they were chums, I think we intruded on his nightly routine. Not that he much minded. Not after three vodka sodas, at least. And certainly not after getting his first couple rides home in a cruiser instead of grapevining down the midnight highway. And so we found ourselves living the townie life, cashing checks and throwing darts, until a twist of circumstances dumped Jim Cooper on that seal nearly a year later. 

It wasn’t raining this time, but it was dark. He looked haggard and the stench wafting off of him left us wishing there’d been a storm after all. Chief was better prepared this time and made his way to Mr. Cooper, cupping his shoulder and steering him to the closest desk in the bullpen. Which was mine. I hastily stood up and made way for Chief to sit in my place, keeping my eye on Cooper. Chief may have thought they were the best of friends, but I’d seen enough men with vices during my time in St. Louis to know that they were unpredictable. Emotions fell in their laps like live grenades, their subsequent mishandling of which tended to result in the injury of those sitting a little too close. Jim collapsed into the chair I had brought over from Hannigan’s desk and placed on the opposite side of the desk. He started wringing his hands and glancing around, as if contemplating the final moment before things changed. Before they became real. Like I said, I knew the type. 

“Jimbo,” Chief proclaimed. “What can we do for you this fine evening?” Jim looked up, his eyes meeting ours in turn and finally illuminated by the weak lamp I kept on my desk, I could see they were rimmed with red. 

He took a shaky breath and whispered, “Molly’s gone.” Chief and I exchanged a quick glance. It wasn’t unheard of for kids to run away from Fairview. In July of ‘07, Miranda Steinmann had shacked up with her thirty year old boyfriend two towns over for a week before his wife came home and ripped the rose-colored glasses right off her nose and sent her on home with her tail tucked. Boys, too. Steven Roberts ran away from the family junkyard business with his guitar strapped to his back and napkin lyrics poking out of his jean pocket when he was fifteen. He lasted about a month and half before a fistfight with the wrong guys landed him in the first taxicab Fairview had seen in two years. His parents were the only ones he could think of who could pay the fare. Mr. Roberts was a strong tempered man but at the sight of his son’s swollen eyes and bloodied lips, he turned right around and carried on with his life. Steven took over the junkyard last year when his old man keeled over. As Chief and I locked eyes, I knew he was thinking the same thing. Molly was gone, sure, but she’d sure as the sun and moon switched places, come back. 

Chief cleared his throat and motioned for me to grab a notepad. “Now, Jim, I wouldn’t worry too hard about that girl of yours. I’m sure she’ll turn up. But we’ll keep an eye out for ya, alright? When did she leave?” I sidled a glance at the Chief and then checked on Cooper, gauging whether he’d react to the idea that Molly’d left, not disappeared. But he just shook his head and raked a grimy hand through his hair. 

“I, uh… I’m not sure. You know, kids these days, runnin’ around at all hours…” He kicked the desk with his boot. Chief nodded knowingly, though how he’d know the plight, I’m not sure. I took note of the way Cooper’s hand trembled when he wasn’t twisting them around in his lap. Drunk, then. Probably had been for days and didn’t ever notice Molly taking off. No wonder he looked guilty as all hell. 

“Do you remember seeing her around yesterday?” I asked. He shook his head again, but looked off to the left, his efforts to parse through the few blurry memories of the last month clearly falling short. For a moment, I felt bad for the man. Nobody prepared for these moments - the ones where after years of the same day playing over and over and over, the day changed. And it was already too late. 

I put my hand on Chief’s shoulder and closed my notepad. “Thanks, Jim. Chief and I are going to see what we can find.” Chief nodded in agreement and followed me into his office and closed the door. I watched Cooper’s head fall into his open palms, his 6’2” silhouette shrinking into a pitiable form. I looked back at Chief. 

“He’s drunk,” I said. “When do you reckon she’ll come back?” Chief leaned back in his chair worn through from three decades of townie sheriff work, which comprised mostly napping. 

“Couple days, I’d hope for the guy. But couple weeks, probably. She hated this place.” I took another note of the past tense he used. So maybe never. I sighed. Even if we wanted to go scouring the whole county for any sign of her, we had no idea where to start, not with Cooper being as unhelpful as he was. Chief knew the same. We strode back out to the bullpen. 

“Alright, Jimbo,” Chief said in that midwestern tone of good-bye. “Let’s get you home and sobered up. We have all our guys on this, alright? And if you remember anything else, you give us a call or come to the station, alright? She’ll come back, I know it.” Cooper hesitated like he wanted to stay hunched over in this chair until the sun came up, but eventually he stood and followed Chief out to the car. That was the second to last time I saw Jim Cooper. If I had known then what I do now, it probably would have been the last. 

*End of Part 1.*
