I woke up with someone else’s breath going into my mouth.

Warm air touched my lips, stopped, then touched them again. I kept my eyes closed. My first thought was that Jess had let herself in after her shift, and my second was that she was being incredibly fucking weird.

I almost said her name.

Then I smelled it.

Sour, like a wet dishcloth left in a bucket. Under that was the smell of soil, strong enough that I pictured the bags of compost stacked outside the hardware store when it rained.

Three clicks by the floorboards. A pause. Two more.

Jess always took her shoes off at the door. I tried to shape the sound into her keys hitting the counter, or the metal clip from her hair.

The tapping came closer.

I cracked my lids. Just enough to see through the veil of lashes.

A head rested beside my pillow.

Bald, and pale like skin kept too long from light. Streetlamp glow laid a dull orange line along one cheek. The rest fell into shadow, except for a wet lower lip hanging slack over small, neat teeth.

It crouched, face level with mine.

Arms too long. One hand braced near the bedpost, elbow jutting above the mattress. The other splayed on the floorboards—fingers shifting softly.

Click.

My eyes sealed shut.

Not a choice. A reflex. I waited for its grip around my throat.

Nothing.

Only breathing—a wet, ragged pull at the end of each exhale, like a clogged drain trying to clear itself.

My own breath hitched and stalled in my lungs like a seized engine.

I knew, somehow, that was wrong. That it could hear the sudden silence where my breathing should have been. Slowly, I let air out through my nose—a thin, controlled stream. In again. Out. Trying to mimic the rhythm of sleep, but every breath was a conscious act now, strained and theatrical in its forced regularity.

A finger touched my cheek.

Cool. Damp with what might have been condensation or something else. It traced a line down to my mouth, catching on the coarse stubble from this morning’s shave, each tiny hair standing alert.

It paused at the corner of my lip.

Then pressed—not hard, but insistently—stretching my mouth sideways into a stiff, unnatural curve.

My jaw went slack. Don’t know how. The rest of me was rigid as stone, but my mouth surrendered completely. A thin silver line of spit seeped onto the cotton pillowcase.

It withdrew.

The bed groaned, a low complaint from the springs beneath the thin mattress.

I thought it was climbing in.

Instead, cold fingers slid under my wrist—slender, probing digits that felt like they had too many joints.

Lifted my hand slowly—elbow still rooted on the mattress—until my forearm stood upright like a dead branch. Its grip tightened; I felt the twin bones of my wrist grind together like stones underwater.

Then release.

My hand dropped against my chest with a soft thud. I left it there, a dead weight.

It took my wrist again.

This time lifted the whole arm. Shoulder tugged forward from the socket. Arm dangled somewhere above me in the dark, waiting.

It let go.

Knuckles cracked against the unfinished wooden frame. A splintered jolt of pain shot up to the elbow. Fingers twitched instinctively to curl into a fist; I forced them limp, let them drape over the edge like wet cloth.

The breathing stopped.

A car hissed by on wet pavement outside. A pipe ticked twice in the wall behind the headboard—the old building settling or something moving within it.

It made a soft, considering noise through its nose—almost a sigh.

Then picked up my hand and laid it gently back on the mattress, arranging my fingers as if I were posed for rest.

That was worse than the drop.

I mapped the escape in my mind: roll left, feet down onto the rough hardwood, three steps past the wicker laundry basket heaped with yesterday’s clothes, turn the cool brass handle. No lock on this door. The bathroom had one—a cheap push-button thing that never quite caught.

My phone glowed on the nightstand, a blueish halo inches from where its head must be.

Hadn’t heard it come in.

Deadbolt was turned. I’d checked it twice—neighbor found someone sleeping in the stairwell last week, curled up like a dog.

But the window was open a few inches. I’d propped it up before bed with a paperback to fight the stuffy heat that clung to the room.

Could something that size fit through that gap? Shoulders like that, limbs that long?

The blanket began to slide.

Short, deliberate tugs. Once it snagged under my hip; a hand pressed firmly into the meat of my thigh through the fabric, working it loose with a patient, persistent tug.

Cold air touched my knees—a damp chill that raised goosebumps along my skin.

Old T-shirt thin from washing, running shorts that suddenly felt like wearing nothing at all. Every exposed patch of skin felt raw, trespassed, hyperaware.

The blanket slid away from my ankles in one fluid pull.

Nothing. No weight on the mattress. No breath but mine. I cracked my eyelids.

It faced my feet. Profile sharp: ear flat to skull, shoulder lifted toward jaw, spine a high arch over gaunt hips. Studying my legs. One hand suspended above my knee—fingers bent like it meant to take something delicate. Its head started to pivot. I sealed my eyes before it finished turning.

The mattress sank near my stomach. A quick pass of air stirred the hairs on my forehead. Its teeth met—one clean, dry click beside my nose. My stomach clenched; acid burned the back of my throat. It didn’t move. I didn’t stop breathing.

Its palm came to rest on my forehead. Fingers dug into my hair—grit ground into my scalp. One fingertip pressed my right eyelid. Not asking. Hard enough to bruise the dark red, to make me feel the shape of my own eye beneath the pressure.

Every muscle wanted to thrash. It pushed harder. Light flared behind the lid. A shaky breath leaked out—unplanned, wet.

I thought that was it—the sound it wanted.

But it let up. Its hand stayed twisted in my hair. I focused on the torn seam of the pillowcase against my mouth. Jess said throw it out. I said it’s still good. Remembered that while it held me there. A stupid fight about a pillow.

After a while, it withdrew. Weight shifted off the bed. A joint popped softly. Nails scratched wood under the frame.

I didn’t move until my hip ached, then until the ache became fire down my leg. Bladder tight and urgent. Right eye crying; a tear rolled over the bridge of my nose into the other.

Much later, headlights swept the room.  
Brightness shifted behind my lids, then the slender shadow of its head passing over my face.

Still there.

I hadn’t been counting seconds. I couldn’t. Each time I tried, I lost track while listening for its breathing.

The only reason I have any idea how long it stayed is that I’d checked the time before falling asleep again. It had been 2:11. When I finally reached for my phone afterward, it was 3:08.

Almost an hour, minus however long I’d slept.

Near the end, it put the blanket back.

Not neatly. It dragged it up as far as my shins and left it bunched there.

Then the floorboards creaked in sequence toward the window.

I listened to the soft, uneven placement of its hands and feet. There was a pause beside the nightstand, and something brushed the charging cable against the wall.

The window frame gave a small wooden knock.

After that, the room was quiet.

I stayed where I was.

A truck went past. The refrigerator started in the kitchen. Someone upstairs flushed a toilet, and water rushed down the pipe behind my wall.

Those ordinary noises were almost enough to make me cry.

When I finally opened my eyes, the floor beside my bed was empty.

The window was raised all the way.

I stared at it for a long time before sitting up.

My legs shook so badly that I couldn’t get my feet into my slippers. I went barefoot instead, slammed the window shut, and turned both catches. Then I switched on the ceiling light.

Nothing underneath the bed. Nothing in the closet.

I checked the apartment door.

Still deadbolted.

In the bathroom, my right eye was red. There was a faint mark on my cheek that could have been from the pillow. My knuckles hurt when I made a fist, but I couldn’t see a bruise yet.

I sat on the closed toilet and searched sleep paralysis on my phone.

Everything fit if I made it fit.

A presence in the room. Pressure. A vivid hallucination. The sense that something was touching you.

I had been unable to move, hadn’t I?

I stopped thinking about the difference between unable and afraid.

At 6:40, I called Jess.

“Are you okay?” she asked. Her voice was thick with sleep.

“Yeah. Sorry. Did you come over last night?”

“No. Why?”

“I had a really weird dream.”

“What happened?”

I looked toward the bedroom.

“Somebody was in here.”

She was quiet for a second.

“Your door locked?”

“Yeah.”

“Probably that sleep thing. My brother gets it. He sees a man standing by his dresser.”

“Yeah,” I said. “That’s what I was reading.”

“Come over if you want.”

“I’ve got work.”

“So do I. You can still come over.”

I told her I’d call her later.

By eight, I had showered, dressed, and made coffee. Daylight made the bedroom look small and a little embarrassing. Laundry on the chair. Dust on the nightstand. A cup I should have taken to the kitchen days earlier.

I could move the window in my sleep. That was possible. I’d sleepwalked once when I had a fever.

I stood there drinking my coffee and building a version of the night I could live with.

Then I noticed the dirt beneath the window.

At first I thought rain had blown in. There were dark smears on the wall below the sill and crumbs of dried mud along the baseboard.

I crouched down.

One mark had a palm.

I set my coffee on the floor.

There were two handprints, side by side, just above the skirting board. Long fingers pointed down toward the floorboards. Mud had collected thickly at the fingertips where they’d pressed against the paint.

From there, a faint trail crossed the floor to my bed.

I followed it on my knees.

Beside the pillow, where I’d first heard the tapping, four small patches of mud had dried in the grain of the wood.

I sat back against the wall and looked at my phone.

Jess had sent a message ten minutes earlier.

"You should come stay tonight. Get some actual sleep."

I started typing yes. Then I looked at the window again. I hadn’t woken when it opened. I hadn’t woken when it crossed the room.

I’d only woken because it was breathing against my face.

There was no reason to think last night had been its first visit.

I spent two nights at Jess’s. On the third, I went home because I needed clothes and because being scared of my own apartment in the middle of the afternoon felt ridiculous.

I woke at 2:46 with the smell in my nose.

The breathing came from farther away this time. Beyond my knees.

I was on my side, facing the nightstand. My phone was still lit from when I’d checked it a few seconds earlier, before I’d understood what had woken me. In that light I could see a pale curve at the foot of the bed.

Its back.

It sat facing the window, shoulders hunched, hands resting between its feet. The blanket pulled tight beneath its weight.

I closed my eyes almost all the way.

For a while, nothing happened. The phone went dark. My left arm, trapped beneath the pillow, began to tingle. Outside, someone started a car and sat with the engine running long enough that I became angry with them. Just drive. Or get out. Do something that changes this.

My hip shifted before I could stop it.

The movement was small, an adjustment to ease the pressure on the bone. The mattress creaked. I waited.

It stayed facing the window.

A few minutes later, my foot jerked beneath the blanket. One of those little kicks that happens when you’re dropping off, except I wasn’t dropping off. I had never been more awake.

Still nothing.

Then a hand slid backward along the mattress.

Not toward me. It was only changing position, taking some weight off its knees. But I sucked in a quick breath.

Its head turned.

Immediately.

I saw the side of its face above its shoulder and stopped myself from holding the air in. Let it out. Took another breath.

Its mouth was open a little.

We stayed that way until the car finally drove off.

Eventually it faced the window again.

I had been treating every movement as the thing that would get me killed. Now I wasn’t sure. It had ignored the bed creaking. Ignored my foot. But that breath had brought its head around before I’d finished taking it.

It was listening to me.

Not waiting for me to move. Waiting for me to give myself away.

I tried to remember how I breathed when I slept. As though that was something a person ought to know. Jess had recordings of me snoring; she’d played one in the kitchen once while I was denying it. I wished I’d listened more carefully.

In through my nose. Out through my mouth.

Too even.

I let the next breath run a little longer. Let my lips part against the pillow. My tongue was pressed so hard against the roof of my mouth that it hurt when I relaxed it.

Saliva gathered beneath it.

I needed to swallow.

The need became absurdly large. There was room in my head for the thing at the foot of the bed and the spit in my mouth, nothing else. I tried swallowing during an exhale. My throat made a small click.

Its head tilted.

I let my mouth hang open and breathed out again, louder this time, a loose, stupid sleeping noise.

Its shoulders lowered.

That could have been a coincidence. All of it could have been. I still used that noise afterward.

My eyes kept drying out through the narrow gap beneath my lids. Closing them helped, but then I couldn’t see it. Opening them again felt like lifting a curtain while someone waited on the other side.

I started blinking with the passing cars. Light crossed the ceiling; I closed my eyes. Darkness returned; I opened them a fraction. I don’t know why that seemed safer.

At some point the blanket slipped off its shoulder.

I hadn’t seen it pick the blanket up.

It caught the edge and rubbed the hem between two fingers. Patiently, almost distractedly. There was a loose thread there. Jess had pulled at it the last time she changed the sheets, and I’d told her she’d unravel the whole thing.

It worked at that thread for a long time.

My shoulder burned. I allowed myself to roll it forward slowly, keeping my breathing loose. The creature didn’t turn.

That was worse in a way. Knowing there was a rule meant I could break it.

The next time I saw the clock, it was 4:31.

It had barely moved for nearly two hours.

I had begun to think it might leave.

Then the mattress dipped at my feet.

Its hands came first. One beside my ankle, the other by my knee. It crawled up the bed with careful placements, stopping after each one while the springs settled. I could feel its weight through my ribs.

I wanted to pull my legs up. Every muscle in my stomach tightened to do it.

I made myself let them go.

A knee sank into the mattress beside my hip. The bed sloped toward it, rolling me slightly forward. I allowed that too. Sleeping people roll when the bed moves. Sleeping people don’t brace themselves.

It lowered its head onto the pillow.

There wasn’t enough room.

Its forehead touched mine, then drew back an inch. One arm folded between us. The other reached across the pillow above my head.

We were lying face-to-face.

At that distance I could see the dirt in the crease beside its nose. A short fiber clung to its lower lip. Its eyes reflected the window, but I couldn’t make out where it was looking.

Then its hand closed around the back of my neck.

I nearly lost it.

The grip was sudden and hard. It pulled me forward until my nose struck its cheek, then held me there while my next breath broke into two little sounds.

Its fingers tightened.

I let my jaw fall open against it. Breathed wetly through my mouth. Forced my shoulders down when everything in me wanted to fight.

After a few seconds, it loosened its grip.

One fingertip moved through the hair behind my ear.

I lay there with tears soaking into the pillow and tried to make the next breath sound unimportant.

The window gradually became a gray rectangle. I could see the wardrobe, then the handles on the drawers. A delivery truck stopped outside. Someone dragged a bin over the pavement.

Its eyes stayed open.

I watched daylight reach the side of its head.

Only when the first direct strip of sunlight touched the wall did it lift itself away from me. The mattress rose. Its hand lingered behind my neck, then slid free.

I kept breathing the same way until I heard the window move.

Even after that, I couldn’t stop.

I stayed on my side until the room was bright enough that I could see dust moving through the sunlight, breathing in through my nose and out through my mouth. Loose. Slow. 

Uneven. 

The way a sleeping person breathes.  
Eventually I sat up. The room was empty, but the window was open again.

 I called Jess from the kitchen, and she wanted me to come over immediately. I told her I would.

Before I left, I stood in the bedroom doorway and looked at the bed. There was a shallow impression on the pillow beside mine. I walked over even though every part of me was telling me not to.

Five thin streaks of dirt crossed the fabric near where its face had been. I stared at them for a long time. The first night, I’d told myself it was sleep paralysis. I couldn’t tell myself that anymore.

But something else bothered me even more.  
It had known I was awake. I was sure of that now. My breathing had changed. I’d moved. I’d swallowed. 

At one point I had practically looked straight at it, and still, it hadn’t hurt me. Not while I kept pretending to sleep.

I didn’t understand why. I didn’t know what would happen if I stopped pretending, and I had no intention of finding out.

At least, that’s what I thought then.

Before I bought the camera.
