
I've worked in digital forensics for almost a decade.

When people think about police investigations, they picture detectives interviewing suspects or crime scene technicians dusting for fingerprints. Hardly anyone thinks about people like me.

I spend my days in a small evidence lab with two monitors, a stack of write-blockers, and shelves full of phones that belong to strangers. My job is to recover whatever someone tried to erase.

Most days it's nothing exciting. Family photos. Embarrassing text messages. Drunk voice notes people regret sending. Every once in a while we recover something important enough to become evidence, but those cases are rare.

After enough years, you stop wondering about the people behind the devices. You just focus on the data.

Phone 714 looked no different.

It was an inexpensive Android recovered during a financial fraud investigation. The detective who dropped it off apologized for giving us "another boring one."

I laughed and told him boring usually meant less paperwork.

The phone itself was cracked beyond repair, but the storage chip was intact. I connected it to our recovery station, started the extraction software, and went to refill my coffee.

When I came back, the scan had finished.

Everything looked normal. Photos, messages, contacts, browser history, deleted app data.

Then I opened the voicemail database.

There were over two hundred deleted recordings. That wasn't unusual.

What caught my attention was that every single voicemail came from the same caller.

UNKNOWN.


Corrupted caller IDs happen.
The timestamps didn't.
According to the software, every voicemail had been received exactly one day in the future.

The recovery program immediately flagged them as corrupted metadata.
I assumed it was a software bug.

Out of curiosity, I played the first recording.
There was a second of weak static.

Then a woman whispered,
"If anyone hears this... don't unlock the back door."
The recording ended.
No name. No address. Nothing else.
I archived it and moved on.

The following afternoon, while eating lunch in the break room, I overheard two detectives discussing a burglary. The homeowner had ignored someone pounding on the front door. The intruder had entered through the back instead.

The voicemail crossed my mind.
Then I forgot about it.
Coincidences happen.

The next morning another voicemail appeared.
Same unknown caller.
Same impossible timestamp.
This one lasted twenty-four seconds.

A man was breathing heavily.
"I shouldn't have taken the shortcut."
Several seconds passed.

Then he whispered,
"I can hear someone walking behind me."
The recording cut off.

That evening a missing-person report came across the department. The victim had disappeared while walking home through a park. His last known route matched the shortcut mentioned in the voicemail almost exactly.

That was the first time I felt genuinely uncomfortable.

Over the next week, a new voicemail appeared every morning.
Always one. Always from tomorrow.
Some described house fires.
Others described accidents.

A few were nothing more than frightened people whispering into their phones because they thought someone was nearby.
Sometimes the people survived.
Sometimes they didn't.

I stopped deleting the recordings.
Instead, I started writing everything into a notebook. Dates. Times. Locations. Anything that might matter.

I thought about telling my supervisor more than once.
Then I imagined trying to explain future voicemails that only existed on one damaged phone.

I kept quiet.
About three weeks later, everything changed.
Instead of a stranger...

I heard my own voice.
I replayed the recording four times before accepting what I was hearing.
It wasn't someone who sounded like me.

It was me.

"I know you think these messages are warnings," my voice said.

"They aren't."

The recording crackled.

"They're records."

That was all.

I barely slept that night.

The next morning another voicemail appeared.

Again, it was my voice.

"You've already listened to this."

I frowned. That wasn't possible.
The recording had only just appeared.
Then I noticed something strange.
The timestamp had changed.

Instead of tomorrow, it now claimed the voicemail had been received yesterday.
I checked the older recordings.
Every timestamp had quietly corrected itself.
Only my notebook still showed the impossible dates.
I copied everything onto a USB drive before leaving work.

When I opened it at home...
every file was gone.
The notebook wasn't.

A week passed.
The recordings continued.
So did the headaches.

Sometimes I'd lose a few minutes without realizing it. I'd find an email already sent or a report already finished. Once I discovered an empty coffee cup on my desk that I didn't remember making.

I blamed stress.
Until last Friday.

I arrived early enough that the office lights were still off. Only the recovery station was running.

The damaged phone was connected exactly where I'd left it.
The software was already open.
A new voicemail was waiting.
Duration:

17 minutes.

I clicked play.
There was no static.
Just silence.

Then I heard myself speaking calmly.

"If you're hearing this... don't disconnect the phone."

I looked down.

My hand was already reaching for the cable.

"You're going to think unplugging it ends this."

I froze.

"It doesn't."

Every sentence arrived one or two seconds before I actually did what it described.

"Now you're looking toward the office door."

I was.

"In about ten seconds, someone's going to knock."

Without thinking, I started counting.

At ten...

Three slow knocks echoed through the lab.
I never answered.

After about a minute, the knocking stopped.
When I finally opened the door, the hallway was empty.
Security reviewed the cameras later that morning.

Nobody had entered the hallway.
Nobody had left it either.

Yesterday, Phone 714 disappeared from our evidence locker.
There was no checkout record.

No security footage.
No explanation.
Everyone assumes it was misplaced during inventory.
Maybe they're right.

This morning, though, I found a voicemail on my personal phone.
No missed call.
No notification.
Just one unheard message from an unknown number.

I haven't listened to it yet.
The timestamp says...
it arrives tomorrow.
