I don’t really know how to tell this story in a way that will make you believe me. Truthfully, I have a hard time believing it myself; if I hadn’t seen it with my own eyes, I never would have believed it.

There are some places in the world that people aren’t meant to see, and if they do, they aren’t meant to come back from. Places so quiet you can’t even hear your own thoughts. Places thought to exist only in dreams—places where life has never existed.

But I feel as though I’m getting ahead of myself. I guess the best place to start is the river.

More people than usual disappear near rivers. Ask anyone who lives by one. I suppose it can be attributed to a mixture of human stupidity and the river’s natural dangers. The currents are strong, yet children swim in them during the summer and fish along their banks when the weather turns cold.

Still, I’ve always felt there was more to it.

My grandmother used to say that a river was a divide between worlds. Each bank held its own story, its own place in time. I always thought it sounded almost poetic—one of those strange little things older people say that you remember without ever really thinking about.

In this part of the country, the glaciers didn’t flatten the land. The Mississippi and its various tributaries carved deep valleys into the sandstone, leaving rolling hills and deep valleys all around. It is some of the most picturesque scenery in the Midwest, which is why I chose to attend college there.

River Falls was a beautiful small town. It was surrounded by forest but was still a quick 20-minute drive to the Twin Cities for any shopping needs. Two rivers ran through it, connecting to the Saint Croix and then the Mississippi just a few miles downstream. The college was smaller but homey and offered various wildlife activities to help me become acquainted with the area; it was also fairly close to home, only about a two-hour drive north. The first day on campus, I joined an outdoor club that took us to various parts of the Driftless Area to fish, canoe, and hike. That’s actually how I met Dale.

We were partnered together on a canoe trip as part of our outdoor recreation club. I had seen him at events before, but the club was large, so this was the first time we ever truly talked. He was quiet at first, but when football and various video games came up, he quickly opened up. Before I knew it, we were cracking jokes, splashing each other, and ramming into other canoes to try and rage-bait the others in the group.

We didn’t have any similar classes, but that didn’t stop us. I had a single dorm, and Dale wasn’t very fond of his roommate, so most days after class he would be at my room. We quickly found out we had a lot in common. We both played guitar, albeit poorly, and tried to learn songs together at night, making our RA pretty pissed. We both liked the same sports teams and video games. It’s always strange meeting someone so much like you. Someone that you could always make time for. While I had many friends in grade school, it was easy to say that Dale, in the short time I had met him, had become one of my closest.

As soon as our first year of school was over, Dale, a few other friends, and I decided to get a house together just off campus. Most of us were paying for our own schooling, so being able to split our rent seven ways and not having to be on the school’s terrible meal plan was a blessing. Although I basically lived in a closet. The house was giant and had plenty of room for all of us. If we wanted to see one another, we could sit in the living room or hang out in the dining room, where we put an old pool table. If we wanted privacy, we all had our own space to ignore the outside world.

During this time, I was looking for a job. All the money I had was from high school or the times I could go home during the year and work at my family's restaurant. Dale had just scored a sweet gig from his girlfriend’s roommate at a local daycare and told me to apply. The place was known for hiring college students and worked well around my school hours. I applied and was contacted the next day about an interview. 

The place was called Treetop Learning Center. It was on the edge of town, right next to the forest and the Whitetail Ridge biking trails. A few other friends and I had biked those trails many times. I had driven past this place often but never put two and two together. The building itself was nice, with many windows and a large, fenced-in backyard with a playground.

The interview went great, and they especially liked that I was going to school for education, saying that the more TED courses at college I completed, the more my salary would increase. The next week, I started training with Dale. We acted basically the same in the room with kids. We made jokes and messed around until our boss eventually decided to separate us. We were there every day in the summer, getting as many hours as possible. I was put in a toddler room, so my day was filled with finger painting and diaper changes while Dale was in the 4K classroom teaching kids about letters and numbers.

I say all this now, but at the time, it was just a normal job and a normal life. I worked there for over a year before the incident, and during all that time, nothing truly strange ever happened.

Sure, the occasional angry parent complained about something trivial, and now and then a child insisted they had seen or heard something strange out on the playground, near the forest. But children see things that aren’t there. They hear noises and turn them into monsters. That’s just childhood imagination running wild.

During that year, only two instances struck me as odd.

The first took place in the second week I worked there. We were outside with the students. It had just rained that day, and we had to check the kids’ hands often to stop them from picking up and eating the worms that had crawled up on the concrete. While we were focused on that, a boy named Jack decided he should try to climb to the top of the old wooden playset.

I had turned around and looked at the playground the moment he fell. The smile on my face from one of Dale’s dumb jokes disappeared almost instantaneously as I ran to try and catch the falling boy, knowing damn well I wouldn’t get there in time. He landed hard on his arm, cracking it as it made contact with the woodchips. He was already crying by the time we got him up. He had long sleeves on, but you could tell the bone had made its way through the skin.

I tried to calm him down as best I could while Dale called 9-1-1 and went to our boss to get her in contact with the parents. The kids were gathered around us, all asking questions and some trying to touch Jack. I had stood Jack up and started walking him to the center entrance when I heard it.

At first I thought it was a cicada buzz, but as it grew louder, I stopped in my tracks. It was like a foghorn, only different, like it was mixed with the sound of an elk’s bugle. The sound grew louder, buzzing in my head. The children all stopped playing and looked toward the woods where the sound emerged.

I turned my head to the forest, expecting to see something. Maybe an animal or some grooming machinery for the bike trails, anything that would help me make sense of the noise. There was nothing. It had been windy that morning; I could see the trees in the playground rustle, but at the forest edge not a single branch shook.

Abruptly it stopped; the playground became quiet. Every child, like me, was looking into the woods. Even Jack, who just moments ago had been screaming, had gone quiet. That realization hit me, and I looked down at him. He was staring into the woods.

He finally looked at me. His face had gone pale. In that moment, I began to panic as I thought he was losing blood. I got down to his level.

“Are you ok? What’s wrong?”

He raised his good arm and pointed toward the trees. My eyes followed his finger. Near the edge of the treeline stood a deer. A white-tailed buck. Its horns were still velveted over. By now, all the kids had run over to the fence to scream and stare at the animal.

“Holy biscuits, Mr. Simon, it’s a deer!”

“It’s so big!”

Some kids even began to throw stones in its general direction as three- and four-year-olds normally do. It was probably thirty or forty yards away, although it was hard to tell, and was hidden behind the brush of a few small maples. I could really only see its head and upper half of its body. What was striking, however, were its almost silver eyes. I had never seen any deer with that eye color. It seemed almost unnatural how silver they were.

It wasn’t particularly large or doing anything out of the ordinary; it just stood there and watched. I was surprised that it hadn’t yet run away, considering all the screaming and commotion; you’d expect any animal to bolt. Instead, it just stood there. Its ears were twitching in our direction, like it was listening.

“Is that what you are looking at, Jack, the deer?” 

He nodded.

“And the man.”

I jolted my head back to where the deer had stood. It was gone. I couldn’t even hear it running in the woods. By now, all the kids had lost interest and were back to swinging and playing in the sandbox.

I looked back down at Jack. “It’s nothing, little dude, just trees.”

*Just trees.*

As I said that, Dale came running back outside.

“Hailey got a hold of his parents, and the ambulance is on the way.” He stopped when he saw us. “What are you guys looking at?”

I snapped out of it. “There was a deer, and it just…” I trailed off.

The deer was gone. It was bothering me more than it should have. Not because the deer had left, but because I hadn’t seen it leave. The woods had enough openings that I should have seen it running, but there was nothing.

“It probably just got spooked. My bet is Liam was chucking rocks at it.” He looked over to the sandbox. “Liam! You throwing rocks at wildlife again?”

Liam smirked and hid his head in his shirt.

“That’s what I thought. Damn kid is going to be a serial killer, already sounds like Ed Gein.”

“Dude.” I nudged him and pointed down to Jack.

“My bad.” Dale helped the kid into the center.

Jack was taken to the hospital, and thankfully the injury wasn’t as bad as it looked. He came to daycare the next day as happy as could be because all his friends got to sign his cast.

I asked Dale and a few other staff about the sound I had heard, and they all said they'd heard it before too and that the owner told them it was just the barge whistle from the large shipping freights going down the river. The deer probably hear it all the time and aren’t scared of it.

None of the kids said anything about it either.

Except Jack.

A few days later, I was helping the kids clean up after lunch when he walked over to me. He tugged on my shirt, and I looked down.

“What’s up, Jack?”

He stared at me for a few seconds, then asked, “Do the deer and his friends live in the woods?”

I smiled. “Yes, they do.”

He nodded and went back to playing. I didn’t think about it for years, as it was just a random question a four-year-old asked. I didn’t understand the difference at the time. He asked me if the deer lived there—the deer and his friends.

This brings us to the second strange occurrence I've had—the spring after the deer incident. A young girl named Olivia drew me a picture in class. I was in the 4K classroom that day as Dale was sick, and although I didn’t get to see these kids often, they seemed to love it when I came to visit. The class was primarily girls, so they would all run up and hug me. Some of them, like Olivia, would draw me some pictures and ask me to bring them home to show Dale and our other roommates.

The picture was nothing special, but it was clear she put a lot of work into it, and I showed her and the rest of them my appreciation with a dance party. The picture was of me and her in the sandbox on the playground. The two people were stick figures, one with long hair and one with three little tussles coming off the top, and arrows pointing to either of us with our initials on them—OW for her and SG for me. 

I took the picture home and hung it on the fridge.

A few weeks went by, and my friends and I decided we wanted to go for a bike ride out on the Whitetail Ridge trail. The ridge itself was quite large and had a small stream cutting through it called the South Fork. About half a mile in, the swing bridge stood. By then, it had become a local landmark, even with a brewery named after it. The bridge itself was wire-suspended and hung over a fairly deep ravine to the river below. The ravine was a spot where kids got busted a lot for underage drinking. Across the wires were wooden boards that got changed regularly but always seemed to rot faster than normal planks. The whole area was covered in dense forest, but from the middle of the old relic you could see down into the valley and the entire town.

At night, it was breathtaking. The city lights illuminated the valley.

On the other side of the bridge was a small trick park where we could attempt to be X games athletes. The trick park was made of old bridge planks and was prone to breaking. We still found it fun even with the danger. The day we rode there, we didn’t see anyone else on the trail.

Whitetail Ridge was very well known and usually packed on the weekends. We had left early, however, and the morning fog hadn’t completely rolled out of the hills. We followed the trail the way we had every time, taking a right at the mossy rock and going down the steep decline to the bridge.

It felt much longer today because of the fog. I knew it wasn’t, but something just felt weird. We made it to the bridge, it creaking and shifting under the weight of our bikes. As we made our way across the bridge, I looked over the side to the ravine below. The only part of town you could see was the campus water tower and its red light on top.

The ravine was still overtaken by fog. It looked as if it went on endlessly. We stopped halfway across the bridge, admiring the sight.

“Smoke and a view,” Dale said and pulled a pre-roll from his pack.

We all snickered as Dale pulled out his lighter and tried to light the joint. We passed it around while we sat on our bikes overlooking the city.

“You ever regret not going somewhere else?” I asked my friend Tyler. He was born and raised in River Falls. I remember him telling me that when he was applying to schools, his parents didn't even expect him to fill out any application besides this one. He grew up watching this Division III team play football and admitted to us drunkenly one night that his dream was to be out on that field.

His dream did come true. He was there starting tight end. His parents couldn’t be prouder.

“Nah,” he said, taking a long pull from the roll. “I like it here.”

“Of course you do,” Dale said. “You're River Falls royalty.”

“Not my fault my grandpa is the best personal injury lawyer in the county.”

Tyler passed the joint to me. I was about to take a pull when the sound began again. It seemed louder than the last time, echoing through the hills. We all stopped and listened. Goosebumps rose on my arms. It went on for so long that the joint was down to the roach. It singed my fingertips, and I instinctively dropped it, letting it fall between the planks.

“Dipshit!” Dale yelled. “There are still one or two good hits off that thing.”

He and Chris, our other friend, went to find a stick so they could pull the roach up from between the boards.

“The barges go downriver in this fog?” I asked.

“Barges?” Tyler said, half laughing. “That’s his horn.”

“His horn?”

He scoffed. “The antlered one.” He stopped for a moment. “It means you're close to the pale country.”

My eyes widened. “Really?”

Tyler slapped me across the back of my head. “No, dumbass.” He laughed hysterically, coughing as his lungs ran out of air.

I rubbed the back of my head and mumbled. “It wasn’t that funny.”

“Both of you shut up and stop moving,” Dale said. Using two sticks, he lifted the remainder of the pre-roll out from the bridge. “Fucking brain surgeon precision.”

We finished the joint and threw it over the ledge into the fog. We then rode the rest of the way across the bridge and into the trick park. We spent a few hours there being dumb and trying our best not to fall when we would hit jumps. We saw a few other bikers by then, and they gave us weird looks, probably because they could smell the weed on us. By noon, we were all tired and realized we hadn’t packed any food.

“I’m beat,” I began. “Let’s bike over to Kinni Café and get a bite.”

We all nodded in agreement, but before we left, Chris stopped us. 

“Hey, Simon,” he said, smiling at me. “I bet you lunch you won’t launch off Old Faithful.”

Old Faithful is what they had called the oldest ramp in the park that was high, unsteady, and dangerously close to the ravine edge.

“You're joking,” I said dryly. 

“Come on, dude, don’t be a pussy. It’s free lunch.”

I looked at all three of them. Tyler was grinning, and Dale had a smile from ear to ear while holding two thumbs up.

“Screw it.” I don’t know if it was pure pressure or the good amount of weed in my system, but I began to bike toward the ramp. I gained speed and finally hit the ramp. It felt fine at first, until it teetered under my weight, bucking me to one side and making me fall off my bike and roll dangerously close to the edge. I caught myself just before reaching the edge; all the breath had escaped me, and I was gasping for air.

In the distance, I could hear my friends yelling my name and pedaling toward me. But that wasn’t what had my attention. In front of me was a small rock with a picture carved on it. It fit into the palm of my hand, and I wondered what something like that would be doing out here. I picked it up, and quickly the daycare crossed my mind. 

They bring the 4K out here every year for an end-of-the-year hike. I bet one of the kids made this. It was very worn, so it must have been from a few years ago. In that moment, I thought it was the coolest thing ever. I quickly put it in my backpack so I could bring it in the next time I worked and show the daycare kids.

My friends helped me up, and we rode out of the woods and back into town.

That night I sat at the kitchen island looking at the rock I had found. It was later in the evening, and Dale emerged from his room to make dinner.

“What’s that?” he asked as he opened the fridge to grab his milk.

I turned the rock around and showed him. “It’s this rock I found in the woods today. I think one of the old 4K groups drew on it.”

“That’s pretty cool,” he said in between gulps of milk. He set his drink back and closed the refrigerator door. “Did you have to draw it on a piece of paper too?”

I was confused by his question and looked up. My heart sank into my stomach. On the fridge, where I had left it weeks ago, sat Olivia’s drawing. I looked down at the rock.

They were the same.

These moments, while weird, never struck me as the kind of thing that deserved more than a moment’s thought. Weird shit happens. The rest of my first year at the daycare was normal. Life was normal. Summer had rolled around, and Dale and I began working full-time again. Each day melted into the next. We enjoyed our jobs, but after countless 60-hour weeks, even the best minds get exhausted. 

On the second-to-last day of the summer program, the normal 4K teacher called in with the stomach flu, but Dale hypothesized she didn’t want to be here. It was the 4K hike day, and all the kids were extremely excited. Reluctantly, our boss put Dale and me together in the 4K classroom because I knew the trail pretty well by this point. Most times when I hike, I like to wear longer pants and shirts, but since the trails themselves were so well packed in and it was a particularly hot August day, I opted for shorts and a breathable Packers jersey.

The hike was very straightforward: we went down the trail to the swing bridge, crossed it, and had the kids play at the trick park while we got a few photos for the parents and newsletter. It started fairly normally. We walked through the woods; I was able to flex my ecology and bug knowledge on the kids and Dale. In that moment, I truly felt like a teacher. The kids themselves were thoroughly engaged. They laughed at Dale’s dumb jokes and me eating basswood leaves, and listened quietly as I explained the bird sounds to them.

Eventually we reached the swing bridge. Some kids were extremely excited to cross the bridge while others cried and were nearly carried by Dale. Jack was one of those kids. He held my hand tight as I told him not to worry about it. I got down low to him and, with some encouragement, got him to walk across with me as I held his hand.

The boards of the swing bridge creaked as they always did. Just before halfway, I told Jack to look. He held me tight, and I could see his eyes were glued closed. I reached down and picked him up, placing him on my shoulders.

“Open your eyes, Jack,” I said. “I have you; there is nothing to worry about.”

It took him a few seconds, but he finally opened them, a giant smile streaking across his face. Dale and the rest of the kids had already made their way to the trick park and were playing around. I decided to enjoy this quiet moment a little longer.

“I have to pee.” Jack blurted out, breaking the serene moment. I laughed a little and set Jack back down on the bridge. As we walked across the middle of the bridge, the temperature dropped noticeably. I shivered a bit as I made my way across the bridge. The temperature wasn’t the only noticeable difference. While the trees on the other side of the bridge were still very much green, these had begun to change color into the oranges and yellows of fall.

I told Dale that Jack needed to go potty, and we went a little deeper into the forest to find a secluded spot for him to pee. We came to the top of a hill that overlooked the trick park. From there I could observe all the students, but they couldn’t see me.

“Alright, Jack, just go right here.”

“On this tree?”

“Yes.”

“My mom says I'm not supposed to pee on trees.”

I sighed. “We are going to break that rule just this once.”

“Promise you won’t tell my mom.”

I smiled and turned away from him. “I promise.”

Jack began to pee, and I watched the other kids play from the hill view. It was a perfect day. It came to me at that moment that the forest had no sounds aside from the children playing. I looked up at the canopy. No birds. It was strange, as if everything had left this place.

Then the noise came.

Before, it had started quietly and gotten louder. This time it rang like I was standing right next to the bells of a church. I covered my ears and turned to Jack. He was crying from the sound. I came down to him and covered his ears with my hands, enduring the sound that rattled my skull. It lasted about 15 seconds before it stopped.

When it ended, I opened my eyes. My ears were still ringing, and I looked around to see if anything had changed. Everything looked the same. I grabbed Jack by the shoulders.

“Are you ok?” I yelled, not realizing how loud I was being.

He nodded his head yes, eyes wide.

“Not you,” a voice whispered in my ear. It was as quiet as a mouse, but it cut through the ringing in my ear. I could almost feel the person who said it breathing on the back of my neck.

I spun around, startled, but there was nothing there but trees. My ears finally stopped ringing, and I took Jack’s hand. “Let’s get back.”

We rounded the top of the hill overlooking the trick park. The woods had become eerily silent, but not like before; the kids had stopped yelling. As we got to the top, we stopped. Dale and the other 15 children were gone. The silence intensified.

At first I thought it was a joke. “Haha, very funny, Dale,” I said aloud, Jack still clenching my hand.

We walked down to the trick park. Not a sign. Not even a sound. I thought about how the hell he had kept these kids this quiet for this long.

“Dale!” I yelled. “4K!”

Nothing.

I tried over and over again for what seemed like hours. 

“Dale! This isn’t funny, dude; you are scaring Jack!”

Silence.

I looked around for anything -wrappers, footprints- but nothing was there. I walked back up to the hill, screaming for Dale as loud as I could. The only sound I heard was Jack’s deep breaths.

“Maybe they forgot and left us here,” I said aloud, trying to convince myself. I saw Jack; he was terrified. “Don’t worry, little man; we will get back. I know the way.”

Jack began to cry.

“Jack, it’s ok we will just head back,” I said with a smile, knowing damn well that as soon as I found Dale, I was going to kick his ass.

We walked to the swing bridge and began to cross. Each step felt heavier than the last. As we neared the middle of the bridge, I felt something. A feeling that told me not to step forward. I grimaced and powered through the thoughts. Jack was upset, and we needed to get back. I took my next step across the threshold. 

It was as if a light switch had been flicked off.

One second I could see the green canopy above me, the brown boards beneath my feet, the river stretching below. The next, there was nothing. No trees. No sky. No bridge or river. I couldn’t even see Jack.

“Jack?” My hand was still wrapped around his; I could feel his trembling.

“I’m here,” he whispered.

I pulled him in tight, covering his head; for several seconds, neither of us moved. Then something touched the back of my neck. I spun around and opened my eyes.

Nothing was there.

I looked forward again, and the blackness dissipated. We were standing on the bridge, but it was now nighttime. I looked around in shock; the wind blew fiercely, biting at my face. The trees and ground were bare, covered in snow. I stared at my arms. The cold prickled the hair on my legs. I was still wearing shorts. My jersey was doing little to keep out the cold.

“What the fuck?”

Jack continued to cry. He wasn’t in winter clothes, and the wind was getting harsher.

“It’s ok,” I said and put him on my back. Terror flowed through every vein. “We just need to get back to the center.”

I took a step forward and realized just how deep the snow was. It had nearly reached my knee. Each step sent cold spikes up my leg as my arms began to go numb from the cold and from carrying Jack. 

“Just hold on, Jack!” I yelled as I pushed myself to walk further and further in the forest.

Every part of me was numb, and I began to sway back and forth through the treeline; I just wanted to rest. I needed to rest. I fell to my knees in the snow. It was getting darker. My eyes began to close when Jack spoke up.

“Simon, look.” Jack pointed to the light coming from the center’s parking lot.

I was so close. Slowly, I stood. I channeled everything I had into keeping my legs moving. I could feel nothing. Nothing but Jack's labored breaths on my back. *Just a little further,* I told myself, ready to collapse from exhaustion.

I reached the door and fell once more, dropping Jack. Slowly, I began to dig in the snow for the spare key; my fingertips had turned black, splitting as I shoveled more snow back to find the rocks, finally finding the fake rock with the key inside.

I struggled to put it into the keyhole; my vision had become blurry. I finally heard it click open and pulled the door as wide as I could with the snow packed in front of it. I pushed Jack in before squeezing myself through the door and collapsed face-first on the tile floor. The heat instantly hit us. I began to weep, lying in the doorway. I looked down at my legs. Parts of them had begun turning black. I was scared to take my shoes off and look at my feet, as they had gone numb before anything else. Jack and I were soaking wet and shivering in the doorway. He just stood there looking at me.

“S… S… Simon?” he whimpered.

I turned my head to look at him, barely conscious. “Get the phone, Jack.”

I pointed to the landline sitting on the receptionist’s desk, my arms barely able to lift. He ran there and grabbed the phone. I was beginning to fall in and out of consciousness. He handed me the phone. Weakly, I dialed 9-1-1. It rang…

“9-1-1, what’s your emergency?”

I tried to speak; nothing came out.

“Help,” Jack yelled to the phone before I dropped it and slipped into the blackness.

I awoke in a white room. A fan above me blew air where I sat. I could hear beeping and a TV playing some sitcom in the background. My eyes darted from side to side, trying to uncover where I was. I saw machines and tubes all over me.  Finally, it clicked: hospital, I’m in a hospital.

“Honey!” I felt a hand touch my face; I flinched and turned my head. It was my mother. “Jared, get the nurse!” she yelled to my dad, and I watched him run out of the room. 

I tried to talk but realized there was a large tube going into my mouth and down my throat.

“Don’t try to talk,” she said, then reached down and gave me a large hug. I looked down at my hands; they were wrapped in bandages and extremely sore. One was also handcuffed to the side of the bed.

I couldn’t speak, and my body was as stiff as a board. I pointed to the door with my eyes. She looked at me, confused. “What is it?”

I pointed again, straining my throat to get any sound out. “Jack.”

Her expression changed. It was small but noticeable—a tightening around her eyes.

“Who is Jack?”

I began to push against my restraints. It shot waves of pain through my body, but I needed to stand. My father rushed in with a nurse. “He just woke up. I don’t know what happened.”

My heart felt like it was going to pound out of my chest. “Jack?” I wheezed out again.

The nurse came toward me, putting her hand on my shoulder. I stared past her. At the hallway. At the fluorescent lights and people walking past, peeking into the room.

There he was.

Standing at the end of the hall, barefoot, wearing the same yellow shirt and blue shorts. His skin was pale; he looked soaked to the bone. He was staring directly at me. My eyes widened. I tried to scream.

The nurse held me down. “We need to sedate him!”

Jack raised one finger to his lip. “Shhh. You won’t hear him.”

He turned and walked away from the door. I ripped at my restraints with my free hand, trying to claw my way out. My mother was weeping as my father and the nurse held me down. I felt the needle go into my arm but couldn’t look away from the hallway.

As Jack disappeared around the corner, the nurse pulled the curtain shut. I began to go limp, the sedative taking effect. I looked over at my father. He was comforting my mom; he said something I couldn’t make out. It was getting darker.

Finally I felt my eyelids shut. I was sitting in blackness, though my ears still worked. I remember that because, as I drifted into that induced sleep, I heard it. Somewhere beyond the hospital walls, impossibly far away and close at the same time. I heard it again. That terrible horn. It drowned out everything else as I drifted to sleep.

“Simon.”

I opened my eyes again to the hospital room. It was quiet; dusk had settled, and it shot rays of crimson into the room around me. I saw my parents sitting in the chairs next to me, watching my eyes. The nurse from before was on the other side, writing down numbers from the machine.

“Simon.” The voice said again.

I turned and looked in front of me. Two police officers stood there. I coughed out a weak “yes.” The tube had been removed from my throat, but it was still sore.

“I’m Officer Clemons, and this is Officer Rodli.” He pointed to the man beside him. “I know you’ve been through a lot, but we would like to ask you a few questions about…”

“Jack,” I said, cutting them off. “Where is Jack?”

The two men looked at one another. “Are you referring to Jack Cunningham?”

“Yes, I carried him out of the woods; he dialed 9-1-1 for me.”

The man took out a notepad and began writing down what I said.

“Is he ok? Where is he?”

“Simon, nobody was found with you when the police and ambulance arrived.”

“That’s impossible,” I said hoarsely, my throat still itching from the tube. “He was there, he got me the phone, I…” I paused.

“What about Dale and the others? We couldn’t find them, and then that snowstorm came out of nowhere.” I looked around at everyone in the room. They all held grim faces. “Please tell me they made it back safe.”

Their hardened faces softened. “They have been declared missing, and so had you until yesterday.”

I scoffed. “Missing? We just walked to the swing bridge; we were only gone a couple of hours.” 

“I’m afraid you were gone much longer than that.”

“How much longer?”

The room fell silent.

Tears began to well in my eyes. “Please tell me they are ok.”

My mother was crying again, my dad’s arms around her, covering her face.

Officer Clemons took his hat off and wiped his brow. “Son,” he began. “You have been missing for two years.”
