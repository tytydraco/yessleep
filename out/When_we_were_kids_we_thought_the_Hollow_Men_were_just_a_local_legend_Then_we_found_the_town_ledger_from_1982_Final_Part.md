The cold roots were crawling up my neck, smelling of wet clay and rotted riverbed. My dad was watching me through the tangled oak lattice, his face pale and eyes wide with a desperate, silent plea for me to run.

I had my pen pressed against page fifty one of the ledger, but I didn't write my name. Instead, I remembered what my dad said before he threw himself into the gym floor: *They aren't ghosts. They're earth and rot. And anything that dry burns.*

The ledger wasn't just a paper record. It was the physical anchor holding the hive mind together.

I didn't sign it. I pulled out my lighter, struck the wheel, and held the open flame directly to the spine of the book.

At the same time, I reached into my coat and pulled out the brass hand bell Mayor Vance had given me. I shoved the ringing bell straight into the gaping, hollow chest cavity of the main root mound while the oilskin cover caught fire.

The high pitched resonance of the brass bell vibrated through the dense wood like a tuning fork, amplifying the heat.

A sound tore through the crater that barely sounded human, a deafening, shrieking roar of a thousand dry trees snapping at once.

The oilskin cover burned violently, a blinding emerald flame spreading rapidly along the interconnected root lines. The petrified oak vines coiling around my chest convulsed, turned brittle, and shattered into showers of glowing ash.

"Dad! *NOW!*" I screamed, lunging into the burning lattice.

I grabbed my dad by his jacket collar and yanked with everything I had. The taproot anchoring him to the mound snapped with a sickening crunch. The towering Hollow Men surrounding the crater began to thrash wildly as the green fire ate through their mud and husk bodies, breaking them down into burning piles of topsoil.

The entire crater floor began to cave in on itself, swallowing the burning mound into a massive subterranean abyss.

I threw my dad's arm over my shoulder and dragged him up the crumbling clay bank. Rocks and dry dirt poured down around us like rain as the underground caverns collapsed for good. We clawed our way over the chain-link perimeter fence, tumbling into the gravel on the other side just as the ground beneath Gate 4 cratered into darkness.

It is 4:00 AM now. My dad and I are sitting in a diner booth just across the West Virginia state line.

My hands are raw, blistered, and caked in dry Ohio topsoil. Across the table, my dad is clutching a mug of black coffee with hands that are permanently scarred and stiffened from the root contact, but his heart is beating. He is alive. Nic is resting in a regional hospital down the road, and his doctors say his vocal cords will eventually recover.

Blackwood Creek is officially gone, buried under fifty feet of collapsed clay and sealed behind federal warning signs. The ledger is ash, buried at the bottom of the sinkhole.

I am writing this from my phone in the diner parking lot because I need a permanent record of what we did to cut that taproot.

If you are ever driving along Route 35 in eastern Ohio and see a fenced off valley where the air smells like stagnant marsh water and burnt corn, don't stop to look. The roots in that valley are dead, but the soil never forgets a bloodline, and we are never going back.
