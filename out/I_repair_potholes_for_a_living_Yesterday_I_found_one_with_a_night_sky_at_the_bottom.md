I've been filling potholes for the county for almost seven years. I've seen roads collapse into old drainage pipes, tree roots push through asphalt, and a sinkhole big enough to swallow the front end of a pickup.

What I saw yesterday was about eight inches across.

And I could see stars inside it.

I know how that sounds. I also know what a reflection looks like.

It was 11:40 in the morning. Clear sky, no clouds, sun directly overhead. The hole was completely dry.

My coworker Ray noticed it first.

We'd been sent to patch a stretch of county road about three miles outside town. Somebody had reported a pothole that kept damaging tires. Pretty standard complaint.

Ray parked our truck on the shoulder, put out the cones, and walked over with a shovel.

"Well, that's a weird one."

I came around the truck.

The hole was nearly perfectly round. No broken chunks around the edges, no spiderweb cracks in the surrounding pavement. Just a dark circle in otherwise decent asphalt.

I crouched beside it.

It looked like somebody had drilled straight through the road.

"Old culvert?" I asked.

"Not on the plans."

Ray poked his shovel into it.

The blade went down about two feet before he stopped.

He lifted it out, turned it over, and frowned.

"What?"

"Nothing on it."

The shovel was clean. No dirt, no mud, not even the black residue you normally get from scraping against asphalt.

He pushed it back in, this time farther.

I leaned over to look.

That's when I saw the stars.

There were maybe twenty visible, tiny white points scattered across what looked like a perfectly black sky.

At first I thought I was seeing something reflective down there. Broken glass, maybe. But when I moved my head, the stars shifted relative to the edge of the hole, exactly the way a distant landscape would.

I could see depth.

A ridiculous amount of depth.

"Ray."

"Yeah?"

"Look down there."

He leaned over.

For a few seconds he didn't say anything.

Then he stood up and looked at the actual sky.

Looked back into the hole.

"That's not right."

That was Ray's entire reaction.

He's fifty-six and has worked roads his whole adult life. When something confuses him, he gets quiet.

He took out his phone and snapped a picture.

The photo showed a black circle in the pavement. No stars.

He tried using the flash. Same thing.

I took a picture with my phone, too. Nothing.

Then Ray dropped a small stone into it.

We waited for the sound.

Nothing.

Another stone.

Nothing.

I suggested we get the truck's inspection camera. We carry a cheap waterproof one for looking into drains, although its cable is only about thirty feet long.

Ray lowered it into the hole.

On the little monitor, the walls looked normal for the first eighteen inches. Rough gray material, some exposed aggregate. Then the camera came out of the bottom of the road.

Not into dirt.

Not into a pipe.

Into open black space.

The camera was pointed straight down. There was nothing to illuminate. The cable kept feeding out, foot after foot, until the entire thirty feet was gone.

The image showed the end of the cable hanging in darkness.

But standing beside the hole, looking down with my own eyes, I could still see the stars.

Ray slowly pulled the camera back.

"Call Dennis."

Dennis is our supervisor.

He answered on the fourth ring.

Ray explained that we'd found a possible void under the road and needed somebody to inspect it.

He didn't mention the stars.

Dennis said to barricade the spot and he'd send somebody over after lunch.

We should have left it there.

Instead, I did something incredibly stupid.

I took one of our fifty-pound bags of cold patch from the truck, cut it open, and started pouring.

I figured there had to be some physical bottom. Maybe we were looking through a weird optical effect in an underground cavity. I wanted to see material collect.

The asphalt fell straight through.

Not down into a visible cavity. It simply vanished past the lower edge of the road.

I poured the rest.

No impact. No pile forming. No sound.

Ray was standing beside me.

"Try another."

I opened a second bag.

Same thing.

We stood there looking into the hole.

The stars were still visible.

Then something moved across them.

Not a shooting star.

Imagine holding your hand in front of a window at night. The stars behind your fingers disappear, and then reappear when you move your hand.

That was what happened.

A dark shape passed across the little patch of sky.

Except it was impossibly far away.

And it was moving toward us.

Ray saw it too.

He grabbed the back of my vest and pulled me away.

"Get in the truck."

We had taken maybe three steps when something hit the road behind us.

A small piece of gravel bounced past my left boot.

Then another.

Then about a dozen more.

They were coming out of the hole.

I turned around.

The rocks we'd dropped earlier were scattered across the pavement. I recognized the first one because it had a stripe of white paint on it.

They were completely dry.

One of the bags we'd emptied had also somehow returned.

Not the bag itself. The asphalt.

A mound of loose black material was slowly building around the hole, as though someone beneath the road was pushing it upward.

Ray started swearing.

I watched one piece of gravel lift out of the darkness, hang in the air for a second, and drop onto the pavement.

Then another.

Then the whole mound shifted.

Something beneath it was pushing.

The road made a noise like a sheet of plywood bending.

A crack shot across the lane.

Ray ran for the truck.

I followed him, but my right foot caught on the edge of a cone and I went down on one knee.

The asphalt underneath my left hand was moving.

Not shaking.

Moving.

It felt soft, almost elastic. My palm sank into the pavement by maybe half an inch before I yanked it free.

I looked back toward the hole.

It wasn't eight inches wide anymore.

It was closer to three feet.

The edge of it was smooth. Still perfectly circular.

And down inside, far beneath us, the dark shape had gotten bigger.

I couldn't see what it was. But there was something about its movement that I understood immediately.

It was getting closer to the opening.

Ray ran back and grabbed me under the arm.

We stumbled to the truck and climbed in.

He threw it into reverse so hard the transmission made a noise I've never heard before.

We backed down the road until we reached the intersection, then stopped.

Neither of us spoke for a while.

Finally Ray said, "You saw that?"

"Yeah."

"The road moved."

"Yeah."

He pressed both hands against the steering wheel.

"That's not a sinkhole."

I called Dennis again.

I told him the road was actively collapsing and that he needed to close it immediately.

This time he listened.

We waited at the intersection until a county vehicle arrived. Then we went back with the supervisor and two other guys.

The hole was gone.

Completely.

The surface was intact, except for a dark circular patch of asphalt about three feet across.

It looked freshly paved.

Dennis got out and walked over.

He touched it with the toe of his boot.

"Did you guys fill this?"

"No."

"Well, somebody did."

He crouched down and pressed his hand against the patch.

"Still warm."

Ray looked at me.

Nobody said anything.

Dennis checked the road around it and said it was safe enough to reopen after a more thorough inspection.

He gave us both a lecture about reporting road failures immediately instead of trying to repair them ourselves.

I didn't argue.

I was watching the black patch.

There was a tiny white point near its center.

At first I thought it was a chip of reflective aggregate.

Then it disappeared.

And another one appeared a few inches away.

I grabbed Ray's sleeve.

"Don't look directly at it."

He didn't ask why.

We got in the truck.

I took the rest of the day off.

I drove home, showered, and spent about two hours sitting on the couch trying to convince myself that some combination of fumes, heat, and exhaustion had caused the whole thing.

Around six, Ray called.

He said he'd checked the inspection camera footage.

Everything was normal until the camera passed through the bottom of the pavement.

Then the screen went black.

But the audio hadn't stopped.

I didn't even know that camera recorded audio.

He said he'd turned the volume all the way up.

There was a constant low rumbling, like traffic heard from far underground.

And beneath it, something else.

Scraping.

Slow, heavy scraping.

Like somebody dragging a shovel over concrete.

Then Ray said something that made me get up and turn on every light in my apartment.

"The scraping stops when you stop feeding the cable down. Starts again when you lower it."

I asked him what that meant.

He was quiet.

Then he said, "I think whatever was down there was trying to get underneath the camera."

I told him to send me the footage.

He said he would.

He hasn't.

I called him three times last night. No answer.

At about eleven, I noticed something on my kitchen floor.

A little black circle.

Smaller than a dime, right beside the refrigerator.

I thought it was a burn mark. I tried rubbing it off with my thumb.

My thumb went into it.

I only pushed maybe a quarter of an inch before I felt open space beneath the tile.

I pulled my hand away so fast I scraped my knuckle on the floor.

I got a flashlight.

The circle wasn't a stain.

There was a hole in the tile.

A perfectly round hole with smooth edges.

And when I turned off the kitchen light and leaned over it, I could see stars.

I left the apartment.

Slept in my car outside a twenty-four-hour grocery store.

This morning I called my landlord and told him there was a structural problem with the kitchen floor. I didn't know what else to say.

He sent a maintenance guy.

I met him outside and followed him upstairs.

The hole was still there.

About an inch across now.

I could see it from the doorway.

The maintenance guy thought it was an old pipe opening somebody had covered with new flooring.

He got down on one knee, shone his flashlight inside, and said he couldn't see anything.

I told him not to put anything in it.

He laughed and said he wasn't planning to.

Then he stood up, went to his toolbox, and got a tape measure.

I grabbed his wrist before he could lower it into the hole.

He got angry.

I don't blame him.

I started talking about the road, about the stars, about the gravel coming back out.

He stared at me like I was having some kind of breakdown.

Then he packed his tools and left.

I don't think my landlord is going to renew my lease.

That's the least of my problems.

About an hour ago I went back into the kitchen.

The hole is now almost four inches wide.

The floor around it is perfectly level.

No cracks. No sagging. Nothing to suggest the tile is breaking.

It's just disappearing.

The stars are getting brighter.

I can make out six of them now, arranged in a kind of crooked line.

I've been watching them from the doorway.

Something keeps passing in front of them.

It's too dark to see what it is.

But I think I finally understand what happened on the road.

We didn't fill a pothole.

We fed something.

And now it knows where I live.

I was about to post this when I heard the noise again.

That slow scraping Ray described.

It's coming from under my kitchen floor.

It stopped a minute ago.

Then I heard something heavy being set down.

A few seconds later, the first piece of gravel came up through the hole.

It bounced twice across the tile and rolled under my refrigerator.

I recognized the white stripe on it.

It's the same stone Ray dropped into the road yesterday.

I don't know how it got here.

I don't know how much of the floor is still solid.

I'm standing in the hallway with my keys and phone, trying to decide whether I can get past the kitchen to reach the back door.

The scraping has started again.

This time it's coming from two different places.
