There is not a day that goes by when I do not hear Eli’s bones breaking in my dreams. For twenty-five years, I have heard the screams, his crying, his bloody coughing. And I hear the bellowing of that hairy, stinking thing. Yellow eyes against a black night, the sounds of the forest suddenly falling silent. There is no therapy that can erase the dreams. Perhaps I do not want that anymore. I do not know if I could even fall asleep without the screams of my best friend. They are all I have left of him. But still, the doctor says I talk too little to others and do not share my worries. I finally have to tell someone about it. About the thing between the trees and my memories of the woods.

Part 1 – Screams

I met Eli in second grade. I was the loud, somewhat annoying class clown. My motto was: If ten jokes bomb and one gets a laugh, that is a good ratio. My teachers, however, did not appreciate this motto at all. So they moved me away from my friends and into the back row, next to the new kid, whom nobody really wanted to talk to yet. With my schoolbag in my hand, I studied this strange boy. Small, even for our age, with red, completely tangled curls and a hole-ridden polo shirt that would have suited a bankrupt yuppie better than a seven-year-old. The boy looked up at me.

“Are you going to sit down, or are you going to keep staring at me for a little while longer?”

Surprisingly, his tone was not hostile. It actually sounded like a sincere question. Thrown off, I shook my head and mumbled:

“Oh, uh, sorry, I mean, sure.”

And I sat down next to him.

“Eli,” he said, extending his small hand toward me.

“Daniel,” I replied, shaking it.

“You’re very loud,” Eli said, smiling at me somewhat awkwardly.

“Uh, thanks?” I answered.

“No, I mean, I wouldn’t dare do that. I can’t do it like that, you know. Telling jokes loudly.”

He seemed so dejected and shyly looked down at his desk. I grinned.

“I’m sure I can help you with that!”

“Really?” he beamed.

We became our teachers’ worst nightmare. We became best of friends.

Two years later, Eli and I heard the screams coming from the woods. It was one of the first days of autumn that were no longer quite so warm. Eli’s house and mine stood on opposite edges of a small wooded area near the city. Yet the two corners of our little town could hardly have been more different. My parents lived in a pretty little place near our school. Parks, terraced houses, and elderly gentlemen walking ugly purebred dogs were part of everyday life. But if you followed the little path along the edge of the woods, you would reach the other side of our town. Apartment buildings and unrenovated houses from another era stood crookedly side by side. Only a few families still lived in this part of town, and those who did usually had no other choice. Eli’s father was one of them.

He never wanted to tell me what had happened to his mother. All I knew was that he lived alone with his father. His dad was a cold and taciturn man. A failed author who had also worked as a journalist for a time, he could barely make ends meet for himself and his son. But in the past, Eli said to me, his father had told the most magnificent stories. Of strange monsters and magical worlds. Sad memories, funny adventures, frightening fairy tales. But now his father had grown silent. Now he only stared at his notepad or at the bottom of a bottle. 

Though he would never have admitted it himself, Eli had the same gift as his dad. When we stayed over at each other’s houses after a long night of video games, he would tell the same fantastic stories. Sometimes he made me laugh until I was in stitches; other times, I wanted to hide under the covers without my friend noticing that I had nearly wet myself. 

Eli himself, however, was not the bravest. Whenever we were at my house and it was time for him to ride home, he would ask me to come along for a little while longer. He wanted to show me something or tell me something. In truth, he simply did not want to ride his bike alone along the old forest path. Even at nine years old, I understood him well, and I always accompanied him. Just as I did on the day we heard the scream from the woods.

“Did you hear that?” Eli asked nervously.

He put one foot down from his bicycle and stopped when we heard the sound.

“I think so,” I replied, peering into the undergrowth.

We had been riding along the bumpy path near the woods just moments earlier when a guttural, deafening croak came from the undergrowth. The sun had just set, and only faint orange scraps of light shone through the treetops. Now we stared into the woods, gripping our handlebars tightly, ready to set off again at a moment’s notice.

There it was again. Almost agonizing, with all its might, a shrill and simultaneously rumbling groan erupted not far from us.

“RIEEEHHHHHHHHHHHHH!” it rang out from the woods.

“Daniel, Daniel, let’s go!”

Eli sounded completely panicked. He got back on his bicycle, on the verge of riding away. Yet he continued to stare at me with wide-open eyes and could not bring himself to flee without me. I was afraid too, without a doubt. But something held me back. A poisonous curiosity, rooted in a child’s belief in his own invulnerability.

“I just want to take one more quick look,” I whispered, turning my head ever so slightly toward the sound.

With my eyes narrowed, I tried to see in the darkness. From a bush no more than three meters away, small, yellow eyes stared back at me.

“Oh, fuck!” I screamed, and threw myself onto my bike with all my strength, shouting in Eli’s direction:

“Go, go, go, go!”

We rode as fast as we could. The screams were right behind us, coming closer, growing louder. Then, suddenly, the horrible sounds stopped abruptly just as we reached the light of a streetlamp, not far from Eli’s apartment. We looked around frantically. Had it really been following us?

“What the hell was that?” Eli gasped after we had made sure nobody was chasing us.

Dry tears were visible on his face. I looked down at myself. There was a wet patch in my crotch, and my ankles had been scraped by my pedals. I whimpered.

“Can I call my parents from your place and stay over tonight, Eli?” I asked, staring at the ground in embarrassment.

Eli saw the state I was in and placed a hand on my shoulder.

“Of course, man,” he smiled beneath his sweaty red curls.

We went to his place. Neither of us slept that night. We both stared at the ceiling. Eli was left with lingering fear, while I simmered with anger.

Part 2 – Tracks

My father was different from Eli’s. Eli’s father was cold, polite, and sad when he drank. My father was the opposite. Loud, funny, and uncontrollably hot-tempered. My entire childhood was marked by uncertainty and stress. One moment, my dad was the funniest person in the world. He would pick up my little sister and me, whirl us around, and play with us for hours. But then there were the other days. The days when something went wrong at the practice. The days when a plan failed. Sometimes all it took was stubbing his toe. Then his other side would come out. He would scream like a madman. Throw his keys against the wall and swear like a sailor. But most of all, he could not stand self-pity. Years later, I confronted him about his behavior once. He admitted, with regret, that it had come from his own father. He had never known anything else. Whenever my dad was already stressed and we cried or whined, he would come dangerously close to us, his face bright red. In his calm, trembling, eerie voice, he would ask:

“Are you really going to pull this right now? Hmm, Danny? After my day?”

Then he would suddenly raise his voice.

“How about you stop acting like a little wimp for once?! Be a man, Daniel! Stop whining! Face your problems and don’t cry like a girl!”

That night, as I stared at Eli’s ceiling and wore his fresh pair of sweatpants, I heard my father screaming. I had not stood my ground. I ran away. But that was not how it was supposed to be. Not this time. And as the sun slowly rose, I came up with a plan.

The next morning, I said goodbye to Eli and climbed onto my bike. I still felt uneasy when the old forest road appeared before me, but I thought of my father again and started pedaling. It had rained a little during the night, so our tracks in the mud had almost completely disappeared. But in one spot, you could still make something out. There were two tracks marked by the treads of our bicycle tires. Behind, between, and in front of them, however, were deep, sharp imprints left by something heavy with small, strange feet.

We really had been followed. This only strengthened my resolve. When I got home, I searched through my dad’s drawer while he was already at work. He had shown it to me once when I was younger. From his time in the Army. At the very bottom, in the lowest compartment, I found it. I pulled out a long, heavy survival knife. Almost as long as my forearm. Whatever it was that had screamed in those woods, I would not run away anymore.

A few days later, Eli and I were sitting together at school. During every break, we whispered to each other almost nonstop.

“What do you think it was?”

“Maybe a werewolf?”

“Hmm, but it sounded as if it was in pain.”

“Aren’t werewolves too strong to get hurt?”

“Maybe there was a werewolf hunter out there?”

“We would’ve heard him, wouldn’t we?”

“True.”

Our teachers kept reprimanding us whenever class began, and we postponed our conversation.

After school, I pulled Eli behind the wall of a house and opened my schoolbag. The handle of the knife was visible inside a leather sheath. Eli’s eyes widened.

“That belongs to your father! Did you—”

“Borrow it,” I interrupted him.

“Eli, how about we go into the woods this weekend?”

Eli took a step back.

“Into the woods? Are you insane? Why?!”

My expression darkened.

“Eli, we ride along this path every day. It’s our woodland trail. We can’t just let something take that away from us! Then we won’t be able to play anymore, or sleep over at each other’s houses, and all that. Our parents certainly aren’t going to drive us around all the time.”

“Whatever that thing in the woods is, we’re going in there and scaring the hell out of it with this knife. Then it won’t chase us anymore.”

Eli looked at me skeptically and considered it.

“Shouldn’t we maybe tell your father about it instead?”

“No!” I interrupted him a little too quickly. “We’ll handle this ourselves. Just like in your stories.”

“Together or not at all, Eli.”

After a long pause, he nodded.

“All right, Daniel.”

He raised his arm for a handshake, smiling uncertainly.

“Together!”

Part 3 – Knife

This is my fourth attempt at writing this. It is not that I cannot remember. Almost every detail is so vivid before my eyes. I fear I simply cannot put into words adequately how I felt up there in the tree, as my world was torn apart in a bloody upheaval.

It was late afternoon on a Saturday when Eli and I went into the woods. We left our bicycles at the edge of the forest. We told my parents that I was staying over at Eli’s, and Eli’s father, as always, did not give a damn. We had a tent, cans of food, flashlights, water, and, above all, my father’s knife. Both of us grew increasingly uneasy. Shadows flitted across the trees at the corners of our eyes. Rustling in the treetops, rustling in the bushes. Eli had brought a hammer from home. We both wore our weapons tucked into our waistbands like knights on a crusade. We clutched the handles tightly.

“And if the thing shows itself? What do we do then?” Eli whispered.

I tried to sound commanding but failed miserably.

“Then we’ll yell at it, swing the hammer and knife at it, and the monster will run off.”

“And what if it’s nice?” Eli suggested.

I thought for a moment.

“Then maybe it wouldn’t hurt to negotiate with it.”

I pointed ahead.

“There’s the clearing. We’ll camp there.”

We pitched our little tent and wolfed down our first can of food. With our sleeping bags on our laps, we sat together, and Eli made up one of his magical stories. To this day, I cannot forgive myself for forgetting what it was about. Whenever I try to remember now, that part is a black hole. Because of what happened after Eli finished telling it.

There was rustling at the edge of our clearing. We jumped to our feet immediately. Knife in hand, I waved it around as the last rays of sunlight disappeared from the clearing. Eli stayed close to me. Even in the darkness, I could see him trembling. Something moved in the undergrowth. To our left, to our right, in front of us, and behind us.

“There’s more than one monster,” Eli whimpered.

Just as I was about to reply, I felt something wet against my leg. In a panic, I kicked wildly. Something small and heavy was hurled through the air in Eli’s direction. It made a shrill, whistling sound.

“There’s something here! There’s something on my leg!” I yelled.

Eli noticed something land beside him. We both pointed our flashlights at the thing.

It was a small, wild, baby boar. Under different circumstances, it would have been incredibly cute. Frightened and probably injured by my kick, it crouched down. It stared in our direction in a panic.

“A pig?” Eli asked in confusion. “The monster was a pig?”

“RIEEEHHHHHHHHHHHHH!”

We froze. At the edge of the clearing, between two trees, something enormous moved in the darkness. Slowly, paralyzed with fear, we turned our flashlights in its direction. Its mother was there. A wild boar whose shoulders reached our chests stood snorting less than ten meters away from us. Behind it was a group of little piglets. Its fur was matted and tangled. An extensive fold of belly skin hung between the animal’s legs. Its expressionless eyes glowed yellow. I dropped the knife.

“Eli, run!” I screamed, sprinting toward the nearest tree.

Eli, who took a moment longer than I did to recover from the shock, ran after me. A roaring grunt was right behind us. Small, heavy hooves drove the massive pig forward like a battering ram. Pure adrenaline propelled me up the tree. My hands were scraped by the bark, my fingernails bleeding as I climbed upward like a wild animal. I looked down. Everything happened in a split second. Eli reached his hand up toward me, his eyes opened unnaturally wide. I wanted to reach out to him. Then something struck him in the back with tremendous force. His initially shrill scream was immediately interrupted by a gurgling sound. A black, furious mass rammed my best friend against the tree I was crouched in, again and again. White tusks glinted in the moonlight. It smelled of tallow and blood. I heard cracking. Whether it was the tree trunk or bones, I could not and did not want to know. I screamed and cried. I threw pinecones and sticks at the animal, but it paid no attention to me. Only when Eli stopped moving did the matted monster trot away. I lost consciousness on the broad branch where I lay. I was still there when the park rangers and our parents found us.

Part 4 – Brother

The time that followed is a hazy blur. I lay in the hospital longer than my wounds required. When I woke up, my mother wanted to explain to me as gently as possible that Eli was no longer with us. But she would not have needed to say anything. I already knew. They tried to explain to me that a wild boar with piglets could be extremely protective and dangerous, but I did not care. I saw Eli’s father once. At the funeral. I can no longer remember a single detail of the ceremony. Only the father’s face. He had never looked healthy or happy. Now he was nothing but a shell. Black eyes in black sockets. Yellowish-white skin, like the tusks of the creature.

We moved away shortly afterward. I was more than happy about that. My father stopped screaming after the incident. Eli’s father disappeared after the funeral. Sometimes I like to imagine that he could still have turned his life around. That he had started over. I know better. But I allow myself that small comfort. The nightmares were terrible at first, but they quickly became a habit. Sometimes, however, I see more than just blood, bones, and fur. Sometimes I hear Eli. Calling my name. Telling stories in front of the tent. Riding our bicycles.

Whenever I pass through my old town these days, I often visit Eli’s grave. Those are the moments that truly bring me some measure of peace. I stand in front of the little stone and have to smile. I have to smile because I always think to myself: If heaven is only half as beautiful as it was in your stories, then, Eli, brother, there is nothing any of us needs to fear.

Thank you for listening to me.
