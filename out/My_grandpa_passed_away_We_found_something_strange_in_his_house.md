A few months ago, my grandpa passed away. This weekend, I drove up to get a few things from the house.

It hurt seeing the house so empty, so quiet. At any point I thought I’d hear the familiar *Hello, pumpkin* and turn around and see my grandpa standing there.

*Make this quick.*

I headed into the living room, to the painting of a beach he’d left me in his will. But as I took it off the wall, I noticed something.

There was a little folded strip of paper, yellowed with age, taped to the wall behind it.

*No way.* I flipped it open.

My eyes burned with tears as I recognized my grandpa’s squiggly handwriting.

**Look in the hall bathroom closet.**

When I was little, my grandpa and I used to play treasure hunt. He’d write clues on a piece of paper, like “Look in the cabinet,” where there’d be another clue. And that would lead to another, and another… until I found a prize at the end. A lot of times I didn’t even finish the game, since there were so many clues.

I guess this was part of one I’d never finished.

*What if the other clues are still there?* I shook my head. That would be crazy. It had been, what, twenty years? Maybe more. Still cool to find one of them, though.

I tucked it in my pocket.

And then I went upstairs.

I swung the door to the bathroom closet open and scanned the wire shelves. I was just about to give up when I saw it—something on the floor. I bent over and grabbed it.

**Look under the bed in the green room.**

“Ah, shit,” I muttered. The bed had been moved out a while ago—it had been willed to one of my other cousins. There was almost no chance the clue was still there.

I walked back across the hall to the green room. That had been where I’d always stayed, when Mom dropped me off for a weekend in the summer. For a fleeting moment I could feel the golden rays coming through the window, smell Grandpa’s spaghetti on the stove, hear him whistling to himself downstairs. I pushed the memory down and swung the door open.

My jaw dropped.

The bed was still there.

It looked just as it did when I’d visited. Yellow quilt, tucked neatly under the mattress. Fluffy pillows leaning against the dark wooden headboard. *I thought I was the last one to come pick up my stuff. It’s still here?*

For a second I sat on it, the springs squeaking under my weight. Imagining the past twenty years washing away, all the pain, all the loss of innocence. Imagining I was playing treasure hunt again, in the middle of summer while the sauce simmered on the stove.

Then I got on my hands and knees and pressed my cheek to the floor.

There, in the middle of the shadowy space under the bed—further than I would think Grandpa could reach—was folded slip of paper.

**Look in the fridge.**

Like a little kid, I ran down the stairs at top speed and yanked the fridge open.

It’d been unplugged for months, and when I opened the door, a sour smell seeped out. It was dark and warm inside, and I turned on my phone flashlight, sweeping across the shelves, searching.

*It’s not here.*

My heart dropped. I pushed two jars of (presumably rotten) jam aside, glass clattering, as I frantically checked.

*It’s not here!*

But it made sense. Behind the mirror, in the bathroom closet, under the bed—those were all relatively inaccessible places. But the fridge wasn’t. There’s no way a note would stay there for twenty years. Even if it did, the moisture would have probably disintegrated it.

I slammed the door shut and suddenly I was crying. I slid down to sit on the floor, hiding my face. *I wish you were still here.*

*What I would do, for one hour more.*

I finally wiped away my tears and began to stand.

That’s when I saw it.

The tiny corner of a yellowed paper, sticking out from under the fridge.

*No. There’s no way.*

Hands shaking, I picked it up and flipped it open. In Grandpa’s handwriting, it read:

**Look under the basement stairs.**

I paused.

Grandpa never wanted me in the basement. The few times I went down there, he yelled at me for touching his bikes.

He’d written a clue once, asking me to go down there? Really?

I walked into the hall and glanced at the basement door. The chain was drawn, the doorknob rusty. *Just go down and see if it’s there.*

*What are you afraid of?*

*Um, everything. What if there’s some squatter down there?* I wasn’t going down there, as a woman, all alone. Just in case. I pulled out my phone and called my cousin Sam, one of the few grandkids who’d stayed in the area.

He was at the door a half hour later, wearing his fedora, the one he wore all through high school. “Aren’t you a little old for that?” I teased.

“No,” he replied with a frown.

“Okay, whatever. Let’s go.”

Sam went ahead of me, pulling out the chain lock with a *clink.* “Ladies first!”

I glared at him and started down the steps.

The stairs led into the darkness, must and damp rising up to meet us. I flipped the switch. Nothing happened. “The lights don’t work down there?”

“Guess not.”

I pulled out my phone, turned on the flashlight, and continued down. *Under the stairs.* The wood creaked under my feet and I cringed at the musty smell. *This was dumb. What’s going to be at the end of this, anyway? Some expired M&Ms?*

My feet hit the cement and I panned the flashlight around. It was empty, the bicycles and other stuff long gone. I turned, panning my flashlight around.

There it was.

A little slip of paper, sitting in the hollow under the stairs.

I reached for it and flipped it open.

All the blood drained out of my face.

**Look behind you.**

*What the fuck?*

*Grandpa wouldn’t write something like this. He wouldn’t.*

“Sam?” I called, my voice thready. It suddenly occurred to me how silent it was. No footsteps, nothing.

I felt paralyzed. But I slowly, *slowly* forced my head around.

No one was there.

Not even Sam.

But there was a slip of paper, right behind me on the ground.

*That wasn’t there before.*

*Was it?*

*What the fuck?*

I bent down and picked it up. My heart dropped as I read three words, *not* in my grandpa’s handwriting.

**Welcome Home, Pumpkin.**

“*SAM!”* I shouted. “Where are you?”

I ran up the stairs and tried the door. Locked. *What the fuck? Why would Sam lock me down here? This doesn’t make any—*

Then I heard it.

The musical notes of the doorbell, echoing upstairs.

Followed by Sam’s voice. “I’m here! Sorry I’m late!”

Every muscle in my body froze.

*If that’s Sam…*

*Who the hell let me in here?!*

“I’m in here!” I shouted, running up the stairs, banging on the door. “Help! Sam, *help!”* Then I stopped. My phone—duh. I dialed him and brought it up to my ear, the flashlight shining on the door, leaving the basement pitch black.

It went straight to voicemail.

*“SAM!”* I screamed.

No reception.

I tried calling again. Screaming and banging. Ten minutes later, I heard the rumble of an engine as Sam’s car pulled out of the driveway.

*Someone will come down here and find me.*

*They have to.*

*John will call the police when I don’t come home tonight and they’ll let me out.*

The sky darkened outside the tiny windows near the ceiling. The stars came out. I shivered on the floor, cold, terrified. Still no reception. I held the note in my hands, staring at the *Welcome Home.* Holding the paper made my fingers tingle, burn. When I finally let go of it, my fingertips were covered in angry red blisters, bleeding at the tips.

And there is something in the corner of the basement, now.

It looks like the bed from upstairs. My childhood bed. With the dark headboard and the yellow quilt. It smells like summer sunshine, cutting through the must, and homemade spaghetti.

And there is someone standing in the corner. Something wearing my grandpa’s overalls, facing the corner of the basement. He’s whistling. Like he always did. But there isn’t a song. It’s just a mash of notes, randomly falling together.

I’ve been down here twelve hours now. I’ve been able to somehow access a few websites, including this one, so I could post. But I still can’t call John, can’t call 911. And soon, I’ll be out of battery.

The thing in the corner has taken a few steps closer.

I’m so tired. The bed looks so soft, and smells just like home. I might lie down for a little while, just while I wait for help to come.

It’s better than this cold floor.
