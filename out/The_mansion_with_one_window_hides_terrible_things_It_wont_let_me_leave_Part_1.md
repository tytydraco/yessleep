Any artist worth their salt knows to die before they spoil. 

It’s not apathy: it’s math. The longer you remain, the more time you have to create. The more time you have to create, the more likely it is that you’ll produce a resounding failure. Death, however, provides stasis. You can’t tarnish your reputation by churning out shit-work if you’re six feet under. I didn’t love where that realization left me, but the logic seemed to track. 

My studio was quiet that night.

I mean, hell, it was quiet *every* night - most long-abandoned sawmills are - but it was a different sort of quiet. Punctuated. Impatient. No chirping cicadas in the grass, no cooing breeze through the surrounding trees, no leaves crunching under mammal paws. Nothing outside dared to move. The bugs and the wind and the deer stayed still, watching from within the forest, waiting for me to decide what was next.

Life, 

or death.

Lying on the dust-caked floor, I squinted my right eye, aiming at the canvas hanging from the rafters. It was a simple test; a way for me to communicate with the soul of a voiceless universe. If I could pierce *Ameliae,* if I could get a dart into it from the floor, that would be my sign to keep going, keep painting, keep trying to recreate that profound, career-defining success.

If I ran out of darts, if I couldn’t throw one high enough, well,

I wasn’t one to overstay my welcome.

I launched another dart into the air. Light glinted off the silver needle, cutting thin shadows out of the yellow haze emanating from the massive, oval-shaped bulb that served as the mill’s microcosmic sun.

The dart’s ascent stalled. 

Then, without fanfare, it turned and began falling. 

My heart sank into my gut. 

Seconds later, I felt it clink against the heel of my boot - a pathetic whimper of a noise. Barely even made it halfway. 

Evidently, I’d strung my target too high up.

Earlier that evening, I’d scoured the mill, digging through dusty piles of discarded equipment and opening storage closets I hadn’t yet bothered to explore, searching for a ladder and some rope. I found both, albeit in less-than-ideal condition. The wooden ladder was missing a few steps. Starving rats had chewed through the rope, leaving it threadbare. I wrapped a sinewy noose around the painting, creaked the ladder open, and began climbing. The penultimate step shattered under my weight, sending fragments of brittle wood clattering to the factory floor a hundred feet below. I almost let myself be carried along by the momentum.

*Almost.*

If I was going to die - if I *needed* to die - an accident wasn’t the *right* death.

So, I turned my nose up at the reaper and gripped the rails tightly. Ancient splinters sank their jagged teeth into my palms. I winced, but I didn’t fall. I lowered my foot onto the next-highest step. It groaned, but it held. Once steady, I threw the end of the rope over a nearby rafter and watched the painting swing silent in the harsh light, an angel with makeshift wings, lynched and heavenly.

Twelve darts, twelve chances. 

After eleven failures, I cradled the twelfth dart in my palm with reverence. I rolled it over tiny splotches of dried blood that had oozed from the buried splinters as I stared daggers at *Ameliae*. It’s strange to look upon your own work with such vitriol, but, truly, I hated it.

I hated it because it represented a time in my life that had passed - a time when I felt loved and awake and virile.

I hated it because it made me obscenely wealthy, and I didn’t like what wealth had done to my soul. *One* measly painting sold to *one* overwrought art collector, and now, I had more money than I knew what to do with. The *Ameliae* hanging from the rafters wasn’t the original. No, it was a remake - something that I made from memory to prove I could still produce art worth selling. After all was said and done, I didn’t get a single offer. 

Didn’t get a single offer I deemed worthy, at least.

As I stared at the rejected copy, gleaming smugly in the artificial sun, it felt like my eyes began to boil over - thick white chowder bubbling around my sockets, charring bone and flaying nerves.

What had I done differently?

Why couldn’t I recreate its success?

What was wrong with *me*?

That’s what I hated the most about *Ameliae*.

Even though I’d made it, I didn’t understand it. Didn’t understand why people adored it, didn’t understand what I’d done so right, didn’t understand what in God’s name separated it from the hundreds of other paintings I’d poured myself into. Before *Ameliae,* I was just a small piece of Philadelphia’s underground, enjoying my meager achievements, feeling the satisfaction of stepwise improvement. Each piece was just that little bit cleaner. Success was surely a matter of *when*, not *if*. With enough patience, with enough effort, I’d unlock some mythologic potential. The world would glimpse my work and come to embrace me, the whole of me, for who I was and what I was able to do.

To that end, making *Ameliae* felt like just another tiny step.

The line work was sharper, sure. Yes, it was surreal without devolving into absurdity: the insects and their crystal teeth, the woman’s face stretched beyond her skull, skin flat and taut like the surface of a trampoline. It was better than some of my other pieces, undeniably, but it wasn’t great, and it definitely was *not* my magnum opus.

*“Prophetic, neatly apocalyptic, and hauntingly timeless,”* one critic said.

But *was* it?

*“Lev has proven himself the preeminent master of the neosurrealist movement,”* claimed another.

But did I? Did I *really*?

*“To say I’m excited to see where he goes next would be an egregious, unforgivable understatement.”*

I’m not sure if anyone actually said this - still, the sentiment stands. I felt immense, unyielding pressure to follow up *Ameliae* with something equally beloved. 

I’d made twelve pieces in the time since *Ameliae’s* success. Each felt better than the last. Crisper. More emotionally refined. Poetry bled from each precise, calculated brushstroke. I loved each of them dearly. Twelve pieces, twelve commercial and critical failures. I keep those paintings away from me. They lurk far from the light of that massive single bulb, nailed to the walls or propped up against old machinery, cloaked in dense shadow, gathering dust.

Because there is no deeper humiliation than a career bookended by failure. 

If I couldn’t recreate the success achieved with *Ameliae*, that meant I had no part in its success to begin with. I wasn’t the maestro behind a timeless piece of art.

I was just lucky.

Without thinking, I fired the last dart heavenward.

*Perhaps I could try a different medium* \- I thought.

The dart flew higher. I felt an atomically small speck of warmth float around my chest. 

*Maybe I just need to practice more. -* I considered.

I tried to hold on to the warmth, feeding it oxygen, praying it’d grow.

*Fuck it. I’ll try again. And if it’s not received well, if it’s not beloved, that shouldn’t make a damn bit of difference, right?*

The dart hesitated.

And fell.

I shut my eyes and awaited the inevitable.

The needle tip rebounded off my stomach.

The universe, in its cruel but infinite wisdom, had spoken.

With a weak, bittersweet smile, I opened my eyes and stood up. Pitter-patters of relief thrummed in my head like a resolving migraine as I walked off the factory floor. I stumbled around in the darkness until I located my supplies. Specifically, the paint I’d employed for the original *Ameliae*. It was intended to function as a totem or a charm, something I carried with me because I believed it held magic. 

Now, it had a new purpose.

In the shadows of the mill, I doused my body with those divine colors. 

The glacial blue-white of the woman’s dress. 

The glittery orange of the insects’ eyes. 

The deep blacks and the blushing pinks and the bare whites; I let every last drop cascade over my tired flesh until I was drenched in its broken magic.

I stepped back into the light, paint dripping off my legs, kaleidoscopic footprints cataloging what were to be my last few movements in this life. Tears welled, but I chuckled. There was a schizophrenic elegance to it all that I couldn’t help but appreciate.

Under the light of the bulb and the faint penumbra of my false *Ameliae*, I’d become the art. Unlucky number thirteen. When I was done, there’d be no jury to determine my value, no critic putting their thumb up or down from the sidelines.

The stale oil, thankfully, had not dried out completely.

It was still flammable.

I struck the match. 

The head became engulfed in flame, a lovely shade of red-yellow that I did not utilize in the making of *Ameliae*.

What more perfect final ingredient could there be?

I dropped the match onto my arm.

The paint caught quickly.

And somewhere above, miles above my burning body and my narcissistic worry and the false *Ameliae* and the massive bulb and the lonely mill, above the sky itself,

Something noticed me.

The heat was suffocating.

Not sure what I expected; it was Nevada in the summer, after all. Death Valley was a proverbial stone’s throw away. Moving this far south meant suffering through some oppressive heat was a guarantee, not a threat.

Lukewarm air puffed from the old sedan’s A/C like burps of gas escaping a newly made corpse. A thin film of sticky sweat gathered over my scarred skin. My body felt slick, uncomfortably gelatinous. I kept asking if there was a way to make the back seats colder. To his credit, the taxi driver seemed sincerely apologetic about the state of his vehicle - this goddamned oven on wheels - but the tone of his voice betrayed annoyance. I caught him rolling his eyes in the rear-view mirror more than once.

I didn’t hold that against him.

Despite the temperature being firmly stuck in the triple digits, I’d lumbered into his car wearing ankle-length chinos, a long-sleeved sweat jacket zipped tight at my collarbone, a cloth face mask, and a pair of tinted Ray-Bans. In other words, full hipster regalia. I assume he viewed me as just another hapless, tone-deaf Yankee whining about the inhospitable temperature, how pitifully different it was compared to NYC, either too dumb or too stubborn to shed my veritable cocoon of stuffy fabric to cool down. 

Hell, if I were him, I’d be annoyed, too.

The heat and the judgment were preferable to revealing what lay beneath, though.

So, I took as deep a breath as I could manage, grabbed a complimentary bottle of tepid water from the car’s middle console, chugged half of it, and tried to relax, resting my hooded head against the window, watching the desert quietly pass by as we departed from the air port.

Nevada wasn’t like I’d imagined. 

Hundreds of velvety green cacti lined the interstate, springing up from the sand like the many fingers of some long-buried plant god lingering inches below the sediment. I drank in the lush flowers and scampering lizards with a smile brewing under my facemask, but the expression never really bloomed. There was just something missing. It all looked…I don’t know…disappointingly empty. People skittered from shadow to shadow, avoiding the sun; human cockroaches vying for the cool safety of darkness. Reminded me of the way Vonnegut described Dresden after it’d been firebombed: vacant, moon-like, stripped of life. That said, my evaluation may have been biased, my brain artificially cynical after forgetting to take my painkillers on time.

Nothing like the sensation of your body pulsing with anguish to sap reality of its color.

I pulled the pill bottle from my pocket, untwisted the lid, and poured a generous helping of narcotics into my damp palm. The ration looked a little *too* generous, so I re-deposited about half the tabs. Without counting how many remained, I slammed the pills back with all the reckless vigor of a *just-signed-the-divorce-papers* tequila shot, chasing the mystery dose with another few glugs of tepid water.

The following hour passed like a daydream.

My bleary eyes drifted above the gas stations and tourist traps of Nye County, floating higher and higher, until my gaze fixed on a specific patch of atmosphere. Nothing remarkable about the spot - just as shiny and blue as the rest of the sky - but I couldn’t look away. Under oxycodone’s hypnotic embrace, the patch of atmosphere became a comfortable enigma; a crack in the ceiling of your bedroom that only seems to take on deeper meaning from within the blurry cyst of half-sleep, insignificant lines morphing into an alien language that’s alarmingly decipherable.

At some point, I passed out, although it’s unclear to me when.

You see, as far as I can recall, I stared at that spot in the sky for the remainder of the trip, patiently waiting to arrive at my newly purchased home-slash-studio, but that wasn’t actually the case, because, apparently, I’d curled up against the door and fallen asleep a minute after I swallowed the pills. Fallen asleep so deeply that, no matter how loudly the driver said my name, no matter how boisterously he informed me we’d reached the destination, I wouldn’t wake up. With his concern mounting, he resorted to swinging open the rear door My body spilled out. I tumbled to the earth absently, like a marionette with its strings cut, skull thudding as it hit the dirt. 

Now *that* woke me up.

My vision was a wash of harsh light and panicked movement. I heard a familiar voice call out:

*“What the hell’s the matter? Good Lord, is he breathing?”*

I felt big hands push into my sternum. I groaned, swatting at the pisspoor CPR.

The scene focused. 

Three silhouettes towered above me.

There was the driver, a Pakistani man in his mid-forties, hands adorned with all sorts of rings, some of which surely had left imprints in the skin covering my breastbone.

There was the man whose voice I’d recognized, slickly dressed and sporting a spray tan, hammering the well-intentioned driver with a barrage of vaguely accusatory questions: why haven’t you found a way to call 9-1-1, what did you do to him, et cetera.

And behind both of them, there was someone else. 

Someone wreathed in shadow despite the blinding sun, wearing the obscuring darkness like thick plumage, the edges of their frame saw-toothed and meekly fizzling like the thrums of a radio wave, with a stretched head that matched their stretched arms and their stretched legs and -

I blinked.

After that, I saw only two silhouettes.

\- - - - - 

*“You sure you don’t want me to call an ambulance, Lev?”* James asked, handing me the glass.

Crisp water ran a river over my cracked lips. I felt the satisfying frost of a cold liquid splashing against my dry throat, traipsing icy footprints into my gut.

*“I’m sure. There’s no point - I was just dehydrated. The docs warned me that these skin grafts don’t hold fluid well. It’s my own fault. Should have been practically drowning myself with water the moment I got off the plane, traversing the desert dressed like the damn Unabomber, skin leakier than paper mache.”*

James grinned. He unbuttoned his pinstripe suit jacket and sat down. The porch recliner squeaked as he leaned back, resting his feet on the nearby cast-iron table. 

*“Well, alright then. You just let me know when you're feelin’ steady, and I’ll start telling you ‘bout this place. No rush. Ain’t like anyone else nippin’ at my heels to buy it, anyway.”*

I was still a little shaken up from the incident, but I managed to hide my discomfort well. It was easy to conceal unwanted emotions behind the disfigurement, my expression indeterminable amidst the fleshy crinkles and redundant folds. I wasn’t sitting on a mountain in Nevada on account of my poker face, however. No, with my old studio reduced to a heap of ash and rubble, I needed a new place to paint and, more importantly, a change of scenery. Something beautiful to inspire me, or something peculiar enough to get the creative juices flowing.

Somehow, I found a property that checked every box. 

Studio space that could double as my new *home-sweet-home*. 

A piece of architecture that was both beautifully peculiar and half a country away from the rotting shambles of my past life.

*“The T.R.”*

Three thousand square feet of floor space that was practically carved into the mountainside, and, for reasons I was more than a little excited to learn about, the property had been built with only a single window.

The story starts, as many often do, with a married pair of wealthy debutantes struggling to surmount an overwhelming sense of boredom. Ida, a prolific sculptor. Diego, a renowned physicist. Each had dominated their particular field, conquered their peers and their art and their science so thoroughly, so incontrovertibly, that each began to feel their passions souring, its once succulent flavor curdled after decades of achievement and praise. In light of this change, they sought to abandon the tedious routine of their city life and retire somewhere off the beaten path, in a home they could truly call their own. 

Thus, in 1972, they commissioned the construction of *The Tabula Rasa* \- or the T.R., for short.

According to James - the couple’s grandson and the eventual inheritor of the T.R. - their specifications were beyond particular; they were painstakingly meticulous. No nail out of place. Not a millimeter too tall, not a millimeter too wide. The foremen believed the couple was just being fastidious. They were masters of their disciplines, after all. It made sense that they’d settle for nothing short of perfection.

Gossip among the workers was different, however. 

They’d seen the couple’s vicious tempers firsthand. They’d come face to face with the burning hunger behind their eyes. They believed their behavior was about more than just precision; their passion more than just the relentless pursuit of a dream home.

It was zeal. Fanaticism. Dogma.

As the project went on, tensions seemed to unwind. The couple appeared content with their progress.

Then, something happened.

By the time James arrived at this point in the story, the sun was beginning its descent, painting the mountainside and the valley below a dulcet shade of red-orange. When we’d discussed scheduling a tour of the property, I’d specifically requested that he not divulge the structure’s history.

Inspiration is a delicate flame. It needs the right chemistry to bloom.

Hearing the story behind *The Tabula Rasa* in person, on the very ground where it all transpired?

That was the right chemistry.

*“It was just a small mistake. Not even a mistake, really - more a miscommunication.”* His eyes were wide, his hands waving in the air. He paused to sip his beer. Despite being due for my painkillers sooner rather than later, I joined him and imbibed.

That, for the record, was *also* the right chemistry.

*“You see, there was one piece of this home that Grandpa Diego and Grandma Ida cherished above all others - ”*

He stood and gestured at the monolithic brick cylinder behind him.

*“The Smokestack.”*  A short belch slipped from the young man’s lips as he hit the last syllable. He appeared slightly embarrassed, but the lapse in etiquette did add a certain percussive melodrama to the statement.

*“Wait - smoke comes out of that thing?”*

Tucked against the mountainside, it was a box-shaped spire composed of dull red bricks that rose well into the atmosphere, at least double as high as the roof. The home’s one window was located at the very top of that spire.

He shook his head and chuckled.

*“ ‘The smokestack’ is a personal nickname - I have no clue if Ida and Diago called it anything in particular. If they did, they never shared it, but that wouldn’t surprise me. They weren’t exactly forthcoming about the rationale behind…well, any of this.”*

James returned to his chair, but turned it directly towards me before sitting. His voice fell to a hush.

*“See, they wanted the smokestack done last. Time and time again, they reminded the foremen that no one should even think about attending to that weird little tower before Ida and Diago deemed it ready. One morning, a man named Alberto was futzing about, looking to make himself useful, but he couldn’t find his place. At that point, the first floor was done. The second floor was still just scaffolding. In other words; nothin’ obvious for him to help with. Dumb bastard knew about the smokestack, knew that it was supposed to be built last, but he either forgot or ignored the instructions.”*

The young man coughed weakly, breaking eye contact and leaning back. His theatrics disappeared. He scratched the back of his neck, gaze anchored to the sunset.

*“I was six. Living down there - in the valley. I swear, hand to God, that Ida was looking after me when it happened, a twenty-five-minute drive away from the site…”*

He trailed off. A devilish impatience writhed within my stomach, frothy, warm and churning, like seasickness.

*“…James?”*

His head snapped towards me, startled, flushed. Seemed like he’d forgotten I was even there.

*“Hah, jeez, sorry about that. Long…long day, I guess.”*

Another pause.

*“…you were saying? About that worker, Alberto? And Ida?”*

Slowly, reluctantly, he began again.

*“Allegedly, Alberto grabbed a brick, walked over to where the smokestack was due to be, and placed it down. According to some of the workers - workers that must of been hungover, or drunk, or high - Ida appeared behind him as he bent over, holding something. Before he even got fully upright, they say she buried the claw of a hammer into the back of his skull and ran off.”*

James sprang up, suddenly animated. 

*“But she was with me the whole goddamn morning! Yeah, she was out of my sight for a few minutes at a time, in the bathroom or whatever, but not nearly long enough to get the T.R. and back.”*

His voice rose, heated, blaring.  He argued with the past; skewered some unseen phantom with gusto. 

*“You know what I think? I think there was an accident, and they were trying to use that accident to squeeze my family for money.”*

He released a heavy exhale.

*“But that’s my own shit to deal with.”*

James crushed the beer can, but instead of throwing it out inside, he crammed it into his pants pocket. He seemed reticent to enter the T.R. after sunset. 

*“There was his death, the media circus, and the court case, yada yada. Nothing came of it. In the end, Ida and Diago finished the construction themselves. That’s why the top half looks so…uhh…unhinged…compared to the bottom half. They were geniuses in their fields, no doubt about it, but they weren’t architects, and they definitely weren’t blue-collar laborers.”*

Then, he insisted that we return to the valley ASAP, suggesting that we could come back for the tour early the following morning.

*“Sorry, gotta be home to tuck my kid in,”* he said, clicking open the driver’s side door to his pickup truck.

Brake lights illuminated the nearly windowless home, casting all but the top half of the brick-made spire in a sharp red glow. The oversized chimney extended too high to be fully captured in the light - half in, half out- a knife plunged into the black flesh of a moonless night, a blade buried so deep that only the shaft remained visible.

And just like that, like the perfect magic it was, inspiration struck. 

The blueprint for my next painting flashed into my mind’s eye. 

A blade driven through the black heart of artistic obscurity, of my own fervent death-wish, and of death itself: surviving the fire covered the last two - this new painting would cover the first.

*“The Blessed Knife of Eventide”*

It would be my comeback. 

My homecoming. 

My reason to go on. 

I watched the Tabula Rasa disappear in the rear-view mirror, swallowed whole by the gloomy murk of the isolated mountainside, and for the first time since *Ameliae,* I felt something flutter in my chest.

I was excited to make something. 

Like I said, 

it was the right chemistry. 

I woke up in my motel room coated in a sticky, membranous sweat, morning light needling my eyes. 

Between my baseline disabilities and the adhesive brine, it took me more than a minute to get upright at the edge of the queen-sized bed, sliding out from under the sodden comforter in a series of squishy, awkward half-movements. I was thankful grafted skin can’t sweat. God only knows the glistening amnion I would’ve woken up in if my body was cranking out sweat at full force.

I limped over to my open suitcase. A pill bottle rested atop a heap of clothes like a fragile offering. I sat rather than bending over to grab it - bending was a dangerous proposition after the fire. My right side was eager to give out. I quickly learned it was my job to minimize those opportunities.

After some stale black coffee and the best-looking bag of chips in a nearby vending machine. Took me a while to find the pay phone. It was behind the motel, nestled between the ice machine and the radiator. I suspected the damn thing didn’t get much use; besides being effectively hidden from view, I could barely hear the cab company over the duet of groaning appliances. After I was reasonably confident the operator had the right address, I hung up, leaned my forehead against the chill metal of the ice machine, shut my eyes, savored the oxycodone sinking into my blood,

and faded away.

I’m alone.

Encased in thick, reflective blackness.

A shimmering tomb made of volcano glass that extends infinitely in every conceivable direction.

I can only move my eyes.

 I wait. 

 It’s not restful, but it’s not taxing, either. 

Then - pale blue light.

It seeps in from above, 

slithers through cracks in my crystalline enclosure like starving maggots.

I can see it getting closer by straining my eyes, forcing them up in their sockets until the muscles keeping them tied to my skull beg for mercy. My trapped heart races, my veins flutter like plucked piano strings, my entire body - every cell, every atom - feels ferociously alive, engorged with the type of carnal blood-rush that only accompanies near-birth or near-death.

Once the light touches me, I know I’ll change.

I’ll be stretched.

Sundered.

Split.

Severed into many separate pieces,

I expect it’ll be the most pleasurable death anyone’s ever experienced.

 A gorgeous end, 

  For a wretch like me.

I jolted awake at the sound of a blaring horn.

A shuddering gasp forced hot air down my throat. My eyes shot open. 

I had crumpled to the ground, arranging myself in the small cubby between the ice machine and the pay phone. My vision focused. I saw the concrete walkway that wrapped around the motel bathed in the shadow of the structure’s awning, the barren landscape of the valley, and someone standing motionless a few feet to my right, tilted strangely, staring right at me.

I floundered upright, brandishing my cane like a police baton,  laboring under the assumption that I was seconds away from being robbed.

But there was no one nearby.

Just a trick of the light.

*“You wouldn’t happen to be waiting for a cab, would you, buddy?”* A man I recognized as the front desk clerk was peeking his head around the corner. The noise of someone laying on their horn once again curled into my ear.

I nodded.

*“Do you mind letting them know I’m on my way, but it’ll be a little?”* I asked, my unburnt hand gesturing vaguely to my crispier half. The honking continued, but whoever was having a meltdown decided to modify the rhythm, switching from one protracted noise to a series of short, spastic *beeps*. The man glanced over his shoulder and then back at me, exasperation creeping into his voice.

*“Yeah, alright. Just go as fast as…you can manage.”*

I shot him a grin.

*“Of course.”*

An uneasy smile slinked along his jaw. I knew what was gathering in his stomach. The slight recoil. The pinching of his eyebrows. The subtle blanching of his cheeks.

He was revolted.

That’s how I realized I wasn’t wearing my face mask.

I put my head down and shambled past him *as fast as I could manage.* It was the kindest thing I could do to repay the man.

He hadn’t done anything to deserve the sight of me.

Best be on my way.

James was pacing aimlessly on the front porch when I arrived at the T.R., two hours late. From the cab window, he appeared relieved. He stalled his manic laps and gave the car a friendly wave as we approached. 

The irate cabbie - who turned out to be the same Pakistani man from the day prior - parked the car on a patch of dirt in front of the smokestack. Before I could even get my fingers around the door handle, he was clicking it open from the outside. I'm not sure exactly what I did to earn such kingly treatment, but after he transported my two suitcases from the trunk to the front door, I tipped him close to a grand, stuffing a crumpled mess of hundred-dollar bills into his sweat-caked palm. I declined the profuse bevy of *thank yous* that followed, shooing them away with my burnt hand like they were a cloud of gnats. After chauffeuring my tired-ass twice in less than a day, he had certainly earned his keep.

James and I watched the cab shudder down the makeshift road that led back to Main Street.

Then, it was finally time.

As the midday sun rained angry heat down on our heads, there was no better moment to escape inside a mansion with only one window. My real estate agent didn’t seem to agree. His body language betrayed a smoldering anxiety: biting his lip, incessantly scratching at his sun-bleached skin, twisting his shoulders back and forth. His uncontrolled superstition was sort of grounding.

I might have issues, but I wasn’t afraid of a house, at least.

*“After you,”* he muttered.

I suppressed the urge to roll my eyes and limped forward. I turned the knob and began leaning against the door, warm sand-colored steel gently simmering against my forearm. 

If you ignored its obviously bizarre geographic placement, the T.R. could almost pass for normal. From the outside, the brick-made smokestack resembled an oversized chimney, and the rest was your normal colonial fare. Three floors with a gabled roof on top. A relatively spacious portico. A conventional color palette for the outside, as well: inoffensive whites and desert beiges that played nice with the brick. The inside, however, was a different story - it discarded the facade of normality with extreme prejudice. 

The foyer was impenetrably dark.

Swathes of jet-black pigment seemed to spill out with every additional inch that the door rotated, threatening to consume us; a hungry deluge of liquid charcoal. The creaking of a poorly oiled hinge resonated through an apparently vast, unseen space, given the way it echoed. A musty odor curled into my nose. The olfactory signature of rampant mildew, no doubt. That said, there was a sweetness to the odor that I had a hard time placing - sharp and syrupy, like fresh cotton candy. 

*“Should be a master switch to your left,”* James said, still cowering somewhere behind me. 

My unscathed hand crawled along the wall until it discovered a small plastic nub. I flicked it. Machinery growled. Then - *voilà*. Harsh, electric light pushed back the darkness. Pairs of uncovered Edison bulbs began shining, two by two by two by two. The floors were made of polished mahogany. Everything else - the walls, the ceiling, even the railing to the stairs - was uniformly painted a fathomless black. Walking into the T.R. was like stepping onto a flimsy wooden bridge spanning the unplumbed depths of the cosmos, with the rest of the structure melting into a single, contiguous gloom. 

I marveled at my new home. 

To my right, there was a set of narrow stairs tucked tightly against the wall, curving into the bedroom hallway as they neared the ceiling. 

To my left, separated from the main corridor by a short, rounded ledge, there was the foyer proper. A domed chamber about ten feet tall, with six equal sides. I was due to inherit Ida and Diago’s furniture as part of the purchase, but there wasn’t anything in the foyer. The chamber appeared empty. I took another few steps, enough to get a better view of the interior, and actually, there was *one* piece of furniture. Lurking in its own distinct alcove, a single folding chair, solitary and forlorn, with a faint ribbon of natural light dancing across its weathered seat. 

Instantly, I knew what I was looking at.

The inside of the smokestack. 

I strode forward, emboldened, fervent, virile. 

The cane fell from my hand and landed on the wood with a resounding *thud.* 

*“I’m…I’m not coming in…”* James said. Despite standing only a few feet away, his voice sounded incredibly distant. Weak. Microscopic. 

Twenty or so paces later, and, somehow, I hadn’t stumbled. My leg felt strong. Impossibly strong. I waved a dismissive hand behind me without bothering to turn my head. 

*“Whatever. Leave the key on the mat. Close the door behind you.”*

*“Lev, I…”*

*“Close the door behind you,”* I repeated. 

I could see it perfectly in my head. *The Blessed Knife of Eventide.* A glassy blade, the color of these black walls, piercing the night sky. Silver blood would gush around the edges of the ethereal wound, falling to the earth as metallic rain.

*“There’s not a lot of food in here, and the landline’s finicky. You shouldn’t stay here…today isn’t the right day…”*

I heard him, but I didn’t answer. 

*“Lev -”*

*“James, for the love of God, enough! Get the fuck out of here. Now.”*

There was a flickering silence, lightbulbs buzzing like a swarm of carrion flies. 

It really is all going to be worth it, I thought. The fire wasn’t a misstep; it was a stepping stone. I was going to produce something timeless, a piece ten times more potent than *Ameliae*. Then that nagging, ceaseless itch that’s been festering inside my skull since the moment I picked up the brush all those years ago would settle. I would finally relax. 

The whining creak of the front door closing shut splintered my trance. 

Suddenly, my leg felt weak. Sapped of its newfound strength in the blink of an eye. 

I collapsed into a pathetic puddle of a man. 

Slammed my good hip into the wall in a futile attempt to steady myself. Cracked my bad knee against the hardwood, dead-on. 

*“James…”* I muttered, groaning. 

Then, louder:

*“James, are you still out there?!”*

Nothing. 

Just the buzz of the bulbs and the sound of my heart thumping against the back of my throat. 

I reached for my cane. It appeared close enough to grab from where I lay, but my hand never grasped it. Distance was difficult to judge within the T.R., especially with the front door shut. The black walls seemed to dilate and retract under the sway of some unknown directive. 

I tried to lift myself, but I proved too feeble. 

So, having been demoted from demigod to earthworm in record time, I began to squirm towards my cane, focusing my eyes squarely forward, because every time I looked away, I saw that silhouette again.

I had no idea why she was following me, or what she wanted from me. 

But before long, I’d learn. 

It wasn’t particular one thing; she wanted the whole of me, and she'd been waiting for months, ready to savor me, every last drop.

A cold hand grasped my scar-kissed ankle and began dragging me across the foyer,

towards the smokestack,

towards the faint ribbon of light,
