Have you ever heard of Nexis? She is a monster of legend, said to dwell on the northwestern shore of the Black Sea. I heard her story one winter while traveling through the northern edge of Sulina, in Romania. Perhaps, even now, you might still meet her somewhere along that coast. The place is a drowned forest of dead trees, standing in flooded shallows near a harbor on the westernmost stretch of the shore, abandoned for a hundred years or more. The sun never reaches it. It is wrapped all year in a damp, clammy fog.

The locals never go near it. They warned me that something horrible lived there, the child of a vampire and a siren. Once it laid eyes on you, or once you heard its singing, you would lose yourself in the waterways. It would eat your heart, and what remained of your corpse would sink into the rotting flats below, your soul trapped there forever.

But I was not one to be cowed by such talk. Of course I didn't believe a story so absurd; it struck me as the most old-fashioned sort of ghost tale. Besides, how could a vampire and a siren, two things with nothing in common, possibly have a child? When I asked what the creature looked like, the locals fell back on the hoariest of excuses, "everyone who's seen it is dead," to explain why the legend said nothing of its face. So, with a sneer, like the hero of a horror film who refuses to believe in anything uncanny, I rented a small boat and set out alone to see what was really going on.

The boatman rowed me to the edge of the black, tangled woods, in the vast marsh between the river and the sea, and went no farther. The look on his face said it plainly: \*another one off to die\*. Judging by his fee, nearly the price of a new boat, he never expected me to come back alive to pay what remained of the rental. I couldn't have cared less.

I took the oars and worked my way to the middle of the channel winding through the trees. The fog thickened into a miasma and, for some reason, took on a faint tinge of dark green.

And then, in that murk where I could scarcely make out anything at all, I actually saw a figure, flickering in and out of the haze.

Eerily, by the time I noticed it, it was already very close, standing as if right at the water's edge.

Something misshapen was pacing slowly back and forth. It was tall and gaunt, and from its bones it seemed to be a woman. But the way it moved was grotesque. Its spine was held stiff and canted backward, its head lolled to one side, and its arms swung limply at its sides, forearms and wrists twitching now and then. Its legs shuffled aimlessly, taking several steps to gain a little ground, as though the creature's brain had no control over its body and the spinal cord alone, by reflex, was dragging it along. It wore a crude one-piece garment that looked stitched together from raw hide. There had been no tanning, not even the most basic treatment, only a rough cutting before it was put on. The skin had a slick, sodden look, as if it had just been torn from something alive. The creature's head swung from side to side, and its ashen hair hung down, hiding nearly the whole of its face.

Before I could even tell whether I was feeling terror or mere astonishment, a hollow sound reached my ears from every direction. It belonged to no language I knew and carried no meaning at all, as if its only purpose were to stuff my skull with endless blank refuse until it went mad. I slammed the heel of my palm against my head and just managed to clear my mind, only to find that I was losing my sense of time. Everything around me seemed to have slowed. At that moment the creature finally seemed to notice me. It turned and came toward me. As its head lolled, the hair slid away from its face, and at last I saw it.

What I noticed first was her right eye. It stared wide, more than twice the size of the left, and it had no pupil, only a lump of dull, turbid white, as if a ball of flesh had been crammed into an equally oversized socket. Her throat bore a swollen, ulcerated patch that looked to have healed and split open again many times over. Half her face, from the right brow down to the shoulder blade, was covered in great swaths of dark brown scarring, like the aftermath of a terrible burn. The right side of her mouth seemed glued shut by clinging flesh, coated in a translucent, membranous tissue the color of old blood, so that it could not open fully. The seam of her lips gaped and closed like a dying fish's, steadily oozing thick black blood. Then the song began to change, and I understood it. Strictly speaking it wasn't saying anything; it was more as if the meaning it wished to convey were being poured straight into my mind. Phonetically, it seemed to repeat a single word over and over: Nexis.

The girl was asking: who are you?

I still can't understand why I didn't faint from fright or turn and run, nor how exactly I communicated with her, or what that exchange was really like. All I know is that she invited me to her home, and I accepted. Or perhaps I never had the choice to refuse. I waded out of the water onto the bank, treading mud so greedy it threatened more than once to suck the boots from my feet, and followed her to a house in the forest, crudely built of wood and stone. The interior was overgrown with mold and moss. I picked a stone that looked clean enough and sat down. The girl began to speak of herself. Strangely, her words carried no direct meaning, at least not to ordinary humans, yet I received their substance in full, and it sounded not the least bit broken or halting. Only the wound in her throat made the whole uncanny scene harder to bear. Each time she made a sound, it seemed to seep yellow-green pus and blood, and dark gouts of it sprayed from her mouth now and then.

The song called up one image after another in my mind. No narration explained them, yet their meaning came easily. The girl was the child of a human and some unknown creature. Hard as it was to believe, it was true. About a hundred and thirty years ago, a Tsarist Russian alchemist's pregnant wife had fallen gravely ill with a lung disease and was not long for this world. In her desperation she begged him to find some way to preserve the child the two of them had made. And so the mad sorcerer committed an act that would have been called heresy then and would be called heresy now. By some means beyond comprehension but undoubtedly blasphemous, a cult rite, he fused the blood of a monster into the unborn child's embryo. What that creature was, no one knows, but its vitality seemed extraordinarily tenacious and its skeletal structure closely resembled a human's, and he hoped it would save both wife and child. But the wife, only five months along, saw her belly swell with terrible speed, and three days later she went into labor and, as might have been expected, died of the ordeal. The child in her womb, however, lived. The strange right eye and the scarred skin were the distortions wrought by that malign bloodline. Unfortunately, the affair came to light. His wife had died in labor, yet he could not produce a stillborn infant to account for it. And the inhuman lumps of flesh the ritual had caused to sprout on her body were noticed at the burial. Given who he was, the religious affairs office of the parish court grew suspicious of him. In the end he fled with the newborn and drifted in exile all the way to the Black Sea. The daughter grew quickly. By her first birthday her body was already that of a half-grown girl, though her mind was slow.

He settled his daughter in this swampy forest, built the crude house, and taught her to catch fish and sew clothes. Then he left. Before going, he told her to hide here and never come out, and that he would come back for her. But for some reason he never did. From then on the girl stayed here, living on the fish of the flats. The clothes she wore were her own shed skin. Perhaps that kind of monster molts, and she had inherited the trait. Every so often she slowly peeled off a layer of slick, tough, thickened skin. She would cut these fresh pelts roughly to shape and stitch them together with her own hair, then wear them directly on her body to shield the tender new skin beneath.

As for the rumors of the outside world, many people had indeed strayed in over the years, but she had not killed them. She could not speak the way ordinary people do, or perhaps she simply did not know how. Her father had always communicated with her through hand signs only the two of them could read, and had never taught her to speak.

Nexis was her mother's name. From birth she had been able to pronounce only that one name, and so her father called her by it. But when she repeated it unconsciously, over and over, this sole inheritance from her mother, her singsong voice produced a kind of mental contamination that sent anyone nearby into a temporary daze. It was not fatal. What truly killed those people were the venomous leeches of the place. She showed me those too, the giant leeches. At the reach of her hand, these creatures, as long as swimming snakes, crawled out of the shadows of standing water in the corner of the room. Their mouths were packed with dense rows of tiny teeth, yet they were fond of her and would never attack her. According to Nexis, the leeches were her only friends, and ever since she had come here they had often crept about near her. Anyone who wandered into the dead forest and had the misfortune to turn up near her might be affected and, without noticing, have the leeches fasten onto their limbs. They would be poisoned into unconsciousness and then devoured.

When she finished, she fell silent. Perhaps this was all her life held that could be told.

Unable to contain my curiosity, I put a hand to my own throat and pointed at hers, trying to ask what had happened to the wound. She was slow to react and took a long time to grasp my meaning. Then she did something horrible. She pressed her right hand against the wound in her throat and bore down hard, and a thick fishbone, slick with tissue fluid, thrust out through the flesh of her neck, cutting her finger and jutting from between her fingers. She drew the yellowed bone out, lodged in her body for who knows how long, and held it up before my eyes. Nexis conveyed that this was quite common. It always happened when she ate raw fish, but her memory was poor and she kept forgetting to remove them. Come to think of it, perhaps that was why I hadn't been affected today: with something stuck in her throat, her voice had been weakened, and the song's mental contamination along with it. Now, with the foreign object gone, the wound healed at a pace visible to the naked eye. Streams of pus and rotted flesh were squeezed out, and what had been a festering sore became layers of old scars and new. I watched Nexis, who had shown no violent reaction through all of this, and wanted to ask whether it didn't hurt. She opened her mouth to answer, and the returning song made my head sink. Before I could make out a single word, I lost consciousness...
