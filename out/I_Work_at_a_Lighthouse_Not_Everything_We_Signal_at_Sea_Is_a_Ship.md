Ever since my dad left me the lighthouse, I’ve been burdened with doing half as good a job as he did, while putting in twice the work. He was a quiet man, earnest. He tried to do what’s best for his family and what’s best for the lighthouse.

When I was a child, he would teach me the inner workings of the lighthouse. Where you store the oil, where to point the light at sea and when not to do it.

Unlike other lighthouse keepers, our job here is sacred. At least that’s what my father used to say.

As the foggy mist permeates the sea, our guiding light will shine the way.

That’s what he used to say, more or less. He thought highly of this job and with good reason. You see we don’t just signal ships to reach land safely.

We signal other… things. Things that shouldn’t be. Beings that the sea casts away.

I was just a boy when I first started seeing what my dad did. His hands red and rough, skin brittle from working all day. I should have suspected something. How can you work near the sea and have your hands so dry?

He would take the oil and put it on the shining light. Cradling it like it was an infant. He would hold it when it was raining and sing it songs to calm it down when there was thunder.

I used to disregard that as the whimsical consequences of a man afflicted by loneliness. Alternatively, it could be the start of a mental illness.

But my father diligently and regularly treated the light like it was his second son. I guess I could have been jealous, but the light did feel special to me too. I never resented it. It felt alive.

Besides, I’m not going to be jealous of a bit of light.

My father started teaching me the ropes when I was a teenager. Tend to the light, watch the sea. Care about the creatures that live in it. He was adamant about telling me about the impossible dealings that occur in those waters. I didn’t believe him then, but now I do.

The first thing my father taught me about the sea, were the animals. Playful pods of whales would often cross the lighthouse. Frolicking yet calm, majestic animals that would grace the surface of the water with a spout of water and then dive back down to the dark depths.

They’re beautiful creatures. They make me feel more attuned with Nature, with my instincts, my purpose.

The first time my father took me to see the whales properly was at night. He said you couldn’t see them during the day, but the lighthouse can shine light on them and that seems to help them somehow.

I was probably only fifteen when he finally told me about the thing that follows the whales.

It was perhaps because of my juvenile mind that at the time I didn’t quite understand the size of that beast. Things seem bigger when you’re a child. Your old house, the toys you used to play with, your room.

I don’t know why it was different here.

The creature that follows the whales cannot really be described in size in human terms. Seeing an island move seems more akin to a description than anything living and breathing. It trails around the whales, always cautiously distant. It doesn’t seem to want to hurt them. It doesn’t seem to care about much else.

It does not like for the light to be pointed directly at it. Like most things at sea, you point it towards the grotto. Both it and the whales then follow the light, and eventually they return to the peaceful place from whence they came.

This passage occurs about once a month.

There are however some less savory people that occupy this sea. A man in fact, a man from another place. Rage and contempt seem to be his only companions in life. He is an enemy of this place. He was an enemy of my father and now he is mine.

His name is Ezequiel.

Ezequiel is my father’s white whale. One that he peacefully and diligently stands firm against. Maybe it would be more accurate to call my father Ezequiel’s white whale, since he seems so obsessed with him.

He passes near the sea in front of us on his ship. Crowded with people that do not speak a single word. Rare are the times he comes to the lighthouse personally; often are the times he stares at the lighthouse with contempt.

The few times he does come to the lighthouse in person, he brings with him a stormy sky. A flick of thunder and a squally demeanor.

He hates everything we do here.

When he finds my father, he yells at him. The argument is always the same:

“Turn off the light! Let my own ones pass!” Ezequiel demands.

My father refuses. He never turns off the light. Not even during the day.

Ezequiel scoffs and hits my father. He falls on the floor but doesn’t retaliate. I never understood why. My father stands solemnly. His pride was stepped on, yet his face hints at a victory. Ezequiel looks at the top of the lighthouse with anger, and then leaves.

Ezequiel seems incapable of going to the top of the lighthouse himself. He doesn’t seem to be able to get close to the light.

My father, on the other hand, refuses to turn it off.

They’re at a stalemate.

I used to question my father. Question why he relented.

“Why don’t you strike back and defend yourself?” I asked.

“I’m not supposed to. I’m the keeper of the light. My job is to guide, not strike.”

Pulsing rage filled my core.

“But I will not guide that man’s work. And neither will you.” he continued.

Why was he like that? I eventually conceded, maybe it was cowardice on his part, but maybe he knew something.

“What do we do then?” I asked.

“We use the light. We guide others. And we don’t point the light at them.”

This had always been one of father’s rules. One that I didn’t understand.

“Why?” I asked.

He paused, a small smile creeping across his face.

“Because the light is to guide, not to expose. Shining the light on them is an accusation. A judgment of what they are or what they did.” He explained.

“I’m not sure I understand.”

“We don’t judge them, son.”

I pause, still unsure of what he meant.

“Ezequiel gets restless when the Shimmering approaches, he will calm down once it passes. Just have to tend the light”.

The Shimmering. I’d heard about it all my life but my father never allowed me to see it. Only the lighthouse keeper can witness it. I had only heard the noise that precedes the Shimmering. A loud boat siren that is more akin to an angel singing than a horn.

Curiosity won me over.

“What is the Shimmering?”

My father remained silent. He seemed reluctant.

“It’s a ship. A large white ship, full of people.”

I’d heard that before.

“Yes, you told me this. But what is it? What is it for?”

“Well… it’s….”

He stopped for a moment and then smiled.

“It’s where good people go when they’re done.”

“Done with what?”

He put a hand on my shoulder as if to say one day I’d understand. I was left confused. Vague words and cryptic ideas were always his favorite. My father was quite right. Most of his lessons insisted upon the same idea. A sin that one is to avoid.

Do not cast the light on them. Not a flicker.

My father wasn’t lying. He never did. The manifestations out at sea do not like the light. It’s a tool, that can be used to help, or to harm.

It was during one of my first times working the lighthouse that I truly understood that lesson.

It was a foggy afternoon when my father put me in charge of guiding the lighthouse. I was alone, I was cold and I wasn’t focused. The misty fog made the sea conceal itself in pale white, but even that wasn’t enough to hide a small boat that slowly and steadily passed through the water.

A single small boat, with a woman wearing all white came into view. Meek, small and unimposing. A bizarre sight that made the hair on my arms stand straight and made me shiver in the depths of my being.

She wasn’t human. Just another thing cast away from the sea.

I was naive. I was young. And I wasn’t focused. The light strayed, I didn’t hold it properly and it drifted from the water to the woman’s face. She shielded her face from the light, seemingly more indifferent than I expected. I quickly corrected my error and pointed the light away again, trying to absolve my sin by casting a direction for her to follow. But I was too late.

Her face contorted. Angry soulless eyes grew darker and more pronounced as she looked directly at me. I was miles away from her, but I felt her gaze pierce my essence. I took my eyes off her for a second, and that was enough to make her disappear from the small boat she was traveling on.

The next few moments are not my proudest. I cried, I soiled myself and I had to be taken to the hospital.

The woman vanished but only for a moment. Without warning or mercy, something grabbed my legs and started pulling me from the top of the lighthouse through the spiral staircase that led outside. At first, I was confused, there was no one in the lighthouse besides me and my father. I realized it wasn’t a person pulling me, but I had no chance to fight back when it started dragging me down the stairwell.

I’ve been up these steps countless times. My life is quite boring, so when I was a child, I counted them on a whim, so I know exactly how many steps it takes to get to the top of the lighthouse.

199 steps. 199 steps that battered my body as my assailant dragged me down mercilessly.

Each stony step hit me with force. I felt all of them. Every little indentation and flaw on their rocky surface that had worn down with time.

They stung, hit and scraped my body. My head, my back, and every limb were bruised and battered. I was a ragdoll or a child’s toy sent down the stairs against my will.

It didn’t take long for my body to instinctively try to grab on to whatever it could, but it was to no avail. In my brief moments of lucidity, I tried to look at my assailant.

Long, thin stretched-out arms grabbed on steadily to my legs. Arms so long that I saw no end to them, not even elbows. The stairwell didn’t curve sharply, so I should be able to see who was around the bend. But I couldn’t see who was grabbing me, their limbs stretched out further than what was visible.

So, I was taken. Kicking and screaming, pulled against my will, closer and closer to the ground, and to the ocean.

Those seconds felt like hours. The arms were relentless, they didn’t break contact. It didn’t matter how much I screamed, how much I begged. I couldn’t see a face, but I knew who it was.

She didn’t care. I asked forgiveness for illuminating her face, but she didn’t care.

I yelled as I was pulled from the lighthouse and was about to be pulled into the sea. And I lost my breath when I finally felt the hands loosen their grip and let me go. They went back to the misty sea, leaving me only a few inches from dropping down into the water.

I think I was catatonic on the ground for a few seconds, before I heard my father cry out as he came to my aid.

We didn’t discuss that incident for years to come. He was right. The things the sea casts are to be treated with care. One mistake almost cost me my life. If I am to do a good job, I had to take this role seriously.

I had to respect the light, the creatures, the Shimmering. Hell, I even had to respect Ezequiel. They are tortured souls from another place, so I can’t provoke them. I can’t…

I can’t do it.

I can’t do it anymore. How… How did you do it, father? How did you wrestle with the damned and the impossible and kept yourself calm and rational?

I… I wish I could still hear your advice. I wish I could have a wiser extra pair of hands to look out for the lighthouse. Ones who know better.

I…It was late fall when we heard the news. My father was always a quiet man, earnest. He didn’t tell me over the following months as his situation deteriorated and the inevitable was approaching.

Cancer. A plight that man still fights against.

It’s almost funny. A man like him. Who dealt with ghouls and demons, monsters from somewhere far. To see him fall to something so mundane. So unfortunately, common.

I miss him. He left me and he left me this role. I am the one who tends to the light. I’m not a boy anymore, but I’m not like him. I’m just not.

The only thing that keeps me going these days, is the coming of the Shimmering. Father never let me see it, but I think I know what it is. And I have to make it through till then.

Since my father’s passing, Ezequiel has been coming by. Either out of spiteful glory that his enemy is dead, or out of some misplaced regret over not doing the deed himself, he comes at me with rage. Demanding the same thing of me as he did of my father.

“Turn off the light!” he orders.

I hold still, mimicking my father.

“I have seen the other side. I have done dealings with the Ferryman and seen the sinners of Bocarossa. You are just a boy! LET MINE PASS!” He yells.

He strikes my arm; the hit is clean and swift. I begin to bleed from a cut.

Is this what winning against them is? To suffer their assaults and just stand? I…Surely this can’t be it. I remember my father’s words; they calm down after the Shimmering. I stand back up, I won’t cede to Ezequiel.

I must hold strong.

Over the last week, I have been unable to rest. I try to sleep in my bed. I’m exhausted, I’m tired. I’m at my limit. But there’s a woman who stands on the top of my bed every night. She stares intently, only a hair’s breadth away from me. She comes closer and closer to my face, knowing I can’t move.

Her nails pressing down on my skin so deeply, that it starts to bleed.

She is with Ezequiel. His sultry servant. A woman as beautiful as she is disturbing to look at.

“Turn the light off.” She whispers.

My arms burn, her nails sink deeper into my skin.

“No.” I meekly reply.

Her face contorts in anger.

“You are not as strong as him. You cannot do it. Give up” she taunts.

I pause for a moment. Her words sting my very soul. I don’t…. like lying.

“I… I know.”

But I can’t stop now. I have to make it through. I have to see the Shimmering.

Days have passed since her routinely assaults began, but I hold.  I turn and toss in my bed. Trying to ignore the wounds on my arm as I wait. And finally, one morning at 5:00 a.m. I hear it. The loud horn of a ship that sounds more angelical than man-made.

I stumble off of my bed. Blood still dripping from my arms. I feel nauseous, white-faced, and weak. I get dressed and race towards the top of the lighthouse as fast as I can.

I rely on this one hope. This one opportunity to see if my theory is correct. I cast my faith on the lighthouse, on this job, on the light and on him.

I make it to the top, and there I see it.

The blood on my arms feels dry as I feel my eyes become wet instead.

Out at sea. Inconspicuous and as promised, there is a large white ship. It’s full of people. Young, old, poor and rich. The sick and the healthy.

I catch myself and I point the light and make their path clear.

They look at me. Smiling faces that seem joyous to see me. They recognize me, to them I’m special. I am the lighthouse keeper.

I see them. Souls sometimes unfairly taken from the world and some who just followed Nature’s course. They’re all waving and smiling at me, the lighthouse keeper. And among them is a man. He’s also waving, he’s also smiling.

I draw a smile. A tired smile, one that feels earned.

I see him. A spectral hint of a man from another world, another plane of existence. One that isn’t our own. He looks at me. I hope he’s proud.

I point the light to make a path. I am their guide now.

One day I do hope to live up to him. To cast this light onto better things and to respect what he left behind.

He was a quiet man, earnest, didn’t say much. A lot was left unsaid. But I think he knew.

I’ll pack my things and go to sleep, to light it again tomorrow. I’ll illuminate the ships and I’ll illuminate the sea. I’ll illuminate the monsters and everything in between. And I’ll cast a light on everything that was left to say.

As the Shimmering ends, and all light dissipates. I’ll stand here, and I’ll watch on. I hope you’re at peace.

Thanks for everything.

I love you, dad.
