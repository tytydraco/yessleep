I turned a patient's dead relatives away from our locked psychiatric ward at a quarter to eight on the first of November. They came in anyway, about two.

What her mother held out was a paper bag. I'm one of the two night nurses, and I was coming up the last flight fifteen minutes early for shift change when I found her waiting on the landing. Ten to eight, maybe, not a quarter to. Visiting hours had ended at six. She held the bag out and said it was for tonight, for Chiara, for the foot of the bed.

I know the rule by heart, I've said it to enough people holding things they'd brought with love: nothing from outside goes onto the patient side. She took it better than I expected, and then she explained, in case I hadn't understood.

"Where we're from, the dead come back tonight," she said. "They come to the children in their sleep and leave them something. You set the shoes at the foot of the bed, heels together, so they know which beds are the children's, and you leave a plate out so they eat and go. If there's no plate they go looking through the house, and they don't leave till they've found something. We've done it wherever she slept. I'm not stopping now."

I told her nobody comes onto our ward at night, the door is locked at both ends and the elevator needs a key, and the bag could go into her daughter's property and come back out with her. She said that was fine. Inside, the afternoon nurse and I counted twenty hard candies, sealed the bag, wrote 19:52 on it and both signed. It is still the most properly handled thing that happened that night.

We took report at eight. Eleven patients, twelve beds, one empty since Friday, bed B in room three, Francesco's until his discharge. Irene was the other nurse and Giulia the nursing assistant, a good night, the two of them.

Chiara was still up at nine thirty, at the dining room table, the TV on low, the windows gone black behind her. She asked whether her mother had come. I said she had, that the bag was in her property. "She still does it. I'm twenty-six." Then she asked whether my family had done it. I said my grandmother had, till I was nine; the year she died I'd already asked for marbles, the big glass ones, and that November I got a scarf from my mother instead, with an explanation about the dead needing a year to learn the way to you. Chiara said she'd have taken the scarf. At ten she went to bed. I watched her set her slippers straight at the foot of it, heels together, toes out, and I was in her doorway with my hand on the frame when my own feet were put together. Not kicked. Set. Heel against heel, toes out, with a pressure around both ankles that lasted as long as it took to move them and then wasn't there.

I looked down at my clogs. Chiara had her back to me, pulling the blanket up. I said goodnight and went back to the station and told Irene and Giulia all of it, the mother's rule, the bag, my grandmother, my feet, because Irene had seen my face before I'd decided whether to. She laughed, and then she looked at my feet, and then she didn't laugh. I capped my pen and put it in my breast pocket and took it out and capped it again. I put my feet on the rung of the chair, both of them, and that's where they stayed for the rest of the night.

Giulia came to us from geriatrics, and on her first round she does a thing I'd never thought about in seven years: she sets every pair of slippers at the foot of every bed like that, heels together, so the ones who get up in the night can find them with their feet. By eleven every occupied bed had its shoes lined up, and I walked past them twice an hour.

The heating wasn't on, because in this hospital the heating comes on by the calendar and not by the thermometer, so we had fleeces under the uniforms and Irene wrote with her sleeves pulled over her hands. She sits on her feet when she writes, cross-legged on the station chair. I've told her about her knees. At twenty to two the pipes started knocking, and her head came up and mine did too. We went and put our hands on the radiator outside room one and it was warm, and in the morning the front desk said somebody upstairs had signed for the cold snap after all. For about ten minutes it made the night feel like a night where things have reasons.

I did the two o'clock round at two, with Irene, because she said she was coming. Giulia stayed at the station door with the corridor in view. There was something at the foot of every bed. Eleven beds, eleven of them: small parcels in brown grocer's paper, tied with string, sitting on the blanket just past where the feet would be. Nothing at the foot of bed B in room three. Irene went for the flashlight. I went through the doors I'd checked at midnight, because that's what my head does: ward door, inner lock; staff exit; the fire door at the end, shut, nothing on the panel; elevator key on my ring, where it still was.

Vincenzo in room three was already up. He's sixty-something and gets up twice a night for the bathroom. He'd found his parcel before we did and had it open, and when I came through the door his slipper was in the air over a glass marble that had rolled off the paper. I told him to stop, and he stopped with his foot up, and I picked the marble up from under it. Clear glass with a twist of blue in the middle. On this ward a thing made of glass is an event. Vincenzo said, "Is it mine?" I said not tonight, that he could ask about it in the morning, and he sat down on his bed in the knitted hat he sleeps in to think about that.

Chiara had her eyes shut, the way people shut them when they're awake, and when I lifted the parcel off her blanket she said, without opening them, "Don't. Not till it's light." I told her it had to go up front till morning. She said, "Then it's still here," and turned over, and I didn't argue with that, because it was true.

We opened them on the dining room table, all eleven, with gloves on. Brown paper. String tied in a bow with a single loop. One marble in each, the same marble, clear with the blue twist, eleven of them in a row under the light. Irene said, "Who gives a marble to a grown man." I didn't answer her, because I'd got there a parcel before she said it. They weren't for them. They were all mine.

I phoned the night supervisor and told her what I had, eleven foreign objects on the patient side, glass, no sign of entry, nobody hurt. She said to lock them up and write it, that she'd ask the front desk, and that she wasn't sending anyone up for marbles at two thirty. The front desk said the stairwell door hadn't opened since the maintenance man left at one thirty. I phoned Dr. Stefano on call, who said the same with fewer words and that he'd see it at seven.

I checked the three doors again with Irene watching me do it and locked the marbles, the paper and the string in the drawer at the station that locks. I put the key on my ring and the ring in my front pocket instead of on my belt. I wrote the report with my feet on the rung. Giulia asked whether, in my family, the children had to be asleep. I said that was the idea. She said, "Well," and went to do the water pitchers, and I understood that she'd worked out what I had.

I was frightened. Between three and five thirty I did two rounds and Irene did one and nothing was at the foot of anything. The shoes were. Giulia had lined up the two pairs Vincenzo and Chiara had moved, and I didn't ask her not to; kicked under a bed, they're a fall at four in the morning. At three thirty I made coffee from the machine in the staff kitchen, which tastes like the last six people's coffee, and drank it standing in the kitchen doorway with the corridor in view. Irene asked down the corridor, quietly, whether I'd left her any, and I had. The drawer was locked every time I touched it, and I touched it a lot.

At five thirty, from the station door, there was brown paper at the foot of the beds in room one. I unlocked the drawer with the key from my pocket and it was empty, and the lock had held, and I had to swallow before I could tell Irene. Giulia came to the station door to watch us go. Eleven parcels again, the same creases in the same paper. Vincenzo was in his doorway in his hat, up for the second time, and Irene went to him, one hand on his elbow, talking about the bathroom and nothing else, and I went past them into room three.

There was nothing at the foot of bed B. The blanket on it had been turned down in a neat triangle, which nobody on nights does to an empty bed. My feet were set together. I knew what it was this time and it made no difference. I tried to step back. My knees bent instead, like a child's when you sit him down, and I was sitting on the edge of the bed, and then my shoulders were on the mattress and I was looking at the ceiling tiles and my clogs were off. I didn't feel them come off. The blanket came up over me from the foot, and the pressure went along the sides of it and under, one side and then the other. I tried to pull my knees up and the blanket held them straight. I got both hands under the edge and pulled, and my shoulders came off the mattress and the blanket stayed where it was, tucked under the end with hospital corners. I said Irene's name, loud, and she was in the doorway before I'd finished it.

She was at the bed in three steps and got her fingers under the edge on my left and pulled, and it didn't come. Vincenzo was in the doorway behind her with his mouth open. Giulia was coming down the corridor. I could see the clock over the door, and I counted, because I do that now. It was forty seconds from my knees going to the moment Irene gave up on the side, went to the foot and pulled the whole blanket off the end of the mattress with her weight behind it. My feet came free, and I sat up so fast I hit her shoulder with my head. On the sheet at the foot of the bed, where my feet had been, there was a parcel. Brown paper. String in a single loop. My name on it, in pencil, in capitals.

Vincenzo said, "Matteo, what are you doing in Francesco's bed?" and I said I didn't know yet, and he nodded as if that was a reasonable thing for a nurse to say at twenty to six and went to the bathroom, which is what he'd got up for. My clogs were under the bed, heels together, toes out.

I put them on standing up. We collected the eleven with the gloves, and the twelfth, and I opened that one at the station because procedure says you open it: one marble, the same marble. It went into the drawer with the rest and with the paper that had my name on it. I kept the key in my hand after that. At ten past six I checked Chiara's property with Irene as the second signature: one paper bag, sealed, twenty candies, our seal from 19:52, nothing touched.

Chiara was at the dining room door at twenty to seven, early, in her slippers, and asked whether anything had been left. I said yes, at every bed, that it had all gone into the drawer because of what it was made of, and that her mother's bag was still sealed and could go home with her. She thought about that. Then she said, "Don't tell her they weren't for me," and I said I wasn't going to tell her mother anything about last night, that it was hers to tell, and she went in and sat down where she'd sat the evening before.

On the way back to the station I stopped at the chapel alcove. Assunta's light was off; it wasn't her day. I reached in and switched it on myself, and stood there longer than it takes to walk past, and didn't say anything to anyone.

I wrote the rest of the report at a quarter to seven. I wrote "found lying on bed 3B" first, because that was true and it was the version I'd have liked. Then I wrote the rest: tucked in, unable to free my feet for about forty seconds, freed by nurse Irene, witnessed, no patient contact, twelfth object found at the foot of the bed with my first name on the wrapping. Irene added what she'd seen from the doorway on and signed under it. Dr. Stefano came at ten past seven, read both, asked Irene whether she'd had to pull the blanket off to free me, and when she said yes he asked me to show him the bed.

Twelve marbles went up to the supervisor's office in twelve small bags, and mine is the only one with a name [on it.](https://www.reddit.com/u/FireDoor_RN/s/QHoNydsAFf)
