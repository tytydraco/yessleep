We moved into the house on Verchères because the street was quiet, the kind of quiet realtors call "established," which really just means everyone on it has lived there longer than we've been alive. The house across from ours belonged to Aline and Gérard, an older couple who'd been married sixty years, since they were teenagers in the same village outside Trois-Rivières, and who came over the second day with a jar of homemade jam and a plate of something wrapped in foil, and Aline held my hands in both of hers on the porch and said, "Oh, it's so good to have young life on this street again," like the street itself had been starving. She wore a cardigan that day, a heavy oatmeal-colored thing with wooden buttons, and I would come to realize I never once saw her without it, in any weather, at any hour, like it had grown into her.

She was warm in a way that disarmed you fast. Within the first week she'd told me about her son, Étienne, who had died young, and about how Gérard had never really talked about any of it, not once, not in sixty years of marriage. She told me this standing in my driveway, and I remember feeling honored, the way you feel when someone hands you something fragile and trusts you not to drop it.

It was only weeks later that I noticed the story didn't hold still. The second time she told it, Étienne had drowned at the lake, seven years old. The third time, he'd been sick a long time first, nine years old, in a hospital bed she described down to the color of the blanket. She told it with the exact same warmth every time, the exact same practiced pause before his name, but the facts underneath kept sliding, and each version ended the same way regardless of how he'd died: with her saying, quietly, "Everyone leaves me eventually. I don't have any real friends left. You understand that, don't you."

Gérard was pleasant in a different way, a way that took me longer to notice was strange. He'd wave from the yard, ask about the weather, and drift back inside before a conversation could go anywhere real. He never once mentioned Étienne. He seemed, more than anything, like a man standing very carefully to one side of his own life.

The small things started before I let myself notice them as a pattern. Aline mentioned once that our bedroom light had still been on past one in the morning, was I having trouble sleeping. My partner brought it up first, actually, asked how she'd know what time our lights went off unless she was watching for it, and I remember defending her. She's lonely, I said. She's got some memory stuff going on, the other neighbors mentioned it. It's not sinister, it's just what loneliness looks like when it doesn't have anywhere else to go.

I believed that for longer than I should have.

The first time I saw her in our backyard at night, I told myself the same story. I woke around 3 a.m. for no reason I could name, and when I looked out the window she was standing at the edge of our yard in that same oatmeal cardigan over her nightgown, perfectly still, facing our bedroom window and nothing else. I watched her for almost a full minute before I understood she wasn't confused or lost, because confused people wander, they shift their weight, they look around. She didn't move at all until I tapped the glass, and even then she only turned and walked back across the street at an unhurried pace, murmuring something to herself I couldn't make out.

The digging started not long after, always in the same corner of our yard, a patch of grass gone bald and turned over again and again no matter how many times we filled it back in. The third time it happened I found, half buried, a single worn baby shoe, cracked leather, decades old, tucked under the dirt like something laid to rest and then dug back up out of doubt. When I finally asked her about it, gently, she looked at the shoe in my hand like she'd never seen it before in her life, and then her face did something I hadn't seen it do yet, a kind of buckling, and she said, "Did I do that? I don't remember doing that," in a voice that had gone thin and frightened, nothing like her usual warmth. For a moment she wasn't performing anything at all. She just looked like an old woman who had woken up standing somewhere she couldn't explain, holding something she didn't remember picking up. Then it passed, the warmth slid back over her face like a screen going up, and she patted my hand and asked if I'd tried the blueberries at the market yet.

We started locking every door religiously after that, both locks, every night, no exceptions. It didn't matter. I came home one afternoon to find our back door standing open and Aline sitting at our dining table with two cups of tea already poured, steam still rising, like she'd known to the minute when I'd walk through the door. "I was just making sure everything's alright, dear," she said, perfectly at ease in a house she had no key to, that we had checked and rechecked was locked.

We asked Gérard about it directly, the two of us standing on his porch, trying to keep our voices level. He looked at the ground for a long moment before he answered. "She was close with Colette," he said, "the woman who had your house before you. Real close, like sisters. I think Colette gave her a key years ago, for emergencies, and I never did find out if she ever gave it back." We had the locks changed within the week, both of us standing in the driveway watching the locksmith work like it was a kind of exorcism. It didn't matter. Two weeks later I found her at our table again, tea poured, cardigan buttoned to the throat, and when I asked her, my voice not level anymore, how she kept getting in, she just smiled and said the house had always been so easy to get into, hadn't I noticed that, like it was a compliment on the architecture.

I found out I was pregnant not long after we changed the locks the first time. We hadn't told anyone yet, not even my own mother. I wasn't even showing at 1 month. Aline asked me, the very next time I saw her, if I was carrying a boy, in a tone so certain it didn't sound like a question at all. I told her we didn't know yet. She smiled and said, "It's a boy. I can always tell," and reached out to touch my stomach before I could step back, and said, so softly I almost didn't catch it, "He'll stay this time."

After that, she started to change in ways that had nothing to do with memory loss. She got thinner, hollow through the cheeks and collarbones, in a matter of weeks rather than years. Her hair, which had always been carefully set, started coming out in thin patches she stopped bothering to hide. Gérard didn't really age, and instead stood around her like a man standing next to something being slowly hollowed out from the inside, concerned but too afraid to do anything, Besides, he stopped answering the door most days at all.

The lucid moments got more frequent and more frightening, not less. She caught me alone in my driveway one afternoon and grabbed my wrist, hard, her grip nothing like the frail thing her body had become, and said, fast and low, "Something's been inside me since he died. It comes and goes. I try to keep it busy so it doesn't notice things it shouldn't notice. Don't let it near your boy." And then, just as fast, her face went slack and pleasant again, and she asked me how the nursery was coming along, like the last ten seconds hadn't happened at all.

I didn't tell my partner about that one for almost a week. I don't fully know why.

We put the house up for sale not long after that, and told ourselves it was purely a business decision. Buyers came and went strangely fast. A woman touring the house mentioned, on her way out, that our elderly neighbor had approached her in the driveway to say, very kindly, that the house had "bad bones," that people didn't stay long here. I found our sign lying flat in the grass another morning, not knocked over by wind, laid down deliberately, the stake still upright a foot away.

My partner went to speak to her alone about it, to ask her, kindly but firmly, to stop. I wasn't there. He came back to the house maybe twenty minutes later and sat down at our kitchen table without taking his coat off, and when I asked him what happened he said he didn't want to describe it, not yet, maybe not ever, that he'd been standing in her front hallway and for a few seconds it hadn't felt like he was looking at a person's face anymore, and that he'd rather I never had to see whatever that was for myself. He wouldn't say more than that. He still hasn't.

We left within the month, faster than the sale even finished processing, renting somewhere four hours away with no shared driveway, no lonely neighbor with a cardigan and a story that changed its own facts every time she told it.

The last time I saw her was through our car window as we pulled away. She was standing on her porch, so thin by then she looked like something sketched rather than lived in, and she lifted one hand, not quite a wave, and mouthed something I couldn't hear through the glass and have tried very hard since not to guess at.

Three nights ago I came downstairs for water and found a teacup sitting on our new dining table, steam still curling off the top, though the kettle had never once been on. Folded beside it, with the same careful hands that used to fold foil around loaf pans, was an oatmeal-colored cardigan with wooden buttons.

I carried it straight outside and shoved it down into the bottom of the bin, under two days of trash, and went back to bed telling myself that was the end of it.



I woke up the next morning wearing it.



I don't remember putting it on. I don't remember going back outside, though there was dew on the hem when I checked, and dirt under my nails, the same rust-colored dirt from our old garden bed, or dirt close enough to it that I've stopped trying to tell the difference. I keep thinking about the way Aline's face used to buckle when I asked her about things she didn't remember doing. I understand now, in a way I wish I didn't, exactly what that felt like from the inside.

Two mornings after that I found the second shoe, the match to the one I dug out of our old yard, half buried in the same corner of this one, though we've only lived here six weeks and I have never once seen anyone back there who wasn't me.

We've put three locks on every door now, blackout curtains on every window. It hasn't touched the feeling that settles in around 2 a.m., the sense of being looked at through walls that are supposed to be solid. Some nights I catch myself standing at the window anyway, curtain pulled back an inch, not checking the treeline for someone else anymore so much as waiting there myself, the same patient way she used to wait, and I can't always account for how long I've been standing there before I notice what I'm doing, or who I'm doing it for.

I don't know anymore if I'm the one being watched, or if I've started to be the one watching. I only know I'm due in ten weeks, and that I've started to be afraid, not of Aline, not really, but of what I might do, or might have already done, in the hours I can't account for, waiting right along with whatever's out there to find out if it's a boy.
