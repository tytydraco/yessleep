All of the potential solutions were wrong. Obviously I couldn’t bring a child to the Witch. That was out of the question. I had been so sure I would be able to handle any request the tent threw at me, but this was obscene. 

I could ignore the request. I could do something entirely different, like Ellis had done with the wine. Maybe the next request wouldn’t be as bad. Even as I mulled the option over in my head, though, I knew it was nonsense. The Witch always escalated until you did what she wanted.

If I didn’t fulfill this request, the next one would be worse.

That left me with one other option. I could go to Nico for help. Maybe together we could come up with a workaround. 

I knew it was against the rules. I knew that if the Witch found out, worse things would come. But maybe she wouldn’t find out. She wasn’t omniscient, was she? Even if she was, maybe this was a lesser offense. I had to hope.

I texted Nico. I asked him to meet me at the dining hall. It would be empty this time of day.

As I walked down toward the main campus, a persistent thought kept nagging at me. Even if the Witch didn’t find out, telling Nico didn’t solve the problem. It only prolonged it.

Still, it was all I could think to do.

\*\*\*

Nico was grinning when he walked into the dining hall, like he had just been thinking about something funny. “What up?” He said as he slid into the chair across from me. His eyes dimmed once he got a good look at my face. “What’s wrong?”

“It wants a kid.”

“What?”

“The tent. It wants a kid.”

He pulled back sharply, “You’re not supposed to tell me….”

He seemed to realize the gravity of what I had just said. “A kid?” He asked, voice suddenly small.

I nodded.

He stared at me for a long time, and though I hadn’t asked for this–we had done everything right–I couldn’t help but feel like I had failed him and the others.

Finally he dropped his head into his hands. Reality settled in. The truth rested between us, heavy as the winter sky. There was no good way out of this. There was no right answer. And now I had pulled Nico into my misery. So much for thinking I could handle whatever the tent threw at me. 

“We did everything right,” he said as he lifted his head again.

“I know. I don’t know what happened. I shouldn’t have told you. I just didn’t know what to do.”

“Okay. Okay. Well, we obviously can’t give it a kid. So that’s out. But if we don’t, she’ll ask something worse.”

“Or *do* something worse,” I said, thinking of Claudette. There’s not a lot worse than sacrificing a child to an unknown end. I had the terrible feeling that if we didn’t do what the Witch said, it would cost us infinitely more than we were willing to pay.

Suddenly Nico looked up. 

“What? What are you thinking?”

“I have an idea,” he said.

“What?”

Something dark passed over his face. It was like I could see the wall going up. “Do you trust me?”

“Yeah.” I did, but I still didn’t like where this was headed.

“Okay. Stay here. I’ll be back in a minute.”

“Not a chance.”

He stood up and zipped up his coat. “I thought you said you trusted me.”

I did. But it wasn’t his job to fix this. It was mine. We didn’t have a lot of time to waste, so I decided that I would verbally agree, and secretly follow him as soon as he left. “Okay.” I said. “But if you’re not back in ten minutes, I’m coming after you.”

He nodded grimly.

Nico left the dining hall, and I watched as he walked past the giant floor-to-ceiling windows and started up the trail to the cabins. As soon as I was sure he wouldn’t hear the creak of the dining hall door, I followed him out. 

It was almost impossible to walk silently up the dirt path. Leaves and branches crunched under every footfall, and the earth itself seemed to echo every movement. I stayed half a football field behind Nico, hoping against hope he wouldn’t turn around. 

What was he going to do? I couldn’t imagine what sort of solution he had come up with. Was he going to try to trick the Witch? Plead with it? As far as I knew, she was an unstoppable force of nature, as sure as the wind and the rain and the wildfires on the other side of the state. She was rigid in her instructions and unforgiving in her methods. Bargaining didn’t seem like an option.

When he crested the hill and disappeared over the ridge, I let myself walk faster, still doing my best to stay quiet. 

I could only hope that Nico had a better understanding of the Witch than I did. Years of serving her whims had almost certainly given him a nuanced view of her that I didn’t share. I took a little comfort in that.

When I reached the top of the hill, I hunched over, hiding myself behind a blackberry bush. Inching forward, I leaned out around the briars. 

The tent sat, still zipped, exactly how I had left it. 

Nico was within steps of it. He didn’t have anything with him. He certainly didn’t have anyone with him. He wasn’t supposed to be doing this. He wasn’t supposed to even *look* at the tent before I finished my task, let alone approach it. I watched him, confused, with a tight knot in my stomach. 

What was he doing?

The answer was worse than I could have imagined. Nico stooped next to the tent. He ran the zipper down to the ground. 

Then he crawled through the opening.

“Nico, no!” I shouted. 

Nico looked up at me. He tilted his head and gave me a grim look, as if to warn me, and then he zipped the tent behind him.

Only then did I understand what he was thinking. His eighteenth birthday hadn’t happened yet. Technically he wasn’t an adult until the end of the summer. 

He’d sacrificed himself to the tent.

And I was ashamed to admit that even in my shock and terror, the thought occurred to me, *would it work? Will she accept an almost-adult?*

I took off running toward the tent. Whether or not it worked, I wasn’t going to let Nico be alone. That was the whole reason I stayed–the whole reason I didn’t just leave when Ava left. I wanted to help my friends. I didn’t want them to end up like Claudette. 

“Nico!” I shouted as I ran toward the tent. 

If it hurt him, I would never forgive myself. 

I reached the tent, skidding to a stop, and yanked down the zipper.

Impossible bulbous eyes met mine. Black holes–pure abyss–stared back at me. Her arm, stretched out over the entire length of the tent, reached for Nico. Spindly fingers landed on his chest.

She stared at me. Just stared. 

I screamed. I didn’t mean to, but I did. 

Her hand clenched the material of Nico’s shirt, and he fell backward, eyes rolling up into his head. 

Then, in a swift movement that I still can’t explain, the Witch seemed to roll in on herself. She balled up, cloak turning to a haze around her, and she careened toward the back of the tent. In the time it took me to blink, she was gone. 

I screamed again, only this time I meant it. I screamed for Maria and Ellis, and scrambled to Nico’s side. 

He was slumped against the tent so that only the canvas and thin poles supported his weight. His neck was bent at a sharp angle, and his eyes were half closed, revealing only a sliver of white.

“Nico!” I cried, grabbing him and trying to prop him upright–to pull him away from the side of the tent. His body went completely limp in my arms, and all I could do was scream for Ellis and Maria until they finally heard me.

The two of them arrived together. Ellis let out a guttural cry and grabbed his brother. I sunk into the corner of the tent, and for a moment, my own thoughts overwhelmed me.

This wasn’t supposed to happen.

This task should have been easy.

It wasn’t supposed to get anyone killed.

I turned back toward the others.

“You have to let me check!” Maria was saying. She pried Ellis’ hand away from Nico’s head and placed two fingers on his neck. “Okay. Okay. He’s alive.”

I let out a ragged breath. *He’s alive.*

“I’m going for help. You two just make sure he keeps breathing,” Maria said.

“Hey. Hey, Nico, can you hear me, Man?” Ellis asked. His voice was tight. I’ll never forget how he sounded. 

Nico’s eyes fluttered. 

I leaned forward. “Nico?” I said, much more quietly than Ellis.

Slowly, he opened his eyes. He looked up at his brother, frowned, and then looked at me. “What…what happened?”

\*\*\*

We took him to the hospital. Of course we took him to the hospital. We agreed that Ellis and I would go with him into the room, and I would be the one to explain what happened, and that I would call it a seizure since no one would ever believe the reality of what happened.

The staff questioned us like they could tell something wasn’t adding up, but I just kept repeating the official story until they stopped asking. 

Nico remained lethargic, half-in and half-out of sleep, rarely saying more than a couple of words at a time. 

I still didn’t know if he remembered what happened, and since his room buzzed with hospital staff, it was impossible to ask him any questions without raising suspicion. 

It took the hospital staff forever to get in touch with Nico’s parents. They always slept with their ringers off.

A battery of tests revealed there was nothing physically wrong with him. That should have been a relief, but it wasn’t. Because in the moments where he did open his eyes, there was something concerning behind them. Or maybe I should say that there was a *lack* of something–a lack of the person I had come to care so much about.

The doctor came to talk to him. She seemed confident when she instructed him to see a neurologist, and told him what to do if something similar happened in the meantime. When I saw two of the staff talking in the hall, though, I could see the truth in their faces. They were as worried as we were.

I never should have told Nico what the tent wanted. If I had kept it to myself, this wouldn’t have happened.

\*\*\*

Ellis and I loaded Nico back in the car. I sat with him in the back while Ellis drove us. Nico leaned into the window and watched the road pass.

“What happened, Nico?” Ellis said. He looked at his brother in the rearview mirror.

“I don’t know,” Nico said.

“Hey. You gotta tell us what happened, Man.”

“Hey,” he snapped back. “I said I don’t *know.*”

I looked over at Nico. Surliness wasn’t entirely out of character for him, but this was something else. There was a fear behind this anger.

Nico met my eyes and sighed.

“Sorry. That whole thing just took it out of me.”

“Understandable,” Ellis said. 

Back at the camp, Ellis and I helped Nico into the cabin. It was late, and we were all tired, but Nico especially needed to rest. We settled him into bed, and then Ellis motioned for me to join him on the porch. 

“I need you to tell me what happened,” he said. “How did Nico end up in that tent? Wasn’t it your turn?”

“I didn’t know how to handle it. I had to tell someone.”

“That’s not how it works.” He started pacing along the cabin porch. “You shouldn’t have dragged him into it. You swore you could handle this–it was *your* responsibility.”

Hearing him say it out loud felt like getting punched in the gut. I had been thinking the same thing all night, of course, but it’s different when it’s coming from someone else, especially if that someone has every right to be mad at you.

“I’m sorry, Ellis, but I couldn’t find a way out. There *was* no solution.”

He turned and kept pacing. “You should have done what she asked!” He snapped. “Isn’t that what we’ve learned over the last few weeks? Whatever she wants *now* is going to be a hundred times better than what she wants *later!”*

“It asked me for *a kid*, Ellis.”

Ellis stopped mid-stride. He swiveled to face me, “*A kid?*”

“Yeah. Everything had been going so smoothly–I didn’t see it coming. I had no idea what to do, so I told Nico.”

“A kid?” Ellis repeated.

I watched as the realizations hit one after another, and his anger deflated like a balloon. Deep down, I think he knew this wasn’t really my fault. It wasn’t Nico’s either. We just all needed someone to blame.

“So what do we do now?” Ellis said after a moment.

“I don’t know.”

 

“I mean we can’t keep going like this. If that thing asked for a kid, what’s it going to do next? Kill one of us?”

*It wouldn’t be the first time.*

“I think we have to figure out how to destroy it,” I said.

“I think you’re right. But how?”

“We should talk to Nico,” I said.

\*\*\*

Nico was already asleep when Ellis and I went in to talk to him. I thought we should wake him up, but Ellis wouldn’t let me. He said we didn’t really know what kind of damage the Witch had done, and it was probably best if his brother got some sleep. Ultimately, it was probably the right call, even if it didn’t feel right at the time. 

Ellis, Maria, and I didn’t sleep at all that night. We sat at the fire behind our cabin, and talked about our problem. We were trapped by this thing. There was no way forward and no way back. 

It was one of those moments where I realized far too late that we should have been planning for this all along. We should have been investigating, or following the thing, or poking around for weaknesses. Instead we let ourselves believe that if we just obeyed it, the entity would let us live–let us continue along in our disgusting little charade of servitude or worship or whatever it was we were doing.

I had been watching the fire all night. I looked at the way it burned through the kindling and gnawed at the logs, and I thought about how dry this forest had become over the last two months. My eyes wandered to the soot on the side of the cabin, where the owl had rolled up to the porch. This place would go up in a fireball with very little provocation. 

The others had gone silent a while ago. I think they were fighting fatigue, even though none of us could imagine a world where we ever slept again. Hadn’t Maria said something about burning it all down? She’d meant it figuratively, of course, but maybe she was on to something. 

“What if we burn it?” I said.

“Burn what?” 

“I don’t know…the tent I guess.” That might not be enough. I thought about seeing the Black Moth Witch in the woods. She wasn’t only confined to the tent anymore. She used to be–but not now. “Or maybe we should burn all of it.”

Neither of them said anything. I expected them to immediately come after me–to tell me I was insane. Instead, they both seemed to be thinking about it.

“Now would be the time to do it–before the next camp starts,” Ellis said.

“How many people are on campus right now?” I asked.

“Wait, guys,” Maria said, as if she was just now thinking through the implications of this conversation. “We’re not serious, right?”

Ellis ignored her and answered my question instead, “I don’t know–like ten? Evacuation would be easy.”

“Yeah.”

“Sphen thinks this place is on its last leg anyway,” Ellis said, as if that was somehow justification for the crime I was suggesting we commit. 

“Guys, this could start a wildfire. And we could get in serious trouble,” Maria said.

“The fire station is like two blocks from here. And it’s not like they have cameras around here,” Ellis leaned back in his chair.

“Yeah, but there *is* such a thing as arson investigators,” Maria said. “No way. No way. I’m not going to let you guys do this.”

“So we just burn the tent,” Ellis said. “Maybe we can start with that.”

“Guys!” Maria snapped. 

We both turned to her. 

“Let’s at least wait until the morning,” She said. “Let’s clear our heads.”

“What if the Witch wants something before then?” Ellis asked.

“We can cross that bridge when we come to it,” Maria replied.

\*\*\*

Around four in the morning, Ellis and Maria went back to the cabins to get showers. I stayed behind to put out the fire. Before I could dump the bucket of water over the coals, though, a dark realization hit me. Maybe I could leave the fire alone and let nature take its course. Maybe some of the sticks and brush around the fire pit would catch. Maybe the cabins and the tents would burn. Maybe I could start a fire through negligence instead of committing active arson. Maybe I would get lucky. 

With a final look at the flames, I turned and walked away. 

As I rounded the corner of the cabin, thinking about the potential consequences of my actions, I saw a figure standing in front of the tent.

I stopped short, squinting into the darkness. I recognized his slightly-slouching frame. It was Nico. What was he doing here? He shouldn’t be up. He certainly shouldn’t be staring at the tent. 

I snuck a look. The tent was unzipped, flaps wide open. I breathed in relief.

“Nico?” I said softly. I didn’t want to startle him.

He didn’t respond. 

I said his name a little louder.

Still nothing.

As I drew closer, I realized that even though he was standing next to the tent, he wasn’t looking at it. He was looking up at the trees. 

I didn’t want to follow his gaze. I didn’t want to know what he was looking at. I had seen things too terrible to believe in this place. I didn’t want to see anything else. Still, I forced myself to turn to the trees and scan the branches. 

It felt like we were being watched, and I expected to see the monstrous eyes staring back at me. Instead I saw nothing but gnarled branches and dying leaves.

Don’t get me wrong–I was relieved. But I had to wonder what Nico was seeing that I couldn’t. 

Reaching Nico’s side, I looked up at him. 

And that’s when I went cold.

His eyes–his bright blue eyes–were completely white again. They had rolled all the way back into his skull. He stood there, motionless, head cocked toward the trees, seeing and hearing absolutely nothing.

“Nico!” I said, moving in front of him and grabbing both his shoulders. Was this sleepwalking? Was it something else? Was *she* here with us?

“Hey, you gotta wake up!” I said. I shook him gently. 

Nico frowned. Then he closed his eyes, and started to sway. For a moment, I thought he was going to fall. I grabbed him tighter, doing my best to steady him.

He opened his eyes. His gaze met mine, and he was himself again. 

He looked at me, looked down at the tent, and then up at the trees, and then back at me again with a bewildered frown. “What…why am I here?”

I shook my head, “I don’t know.”

“Okay,” he said softly.

I guided him back to his cabin. Ellis was still in the shower. I sat Nico down on his bed, and went to sit on the opposite bunk. 

“You shouldn’t have gone into that tent,” I said. 

“We didn’t have a choice.”

“Have you remembered anything?”

He shook his head. “I don’t remember any of it. One second, I saw…I saw her in the tent. The next thing I knew, I was waking up in the ER. All I know…” he stopped himself. 

“What? What do you know?”

“Nothing.”

“Nico.”

He looked up at me. “I know that you and I were chosen. For some reason. When I saw her in that tent it was like…it was like she knew me. I don’t know. It was like she knew everything about me.”

“How do you know that? Did she talk to you?”

“No,” he dropped his head into his hands. “I don’t know how to explain it. But I *know* there’s a reason she picked us.”

We sat in the discomfort of that statement for a moment. Then I turned toward him. “Nico,” I said. “We had an idea out there.”

“Oh yeah?”

“Yeah. But you’re not going to like it.”

“Tell me.”

“I think we need to burn the camp.”

He didn’t respond like I thought he would. His expression remained blank.

“Or at least the tent. We can’t just keep waiting for her to demand something horrific. We have to destroy her.”

Slowly, Nico shook his head. “No.” He said.

“Look, I know it’s crazy. I know this place is your home. But we have to do something.”

“That’s not why.”

“Then why?”

“Because it won’t work.” 

“How do you know?”

“I just do.”

I tried to ask him more, but he wouldn’t say anything else. He just stared out the window as the sky turned a lighter shade of gray in the morning sun.

\*\*\*

Camp Cradlewood was basically empty, and the four of us didn’t have any responsibilities until after lunch. Maria joined us in Nico’s cabin, and I drifted off while the others talked. 

When I woke up, Maria and Ellis were asleep, too. Nico was sitting alone in the corner, hunched over a small table. His back was to me, and I couldn’t see the table, but I could see his arm. He was mixing something.

“Whatcha doing, Nico?” I whispered.

He didn’t answer. He kept stirring. 

“Nico?”

Nothing.

Something was wrong. It’s a terrible thing to look at someone you care about, and to feel afraid. But as I watched Nico lost in whatever he was doing, I knew that he wasn’t himself.

I got up and walked across the room. I wasn’t even particularly quiet about it, but Nico never looked up. 

As I drew closer, I could hear the spoon thudding dully. I looked over his shoulder, and what I saw made me sick.

Spread out on the table before him were bird feathers, and coal, and a bloody clump of hair, and a sprig of pine needles. He had one hand wrapped around a mug–one of the mugs we used for coffee in the dining hall. He was stirring the contents of the cup. He lifted the spoon slowly and a thick gray liquid dripped down and hung in mucousy strings.

My stomach twisted.

“Nico, what are you doing?”

He looked up then, and his eyes were milky white. 

I stumbled backwards, clattering into the shoe rack by the door.

Nico blinked, and his eyes were blue again.

“What the hell?” Ellis said, now fully awake. He jumped down from his bunk. “Whoa. Hey there, Bud, what are you doing? What is this?”

Nico looked up at us. He looked down at the display in front of him. 

Then his shoulders sagged and he said, “I don’t know. I don’t know. *I don’t know.*” His voice cracked on the last one.

Ellis’ eyes roamed the display. He took the cup out of Nico’s hand, and Nico let him.

“Why don’t you grab a shower,” Ellis said. “See if you can shake whatever is going on.”

Nico nodded slowly. He got up, grabbed the mini trash can from beside the door, and swept the contents of the table–including the cup–into the trash. Then he turned and slowly walked toward the bathroom. 

Ellis and I stared at each other for a second. Then he said, “I need air.”

We stepped out onto the porch. Ellis took a deep breath and let it out slowly. “Well, that’s above my pay grade. What the hell was he doing?”

I thought about all the items on the table. It seemed both too simple and too ridiculous to say out loud. “Did it seem a little….” I didn’t want to say it.

“Witchy?”

“Yeah.”

\*\*\*

Something was very wrong with Nico, and the three of us spent the morning babysitting him. We tried not to make it obvious, but he definitely knew. He was quiet, and irritable, and he kept looking at me out of the corner of his eye. 

The four of us ate lunch in the dining hall with the Geriatrics. Nico’s parents babied him, bringing him coffee and food and telling him not to get up. Sphen asked Nico how he was feeling, and he said he was okay, but after that we all said as little as we could. 

One by one, the rest of them filed out until it was just the four of us. Then Ellis said he and Maria were going to go *do some office work*, which I had a feeling was code for *scouring the dark corners of the internet for answers as to why my buddy is blacking out and making potions.* 

It wasn’t until the others were gone that Nico finally said the thing he must have been thinking all day. “She did something to me.”

“Oh, I know.” I forced a smile. “But we’re going to figure out what’s going on.” I didn’t feel nearly as confident as I sounded. 

He smiled grimly. “Want a coffee?” He asked.

“More than anything.”

Nico walked over to the giant stainless steel urn and filled a couple of diner mugs with Sphen’s terrible coffee. I watched him crack a raw sugar packet and a couple of creamers into mine. I wondered at what point in the summer he had noticed how I liked my coffee. It made me smile, and it made me sad. All of this could have been so normal if it wasn’t for the tent. 

My phone buzzed. I looked down at it and saw a text from Ellis to Maria and I.

*Tent’s zipped. I’ll take care of it.* 

My chest tightened, but I decided not to fight him on it. Nico and I needed a break from the tent, and after everything we had been through over the last couple of days, I was positive Ellis was as serious about this as any of us.

Still, I wasn’t going to wait around the dining hall.

*Okay. We’ll wait for you at the ridge. Let us know as soon as you’re done.* 

I shoved my phone back in my pocket. 

“We gotta go,” I said. Nico handed me a mug of coffee, and we headed up the hill toward the cabins. 

\*\*\*

Nico and I stood just below the crest of the hill, just out of view of the tent, waiting for Ellis to be done.

“The coffee is especially bad today,” I said, doing my best to keep the mood light, even though I knew it wouldn’t work. Even though I knew it was way too late.

Nico was facing the dining hall, sipping his own coffee. “I’m really sorry,” he said.

“For what?”

“I’m sorry we got you into all of this. This can’t be how you were expecting the summer to go.”

I laughed. “Not exactly. It’s not your fault though. She called me out specifically, remember?”

“I wish I knew why.”

“Maybe it’s random,” I said. For some reason that felt like the least scary possibility. It felt a lot better than saying she’d chosen us for some specific reason. I didn’t want to think about it too hard. 

I took another sip of my coffee and wrinkled my nose. It tasted burnt and muddy and kind of gross. 

“I don’t think it’s random,” Nico said softly. 

Shoes crunched on the hill above us. I looked up and saw Maria and Ellis walking toward us. I tried to read their faces, but there was nothing there.

Nico and I hurried up to join them. 

“What did it want?” I asked.

Ellis gave a half smile. “Nothing. I mean, it just wanted some sticks. Just some sticks and some pine cones and stuff.” 

I let out a breath. That was good. But it was also weird. Why was she going back and forth like this? Demanding something impossible and then something utterly meaningless and trivial?

I took another drink of my coffee. This time it was bitter. Bitter, and sandy, and something scraped the back of my throat as I swallowed. I gagged and coughed, and looked down into my mug.

It wasn’t coffee anymore. It was a thick, gray substance with pine needles and dirt, and a swirl of something that looked like blood. 

It was the same stuff Nico had been mixing in the cabin.

I dropped the mug on the ground. It rolled a few yards down the hill and the gray substance oozed into the dirt. 

“What’s wrong?” Maria asked.

I stormed over to Nico, half furious, half terrified, and ripped the mug out of his hands. I looked down at it. It was plain coffee. I poured it out slowly. Just coffee.

“What did you do?” I asked.

“What do you mean?” 

His expression was so clear and so bewildered that I was sure he didn’t know. He must have blacked out again. I thought about the brief moment in the dining hall when he had his back to me. The three seconds when I looked down at my phone. If something like this could happen that fast, what hope did we have?

I knelt down in the path and forced myself to throw up. 

Somehow, I was sure it wasn’t enough. 

\*\*\*

That night, we all stayed in the same cabin again, though now, there was a new layer to the decision. We knew that Nico was compromised, and we didn’t want to leave Ellis alone with him. 

Before I went to bed, I smelled smoke on the breeze and remembered the campfire I had left burning last night. I walked around to the back of the cabin, and was disappointed to find that the fire had smoldered down to nothing. I kicked the ash and found a layer of hot coals, but I knew it wasn’t going to do anything on its own. If we were going to burn this place down, we would have to burn it ourselves.

\*\*\*

That night, I woke up to the sound of the cabin door closing. I watched as the door knob rotated back into place and latched silently. I saw the muddy footprints in the moonlight leading from the door to my bed, and then back to the door.

And I knew that it was time. 

I knew, without looking, that the tent was zipped. 

I knew that she knew me.

I knew that she was waiting. 

A half moon hung in the sky, just visible through a thin spot in the trees. It was beautiful out here. That was part of why I came. I should have known this place was too good to be true. 

I walked to the tent.

It could want something innocent. It could want something terrible. There was no way to know anymore. There was no pattern. Nothing was right. Nothing was how it should be.

I leaned down and pulled the note out of the zipper. 

The message was simple. Horrifically simple.

*Bring me Nico’s body.* 

My heart buckled inside of me, even as my knees threatened to do the same. I heard something like a whimper escape my throat, though the sound didn’t feel like it was coming from me. 

My stomach twisted. It was as if I could feel that gray sludge boiling inside me.

I stared down at the note. 

*No. No. No.*

I turned back to my cabin. I went straight for my backpack, and I pulled out the bottle of rubbing alcohol. It would be more than enough. 

For a moment, looking at the three sleeping forms of my friends, I debated whether I should wake them up. No. Not yet. After I did what I had to. I wouldn’t give them a chance to try to stop me. 

I felt remarkably calm then. It was as if the act of making the decision was enough. 

I walked out behind the cabin, and I dug through the ash until I found a stick that was still burning. I carried it to the tent. 

This place couldn’t have us. The Witch couldn’t have us. I wouldn’t let it. 

I circled the tent, pouring the alcohol over the canvas, saturating the entire thing in the flammable liquid. I soaked it through, letting the remainder of the alcohol dribble down into the blanket of pine needles on the ground. Then I took the stick, and I touched it to the tent. 

The flames shot up, and for a second, nothing but the alcohol fumes burned. The tent itself remained untouched as if preserved by some magic. For a moment, I entertained the terrible thought that it might not burn at all.

 

Then, it began to melt. 

I took several steps back. I balled up the note in my hand, furious at it, furious at Her, furious at everything She had made us do. I threw the note into the fire, and I watched as it burned.

The walls of the tent began to sag. The fire ate a hole in the side, near the zipper. I could see inside the tent. I expected it to be empty, like it always was.

But the tent wasn’t empty. 

It was packed to the top with sticks and brush. 

When Ellis said the Witch asked for sticks, I had assumed he meant a few. 

He hadn’t. He had meant a whole bonfire’s worth. 

As the flames reached up into the sky, my gaze turned upward with them. And when I looked beyond them, into the trees, I saw her eyes. I saw silvery orbs reflecting the firelight. I saw her cloak, billowing in the wind. 

I felt her eyes reaching into me.

As my head began to swirl and the world started to feel blurry, all I could think was that the Witch knew me. She had called me. I was here for a reason. The last thing I felt was my knees give out beneath me. I slumped to the ground, and pure darkness overtook me before I felt myself land.

\*\*\*

When I woke up, the first thing I saw were the city lights in the distance. I was leaning against a car window. Everything ached. I turned slowly and saw I was sitting beside Nico. Maria was driving, and Ellis was in the passenger seat. 

Nico looked at me, but he didn’t ask how I was or announce that I was awake. Instead he put his hand on mine, and we stayed silent until Maria finally looked in the rear view mirror.

“What happened?” I asked softly. “Did everyone get out?”

“Yeah,” Ellis said. “Everyone’s fine.”

Ellis and Maria exchange a look.

“What? What happened?”

“We were hoping you could tell us,” Maria said. “By the time we woke up, half the trees outside the cabin were on fire.”

I took a shaky breath. I never meant to put them in danger. I never expected I would black out.

“*Half* the trees,” Ellis said.

I didn’t understand why that was the important part.

“We found you in the clearing. And when we did, there was a perfect half circle of fire around you. It was as if something invisible was holding it back. Like some sort of forcefield.”

\*\*\*

We stayed with Maria’s parents for a few nights after that. It was weird. And peaceful. We drank cheap strawberry soda spiked with cheaper vodka. We ate pancakes and popcorn and watched movies in the loft.

And one night, when everyone else had gone to bed, I walked to the window.

There, in the cross section of a streetlight and a neon sign on a bar, I saw her. And that’s when I knew beyond any doubt that we hadn’t won.

She knew me. She called to me. And worst of all, as I stood there looking at her, I wondered if she had received the exact thing she wanted.

\*\*\*

And here we are ten years later.

After the fire, there was an investigation, but either the job was botched or the magic of the place overwhelmed the evidence, because no one ever came knocking on my door. I was lucky. 

But I don’t feel lucky.

I’m not sure why I finally decided to share this story after all of these years. I think maybe it’s because I saw him again recently. Well–I saw both of them. I saw Nico for dinner. He’s doing a lot better. It took a couple of years, but he’s back to that boy I met at camp all those years ago–sadder, wiser, but better, too. Deeper. Kinder. He doesn’t black out anymore. He doesn’t mix up weird potions and put them in people’s coffee. We still meet up every year, and I think we both want to meet up more. We might actually do it if I didn’t always avoid his calls– if it wasn’t for the other person that I see.

Because the secret that I keep is that I still see *her*. I see the thing with the bulging eyes that hunches over my rafters and peers at me from my ceiling. I see her tilt her head, watching, still waiting for something after all these years. 

Sometimes, in the darkest nights, when I’m home alone, I think back ruefully to that night that Ava blamed me. How she said that I must have done something wrong. And I wonder if she was right all along. Because even if I didn’t technically do anything wrong, I think there was something different about me from the beginning. I think the Witch wanted me. And I don’t know if she’ll ever let me go.

\*\*\*

“Do you ever think about why it chose us? You and me?” 

Nico asked me that one night over a beer at a bar that neither one of us particularly liked.

I thought about it for a long time. “Maybe she knew we would be the ones to set her free.”
