I can still taste the metal. A damp battery. The kind that's been bleeding white crust in a junk drawer since 2009. That's what my mouth tastes like when the nausea hits. Which is always. The metal doesn't fix the sick. It just gives it a flavor, which is weirdly better than nothing. That, and the other thing.

Yeah, I know. You're already judging me for the other thing. I can feel it through the screen. Whatever. Unless you've spent every morning of your junior year dry-heaving into a sink while your mom bangs on the door screaming about how ungrateful you are, you don't get a vote. So here it is: sometimes I'd lock myself in the third-floor bathroom stall, the one by the art wing that nobody uses because the lock is a piece of shit, and I'd prop my phone on the little shelf above the toilet paper. Hit record. Just for me. So later I could watch and go, Oh, okay. That's me. That's my hand. That's my ribs moving. Proof I was still in my own body.

Half the time, watching myself breathe was enough. The other half, I needed more. Dr. Halloway called it a "grounding technique," but she said it like she was describing a cockroach infestation. I called it getting through third period without puking on my calculator. If that meant touching myself in a bathroom stall while my stomach tried to crawl out my throat, then fine. You weren't there. You don't know.

The vice principal was, though.

I had my gown hiked up, phone balanced on the soap dispenser. I wasn't even doing anything yet. Just breathing, just trying to get the saliva to stop flooding my mouth. Camera on because I wanted to watch my ribs move. Proof I was alive. Then the stall door rattled. He didn't knock, because why would he. I heard a sharp click in his throat.

I yanked my hand back so fast I scraped my knuckle on the toilet paper dispenser. Opened the door, and there he was, holding my phone. He looked at the screen. Looked at my hand. Looked at me like I was a used tampon he'd found in the parking lot. Didn't say a word. Just slipped my phone into his stupid breast pocket and walked away.

My parents didn't say much either when they came. My mom cried in the parking lot, but it was the performative kind. The kind where you know she's already texting her friends about it. My dad just drove. I don't remember the drive. Just streetlights turning into highway lights, and then nothing.

I woke up in a room that looked like a Motel 6 designed by someone who hates color.

Beige walls. Beige ceiling. Beige blanket so thin I could see my knees through it. Someone else had dressed me in this cotton gown. Gross. No memory of signing anything. No memory of even arriving. Just a camera in the corner, blinking.

For three days, they wouldn't let me leave the viewing room. Just a chair and a flatscreen. And for seventy-two hours straight, I watched this woman with a totally blank face demonstrate how to eat. Chew twenty times. Let the saliva soften the mass. Press your tongue flat if you feel the rise. I almost gagged just watching her.

The orderly brought my meals on a plastic tray. Just gray paste, always the same, always room temperature. I asked him where I was. He was huge. Shoulders blocked the whole doorway. Dead eyes. He didn't answer. Just set the tray down and left. It bothered me more than it should have that the tray was blue. Like, a really ugly shade of blue. Cornflower, I think? It reminded me of this girl in fourth grade who brought a cornflower-blue lunchbox and insisted it was "vintage" when it was clearly just old and gross. I don't know why I thought about that. I was in a windowless room being fed paste and I was mad about the color of a tray. That's when I knew I was losing it.

The first night, at exactly 2:00 AM, the walls started to hum. Low and wet, shaking the floor. It rattled my teeth. I pressed my palms to the wall and felt it in my fillings. I told myself it was the HVAC. Old plumbing. Something normal. I told myself that because the alternative made me want to crawl out of my own skin.

Day four, they moved me to the ward.

Six beds. Six girls. Five of them were so sedated they just slumped in their chairs during group, drool soaking the bibs tied around their necks. Only one looked alive. Maya. She had this bruise along her jaw, yellow-purple, and dark circles so deep she looked completely exhausted. She was the only one who looked back at me.

Group was a joke. Some counselor named Patricia with soft hands and a smile that didn't reach her eyes asked how we felt about our "recovery journey." The sedated girls gurgled. Maya stared at the camera. I said I wanted to call my parents.

"Family visits resume once you meet your dietary goals," Patricia said, beaming.

The force-feeding came after.

Orderlies brought in trays of that gray paste. It looked like wet cement and smelled like nothing, which somehow made it worse. The sedated girls didn't wake up, so the orderlies pinched their noses, pried their jaws open with these plastic wedges, and spooned it in. One of them gagged. Her whole body jerked. The orderly just clamped her jaw shut with one hand and massaged her throat with the other until she swallowed. Her eyes were open but nobody was home. She swallowed. They always swallowed.

Yard time was a concrete square with twenty-foot walls and one dead tree. I sat with Maya. The others just sat in their chairs, blinking at the weak sun, their bodies looking too heavy, too soft. They'd all been thin when they arrived, I realized. Now they looked stuffed.

"Those orderlies," I said. "They ever talk to you?"

Maya shook her head. "They're not supposed to."

I kicked at a crack in the concrete. "It's weird. Like, they're people. They have lives. But they just do what they're told."

"Don't think about that," she said. "That's how you go crazy. Thinking about who they are."

"Too late."

She laughed. It was a dry, dead sound. Then she glanced at the camera. She leaned in close. Close enough that I could smell the paste on her breath. Sweet and wrong. Fermented.

"Listen," she said. "Have you noticed how they weigh us every morning?"

I shrugged. "Yeah. For the program."

"They're waiting for something." Her voice was barely a whisper. "A number. Two-fifty."

I stared at her. "Two-fifty pounds?"

"I don't know. Maybe. My scale readout doesn't have the label. Just the number."

"Okay." I drew it out slow, like I was talking to a child. "And why two-fifty?"

She pressed her palm flat against her stomach. "Because that's when you're ready. That's when you've got enough."

"Enough for what?"

She didn't answer. Just looked at the camera again, then back at me. Her eyes were too bright. Unhinged-bright. "I don't know. They won't tell me. But I saw them take someone. The girl who used to be in my bed. She hit two-fifty and they wheeled her out in the middle of the night. I didn't see her come back."

"That doesn't mean—"

"Whatever. Believe what you want." She stood up, brushed off her gown. "But I'm telling you. There's something down there. Under the building. The suction sound? That's not pipes."

She walked away. I sat there for a while, staring at the dead tree.

I wanted to believe she was crazy. I really did. She was paranoid. Trauma brain. Something I'd read about in AP Psych. That made sense.

But the suction sound. I'd heard it every night. I'd felt it in my molars.

I tried the ward phone that night. Dead. Completely dead. When an orderly found me holding the receiver, he took it gently, almost sweetly, and said, "Dietary goals first, sweetheart."

I started counting the ceiling tiles. Thirty-three across. Twenty-one down. That's six hundred and ninety-three. Which is a lot of ceiling tiles. I started trying to figure out if that was even right. Maybe it was thirty-four across. I couldn't remember. The lights were buzzing. The buzz had this frequency that was just sharp enough to be annoying but not sharp enough to complain about. That annoyed me more than the actual buzzing. I couldn't win.

A week later, an orderly left my door unlocked.

I don't know if it was an accident or if they wanted me to see. I've replayed it a thousand times and I still can't decide. But the hallway was dark, and the suction sound was louder than ever, so I followed it.

Two orderlies pushing a medical cart down the stairwell. The wheels were groaning under the weight. They took it to the sub-basement, to a door I'd never seen open, marked AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY in sloppy painted letters.

I waited until they passed. Then I slipped through.

The sub-basement wasn't a medical wing. It was industrial. Concrete floors, rusted pipes, air so hot and thick it felt like breathing through a towel. The smell hit first. Copper. Blood. Something electrical burning. It made my mouth water in the worst way.

I followed the cart around a corner. Stayed low. The orderlies had stopped by this wide, open space in the middle of the room. There was a grate in the floor. I crawled to it.

Through the grate, I could see them. The orderlies. The cart. And—

I stopped trying to understand it.

There was something in the depression below. Something wet. I caught a glimpse of it between the orderlies' legs. A pulse, maybe. A rhythm. Dark meat with veins like garden hoses. I saw a tube. It was moving. Not mechanical. Writhing.

I looked away.

The fluorescent lights down there were buzzing, and I suddenly couldn't stop focusing on it. That high-pitched whine. It was worse than the ones in the ward. One of the bulbs was flickering, just slightly, in a way that made me want to scream. I stared at it until my eyes watered. I counted the pulses of the flicker. One. Two. Three. Four. It wasn't regular. It would skip a beat sometimes. That bothered me so much. Why would it skip? Did they wire it wrong? Was it on a timer? I was watching something I couldn't comprehend and all I could think about was the goddamn flickering light.

Then I heard the girls.

I looked back. The cart was open. Three of them. The quiet ones from the ward. They were awake now. Eyes open. Tears on their faces. Their bodies twitched. Helpless. Like they were trying to run but couldn't remember how.

I heard the suction. Wet. Deep. Like a drain pulling sludge through a pipe too small for it. It came from the thing in the center of the room. I didn't want to see. I looked at the ceiling instead. There was a brown stain above the light. Water damage. I tried to figure out if it looked like a shape. A map. A face. I couldn't tell. It was just brown. I hated it. I hated that I couldn't make it into something else.

The girls made a sound. Not a scream. Something worse. A wet, choked click. Their throats working.

I looked down.

The tubes were in them. In their mouths. Their nostrils. The hollows of their necks. The suction sound was louder now. The meat thing was drinking.

I heard a gurney roll. I looked up. Behind a glass wall—through a gap in the pipes—I saw an orderly wheel something toward flames. A husk. Skin over bones. Hollow. Everything was hollow.

I backed up. My heel hit a pipe. Clink.

The orderlies turned. One of them shouted. I didn't hear the words. I was already running.

I don't remember the stairs. Just my lungs burning, my bare feet slapping tile, the suction sound getting louder and softer and louder again as I twisted through hallways I'd never seen. I made it to my ward. Dove into my bed. Pulled the paper-thin blanket up to my chin, shaking so hard the mattress squeaked.

Heavy footsteps stopped right outside my door.

They didn't come in. Just stood there. Breathing. Then they walked away.

I didn't sleep.

I just lay there, replaying it. But the memory was already getting messy. I couldn't remember if I'd seen one tube or multiple. I couldn't remember how many girls were on the cart. Three? Four? I kept thinking three but then I'd picture four and I couldn't tell which was real. The flickering light. Was it the left one or the right one? I remembered a brown stain. But maybe that was from something else. Maybe I was mixing it up with the bathroom at home. There was a brown stain on the ceiling of the bathroom at home, from when I was a kid and my brother left the shower running too long. I couldn't remember where one memory ended and the other began.

And the thing—the thing under the building. I kept thinking it had eyes. I knew it didn't have eyes. I'd seen it. No face. No features. Just meat. But I kept seeing eyes anyway. Wet, dark, like a dog's eyes in the dark. Reflecting light. That wasn't real. I knew it wasn't real. But I couldn't unsee it.

In the morning, they didn't search the rooms.

They wheeled in the scale.

I stepped on. I've known my weight every morning since I was twelve. It's the number that decides if I'm allowed to take up space that day. I'd lost ten pounds since I got there. Felt it in my hips, in the way the gown hung loose on my shoulders, in the way I could count my ribs again.

The digital readout said I'd gained twelve.

I stepped off. Stepped back on. Same number.

My gown felt tighter. My stomach, too. I pressed my palm against it. Something was inside me. Expanding. Filling the spaces where I'd been hollow.

I stared at the camera in the corner. Its light was blinking. One. Two. Three. Four. Steady. No flickering. That was good. That was normal.

Then the door opened, and they wheeled Maya out.

She didn't look at me. Just stared at the ceiling, eyes wide and empty, her body soft and heavy under the blanket.

I opened my mouth to say something. I don't know what. Don't take her. Wake up. Fight.

Nothing came out.

They rolled her down the hallway, toward the elevator, toward the stairs.

I sat on my bed. I tried to remember what she'd said about the number. Two-fifty. What was that in kilograms? I tried to do the math in my head. Divide by two point two. I got lost. I started over. I couldn't focus. My stomach ached. The hunger was back.

It wasn't my hunger.

It rose from below my ribs. Gnawing. Insistent. It made my mouth flood with saliva. I swallowed. My throat worked. The hunger spread, warm and greedy.

And I understood, then, what was inside me. What had been growing. What they'd been feeding.

The thing downstairs. The harvest. The weight.

It was already in me. And it was hungry.

It's 1:47 AM now. The walls are starting to hum. The suction sound is early tonight.

I can feel it in my molars. In my chest. In my stomach.

It's not coming from the vents anymore. It's coming from the floorboards. The walls. Closer.

I don't think it's under the building anymore.

I think it's outside my door.
