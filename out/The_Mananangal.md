I'd like to tell you all a story from my time in the Philippines, when my hair was still full and black. It's about a Manananggal, a mythical ghoul that shapeshifts and feeds off of fetuses straight from the wombs of mothers, human or not. I guess this story is about a very special Manananggal for that very reason, but I will save that for later. The myth must sound very far fetched, especially for folks not from my motherland. But they are real, and terrorize small province towns to this day.

Funnily enough, the name translates roughly to *Remover* or *Taker*, because that it was it did. It Removed hope from its cradle in the womb, taking and taking, never to create anything of its own. Here is, to the best of my fading memory, the story of me, my last and only love, Andrea, and our tragedy. I hope you can all get something from my story.

It was in that dreary summer morning when I woke up to find Andrea staring out the bedroom window, eyes glassed with terrible fright. 

“The pigs are still alive.” she whispered to herself as she looked over our animals from bed, the yipping of pigs bubbling over bleating sheep and chickens heralding the morning. The sow was still alive and pregnant. At that point it had been weeks since any pregnant animal was taken. I wrap my arms around my wife, feeling her chest heaving with terrible fear. 

“Should we warn Aricelli? Miriam?” She whispered, eyes fixed to the window, words struggling between sobs.

“I will. You stay in bed. I’ll handle it.” I got up and found my starchy jeans on the floor, and prepared to set off. I made her a cup of coffee on my way out. 

“No. I heard coffee’s bad for the baby.” I kissed her, tasting the morning in her tongue, along with the salt of her tears. I told her I loved her before heading out to the living room to make a phone call to Michael and his pregnant wife, Aracelli. No one picked up. I head out to the town in nothing but my jeans, some slippers, and an English hat

 

As I walked, I could hear sounds of wailing coming from town. We were a long way from the square, but the wind carried the scent of blood, gas lamps, and smoke. Something happened last night. Arriving at town, I saw from afar, most of the townsfolk gathered outside Michael’s home. I could see him, the town’s only barber, smoking as he shivered over a white sheet in the shape of a person, stained black stripes as though zebra skin. I could see a large blot of red radiating from the middle. The Witch Doctor stood with the Mayor, the two locked in a brutal argument. *Mother fucker! I couldn’t have done anything!* Yelled the Witch Doctor. Michael broke from his stupor, barking out something I couldn’t quite hear. In the crowd, I saw the third pregnant woman in town. Miriam, standing amongst the spectators, her hands over her mouth. The poor woman must’ve been shaken out of her mind, not having the protection of a husband and all.

I finally got to the commotion. It smelled like garlic and death. One of the folks tells me what happened. *Aracelli is dead,* he told me. *She’s under the sheet.* I couldn’t believe it! Aracelli was due in just a few days! Last I saw of her, she had already begun lactating, her womb as full as a generous bowl of rice. How could it have been her under those sheets if it was so flat?

“Get out!” Yelled Michael, the poor man. Eyes as black as coffee beans, white shirt covered with something awfully like blood, but black. That smell, the one of garlic and death, was strongest with him, Aracelli’s corpse, and the Witch Doctor. *That’s what the beast smells like.* 

“Get the hell out of here!” Michael pulled out his bolo, swinging it wildly in the air. Instantly the Witch Doctor tackles him to the ground. The Mayor and the other men pile on him, taking away his blade.

“You heard the man! Go back to your homes!” Cried out the Witch Doctor.

And so the crowd dispersed, children running off squealing. I took a long look at that white sheet that covered two deaths. In an instant, I imagined Andrea under it. Her mouth would taste like death, skin become as brown as coffee and the earth beneath her. I gagged.

“Brother Amon.” I heard Miriam, the pregnant school teacher from behind me. “I’m so sorry.” The moment those words left her lips, she sobbed wildly. “I’m so sorry.” Miriam was a special case. She'd been forced upon. At least, we thought so. Even when the Manananggal had begun taking people, she refused to have anyone in her home for protection. She was alone, and she wanted it to stay that way 

“Don’t be scared. We’ll all pull through together.” There were only three pregnant women left of the original six. It had picked them down one by one. Thomas’ wife, Corazon, was the first. A great racket that involved musket fire and two deaths. Corazon and their unborn child. Then there was Corry and his wife Althea. Corry ran away. He was a coward, but in retrospect, I guess he knew what we were dealing with. Then Michael and his wife Araceli. Then there was Andrea, Miriam, and Divina, an older stout woman who’d lost her husband while he was deployed overseas.

Divina was next in line, with Andrea and Miriam not due for another month. I put my hand on Miriam’s shoulder, and in an instant, she pulled away, running off into town, crying all the while.

I got together with the Mayor, Michael, and the Witch Doctor. That evening they were doing their nightly watch when it finally happened. 

“That tongue,” rambled the Witch Doctor, his tired eyes glossing over with horrified remembering. “It came through the window. It was as hard as steel, I– I couldn’t even hurt it. It was as black as night, and had that horrible smell of garlic. It stabbed Aracelli and sucked up the –”

“Keep her name out of your mouth!” Hissed Michael, who was bruised after being put to the ground.

“That’s enough! Both of you!” barked the Mayor. The two fell into silence. Once the Witch Doctor parted, and the widower was taken to the clinic, I walked with the mayor.

“This Manananggal ordeal’s out of hand. One day, nothing, then suddenly pregnant women dropping like flies. How are things with that sow of yours?” He asked genially, as though he hadn’t seen a dead woman. 

“The sow lived, belly still as full as a gorged tick.”

“Shit.” Whispered the mayor. “It’s really sticking to human fetuses. I guess that’s a relief. Means we couldn't have prepared  for Aracelli and Corry’s girl.” He was speaking to himself mostly.

“Ah! Pardon! That was grim of me. How are things with Andrea?”

“Well,” I hesitated. I was afraid of what I’d say. Afraid of hearing the words coming out of my mouth. It made the looming threat of the Manananggal feel all too real. “She’s terrified, barely sleeps anymore. Then she worries the sleepless nights are hurting the baby. And that…” I feel a lump in my throat. “That keeps her up at night.” We walked in silence for a while towards the river. He had something important to tell me, he said.

“Listen” said the Mayor as we walked, “I have a lead on who it might be.” I was speechless. He continued. 

“I’m still too unsure of things myself, but the Witch Doctor thinks it’s that Maria lady.”

“The barmaid? The one who moved into town? She’s the Manananggal?” I asked. The Mayor lit up a cigarette and took a long drag. He hands it to me, and I huff it gratefully.

“The Manananggal is a young woman. About her age. Michael said they got a glimpse of the thing as it flew away. Looked an awful lot like her. Long black straight hair, thin eyes.”

“Wasn’t it dark out? I doubt they saw much.” I said, passing the cigarette back to him, the quiet bubbling of the river easing us both as the terrible sun beat down on us. It was like a swamp. It smelled like lilacs and tobacco. “And besides, plenty of women have that kind of hair. Maria, Soccorro, Miriam, don’t you find it a little vague?”

“I wasn’t finished.” Said the Mayor, blowing smoke as he did.

“Folks passing by her house smelled it the night before. That smell.” A deep bitter fragrance of black garlic and pig carcass. “That same smell that comes from its long, endless tongue.”

“Why are you telling me this?” I hold my hand out, he passes me the cigarette. Huff, puff. Tobacco and lilacs.

“We’ve got a plan.” That was when I saw, sitting by the riverbed with a fishing pole, Thomas the shooter and Corry the coward.

“Where’s Michael?” Bleated Corry. No one answered him.

That afternoon, we discussed the possibility of killing the poor woman. Kill her before she had a chance to rip off her legs and spread her black wings into the night. Before another death. 

“I know that smell anywhere,” cried Thomas, “It’s unmistakable! We ought to kill her!”

“Mind your tongue! This is a life we’re talking about!” shot the Mayor.

“Mayor’s right, Tom.” swanked Corry “We ought to stay ourselves until it’s undeniable.”

“What do you suggest we do then?” I asked Corry.

“How the hell’re we supposed to prove anything? See her take her legs off like we're watching her strip?” Barked Thomas.

“And what’s your alternative? Shoot the woman in the head!?” The Mayor yelled, blowing smoke into Thomas' face. “And what if we’re wrong?” he hissed.

“And what if we’re right?”

It was begging to get dark. Long shadows stretched from little flowers, the river murmured louder.

“What if we just, I dunno, keep her under lock and key? Watch over her?” I asked no one in particular

“Thomas is the only man here who knows how to handle a gun.” growled the Mayor reluctantly. “If we lock up the wrong woman, and Thomas ain’t standing by Divina, then the cemetery’s gonna grow.” Thomas and the Witch Doctor had been taking shifts watching with Michael. Maybe if it was Thomas’ shift that night, things could have ended there. “God knows the Witch Doctor’s as useful as a rock.”

“Why don’t we tail her then? Like what Thomas said earlier? The beast eats weekly, no?” Said corry to no one in particular. 

And so it was settled. We would tail the woman until the next feeding.

That was the plan, anyway. Divina went into labor in the afternoon the next day. Thomas went into Maria’s house and held her at gunpoint until the delivery finished, the Witch Doctor acting as the midwife for Divina.

The baby lived. Divina named her Grace. Divine Grace indeed, except for Maria. The poor woman. The Mayor could only watch as folks drove that girl into the streets, tearing at her hair, beating her. Such horrible screaming. So much terror, so much pain, such anger. She would cry out her innocence before an unhearing mob before Thomas came and gave her a coup de grâce. *BANG*. Her face looked like it was hit by a cannonball. 

I decided not to tell Andrea about it. After all, the next morning, one of our sows was dead, with black stripes wrapping around it, and a big gaping gash on its belly. The Manananggal had feasted on an animal. Maria was innocent.

The Mayor wanted nothing more than to arrest Thomas. Arrest everyone who laid hands on the poor, innocent woman. So many bloody hands. Too many to fit our small town’s prison. Besides, I would be needing his protection in the coming month.

And so a month went by, the Witch Doctor, Michael, Thomas, and sometimes Corry took turns watching with me over Andrea. Michael would end up drinking most nights with Thomas blowing lazy smoke out the window every evening, occasionally showing me how to load and fire his musket. The Witch Doctor and Corry, both armed with cudgels and bolos, watched vigilantly. There were only two people in the whole town who needed protection now, so there was usually two of them watching every night. 

Most nights, I’d wake up during one of their shifts, hearing Corry grind his blade against steel, the witch doctor daubing chicken’s blood on the window sill to mask the smell of a pregnant woman. The bedroom looked like a temple, metal crosses and paper charms dangling like crib mobiles, every inch of the walls covered in talismans.

And then, one night, it finally happened.

We heard it before we saw it. Great black wings beat heavily in the night sky. Garlic and death. The hope Eater, the remover, the taker. The Manananggal had arrived.

It was me, Thomas, the Witch Doctor, and Michael who’d been on shift that night. Thomas was the first to notice, pulling his musket at his side, cigarette dangling on his lip.

“You hear that?” He whispered. Corry stood up, shuffling through his pockets, searching. The Witch Doctor let out a steady chant, readying his blade.

I was by my wife then, holding her, caressing her sweaty forehead. She was asleep.

And then, slowly, carefully, a black thing grew from above the window. Black tongue. Long, endless, as black as the night sky. It promised only death. I saw it descend from the window, easing through the glass with its acidic spit. It smelled like a dead animal.

“I– I need to grab something from the kitchen.” stuttered Corry, sweat beading his face, hands trembling.

“Where the hell are you going?” whispered Thomas, not daring to take his eyes off the tongue, his gun aimed as he stood beside the window.

“Let him go.” the Witch Doctor hissed no louder than a heartbeat, his eyes fixed on that black serpent. “Someone needs to find its other half, salt it. Make sure it can’t get its legs back.” It drew closer and closer, inching like creepvine. It made its way through the bedroom, zigzagging across and neared the foot of the bed. I looked at Thomas, his face was nearly inches away from the thing, the barrel of his gun all but pressed against the endless snake, now as long as the bedroom itself. It was quiet. Nothing dared challenge that deathly silence. It was as though a gun was pointed at all of our heads. 

That’s when Andrea woke up. She let out a horrifying scream, every hair on her body standing up, desperate to get away from her, from the threat. I pushed my hands over her mouth immediately, but it was too late. 

*BANG*

Thomas let out a shot, blasting the tongue to the floor. It let out a wet thud. 

But it wasn’t enough. 

The tongue did not cut.

It began trashing around the room with the frenzy of a salted worm, the mobile of crosses strewn around the room calling like wind chimes as the tongue writhed with horrible fury. The tongue hit Thomas straight across the jaw. He falls to the floor. I heard no sound from him. As the tongue trashed, I crawled to Thomas, hoping to take the gun, dodging the terrible whips of the corpsely tongue. As I did, I turned back to Andrea and realized that my worst fears had come to life.

The tongue had wrapped around Andrea, the witch doctor desperately, futilely, slashing away at the thing.  In a blink of an eye, the evil tongue stabbed into her womb. It took out the placenta. Only the placenta. It had just put our child to death. Before I could do anything else, it retracted back out the window, carrying the still beating pillow of our sleeping baby.

I wanted to do so many things then. Every cell in my body was in contradiction. To run, to hide, scream, beg, kill. I heard great black wings spreading across the starry night, beating with frenzy. 

“Go! I will tend to her!” yelled the Witch Doctor.  And so with quivering legs I jumped out the window and gave chase, all the while fumbling and stopping to load the rifle with shaking hands. I yelled out into the streets, shouting out into the night, but no one ever yelled back.

I could see it in the moonlight, flying a good ten meters in the air, half of a woman flapping membranous wings painted in the palette of rotting anatomy. It wore a night gown, its spine cut by the lumbar, spinal cord dangling from the bone like a worm. Its intestines hung loose like a loin cloth, each beat of its wings letting out a disgusting squawk as its contents shifted. I saw its hair sticking to the naked intestines, long and black. It looked familiar. 

It knew I was following it, so it spun in giant circles far from town, trying to shake me off, keep me from finding its other half, all the while black old blood dripped beneath it. I chased it for hours. It was the only way I could find its half. Corry was nowhere to be found.

And so I did chase, until dusk began cracking the sky, as a soft golden hue began to swallow the stars. The Manananggal flew lower and lower, jumping from tree to tree and even running on the ground with its arms and wings like a spider, avoiding the soft rays of light, until finally, it could not avoid the sun. It broke its circling and began flying towards town. I gave chase, nearly losing sight of it, but every time I lost its trail, I looked for the black blood on the ground. 

As I got to town, I could hear terrified shrieks. The townsfolk had seen it too. The sun was high in the sky, so the beast was left to crawl on its arms and corpsely wings, the smell of garlic and pig carcass crawling up the nostrils like astringent, bitter tea. No one dared to get close to it as it staggered wildly, desperate and in pain.

The beast began slowing down in front of a house. Miriam’s house. The school teacher. The widow. The incoming mother. Folks from all over town gathered as they watched it, skin burning up in sunlight, singeing and smouldering, as it crawled up the walls and into the second floor of Miriam’s house. Her bedroom.

Immediately I ran up, musket in my arms like a newborn. I bust down the door and am greeted with that horrible scent. Plush carpets, wooden floors, a nice bookshelf and a floor TV. A homely place. I rushed upstairs, readying the rifle with unsteady hands.

“Miriam!” I called, “Miriam, you need to get out of here!” I burst through the door and am greeted by a pair of legs, standing in the middle of the bedroom. It was in pyjamas, two thin bow legs wrapped loosely in blue and white stripes. Above it was a navel, and above the navel was red. You could count the layers. Skin, fat, muscle, bone, but there was something else. Sitting on top of the disembodied legs, sitting like an egg in a bloody nest, was a pinkish sack of fibrous muscle, bulging out and distended like a gorged tick. Pregnant. 

Miriam was the Manananggal.

Just then, I look to the far left of the room and see a tired Miriam, half of her anyway, crawling with beaten arms, her wings crumbling. It looked like a chicken’s.

I aimed my rifle at her head and *BANG!* Blood smeared the plush carpets and flower vases with beautiful flowers. Except, I didn’t shoot. Couldn’t. I watched her crawl towards her other half, which was still standing there in the middle of the room. I watched her feebly heave herself up on the bones of her rotting wings, setting the legs beneath her. I wondered if she’d kill me then, once she recombined.

But then she shrieked. Such a terrible sound. Ear piercing. She tried again, frantic to make herself whole, but it would not work. Correy had done his job. He had salted her other half. She had been  put to death. 

I watched her desperately try, but to no avail. I watched that giant sack of muscle on her legs writhe, hear it cry. A baby’s cry coming from that disgusting ball of muscle. A hungry cry. She began crying too. Such gentle, soft crying. I wondered if Andrea would’ve cried like that too when –if… if our baby could have been born. I

“I’m sorry” Miriam sobbed. “I’m so sorry. My baby’s hungry. He doesn’t eat from pigs anymore! Please believe me, I tried!” I aimed the rifle at her head. “Please… Please take care of my boy.”

I pressed the barrel right at her head and fired. Bang. She fell. No more flying. Her legs remained standing, towering over her crumpled torso.

Just then, all the townsfolk came running up the stairs. Some were sick, some vomited, but most of them held their tongues. No one said a word.

Miriam's child would not grow up. I made sure of it. Eye for an eye. to this day, I wonder what would have been if I had split open that standing womb and raised the boy. What kind of person would he have become?
