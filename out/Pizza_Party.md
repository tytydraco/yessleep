To start this off, let me be perfectly clear: I'm not seeking closure. God already knows I've found more of that than I ever wanted. No, I just... need to get all of this off my chest. If I type it out, if I share this with all of you... maybe I can pretend this is all just another creepy Reddit story.

Let's begin with me. I am posting this from the office I've inherited from my parents—the once-crown of our local community, Doughy Doug’s Artisan Pizza Parlor. Doug being my father, of course. I loved my dad; everyone did. His popcorn of flour on his nose perfectly matched his jolly, dusty apron, and he always had the appropriate joke to tell a person of any age. He loved good humor and good food, which he equally loved to provide. My dad, the master baker, he was my personal hero.As I remember him, it leaves a lot of sadness within me. I wish I could remember this as I used to, through the lens of an ignorant child, only caring about the next legendary Pokémon waiting for me in the tall grass. I wish I could go back to the kitchen and knead the dough for the yearly send-off pizza party. Those were some of my best memories. But memories have a way of becoming the ghosts that haunt our past, and memories bury the truth we were never meant to understand.

For most kids in the '90s and '00s, catching that Pizza Hut "Book It!" deal or a slice of Sbarro was the bee's knees for us middle school kids. Those kids were missing out, though. Our small kitchen produced the best pies outside of Brooklyn or Detroit. The parlor, as my mom would lovingly recall, was "the little marrow that gives life to the bones of our small town." She loved poetic phrases like that. In another life, she could have been a poet.There was always something special about our town that always seemed... well, nothing out of place to me. The surrounding communities fell into economic collapse during the Bush years... Republicans, am I right? Lol, politics aside, there was just something we seemed to produce that the towns around us couldn't. It wasn't anything a kid would notice, nor any adult, unless you stayed in town long enough.But for those of us who lived there, it was a quiet slice of pure Americana paradise. It wasn't perfect, as every year some family would move out of town, but before they did, my parlor would always throw them a grand send-off pizza party. And us kids would have the most important role of the party: to crown the leaving King or Queen of the Oven. Of course, that role was played by the oldest kid moving away.I never understood why every year another family would leave this amazing cove of Midwestern hospitality... but then again, there was always another family moving in to replace them.That was until during my first Send-Off party as the official dough kneader... I met my best friend, Kenny.
