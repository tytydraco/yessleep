There are places in the Himalayas where the forest does not end at the roadside. It comes right up to the edge of the mountain, crowds the bends, hangs over the ravines and sometimes seems to follow the road long after the last village has disappeared behind you.

In October, the deodar forests above the Sutlej valley have a peculiar smell after rain. The wet earth, fallen needles and rotting leaves give off a fragrance that is almost sweet. During the day, it is pleasant. At night, when the cold settles between the trees and the mist begins to climb from the valleys, it becomes something else.

I remember that smell particularly well from the night Leela and I drove through the forests above Kotkhai.

We had left Shimla rather late on a Thursday afternoon in October 2005. Our destination was a small guesthouse near Rohru, where we had arranged to spend two nights. The journey was not particularly long, but the mountain roads had been damaged by recent rain, and we had lost nearly two hours behind a road-clearing vehicle.

By eight, we had passed the last sizeable settlement.

The car's headlights illuminated a narrow strip of road, bordered on one side by a steep bank and on the other by a wooded drop. The forest was dense. Tall deodars stood close together, their trunks disappearing into the darkness above. Every now and then, a branch brushed the roof of the car.

Leela had been unusually quiet for some time.

"Are you hungry?" I asked.

"I was hungry an hour ago."

"There's a dhaba marked on the map."

"You said that about the last one."

"This one is definitely there."

She looked at the map in my hand.

"That map was printed in 1998."

I folded it and put it away.

The car climbed around another bend. A few minutes later, we saw a yellow light ahead.

It belonged to a roadside dhaba.

The establishment was little more than a wooden shed with a tin roof, built against the hillside. A hand-painted board announced tea, parathas and dal. Two trucks were parked outside, but there were no customers.

I pulled in.

An elderly man was sitting beside a wood-burning stove. He wore a dark woollen cap and a brown waistcoat. His beard was white, and his face had the weathered look of someone who had spent a lifetime outdoors.

"Two teas," I said, "and whatever you can prepare quickly."

"Eggs?"

"Yes."

"Parathas?"

"Certainly."

He went inside.

We sat on a wooden bench beneath the awning. The forest began almost immediately behind the dhaba. I could hear water running somewhere below, though the stream itself was hidden by the trees.

The old man returned with tea.

"Where are you going?"

"Rohru."

He glanced at the road.

"Tonight?"

"Yes."

He looked at the dark sky and then at Leela.

"Better to reach before midnight."

I smiled.

"That is the intention."

He did not smile back.

When the food arrived, we ate with considerable enthusiasm. The parathas were hot and crisp, the eggs generously spiced, and the tea was strong enough to restore my faith in mountain hospitality.

The old man sat opposite us, warming his hands.

"Have you travelled this road before?" he asked.

"Once, during the summer."

"Then you know the old forest section?"

I nodded.

He pointed towards the road beyond the dhaba.

"After the next bridge, keep to the main road. There is another track which goes up towards the old orchards. Do not take it."

"Is it a shortcut?"

"It was."

"Is it damaged?"

He looked at me for a moment.

"Some roads are better left unused."

He rose to collect our plates. I thought the conversation was over, but he stopped beside the table.

"Listen carefully. If you see a woman standing on the road, do not get out. Do not lower your window, even if she appears injured. And whatever she says, do not answer her."

Leela looked up.

"Is there a woman who troubles travellers here?"

The old man took a moment before replying.

"She has been seen by people who have never met one another. Some hear her asking for help. Others hear her calling someone by name. Once she has spoken to you, she will not always remain where you first saw her."

I glanced at him.

"What happens if someone stops?"

"One driver got out to help her. They found his vehicle the next morning, with the engine running and the driver's door open. His shoes were on the road. They found him much further down the slope."

He paused.

"And there was no path down there."

Leela's expression had changed.

"Was he attacked by an animal?"

The old man shook his head.

"No animal would have taken him there."

He went inside and returned carrying a small square of folded cloth.

"Keep this in the car."

I opened it. There was a little coarse salt mixed with grey ash.

"What is this?"

"Salt and ash from the hearth of the old temple. You may not need it. But carry it."

I smiled politely.

"Is it some kind of local remedy?"

"An old custom. If you hear the temple bell in the forest, stop the car, switch off the lights and scatter a little of this across the inside of your doors. Keep the windows shut. Do not answer anyone outside."

He looked directly at me.

"And do not mistake a familiar voice for a familiar person."

I folded the cloth and put it in my jacket pocket.

"Thank you. We shall keep it."

When we were ready to leave, I asked his name.

"Dev Singh."

I thanked him for the food.

He looked at the car as I opened the driver's door.

"One more thing, sahib. If you find that you have passed the same tree twice, stop the car. Do not reverse. Wait until you hear the stream."

I looked at him, uncertain whether he was joking.

"Why?"

"Because the stream will tell you which way the road is."

He turned away before I could ask him to explain.

The first twenty minutes were uneventful.

The road crossed a small bridge, then climbed through a forest of deodar and oak. The moon was hidden behind clouds, and the headlights revealed only the immediate bends.

Leela had fallen asleep.

I was beginning to think that Dev Singh's warnings were nothing more than a way of discouraging late-night travellers from taking the old track when something appeared in the road.

A woman.

She was standing directly in the path of the car, a little beyond the outer edge of a sharp bend. Her back was towards us. She wore a pale shawl over a dark dress, and her long hair hung loosely down her back.

I slowed down.

There was no house nearby, no vehicle and no sign of habitation. The forest rose steeply on one side of the road and fell away on the other.

I stopped several yards before reaching her.

For a moment, she remained perfectly still.

Then she lifted one hand and began waving it slowly, as though beckoning us forward.

I looked at Leela.

"That's what Dev Singh was talking about."

She sat up and looked through the windscreen.

"Do you think it's the same woman?"

"I don't know. He did say not to stop."

"Well, we have stopped."

I glanced at the rear-view mirror. The road behind us was empty.

"Perhaps we should just ask what's happened. We won't get out."

Leela hesitated.

"Keep the doors locked. And don't open the window."

I nodded and lowered it by barely an inch.

"Madam? Are you all right?"

The woman did not turn.

I tried again.

"Do you need help?"

Her hand dropped.

Then she began walking towards the car.

There was nothing particularly unusual about the way she walked, but something about her pace troubled me. She did not hurry, nor did she seem concerned that a vehicle was approaching her. She simply walked towards us with her head slightly bowed.

Leela gripped my arm.

"Close the window."

I did so.

The woman stopped a few feet from the bonnet.

I could now see her face in the headlights.

She was young, perhaps thirty, with a pale, narrow face and dark hair clinging to her cheeks. Her eyes were fixed on me.

"Please," she said.

Her voice was very soft.

"Please take me home."

I glanced towards the trees.

"Where do you live?"

She pointed behind the car.

"Down there."

I looked in the rear-view mirror. There was only the dark road we had travelled.

"Is someone waiting for you?"

She shook her head.

Then she looked at Leela.

"Your sister is cold."

Leela sat rigid.

"What?"

The woman smiled.

"She should not be left outside."

I felt a sudden tightening in my chest.

Leela had no sister.

I raised the window fully.

The woman moved closer. Her face was now inches from the glass.

"Open the door," she whispered.

I put the car into gear.

She placed both palms against the bonnet.

Her fingers were unusually long and pale. The nails were dark, and the skin around them looked bruised.

Then she bent forward and struck the bonnet with her forehead.

Once.

Twice.

A third time.

Leela screamed.

I drove forward.

The woman did not move aside.

For one terrible second, I thought we would hit her. But just as the bonnet reached her, she seemed to fold sideways, her body disappearing beneath the level of the headlights.

I drove past.

In the rear-view mirror, I saw her standing in the road again.

She was facing the car now.

Her arms hung loosely at her sides.

Then she raised one hand and pointed at us.

I accelerated.

The next strange occurrence happened perhaps fifteen minutes later.

We came to a bend where a large deodar tree leaned over the road. Its lower branches were covered in moss, and one thick root had broken through the retaining wall.

I slowed down.

There was something familiar about the tree.

I could not immediately place it.

We continued for another kilometre, passed a narrow culvert and climbed a long curve. Then the road opened into a small clearing.

The deodar tree stood ahead of us.

I recognised it at once.

The same broken retaining wall. The same exposed root. The same branch hanging low over the bend.

I stopped.

Leela looked at me.

"What is it?"

"That tree."

"What about it?"

"We've passed it already."

She glanced outside.

"Are you sure?"

"Quite sure."

I looked at the map, though I knew it would be of little use.

Then I remembered Dev Singh's words.

If you find that you have passed the same tree twice, stop the car. Do not reverse. Wait until you hear the stream.

I looked at Leela.

"That's exactly what he told us."

She had gone pale.

"Do what he said."

Until that moment, I had been reluctant to accept that anything beyond an unusual series of coincidences was happening. But there was no sensible explanation for being back at the same tree after travelling forward for more than a kilometre.

I switched off the engine.

We sat in silence.

For several minutes, nothing happened.

Then, from somewhere below the road, I heard the sound of running water.

It was faint at first, almost indistinguishable from the wind. Gradually it became clearer.

A stream.

I started the car and drove forward.

The road descended sharply around the next bend, and the sound of water grew louder.

At the bottom of the slope was a small wooden bridge.

We crossed it.

I glanced in the mirror.

The forest behind us seemed to have closed over the road.

Leela was no longer sleepy.

"Who was that woman?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"She knew I had a sister."

"You don't have a sister."

"I know."

She turned towards me.

"She said she was cold."

I kept my eyes on the road.

"Perhaps she was trying to frighten us."

"How did she know what to say?"

I had no answer.

The car radio switched on by itself.

It was an old cassette player, and I had left it switched off when we stopped at the dhaba. Now it emitted a faint crackling sound.

I reached towards the controls.

A woman's voice came through.

"Come down."

I switched off the radio.

The voice continued for another second, then disappeared.

We were both silent.

A few moments later, the headlights caught something moving between the trees.

It was a small figure.

At first I thought it was a person walking along a forest path, but there was no path. It was moving parallel to the road, keeping pace with the car.

I increased speed.

The figure disappeared behind a tree.

Then it appeared again, farther ahead.

It was the woman.

She was walking between the trunks, on ground that sloped sharply away from the road. Her pale shawl moved behind her, but her feet seemed to touch the ground only occasionally.

I could not understand how she was keeping up with us.

Leela had seen her too.

"Don't slow down."

The woman reached a break between two trees and stopped.

As the car passed, she turned her head towards us.

Her face was now different.

The skin seemed stretched tightly across her cheeks, and her mouth was open in a wide, soundless smile. Her eyes were black in the headlights, without any visible reflection.

Then she lifted one arm and struck the trunk beside her.

The sound came through the closed windows.

A heavy, hollow blow.

Something struck the rear of our car a moment later.

I heard the metal buckle.

Leela screamed again.

I pressed the accelerator.

The road began to climb.

I could see no houses, no lights and no sign of another vehicle. The forest had become so dense that the headlights seemed to vanish into it.

Then the engine lost power.

I pressed the accelerator. The car slowed, shuddered and stopped.

I tried the ignition.

Nothing.

I tried again.

The starter turned, but the engine would not catch.

Leela was breathing rapidly.

"Don't get out," she said.

I had no intention of doing so.

We sat in the darkness, with only the dashboard lights illuminating the interior.

Then we heard footsteps.

They were coming from behind the car.

Slow, measured footsteps on the gravel.

They stopped beside the rear door.

Someone knocked.

Three times.

A woman's voice spoke from outside.

"Open the door."

It was the same voice we had heard on the radio.

I did not move.

The handle of the rear door began to turn.

I pressed the central locking button, though the doors were already locked.

The handle stopped.

Then came another knock.

"Please."

The voice sounded different now. Almost human. Almost frightened.

"Please, I have lost my way."

Leela covered her mouth.

The footsteps moved around the car.

I could hear them passing the rear bumper, then approaching the passenger side.

A pale hand appeared against Leela's window.

It was pressed flat against the glass.

The fingers slowly spread apart.

Then another hand appeared beside it.

The woman was standing outside, bent forward, her face level with the window. Her hair hung over her cheeks, but I could see her mouth moving.

Leela began whispering a prayer.

The woman smiled.

Then she began tapping the glass with one fingernail.

A slow, deliberate rhythm.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

She stopped and leaned closer.

Her lips moved again.

This time, I could read the words.

I know you are in there.

The rear window suddenly clouded over from the outside, as though someone had breathed upon it.

A dark shape began to form in the condensation.

A handprint.

Then another.

Then a child's face.

I reached for the ignition.

The engine started.

I put the car into gear and drove forward.

The woman remained beside the window for a moment, her head turning to follow us.

Then she was gone.

We drove without speaking.

After some time, I saw a small light ahead.

It was not a house. It was a lantern.

Someone was standing beside the road.

A man.

He was middle-aged, wearing a dark woollen coat and carrying a long walking stick. His face was partly hidden by a scarf.

He raised his hand.

I stopped a few yards away, keeping the engine running.

"Where are you going?" he asked.

"Rohru."

He looked at the road behind us.

"Have you crossed the stream?"

"Yes."

"Did you hear the bell?"

I stared at him.

"What bell?"

He came closer.

"Have you heard a bell ringing?"

Leela shook her head.

The man looked at her, then at me.

"Get out of this forest before midnight."

I glanced at my watch.

It was 11:47.

"Who are you?"

"Someone who knows these hills."

He pointed towards a narrow track.

"Follow this road. There is a dhaba about two kilometres ahead. Stay there until morning."

I hesitated.

"Is the main road closed?"

"No."

"Then why should we stop?"

He looked at me with an expression that was difficult to read.

"Because the road is not taking you where you think it is."

I remembered the tree, the woman and the voice from the radio.

I switched off the engine.

The man leaned towards the window.

"Listen carefully. There are things in these forests that are not like the spirits people speak of in stories. They do not always want to frighten you. Sometimes they want you to follow them."

"What are they?"

"In some villages, they call them Airi. The names change from one valley to another. The old people say they belong to places where someone died violently and was never properly mourned."

He looked at Leela.

"The woman you saw is not asking for help. She is asking you to acknowledge her. Once you answer her, she can use your voice against you. Once you open the door, she can enter."

I felt a chill pass through me.

"How do we get away from her?"

The man looked at the dashboard.

"Do you have salt?"

Leela opened the glove compartment and found the small cloth packet Dev Singh had given us.

The man nodded.

"Good. Keep it close. And do not answer anyone who calls you by name."

He then gave us a curious instruction.

"If you hear the bell, stop. Turn off every light. Put the salt across the doors. Do not look outside until the bell has rung thirteen times."

"Thirteen?"

He nodded.

"Not twelve. Not fourteen."

"Why?"

"Because the first twelve are the calls of the living. They tell the people in the village to shut their doors and keep to their homes. The thirteenth is for the one who is not living among them. It marks the end of her wandering."

He looked towards the trees.

"After the thirteenth, she must return to the water. That is why you must listen for the stream. It will lead you back to the road that belongs to the living."

I was about to ask him more when he stepped back.

"Go now."

I looked at him again. He had appeared so suddenly that I could not remember seeing any light approaching, nor had I heard footsteps before he stood beside us. I was too shaken to question it.

Before leaving, I asked his name.

"Bhagat Ram."

He turned and walked into the darkness.

We drove on.

The road descended now, and I could see the valley opening below us. The engine was running smoothly.

Leela held the salt packet in her lap.

"Do you believe him?" she asked.

"I believe we should get out of this forest."

"That wasn't my question."

I did not answer.

A few minutes later, the first bell rang.

It came from somewhere above the road.

A single, deep note.

Then silence.

I slowed down.

The second bell rang.

It was closer.

Then the third.

I stopped the car.

The fourth rang.

I switched off the engine and headlights.

The fifth.

The sixth.

We sat in complete darkness.

Leela opened the salt packet and began scattering it along the inside edges of the doors. Her hands trembled so badly that some of it fell onto the seats.

The seventh bell rang.

Then the eighth.

A sound came from outside.

Something was walking around the car.

The ninth bell.

The footsteps stopped beside Leela's door.

The tenth.

A woman's voice spoke.

"Leela."

My wife froze.

It was not the voice we had heard earlier.

It was familiar.

Very familiar.

"Leela, beta."

Her mother had died when she was twenty-three.

The voice came again.

"Open the door. I am cold."

Leela began to cry.

I reached across and held her hand.

"Don't answer."

The eleventh bell rang.

Something touched the roof.

A slow scraping sound travelled from the front of the car to the rear.

Then came a soft knock on the windscreen.

I could not see anything outside, but I heard a woman's voice directly in front of us.

"You have taken my place."

The twelfth bell rang.

Silence followed.

Then something began to laugh.

It was a low, breathless sound, like someone trying to laugh with a mouth full of water.

The thirteenth bell rang.

It was different from the others.

The sound came from below the road, from the direction of the stream.

I started the car.

The engine responded.

I switched on the headlights and drove forward.

The road descended sharply, and within a minute we reached a small stream crossing. There was a concrete bridge, and beyond it stood a roadside dhaba with a lantern burning outside.

I pulled in.

The dhaba was a modest establishment, built of stone and timber. A man in his early fifties was sitting near the stove, reading a newspaper.

He looked up as we approached.

"Late travellers," he said.

I could barely manage a reply.

"Two teas, please."

He looked at Leela and then at me.

"You have had a difficult journey."

I stared at him.

"How do you know?"

He did not answer immediately. Instead, he brought us two glasses of tea and a plate of hot pakoras.

The warmth of the room and the smell of frying onions seemed to bring us back to our senses.

I told him what had happened.

I described the woman, the repeated tree, the voice outside the car and the thirteen bells. I also told him about the man who had stopped us and given us directions.

The dhaba keeper listened without interrupting.

When I mentioned the man's walking stick, he put down his newspaper.

"Was it a long stick, made of dark wood? With a brass ring near the handle?"

I nodded.

The man looked towards the door.

"That was Bhagat Ram."

"Who was he?"

"A forest guard. He knew every path in these hills. He died in a landslide in 1974, while trying to reach a village cut off by heavy rain."

I looked at Leela.

"He told us where to go."

The dhaba keeper nodded slowly.

"People say he still does."

He went behind the counter and returned with an old photograph.

It showed a group of forest guards standing outside a wooden building. One of them was holding a long walking stick.

It was the man we had met.

I examined the photograph more closely.

Then I noticed someone standing beside him.

An elderly man in a brown waistcoat and woollen cap.

I felt the blood drain from my face.

"That is Dev Singh."

The dhaba keeper looked at me sharply.

"You knew Dev Singh?"

"He served us dinner a few hours ago. At a dhaba further up the road. He gave us the salt and ash."

The man slowly took the photograph from my hand.

"Where did you say you met him?"

I described the wooden shed, the two parked trucks, the stove and the hand-painted sign.

The dhaba keeper shook his head.

"There is no dhaba there now."

"But I was sitting in it. I ate there."

He turned the photograph towards me.

"This is Dev Singh, Bhagat Ram's younger brother. He ran a dhaba on the old forest road for many years. A generous man. He used to give food to travellers who came through late at night."

He paused.

"He died in 1986. His dhaba burned down that same year."

I looked at the photograph again.

It was unmistakably the man who had served us tea and parathas.

"Are you certain?"

The keeper nodded.

"I knew him as a boy. There is nothing left of his dhaba except a stone platform and a few pieces of the old stove. No one has operated a business there since the fire."

For a while, I could hear nothing except the crackling of the stove.

The man folded the photograph and put it away.

"Bhagat Ram and Dev Singh were brothers," he said. "One knew the forests better than anyone in the region. The other knew the travellers who passed through them. They both died long ago, but there are people who believe they still look after those who get lost."

I looked at the little cloth packet on the table.

The salt and ash had spilled onto the wooden surface.

I remembered Dev Singh's words: Do not mistake a familiar voice for a familiar person.

Then I remembered Bhagat Ram appearing beside our car without warning, his lantern illuminating a face that should have belonged to a man dead for more than thirty years.

I had not questioned him. I had simply listened.

And now I understood why.

The two brothers had met us on the same road, in the same forest, on a night when we might otherwise have disappeared into it.

One had given us the means to resist whatever was following us.

The other had shown us how to get out.

Neither had asked for anything in return.

Leela reached across the table and held my hand.

"Let's leave," she whispered.

I nodded.

The dhaba keeper accompanied us to the car. Before we departed, I asked him one last question.

"What was the woman?"

He looked towards the dark forest beyond the road.

"The old people call her an Airi. Some say she is the spirit of a woman who died alone in the forest. Others say she is older than any village here. No one knows for certain."

He lowered his voice.

"But there is one thing the elders always say. If she calls you by the name of someone you love, she has already come too close."

He stepped back from the car.

"Go straight to Rohru. And if you hear the bell again, do not count it."

We left shortly before dawn.

The forest gradually thinned as the road descended towards the valley. The first signs of morning appeared behind the eastern ridges, and soon the dark trees began to reveal their familiar shapes.

Neither of us spoke much.

The little cloth packet lay between us on the dashboard. I had folded it carefully and placed it there, though I could not say why. It seemed wrong to discard it.

We reached our guesthouse after sunrise.

For several days, the events of that night remained between us. We did not discuss them with friends, and we never attempted to find the old dhaba again. In fact, whenever our travels took us near Kotkhai, we chose the longer route.

There are things one can explain away, given enough time and a sufficient appetite for ordinary explanations.

But there are also nights after which a person develops a healthy respect for certain roads.

The people of those mountains have a saying that I remember even now:

When the thirteenth bell rings, the lost are shown the way home. But if you hear it from behind you, do not look back.

For the mountains, they say, are generous enough to send someone to bring you home.

They are not always generous enough to let you know who has followed you.
