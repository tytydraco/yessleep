Before any of this happened, Mara used to put her cold hands under my shirt when we were washing up.

I’d swear. She’d laugh against my back. Sometimes she stayed there while I finished, her cheek between my shoulder blades, telling me things that had happened that day.

I keep coming back to that.

There are other things I need to tell you, but I want you to understand that when she first asked me to keep thinking about her, I thought she was asking to be loved a little more carefully.

That seemed like something I could do.

We’d been together eight months when she lost her first afternoon.

I came home and found her standing in the garden beside a basket of wet washing.

It had rained on my drive back. Water still dripped from the line. She wasn’t wearing a coat.

“You all right?”

She looked at me, then down at the basket.

“These were dry.”

I thought she was annoyed about the rain.

“We can stick them on the airer.”

“No. I was bringing them in.”

She’d taken the washing off the line just after one. She remembered folding a pillowcase over the top of the basket, picking it up, and turning towards the house.

Then the basket was on the ground, everything was soaked, and I was opening the back door.

It was nearly six.

I suggested she might have fallen asleep.

“Standing up?”

I didn’t have anything better.

That evening, she kept checking her phone. Once, while we were eating, she asked how long we’d been sitting there.

We rang the GP the next morning. There were questions, an appointment, blood tests. I started keeping a record of what happened and when. It felt useful. There was a problem, and we were doing the things you did when someone you loved had a problem.

Then, on Sunday, I saw it happen.

Mara was sitting opposite me at the kitchen table, peeling an orange. I looked down to read a message from my brother.

Something tapped against the table.

Half an orange rolled past her plate.

Her chair was empty.

I stared at it with my phone still in my hand.

“Mara?”

She was there again.

Her chair scraped backwards. She looked at her empty hands, then at the orange on the floor.

Neither of us moved.

“Did it happen?” she asked.

Outside, someone was putting bottles into a recycling bin. I remember being able to hear each one.

“Did you see?”

I nodded.

She started crying with such relief that, for a few seconds, I hugged her without understanding what I was comforting her about.

Then she said, “Thank God. You can help me now.”

We bought a camera.

There’s a particular kind of humiliation in searching the internet for security cameras while your girlfriend sits beside you, asking whether the cheap ones will be good enough to record her ceasing to exist.

I bought the expensive one.

We put it on the living-room bookshelf.

On Monday, at 10:06, Mara disappeared from the sofa. The book in her hands fell onto the cushion and slid to the floor.

At 10:22, she returned.

She was still sitting. Still looking down. Her fingers were spread around a book she wasn’t holding.

She stayed like that for a moment before she understood.

Then she put her face in her hands.

I watched that part alone, later. When we watched it together, she made me skip ahead.

By Thursday, she’d found a connection.

At 10:22 on Monday, I’d texted to ask whether she wanted anything from the shop on my way home. During another missing spell, she’d returned when I’d opened a photograph of us to show someone at work.

We compared the recordings with my messages, my calls, anything that could put a time to when I’d thought about her.

The matches weren’t proof of everything she suspected. I told her that.

She asked how many more times we needed to watch her disappear.

I stopped saying it.

We couldn’t explain why it seemed to be me. Her mother called most days. Her sister sent messages. Mara had a job, friends, people who must have thought about her when she wasn’t there.

She hadn’t always been like this. There were photographs of holidays she’d taken before we met. Her mother remembered raising her. There were school reports in a box under her bed.

Mara got them out one evening and spread them across the duvet.

I found her reading a comment about how much she talked in class.

“I was there,” she said.

“I know.”

“All that time. Even when nobody liked me.”

I sat beside her.

She kept her finger on the teacher’s handwriting.

We tried to test it deliberately.

She sat on the bed. I stood in the doorway.

“Think about something else.”

“That’s not how thinking works.”

“Please.”

I tried multiplication. Then the route to work. All the time, I was aware of her sitting there, waiting to stop existing.

Nothing happened.

Eventually, she asked me to fetch a glass of water.

I went downstairs. In the kitchen, I noticed water leaking from beneath the washing machine. I crouched to pull a towel out of the cupboard.

There was a clatter on the stairs.

I thought of Mara.

Then she cried out.

I found her sitting halfway down, holding her mouth. Her phone lay at the bottom.

She’d followed me. The last thing she remembered was stepping onto the next stair with the phone in her hand. When she came back, her hand was empty and she lost her footing.

We thought the phone must have fallen when she vanished. I’d heard it and remembered her.

She’d struck her mouth against the banister.

I knelt beside her. There was blood between her front teeth.

“Sorry,” I said.

She looked up at me.

It was the first time I’d apologised for it.

We put photographs around the house. Notes on my computer. An alarm on my phone that went off every ten minutes with her name on the screen.

They brought her back. They couldn’t reliably keep her here.

At first, she apologised when an alarm interrupted something.

Then she started watching how quickly I dismissed it.

I took time off work. We spent whole days together. She came into the bathroom while I showered and sat on the closed toilet, talking through the steam.

Sometimes we almost managed to have a normal afternoon.

Once, she laughed so hard at something on television that tea came out of her nose. I laughed too. She leaned against me, wiping her face with her sleeve, and for a minute I thought we could live around it.

Then she asked what I was thinking about.

“You.”

“What about me?”

“That you’re disgusting.”

She smiled.

“Good.”

At night, she slept with my hand against her chest.

I don’t know how much that helped. Sometimes I woke and found her there. Sometimes I woke to an empty bed, thought her name, and felt the mattress dip beside me.

Once, while I was drifting off, my hand dropped onto the sheet where her body had been.

She returned before I’d fully opened my eyes.

She asked how long she’d been gone.

“I don’t know. A second.”

“You don’t know?”

“I felt you go. I woke up.”

“But you don’t know.”

After that, she asked me to stay awake until she fell asleep.

Then until morning.

When I said I couldn’t, she nodded as if I’d explained something reasonable.

Twenty minutes later, she asked again.

Yesterday afternoon, I found her recording herself on my phone.

Just her face, close to the camera.

“Think about me,” she said.

A pause.

“Think about me.”

Another pause.

She looked exhausted. There was a small healing cut on her lip.

I asked what she was doing.

“You could listen to it. With headphones. When you’re busy.”

I imagined sitting at my desk with her voice repeating in my ears. Driving to work with it. Trying to sleep.

She must have seen something in my face.

“It’s only my voice.”

“I know.”

“You used to ring me because you wanted to hear it.”

I sat beside her and said I still did.

She deleted the recording while I watched, and I felt so cruel that I asked her to make it again.

Last night, I woke on the sofa to breaking glass.

I’d only meant to close my eyes.

Mara had been standing beside the coffee table with a glass of water. When she disappeared, it fell. The noise woke me. I looked for her, and she returned.

I saw that part.

She appeared standing over the broken glass, already shifting her weight to take a step.

One foot came down on a large piece.

I remember the blood more clearly than anything else. How quickly it spread between her toes. The wet footprint she left when she stepped towards me.

I carried her into the kitchen and sat her on the counter. She clung to my shirt while I pressed a clean towel against her foot.

“You said you wouldn’t.”

“I know.”

“You said.”

“I’m sorry.”

I wanted to look at the cut. She wouldn’t let me move far enough away.

Eventually, the bleeding slowed. I dressed her foot, then sat beside her until she stopped shaking.

It was almost four.

She asked for tea.

I filled the kettle. She watched me from the counter, her injured foot resting on a chair.

“I wasn’t anywhere,” she said.

I turned towards her.

“When it happens. I don’t go somewhere. There isn’t a room or darkness or anything.”

“Okay.”

“You get to have the time. I don’t.”

I put two mugs beside the kettle.

“The washing got wet,” she said. “That first day. The basket must have been out there for hours.”

I waited.

“If you hadn’t come home, it would have stayed there. It could have rained again. Gone dark. Anything.”

I wanted to say something useful. I was so tired I couldn’t remember whether I’d put sugar in her mug.

“What if you stopped thinking about me?”

“We’ve been through this.”

“What if you wanted to?”

That made me look at her.

She was picking at a loose edge of the dressing.

“I don’t,” I said.

“But you could.”

The kettle clicked off.

She asked me to come closer.

I thought she wanted a hug.

Her left hand closed around my right wrist. With her other hand, she picked up the kettle.

For a second, I thought she was trying to help me pour.

Then she tipped it over my hand.

I shouted and tried to pull away. Her fingers tightened. Water hit my knuckles and splashed across the counter.

It lasted perhaps a second.

I knocked the kettle against the cupboard. She let go.

I stumbled to the sink and turned on the cold tap. I was making a noise I’d never heard myself make before.

Behind me, Mara began to cry.

“I’m sorry.”

I kept my hand under the water.

“I’m sorry. I didn’t know how much—”

“Get away from me.”

She went quiet.

There was a pause. Then she said my name.

I turned.

She was staring at my hand beneath the tap.

“Are you thinking about me?”

For a moment, I couldn’t understand the question.

Then I did.

At the hospital, she sat beside me with her bandaged foot tucked beneath her chair.

I told the nurse I’d spilled the kettle.

I had the truth ready. When the nurse asked what happened, I looked at Mara and watched her lower her eyes.

I said I’d spilled it.

She didn’t try to hold my other hand. Somehow, that made it worse. She sat close enough that she could have, with her fingers tucked between her knees.

That was six hours ago.

She has apologised so many times that I’ve started saying “I know” to make her stop.

Back home, she asked whether I wanted her to leave.

I said yes.

She called her mother and asked her to come. She said she’d hurt her foot and we’d had an argument.

While we waited, Mara put her things into an overnight bag. I sat at the kitchen table. Each time I heard her upstairs, I pictured what she was doing.

A drawer opening. The wardrobe. Her bottles being lifted from the bathroom shelf.

I couldn’t seem to stop.

When her mother arrived, Mara stood in the hallway holding her coat.

“You’ll still think about me?”

“Please go.”

She looked at my dressing.

Then she went.

I locked the door behind her. I rang my brother and asked if I could stay with him. I told him we’d had a fight. He said he’d make up the spare bed.

While I was packing, my phone lit up.

A message from Mara.

I’m sorry. Please believe that.

Then another.

Mum’s gone out. I’m on my own but I’m still here.

I put the phone down.

I could hear the fridge running downstairs. Somewhere outside, a neighbour was mowing their lawn.

I tried to finish packing.

My hand throbbed. I thought of the kettle, and her fingers around my wrist.

The phone lit up again.

It’s been nearly an hour. I haven’t missed any of it.

I watched the little dots appear beneath her message.

I think I’ll be able to sleep tonight.
