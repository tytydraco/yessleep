My name is Kyle Mercer. I'm sixteen and I live in Riverton, a mid-sized town of about forty thousand people. I'm going to tell you what happened last year. I won't dress it up. I'll just tell it.    
Riverton always had a legend. Every mid-sized town does. Ours was about a killer who poisoned people. They said he used a silver powder, that it left no mark, that the victim died in two or three days and nobody understood why. People called him Alastor Henderson. I don't know where they got that name. Maybe from some book, some movie, some story someone told wrong and it became truth. The legend said he showed up every now and then, killed someone who "deserved it," and disappeared again. Some people laughed. Some took it seriously. My grandmother took it seriously. She told me not to walk alone after nine.    
I always thought it was nonsense. Until the day they found old Anselmo's body.    
Anselmo owned the hardware store on Seventh Street. About sixty, heavy, loud. They found him collapsed in the back room, in the morning, when his nephew opened up. No blood, no struggle, nothing. Just his face a little swollen and a gray-silver powder on the floor near his mouth. The report came out a week later and said beryllium poisoning. I didn't even know what beryllium was. I looked it up on my phone. It's a metal, used in industry, very toxic if you breathe it or swallow it. That's when people remembered the legend. And the town started talking about Alastor Henderson again, except now nobody was laughing.    
Two weeks later Cleide died, the woman who worked at the clinic. Same thing. Silver powder, beryllium. Then Professor Nilton, the history teacher at the state school. They found him inside his car, in the school parking lot. That's when it got serious for me, because Professor Nilton was my teacher. And because the powder was on the passenger seat, like someone had sat there with him.    
The police said nothing. The chief was a guy named Mauro, who gave short interviews and always said the same line: "we're investigating, we won't speculate." People started doing what they always do. Gossip. Suspect lists. Pointing at everyone. A kid got beaten up in the square because someone said he was related to somebody.    
That's when I met the man.    
I worked afternoons at my uncle's grocery store, on Station Street. One day a guy walked in I'd never seen before. About thirty-five, thirty-eight. Thin, dark hair, ordinary clothes, gray shirt and jeans. Too polite. Bought two things, paid, and stood staring at the shelf like he was reading all of it. Then he turned to me and asked if I lived nearby. I said yes. He asked if I was afraid of the killer. I said no, that it was a legend. He smiled and said something I never forgot: "A legend is how people find a way to talk about someone they can't catch." Then he left. My uncle asked who that was. I said I didn't know.    
Three days later, the silver powder showed up at the grocery store. They found a little bit at the bottom of an open coffee can on the counter. My uncle got sick, went to the hospital, but he survived because he hadn't used the coffee yet. The police came, took statements, sealed the store for two days. I told them about the man. They asked me to describe him. I did. They nodded and wrote it down and I never heard anything about it again.    
After that I started paying attention. Not because I'm brave. Because I was scared and scared makes you notice things. I started writing down dates, places, what people said. I still have the notebook. It's not much. But it's more than the police had.    
Here's what I figured out. All the victims had something in common, and it wasn't what people said. It wasn't that they "deserved it." It was that they had all been on a town committee about ten years ago. The one that handled a land deal near the river. I found old minutes at the library, on microfilm, because the librarian let me look. Five names. Anselmo, Cleide, Nilton, and two others. One of the two others moved away years ago. The other one was a man named Rubens Vale, who used to be the town accountant.    
Rubens Vale died eight years ago. Car accident. Nobody thought much of it.    
But I found his obituary, and it listed his son. And the son's name in the school records, from when he studied here, was different from what he goes by now. The school records said his name was Álvaro Vale. But everybody in town, when they talked about the man who used to be the accountant's kid, called him something else. They called him Alastor. As a joke, at first, because he was a weird kid who liked chemistry and didn't talk much. Then the joke turned into the legend, and the legend turned into a monster, and the monster turned into a name that people whispered without knowing where it came from.    
I never told anyone this. Not the police. Not my uncle. I'm telling it now because I'm leaving Riverton next month and I don't want to carry it alone.    
The man in the grocery store was Rubens Vale's son. I'm almost sure. I saw him one more time, in January, at the bus station. He looked at me and nodded, like we knew each other. Then he got on a bus heading west. I didn't say anything. I just watched the bus leave.    
I don't know if he's still out there. I don't know if the legend is over. I just know that in Riverton, when the fog comes down, some people still swear they smell something sweet and metallic in the air. And I know that the worst person I ever met wasn't a monster from a story. He was a polite man in a gray shirt who bought two things at a grocery store and asked a kid if he was afraid.
