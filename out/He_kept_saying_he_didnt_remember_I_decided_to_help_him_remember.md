I don’t remember when people started laughing at me.

I only remember the first time I laughed with them.

It happened during lunch. Someone repeated something I had said earlier, changing their voice to sound slow and nervous. Everyone at the table laughed.

So I did too.

I thought that was the safest thing.

The person sitting beside me didn’t laugh.

He looked at me for a second, then told them to stop.

That was how we became friends.

At least, that is how I remember it.

For several years, we did almost everything together. We sat beside each other whenever we could. We walked home together. He came to my house most weekends, although he never liked staying late.

My mother used to joke that I spoke about him more than I spoke to him.

I didn’t understand what she meant.

He was the first person who made me feel like there wasn’t something fundamentally wrong with me.

Most people seemed to tolerate me in small amounts. They would talk to me during lessons or ask for help with something, but their eyes always drifted toward the door after a few minutes.

He never did that.

When I spoke, he listened.

When I became too loud, he told me quietly instead of laughing.

When I said something strange, he asked what I meant.

There is a difference between those things.

You notice it when you have spent most of your life being treated like a joke people are waiting to understand.

Things changed during our final years at school.

There wasn’t an argument.

I almost wish there had been.

An argument would have given me a single moment to hate. A sentence I could replay. A door slamming. Something solid enough to blame.

Instead, he slowly became harder to reach.

He started sitting with other people during lunch.

At first, he told me there wasn’t enough room at their table.

The next day, there were two empty chairs.

He said he hadn’t noticed.

I stood there holding my tray until one of them asked whether I was waiting for written permission to leave.

Everyone laughed.

He looked down at his food.

I laughed too.

That became a habit.

Whenever someone made a joke about me,  
I laughed before anyone could look at my face.

It made them think I didn’t care.

It also made it impossible to ask them to stop.

The jokes were small in the beginning.

The way I walked.

The way I pronounced certain words.

The fact that I always wore similar clothes.

The fact that I sent long messages.

The fact that I remembered things other people forgot.

One of them once asked why I knew exactly how many days it had been since the group first started meeting without me.

I told him it was because I had looked at the dates on their photographs.

He asked why I had looked through all of them.

I didn’t have an answer that sounded normal.

After that, they started calling me the archivist.

I suppose it could have been worse.

People have been called worse things.

That is what adults tell you when they want the conversation to end.

They say things like, “Ignore them.”

Or, “They’ll get bored eventually.”

Or, “You’re giving them the reaction they want.”

I tried not reacting.

They mistook that for permission.

Someone took a photograph of me eating alone and passed it around. Someone else created an account pretending to be me and posted things I had supposedly said.

Most of it was ridiculous.

Some of it was close enough to things I might have said that people believed it.

He knew the account wasn’t mine.

I asked him to tell them.

He said everyone already knew it was a joke.

I told him it wasn’t funny.

He said that was exactly why they kept doing it.

Then he asked me to stop messaging him about it.

That was the first time he used the word “obsessed.”

I remember it because I read the message so many times that the letters stopped looking real.

You’re getting obsessed with this.

Not them.

This.

As though the things they did existed separately from the people doing them.

I typed a reply, deleted it, typed another, and deleted that one too.

In the end, I sent him a single word.

Okay\*.\*

He didn’t answer.

The next morning, one of them repeated it to me in the corridor.

He said “okay” in a small voice, then pretended to wipe tears from his eyes.

That was when I understood that my former friend had shown them the messages.

I asked him about it after school.

He denied it.

I told him nobody else could have seen them.

He said he had shown one person because he was worried about me.

I asked which person.

He told me that wasn’t important.

I asked what exactly he had told them.

He said I was proving his point.

I followed him for half the walk home, trying to explain that I wasn’t angry. I only wanted to understand.

He kept walking faster.

Eventually, he turned around and told me to leave him alone.

There were other people nearby.

He said it loudly enough for them to hear.

I stopped walking.

He didn’t.

For a while, I thought things would return to normal if I gave him space.

I waited three days before sending another message.

Then five.

Then a week.

I wrote them in my notes first so they wouldn’t sound emotional.

I kept them short.

I removed every question that might have seemed accusatory.

I apologized for making him uncomfortable.

He never replied.

At school, the jokes became less subtle.

People would move their bags when I approached.

They whispered my name and then stopped speaking when I looked at them.

Once, I found a dead insect folded into my notebook.

Someone had written „*your girlfriend“* beneath it.

I didn’t have a girlfriend.

That was apparently part of the joke.

He was there when I found it.

I watched him look at the page.

His mouth moved slightly, like he was trying not to smile.

He says now that he wasn’t smiling.

I have asked him several times.

His answer hasn’t changed.

Maybe that is because it is true.

Maybe it is because people become very consistent when consistency is the only thing keeping them alive.

I left school before the year ended.

Officially, it was temporary.

I was supposed to complete my work from home until things settled down.

Nothing ever settles down when you remove yourself from it.

It simply continues without you.

I saw photographs of them at parties, sitting together in rooms where I had once been invited. I saw him wearing a shirt I had left at his house.

When I asked for it back, he blocked me.

That hurt more than it should have.

It wasn’t an expensive shirt.

I don’t think I even liked it.

But it meant something that he could wear a thing belonging to me while pretending I no longer existed.

I created another account so I could ask him why.

He blocked that one too.

The third time, he replied.

He told me I needed help.

I told him I had needed his help months ago.

He said that was not his responsibility.

That sentence stayed with me.

I think that is when something changed.

Not because he was wrong.

People are allowed to leave.

People are allowed to decide that knowing you has become exhausting.

People are even allowed to join the people who hurt you, as long as they call it moving on.

I understand that now.

What I did not understand was why only one of us was expected to accept consequences.

He could tell people private things about me.

He could let them photograph me.

He could laugh when they mocked the way I spoke.

He could tell everyone I was unstable, obsessive, frightening.

But when I wanted an explanation, that was harassment.

When I appeared outside his house, that was stalking.

When I waited near the place where he worked because it was the only place he couldn’t block me, that was a threat.

I never threatened him.

Not then.

I only wanted five minutes.

He called someone before I even reached the entrance.

I watched him through the glass while he spoke on the phone.

He looked afraid.

For a moment, that made me feel powerful.

I hated myself for enjoying it.

Then I hated him for making me into someone who could.

I went home before anyone arrived.

Several days later, I received a formal warning telling me not to contact him again.

It listed the accounts I had made.

It mentioned the times I had waited near his house.

It described several messages as threatening.

None of them contained threats.

I checked.

I still had screenshots.

One message said:  
„*You will understand eventually.“*

That could mean anything.

They decided what it meant because of what he had told them about me.

After that, people who had never spoken to me began looking at me as though they knew something.

The bullying stopped.

That sounds like a good thing.

It wasn’t.

The laughter had been easier.

At least laughter acknowledged that I was there.

Now people lowered their voices when I entered a room. They watched my hands. They stepped aside before I reached them.

I had become dangerous without doing anything dangerous.

That is the part nobody understands.

Once people decide what you are, resisting only confirms it.

If you defend yourself, you are angry.

If you stay quiet, you are hiding something.

If you apologize, you are admitting guilt.

So I stopped trying.

I found work that required very little conversation.

I moved into a small house that had belonged to a relative. It sits far enough from the road that people rarely notice when the lights are on.

The basement was already finished when I arrived.

The walls are thick.

The previous owner used it as a workshop.

For almost a year, I did nothing.

I went to work.

I came home.

I ate alone.

Sometimes I searched for him online, but less often than before.

He seemed happy.

He had new friends.

A partner for a while.

A different job.

He had become the kind of person who posted photographs of coffee, holidays and meals he probably didn’t finish.

Normal things.

I wondered whether he ever told anyone about me.

I found out when someone I used to know contacted me.

They said he still talked about what happened.

According to him, he had tried for years to help me.

He said I had become possessive.

He said I monitored where he went and became angry when he spent time with other people.

He said the jokes began only after I started making everyone uncomfortable.

That isn’t how I remember it.

There were parts I could not immediately disprove.

I had kept track of where he went, but only after he began lying about being busy.

I had read through every photograph his friends posted, but only because I wanted to understand why he preferred them.

I had waited outside his house more than once.

I had watched his bedroom window.

But I never intended to frighten him.

Intent should matter.

It doesn’t, apparently.

I asked the person who contacted me whether they believed him.

They didn’t answer.

A week later, they blocked me too.

That was when I started planning.

Not revenge.

I know how that sounds, given where he is now.

But revenge is about pain.

I wanted honesty.

I wanted him in a place where he couldn’t walk away halfway through a sentence.

Somewhere he couldn’t look at his phone or call someone else or say I was making him uncomfortable.

I wanted him to listen for once.

I spent months learning his routine again.

He had become less careful over the years.

People stop looking behind them when they believe the danger has passed.

He took the same route home most evenings. On certain nights, he left later and walked alone through a service road behind several closed buildings.

There were no houses nearby.

No cameras that I could see.

I know what you are thinking.

That I proved him right.

Perhaps I did.

But ask yourself this:

How many times can someone tell the world you are a monster before you begin wondering what monsters are allowed to do?

The night I took him, it was raining.

Not heavily.

Just enough to keep people indoors.

He didn’t recognize me at first.

I had lost weight. My hair was shorter. He had spent years trying not to see me.

When he finally did, he froze.

I had imagined that moment hundreds of times.

In most versions, he was angry.

Sometimes he laughed.

Once or twice, he apologized before I said anything.

He didn’t do any of those things.

He said my name.

I’m not including it here.

There are enough details already.

He said it quietly, almost like a question.

Then he looked toward the road.

I asked him not to run.

He ran.

It didn’t last long.

He woke up downstairs.

I was sitting across from him.

For the first few minutes, he pulled against the restraints so hard that the chair scraped across the floor.

I told him he would hurt himself.

He told me to let him go.

I said I would.

That was not a lie.

I still intend to.

Eventually.

The first thing I asked was whether he remembered the insect in my notebook.

He stared at me.

I asked again.

He said he didn’t know what I was talking about.

That hurt.

Not because he denied it.

Because he had forgotten.

There are things that divide your life into before and after, and to the person who caused them, they were a Tuesday.

I reminded him.

He said he hadn’t put it there.

I told him I knew that.

He said he hadn’t laughed.

I told him I had seen him.

He said he had been uncomfortable.

I asked what the difference was.

He didn’t answer.

For the next hour, we spoke about school.

At least, I spoke.

He cried.

Not constantly.

Mostly when I stood up or walked behind him.

I hadn’t touched him.

I kept telling him that.

It didn’t seem to help.

He apologized several times, but the apologies changed depending on what I asked.

First he was sorry for leaving me.

Then he was sorry for not stopping them.

Then he was sorry I had felt bullied.

I made him repeat that one.

*„I’m sorry you felt bullied.“*

I asked whether he heard it.

He said yes.

I asked whether he understood why it wasn’t an apology.

He nodded.

Then he gave me a better one.

It sounded almost real.

That was two nights ago.

He has been downstairs since then.

I’ve given him water.

Food too, although he hasn’t eaten much.

I placed a bucket near the chair before he woke up. He seemed embarrassed when he realized why it was there.

I told him embarrassment wouldn’t kill him.

He said neither would letting him use the bathroom.

That almost made me laugh.

He used to tell me the same thing whenever I asked to leave a room because people were mocking me.

*„It won’t kill you.“*

People say that as if survival is the only standard by which suffering should be measured.

Yesterday, he tried another approach.

He told me I had been his friend.

Not that he had been mine.

I noticed the difference.

He said he had cared about me but didn’t know how to help.

He said I frightened him long before the others started making jokes.

I asked him what I had done.

He mentioned the messages.

The waiting.

The way I became angry when he didn’t tell me where he was going.

He said he sometimes lied because telling me the truth caused arguments.

I told him arguments require two people.

He said that was exactly the kind of answer he meant.

For a moment, I almost untied him.

I don’t know why.

Maybe because he looked tired.

Maybe because there was something familiar in the way he said it.

Then I saw the red marks around his wrists.

I remembered him showing other people my messages.

I remembered the photograph of me eating alone.

I remembered the shirt.

I tightened the restraint that had started to loosen.

He began crying again.

Today, people came to my door.

Two of them.

They asked whether I had seen him.

I knew they would eventually.

I had prepared what to say.

I told them I hadn’t spoken to him in years.

That part was technically true until recently.

They asked where I had been on the night he disappeared.

I told them I had been home.

They asked whether anyone could confirm it.

I smiled before I could stop myself.

One of them noticed.

They didn’t search the house.

Not today.

After they left, I stood in the kitchen for several minutes, listening to their vehicle disappear.

Then I went downstairs.

He had heard the voices through the ceiling.

He asked why I hadn’t told them.

I said we weren’t finished.

He said there was nothing left to discuss.

I brought the box down with me.

It is an old metal box from the workshop.

He watched me place it on the table.

Until then, I think some part of him believed I only wanted to talk.

That belief disappeared when I opened it.

There isn’t anything unusual inside.

Nothing you couldn’t find in thousands of homes.

Tape.

Wire.

A hammer.

A pair of pliers.

Several clean cloths.

A small hand saw.

Things people use to repair what is broken.

He started saying my name again.

Over and over.

I asked him to stop.

He didn’t.

So I placed a strip of tape over his mouth.

I wish he could see his face.

Not because it is funny.

Because I recognize it.

It is the same expression I must have had every time I walked toward a table and watched someone move their bag onto the empty chair.

The moment when you realize the other person has already decided what is going to happen to you.

I sat across from him and explained the rules.

I told him I would ask questions.

I told him honesty would make everything easier.

I told him I didn’t want to hurt him.

He made a sound behind the tape.

I think he was trying to say that people who don’t want to hurt someone don’t tie them to chairs.

He always was good at making complicated things sound simple.

I removed the tape.

He asked whether I was going to kill him.

I said I hadn’t decided.

That was the first completely honest answer either of us had given all night.

Then he asked me something I wasn’t expecting.

He asked whether I believed I had done nothing wrong.

I told him that wasn’t the point.

He said it was the only point.

I stood up.

He flinched.

I picked up the pliers.

He began apologizing again.

Not for anything specific this time.

For everything.

For leaving.

For laughing.

For being afraid of me.

For telling people I was dangerous.

He said he had been wrong.

I asked which part.

He couldn’t answer.

That is the problem with apologies made under pressure.

You never know whether the person understands or simply wants the pain to stop.

So I put the pliers down.

Not because I forgave him.

Because I realized one night would never be enough.

He had years to teach me what it felt like to be powerless.

It seems only fair that I take my time returning the lesson.

The people who came today will probably return.

They might search the house next time.

I have prepared for that too.

The basement door is hidden behind shelving, and the walls do not carry sound well. He has learned that already.

Still, I know I cannot keep him here forever.

Sooner or later, I will have to decide what happens when we are finished.

He keeps insisting we already are.

He keeps telling me I’ve made my point.

But he is wrong.

My mother always said the first step to fixing anything was telling the truth.

I haven’t even started yet.
