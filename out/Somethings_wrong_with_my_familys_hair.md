I never thought I’d have a house at twenty four. Grandma left it to me in the will, this little ranch in a quiet neighborhood with a lawn that somehow stays green and a backyard Bean can run around in without us worrying about the neighbors. Lena cried when we got the keys. Not the big dramatic kind. Just the quiet kind that hits when something good actually sticks for once. We moved in with Bean, who sheds like it’s his full-time job. Golden hair everywhere. I vacuum twice a week and still find it stuck to the couch.

The first couple months felt almost stupidly normal. We argued about the dishwasher. Lena left her boots by the door after shifts and tracked mud across the kitchen. I complained. She rolled her eyes. Bean left fur on everything. Then one week the vacuum wasn’t full of dog hair the way it always was. The next week there was barely any. Lena noticed first. She ran her hand down his back and said he wasn’t blowing his coat. Usually this time of year he’s a mess. We figured dogs change. Seasons change. We had other things going on. Her overtime, the leaky faucet, the fact that every wall in this place is the same beige that makes me want to scream.

Lena found her first bald spot a few weeks later. A small one near her temple. Another at the crown. She showed me in the bathroom mirror one morning, frowning at her reflection. "Stress, right?" That last call was bad, a kid in a hot car. I kissed the top of her head and said "Yeah, stress.", and we left it there. Her job eats people alive. Everyone knows that. It takes a special kind of person to do what she does. The department’s doctor called it alopecia areata and gave her a cream and told her to sleep more. But it's Lena, she didn’t sleep any more than usual. Bean’s coat stayed weirdly clean. Soon my hairbrush started coming away without the usual tangle of dark hair. I noticed it one Tuesday while getting ready and just stared at the brush for a moment before letting it go.

By the time Lena’s hair was mostly gone, mine had started falling out. It came out in the shower first. Thick clumps I stuck onto the tile. I told myself it was sympathy shedding or whatever people say when their partner is going through something hard. I didn’t tell her right away. She was already walking around in beanies and pretending it didn’t bother her. I kept finding more hair in the drain every morning and started using a catcher. It filled up fast.

Tonight I was alone in the shower. Lena was about to leave for a night shift. I washed my hair the way I always do, fingers working through the thinning spots, and when I rinsed I watched a dark clump slide down the wall and stick near the faucet. I reached to peel it off and stopped.

It moved.

Not the way wet hair slides. It shifted on its own, a slow deliberate curl, and then it started crawling. Down the tile. Sideways. Toward the drain. I shut the water off. The hair kept going. It reached the edge of the drain, circled once like it was tasting the air, and slipped inside.

I crouched there staring into the dark circle. Something shifted below the grate. A soft wet rearrangement. Then a thick rope of hair shot up and wrapped around my head.

It tightened so fast I barely got a sound out. The pressure built in a way that felt wrong, too even, too strong, like someone placing too many rubber bands around a watermelon. I screamed Lena’s name without thinking. The bathroom door banged open. She was already in her uniform, she froze in the doorway unprepared for what she was about to see.

The creature kept squeezing. My vision went white at the edges. There was a sound like wet wood creaking, then splitting. The last thing I remember is a sudden pop and Lena’s screams when the pressure finally relieved, leaving a thick layer of viscera splattered against the bathroom walls. 

The mass of hair dropped Maya’s body and went searching for her scalp, slowly feeling around the floor with its ungodly tentacles reeking of wet dog. It found it quickly, pulling the loose skin and hair into itself the way a wave pulls seaweed under, and then it simply withdrew. Back down the drain. Leaving me standing in a bathroom that smelled like copper and Bean after a bath, I just kept staring at all that was left of Maya, the woman I loved. 

The police showed up twenty minutes later. Two dipshits I was supposed to share the third shift with tonight. Both looked tired in that same specific way two cops who’d already decided the investigation was a waste of time usually look. I tried to tell them what I saw. The words came out wrong. Too fast, Too detailed, Too frantic. One of them kept glancing at the blood on the walls and then back at my hands covered in blood from holding her in my grief. The other asked if I’d been drinking. I was speechless, they didn't believe me. The love of my life is dead, and they think I'm responsible. They cuffed my hands and put me in the back of a squad car. Someone read my rights all while Bean barked frantically from the living room, his yelps suddenly being cut off just as soon as they drove me away. 

The trial was short. No one believed the story about the drain and the hair. The medical examiner talked about blunt force and possible rage issues caused by the PTSD of being law enforcement. My lawyer tried the mental-health angle but it didn’t stick. The jury was unanimous and I was sentenced to Twenty five years to life.

The journal entry trembled under my pen. Day 47, My hair has begun to regrow. Soft dark fuzz along the scalp, longer strands are already reaching past my ears. I keep thinking about my shower this morning. I had been careful. I always am. After I washed, I gathered the loose hairs from the drain grate in the way I always do, and pressed the rogue strands against the wet tile wall so I could dispose of them properly later. After what happened to Maya, no way I'm leaving any of my hair anywhere close to the drain. I turned away to dry my face. When I looked back, the strand was gone from the wall. I thought it had simply fallen, but then I saw it.

It was moving. Slow, deliberate, the way something alive moves when it does not want to be noticed. The loose strands of my own hair had peeled itself from the tile and was sliding, inch by inch, toward the open drain. Not falling, crawling. The individual strands flexed and contracted like the body of a worm, pulling the rest of itself forward. When it reached the edge of the grate it did not stop. It poured itself through the holes, vanishing into the dark water below with a soft, wet sound that should not have been possible, not here anyway. Not behind these concrete walls and steel bars.

I stood there until the last strand disappeared. No one else was in the showers. No one saw it but me. I keep telling myself it was the light, or the water, or the medication they give me. But I know what I saw. And I know it will be coming back for me.
