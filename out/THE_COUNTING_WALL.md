*Adachi Ward, Tokyo, Japan*  
  
My job is to clean up after people nobody came to see.

In Japan, there’s a word for it. Kodokushi. A lonely death. Someone dies alone, at home, and no one notices. Not for days. Sometimes not for months.

I’ve been a special cleaner in Tokyo for eight years. Full suit. Filtered mask. Ozone machines. The families rarely come. Often, there’s no family left to call.

My own father died alone, in Aomori. He phoned me four times that last winter. I watched the screen light up. I told myself I’d call back when I wasn’t so tired. I never did.

So I take my time in other people’s rooms. It’s the only apology I know how to make.

The call came in October. Adachi Ward, north-east Tokyo. A public housing complex from the nineteen-sixties. Five floors, no elevator, long open balconies, laundry hanging out like flags. Room 507.

The tenant was Mr. Ōkubo. Seventy-four years old. A retired Metro conductor. He had been dead for forty-one days. Nobody found him by the smell, which is how it usually goes. A woman downstairs noticed a brown stain spreading across her ceiling.

I won’t describe what forty-one days of a Tokyo summer does. You can imagine it. Or you can choose not to.

What I want you to picture is the room. It was spotless. The futon folded in the corner. Shoes lined up at the entrance, toes facing the wall. One cup, one bowl, one pair of chopsticks in the dish rack. The room of a man who had been preparing, very politely, to be found.

The bedroom had no door, just a frame. And down the inside of that frame, from the floor to above my head, were pencil marks. Small vertical strokes. Four lines, and a slash through them. Groups of five. Hundreds and hundreds of groups, packed so tightly the wood looked like it had grown fur.

On the low table sat a notebook, the cheap kind with a green cover. Every page held the same line, in the same careful handwriting, with a date. “Two-forty a.m. Two taps on the wall. Tapped back.” Day after day. Page after page. And the last entry: “August twenty-ninth. Two taps. Tapped back. Tired.”

I count things when I’m uneasy. It’s a habit. So I counted. One thousand and ninety-seven entries in the notebook. One thousand, one hundred and six marks on the doorframe. I counted again. Nine extra.

I looked closer. The last nine marks were different. Darker. Pressed harder into the wood. Whoever made them wasn’t tired. Whoever made them was in a hurry, or afraid.

The medical examiner put Mr. Ōkubo’s death on the night of August thirtieth. The notebook stops on the twenty-ninth. Which means someone made nine marks on that wall after the man who kept the count was already dead.

That evening I carried the last bags down the stairwell, and a door opened on the fifth floor. 508. A tiny woman, nearly eighty, in a cardigan buttoned to the throat. Mrs. Tanabe. She looked at my suit, then at my face. “Is he gone now?”

I told her yes. She nodded. She didn’t cry. She asked, very politely, whether the wall would be cleaned too. The wall between their rooms.

I asked about the marks. She said she had never been inside. Then she looked at the floor. “I had the key.”

“We tapped, every night, for three years. Two taps at two-forty, when neither of us could sleep. It meant: I’m still here. On the thirty-first, he didn’t answer. I went in. I knew. I sat with him until morning. And I made his mark, because he would have made it.”

“Nine nights I went in. I couldn’t tell anyone. If they took him, the wall would just be a wall.” “Then it got too hot, and I was ashamed, and I stopped. I told myself, tomorrow I’ll call. Tomorrow. Forty-one days.”

Then she said the thing I haven’t been able to put down. “Those nine nights, Mr. Mori... when I tapped, he tapped back.”

I said nothing. She said, “I know what I found in that room. I know. And still, two taps.” “On the tenth night, nothing came back. That’s when I stopped.”

I thought: grief. A lonely mind, a thin wall, a hope so strong it makes its own sound. I told myself that. Then I went back to 507 to lock up, and I counted the marks one last time. One thousand, one hundred and seven.

One more. Fresh. Dark. I had been in that room since eight that morning, with my whole crew. Nobody had touched that doorframe.

I told my boss I needed a final inspection. I sat on the floor of Room 507, in the dark, with my back against the wall that Mrs. Tanabe called a wall.

Two-forty a.m. Two taps. From 508.

I didn’t decide to do it. My knuckle did. I tapped back. Silence. And then, from the opposite wall, 506, empty since spring. Two taps. Then from above. Two taps. From below. Two taps. All around me, through the concrete, a whole building, awake in the dark, answering each other. I’m still here. I’m still here. I’m still here.

In the morning, they found Mrs. Tanabe in her bed. Peaceful. The doctor put the time of death at about one-forty a.m. An hour before the taps I answered.

I don’t know whose hand it was. I don’t know who I answered. I know that the wall behind my own bed, in my own apartment, is an outside wall. Fourth floor. Corner unit. No neighbor on that side. Just air.

Last night at two-forty. Two taps.

I tapped back.

I’m keeping a tally now, in pencil, on the frame of my own door. I’m up to nine. I’m not afraid of the sound. I’m afraid of the night it stops. Because now I know what it is to be the one who waits. My father phoned me four times. I will never let it ring again.
