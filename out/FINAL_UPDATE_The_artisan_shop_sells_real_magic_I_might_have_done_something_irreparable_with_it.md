My mother wasn’t recovering, so I knew I had any choice but to go back to that damn shop. Who could have guessed the same girl would still be behind the counter?

She was lazily scrolling on her phone, slumped over the wooden surface and resting her head on her right forearm. I could now plainly see that the same wild ink covered the back of her left hand, reaching all the way to her knuckles. Her right hand, still clutching her vape, was white as snow.

“Bro, if you want to ask me out on a date, just say so,” she said without looking up. “I’m not my mom: I don’t bite.”

“I don’t have time for your bullshit,” I replied as I stomped up to her.

“Feisty,” she snarked, barely raising her head toward me. “What’s up?”

“My mother is dying!”

She rolled her eyes, evidently annoyed by my outburst.

“More like you’re killing your mother. Just stop thinking about the damn thing! Do you see me worrying about it? No, because I’m trying to help you!”

It took everything I had not to reach over the counter and grab her by the collar.  Some part of me knew threats wouldn’t convince her to help me, but most of me just worried she had other tricks up her sleeve.

“I can’t stop thinking about it, okay!” I conceded. “Every time I look at my mother, I see its blank stare looking at me from over her shoulder. I’m not even sure it’s just my imagination anymore.”

“Ha,” she snorted. “Nah bro, I promise you haven’t opened your own third eye; you’re not that guy. If you’re not wearing my gift, you ain’t peering through the veil.”

“Will you PLEASE stop!?” I begged. “I fucking get it. You’re so much smarter than me and all mysterious and shit. You’re SO great! Do you even have a heart? I’m just trying to save my mom.”

She hit the power button on her phone and laid it down on the counter. She brought the vape she kept in her other hand to her mouth, inhaled deeply and tilted her head away from me.

“Banishing,” she exhaled. “Normally they’re not worth it for shells because they’re so weak they can’t cling to us for very long. But you made yours into a real problem, so it’ll require a real solution.”

I slapped my palms against the counter in excitement.

“Can you do it?” I asked.

“Can a plumber unclog a toilet?” she replied sarcastically.

At that point, I had accepted I would never get a straight answer out of her. This was good enough.

“How much?”

“Dinner,” she shot back. “I already got a place in mind, and you should probably take out a loan: no prices on the menu and random French everywhere.”

“Sure, whatever,” I agreed.

She gave me a thumbs-up while she vaped again.

“Cool,” she puffed. “Shoot me your address, I’ll come by after close. I can probably do eight thirty.”

I wanted to argue and make her leave with me right now, but at that point, I knew better. I gave her the information and left.

 

The wait was agony. I sat in my room, trying my best to distract myself with textbooks and notes. Every time I heard bedsprings creak or the shuffling of bed covers from the other room, I swear I could feel its presence right behind me, its blank stare digging a hole in the back of my head.

“Honey,” whimpered a voice too weak to be my mother’s.

“Yeah?”

“Can you…” she pleaded. “Can you come here for a sec?”

I couldn’t. If I did, I knew I would see it. Then again, I might have been feeding it by having this very thought. I got up and walked over to my mother’s room.

From the doorway, she appeared only as a mound under the duvet. The mass seemed just a bit too big, as if she wasn’t alone under there.

I closed my eyes.

“Mom?” I called out tentatively.

I heard the rustling of sheets as she turned to face me.

“It’s getting bad,” she admitted. “Could you call an ambulance, please?”

Maybe this was the right path. A trip to the hospital, where they could keep her vitals in check while it faded. But what if it didn’t? There would be no getting her out once she got in if her health kept getting worse. And it would, because I couldn’t stop thinking about it. Even now, its grim visage appeared on the darkness of my eyelids. I imagined it baring two rows of rotten teeth at me.

I was terrified of it.

“I… uh… A friend of mine… a nurse is coming over after her shift. She said she’ll take a look. Maybe spare us an ordeal.”

“Please call,” she muttered.

“Mom,” I said, trying to sound confident. “It’ll be fine, I promise.”

“Fine,” she spat.

I heard her throw back the covers.

I opened my eyes to see the husk of my mother. Her skin was pale, except for two dark bags under her eyes. Her cheeks were sunken, and I knew the rest of her body, currently hidden inside a bulky wool robe, couldn’t be faring much better. While her muscles strained to lift her own torso, I saw it.

The corpse was lying over her, its head buried against her neck. As she rose from the bed, it slowly shifted its limbs around her, adjusting its grip like a child climbing its mother.

A moment later, she was alone once more, mustering all her strength simply to get up. She reached for her phone on the nightstand. I ran through the room and snatched it before she got to it.

I heard it chuckle at the scene.

She didn’t utter a word, but the look of disdain on her face, underlined by pooling tears, said it all: she couldn’t understand why I was leaving her to die.

"I’ll fix it, I swear,” I told her, hoping to reassure both of us.

She swung her arm in my direction, a pathetic attempt at grabbing her phone from my hands. Then, she fell on her back, seemingly resigning herself to the fate I had sealed.

I spent the rest of the evening sitting in the living room. The only sound in the apartment was the wheezing of my mother’s lungs. I was disgusted that every hiss relieved me.

 

The rhythm of *shave and a haircut, two bits* sounded against the door. I hurried over and threw it open.

The cashier stood in the hallway, still dressed just as casually. She held a black canvas backpack in her right hand, one that was covered in patches depicting anything and everything. Her bright red vape poked out from one of the side pockets.

She threw me a two-finger salute with her free hand.

“Yo,” she said as she invited herself in.

She put her bag on the floor and made a show of taking off her hoodie, deliberately catching the hem of her tank top and lifting it high enough to expose her stomach. Her body was covered with the same intricate work I had seen on her neck and hand: rows and rows of symbols and signs. The sole exception was her right forearm, the markings stopping clean before the elbow.

She knelt beside the bag and threw the flap open. From it, she pulled out a black candle, a weathered leather notebook, multiple jars filled with what looked to me like rocks and herbs, and a small gray metal cube etched with symbols resembling her tattoos, all of which she piled on the floor.

She tapped the pockets of her jeans.

“Shit,” she exclaimed. “I should pick up smoking again. Tell me you’ve got a light.”

I ran to the kitchen and opened every cabinet and drawer until I found a wand lighter, which I brought back to her. She snatched it out of my hands and added it to her hoard.

“Now,” she resumed, “I want you to find my gift and put it on: you’re going to help me.”

“What? No!”

“I could feel that thing from the moment I stepped onto this floor. I don’t know how the fuck you managed it, but you pissed your pants so much it’s turning into a full-on haunt. I’m not dealing with this shit alone.”

For a moment, she almost seemed serious.

“I just did my nails,” she added, proudly presenting her black claws.

I went to my room and lifted my mattress to uncover the box I had hidden away. I took a deep breath and seized it. I rushed back to her before opening it and putting the necklace on.

As soon as the stone landed on my chest, I understood what she had meant.

There was a new heft to the world. Something I can only describe as walls erected around me to keep me from the air.

I couldn’t breathe.

I didn’t want to breathe.

I had ruined everything: that truth imprinted itself on my soul.

Then, a spark of warmth ignited my hand, and a blaze crawled its way up my arm, burning away the cold certainty of death.

She had wrapped her right hand around mine.

“Just focus on me, okay?” she whispered so loudly it shattered the walls, flooding my body with oxygen.

I looked up from her pale hand, and I finally understood just how little I did.

Her tattoos were building blocks. Pieces of a forbidden reality assembled into her skin. I still couldn’t understand them, but I knew for a fact she existed on the brink between here and … elsewhere.

She walked the line so easily, using her steady hand and her yellowed smile to ground me to this side.

“Can you help me with the tools?” she asked while gesturing with her chin toward the stuff she had laid out on the floor. Even with my new senses, it all seemed to be mundane enough, all except for the small cube, which she picked up herself.

Following her instructions, I dragged the dining table into the middle of the apartment and arranged bowls of herbs and salt on its surface, building an altar with the black candle burning at its center.

She had me stand across the table from her and take her right hand, while she clutched the cube in her left.

“Let my words fill you,” she ordered. “Live them.”

“Spirit!” she bellowed with the voice of thunder. “Show yourself! By my birthright and by the power I have made my own: I command you to appear before me.”

I sensed a pull from her being, tugging at my very existence like a child pulling at its parent’s pants.

The thing must have experienced something far more intense, because it reacted immediately. I saw cracks appear in her iron confidence as a loud bang resounded from my mother’s room.

“How much did you feed that thing?” she grumbled, visibly surprised.

A quick succession of thumps shook the floor behind me. I let go of the clerk’s hand and turned around to face my mother as she burst out of her room.

Her arms were limp at her sides; her knees were bent inward and her head was slumped over. She shouldn’t have been standing. Her body twitched as the thing inside her struggled against her skin. Its withered skull rose from my mother's, splitting from her head to fix me with eyes now alive with anger. Then my mother’s head jerked upward and swallowed the creature back up, their faces briefly overlapping before becoming one—a visage twisted by rage.

“Mom?” I asked.

It rushed me, darting across the room in an instant. I had the foresight to put my arms up to my face as it tried to reach for my throat, so it wrapped my mother’s arms around my neck, pinning my raised forearms against my throat. Unable to strangle me directly, it squeezed me as hard as it could.

“Mom, it’s me, please,” I begged.

The creature’s hold tightened, now crushing me with unnatural strength.

“Mom, I’m scared,” I cried, tears streaming down my face.

Something flickered in her eyes. It was no more than the flash of a single star in the endless void, but I recognized it instantly: a glint of my mother in her own eyes.

An inked hand drove the metal cube into the side of her skull. As my mother collapsed, she tore herself free from the corpse, which remained suspended in the air. Its bones snapped and bent inward, its limbs folding against its torso as an invisible force compressed it into the cube. Before it disappeared completely, it reached a pleading hand up to me. There was no malice left inside of it, only the last prayers of a man twice condemned.

I snapped the necklace’s cord, threw it as far as I could and knelt beside my mother.

“Relax,” the cashier laughed. “She’ll be fine. A bit tired and maybe a concussion, but she’ll live. Quick thinking with the crying son shtick. Always works with mothers.”

I stared daggers at her.

“Don’t look at me like that,” she said while juggling the cube in her hand. “This thing doesn’t work on fully anchored spirits; I needed her back in control.”

No matter how much I wanted to hate her, she had done it: she had saved my mother. Her breathing was growing steadier and color returned to her skin.

“What happened to it?” I asked, its final plea playing back in my mind.

“You thought I would let a perfectly good soul move on?”

She headed for the door, slipped the cube into her backpack and closed it. Then she slung the bag over one shoulder and threw her hoodie over the other.

“Could you pack up the components and bring them with you on Friday when you come pick me up at the store? I’ll close at four to prep,” she said, turning back toward me. “I got a reservation. Had to drop my mom’s name and everything. Oh, and wear a fucking suit, please.”

“Whatever,” I mumbled while dialing 911.
