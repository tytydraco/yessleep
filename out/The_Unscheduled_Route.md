If you ever take a job as an overnight medical courier, and your dispatch tablet chimes with a pickup that is not on your printed manifest, turn your car around and drive straight home. Three nights ago, I picked up a sealed silver cooler from a clinic that burned to the ground in 1998. When I checked my dashcam footage the next morning, I realized I was not the one driving my car.

I work the graveyard shift for a regional logistics contractor in rural Maine, running routes between small coastal clinics and the central diagnostic laboratory in Bangor. The hours are brutal, eleven at night until six in the morning, and the pay is barely enough to cover rent and gas. Still, when you are dealing with chronic insomnia and a mountain of debt, driving empty highways in the middle of the night feels easier than lying awake in a dark apartment staring at the ceiling.

I drive a beat-up 2007 Subaru Outback with two hundred thousand miles on the odometer, a rattling dashboard, and an exhaust manifold leak that smells faintly of burnt oil whenever I sit idling at a red light. The company gives us three strict rules on our first day of training, and they make you sign a liability waiver acknowledging every single one of them. First, scan every barcode upon pickup and delivery so the GPS timestamp is logged. Second, never break the red plastic tamper seals on the transport coolers. Third, and most importantly, never open a cooler under any circumstances. You do not ask what is inside, you do not check the contents, and you do not deviate from the route generated by the dispatch server.

For eight months, the job was completely uneventful. Most nights, I hauled racks of routine blood vials, pathology slides, and tissue biopsies packed in dry ice. I drank lukewarm gas station coffee, listened to grainy AM talk radio, and watched the endless wall of black pine trees roll past my headlights.

Tuesday night started just like any other. A thick coastal fog had rolled inland off the Atlantic just after midnight, swallowing Route 9 in a dense, suffocating grey blanket. Visibility was down to maybe fifty feet, and the yellow center lines vanished into the mist twenty yards ahead of my bumper. I had already completed my three scheduled pickups in Machias and Cherryfield, and the insulated coolers were strapped neatly into the backseat with bungee cords.

At 2:18 AM, my company tablet mounted on the dashboard flashed bright amber and let out a sharp, two-tone chime.

I glanced over, squinting against the glare of the screen. A new job ticket had overridden my navigation map.

*PRIORITY STAT PICKUP. CLIENT: SAINT JUDE RESPIRATORY ANNEX. ADDRESS: 412 OLD MILL ROAD. DESTINATION: BANGOR CENTRAL LAB, BAY 4. HAZARD BONUS: $120.00.*

I frowned, slowing down as I approached the next mile marker. I knew every clinic, nursing home, and urgent care center within a hundred miles of the coast, and I had never heard of Saint Jude Respiratory Annex. Worse, there was no cellular reception out in this stretch of timberland, which meant the dispatch server should not have been able to push an unscheduled update to my tablet in the first place.

I tapped the headset button on my steering wheel to call Ray, the night dispatcher back at the hub. The line connected, but instead of Ray's gravelly voice, all I heard was low, hollow static, underneath which was a faint, rhythmic clicking sound, like someone tapping a fingernail slowly against a plastic microphone.

"Ray?" I said into the mic. "You there? My tablet just threw a stat pickup off Old Mill Road. Is this legit?"

The clicking stopped. For three seconds, there was dead silence on the line, followed by a soft, wet intake of breath. Then the call dropped. Before I could reach over to hit the decline button on the tablet, the screen flickered violently and switched to *ROUTE ACCEPTED. TURN LEFT IN 400 FEET.*

I hesitated, my foot hovering over the brake pedal. Every instinct told me to ignore it and keep heading north toward Bangor. I had been running on three hours of sleep a day for nearly a month, my eyes burned from staring into the fog, and a dull ache throbbed behind my temples. On the other hand, a hundred and twenty dollars was nearly half a week's pay, and if I ignored a logged priority ticket, corporate would dock my bonus for the entire month.

Grumbling under my breath, I hit my turn signal and swung the Subaru onto Old Mill Road.

The asphalt ended almost immediately, giving way to a narrow, rutted logging track hemmed in by towering black spruce trees. Branches scraped like dry fingers against my passenger-side doors as the road wound deeper into the woods, climbing a steep ridge where the fog grew so thick my high beams bounced right back into my eyes. After nearly four miles of bouncing over washed-out gravel, the trees suddenly parted, opening onto a cracked, weed-choked parking lot.

Sitting at the far edge of the lot was a low, single-story cinderblock building. Most of the windows were boarded up with weathered sheets of grey plywood, and ivy crawled thickly up the concrete walls. There were no cars in the lot. No exterior security lights. Only a single, dim amber bulb buzzed weakly inside a glass entrance vestibule at the center of the building.

I killed the headlights, left the engine idling, and grabbed my handheld barcode scanner from the center console. As soon as I stepped out of the car, the cold hit me. It was late October, so a chill was expected, yet the air here felt unnatural, heavy and damp, smelling strongly of stagnant creek water, wet ash, and ozone.

I walked across the broken pavement toward the glass double doors. One of the doors stood ajar by about two inches, leaking a thin strip of pale, flickering fluorescent light onto the concrete steps. I pulled the door open and stepped inside.

The vestibule was tiny, smelling of mildewed plaster and cold stone. Directly beneath the buzzing fluorescent tube, sitting on a rusted metal folding chair, was a woman.

I stopped dead in my tracks, my hand tightening around the barcode scanner.

She looked to be in her mid-twenties, painfully thin, with collarbone and ribs protruding sharply beneath skin the color of wet chalk. She was wearing nothing but a damp, oversized white cotton hospital gown that had slipped off her left shoulder, exposing a pale, bruised-looking collarbone. Long strands of soaking-wet dark hair were plastered flat against her skull and cheeks, dripping slowly onto her bare thighs. Thick, smudged streaks of black makeup ran down from beneath her eyes like dried ink.

She did not blink. She did not shiver, even though my own breath was pluming in the freezing air of the lobby. Resting on the cracked linoleum floor between her bare, mud-stained feet was a heavy silver metal transport cooler, the old-fashioned kind with thick steel latches.

"Ma'am?" I said, my voice sounding strangely loud in the cramped space. "I am with BioTrack Logistics. I got a priority dispatch for a pickup here."

She did not look up at my face. Slowly, stiffly, she tilted her head to the left at an angle that looked painful, staring fixedly at the base of my throat.

"You took too long," she whispered. Her voice sounded dry and papery, like two dead leaves rubbing together on concrete. "He is getting cold."

A chill crawled down the back of my neck. "Is there a nurse on duty who needs to sign the manifest?" I asked, glancing toward the dark, padlocked interior doors behind her.

"Nobody is left," she murmured, her grey lips barely moving. "Just take the box. And turn the heater off in your car. He does not like the heat."

I wanted to turn around and walk out right then. Nothing about this place was right. There were no computers, no reception desk, no electricity beyond that single buzzing light overhead. Yet my eyes dropped to the silver cooler at her feet. Aff标准ed to the top of the lid was a standard BioTrack barcode label and a red plastic tamper seal.

Deciding I would get out of there in five seconds flat and let my supervisor sort it out in the morning, I stepped forward, aimed my scanner at the barcode, and pulled the trigger. The scanner let out a flat, sluggish beep, and the green confirmation light flashed.

When I bent down to grip the metal handle of the cooler, the woman reached out. Her bare fingers brushed across the back of my right hand. Her skin was like ice, slick with cold sweat, leaving a faint, greasy grey smudge across my knuckles. I jerked my hand back, hoisted the cooler off the floor, and hurried out through the glass doors without looking back.

The cooler was heavy. Easily forty or fifty pounds, far heavier than standard lab samples, and as I carried it across the lot, something inside shifted with a dull, wet thud.

I threw it onto the front passenger seat, buckled the seatbelt across the lid, and put the Subaru in reverse. As I backed out of the lot, my headlights swept across the glass doors of the vestibule. The metal folding chair was empty. The woman was gone.

I drove down Old Mill Road twice as fast as I had come up, tires spitting gravel until I finally hit the smooth asphalt of Route 9. My heart was hammering against my ribs, and the dull headache behind my temples had sharpened into a steady throbbing behind my eyes.

Beside me on the passenger seat, the small digital thermometer built into the lid of the silver cooler suddenly flickered to life. In glowing red numbers, it read *36°F*. As I watched, the numbers began dropping. *34. 31. 28.* Within two minutes, a thin layer of white frost spiderwebbed across the brushed aluminum casing, and a wave of freezing air radiated off the box, filling the cabin with that same suffocating stench of stagnant creek water and wet ash.

Without even thinking about it, my hand reached out and switched off the car's heater. The sudden silence of the fan made the hum of the tires on the wet highway feel deafening.

Ten miles north of the turnoff, I heard it.

It came from inside the silver cooler. A soft, wet *thump*. A pause of two seconds. Then *thump-thump*.

It sounded exactly like a slow, heavy heartbeat muffled through thick insulation. A moment later, there was a faint scraping sound, like wet fingernails dragging slowly across styrofoam.

My grip tightened on the steering wheel until my knuckles turned white. "It is just dry ice shifting," I muttered aloud, trying to steady my breathing. "Just gas expanding in the seal."

*Thump. Thump-thump.*

I glanced up into the rearview mirror, checking the road behind me. The rear windshield was completely fogged over from the inside, even though the air in the car was freezing. As I stared at the glass, the headlights of an oncoming logging truck crested the hill ahead, flooding the interior of my Subaru with blinding white light for a fraction of a second.

In that brief flash of glare, reflected in the rearview mirror, I saw a pale, hollow-eyed face leaning forward between the front headrests, inches behind my right shoulder.

I slammed my foot onto the brake pedal. The tires locked on the wet pavement, sending the Subaru skidding sideways onto the gravel shoulder before jerking to a halt. Gasping for air, I unbuckled my seatbelt and whipped around, shining my flashlight across the backseat.

Nothing. Only my three regular plastic coolers sat strapped tightly in place. My breath came in ragged, shallow gasps as I aimed the beam down at the floorboards.

There, stamped clearly onto the dark carpet directly behind my driver's seat, was a single, soaking-wet bare footprint.

I did not get out of the car. I slammed the transmission back into drive, floored the accelerator, and drove the remaining forty miles into Bangor well over the speed limit, gripping the wheel with both hands and refusing to look at the mirror again.

By the time I pulled into the brightly lit receiving bay at Bangor Central Lab just before four in the morning, my shirt was soaked with cold sweat. I hauled the frosted silver cooler out of the passenger seat, carried it up to the night intake window, and pressed the buzzer.

Greg, an older night technician who had worked the receiving desk for fifteen years, slid open the glass partition with a tired nod. He took one look at the metal cooler I set on the stainless-steel counter and frowned.

"Where did you dig this relic up, Marcus?" Greg asked, leaning forward over his keyboard. "We haven't used heavy-gauge aluminum transport boxes since the late nineties."

"Saint Jude Annex off Old Mill Road," I said, my voice trembling slightly as I wiped my sweaty palms on my jeans. "It came through as a priority stat on my tablet."

Greg stared at me for a long moment, his expression shifting from mild curiosity to confusion. He picked up his corded laser scanner and zapped the faded barcode sticker on the lid.

The computer terminal behind him let out three sharp, dissonant error tones. On the green monitor, a red warning box flashed across the screen: *ARCHIVE RECORD 10-14-1998. FACILITY DECOMMISSIONED. INVALID MANIFEST.*

Greg slowly set the scanner down on the counter. The color had drained from his face.

"Marcus, is this some kind of sick joke?" he asked quietly. "Saint Jude Respiratory Annex burned down twenty-eight years ago. Faulty boiler in the basement. Four patients and a night nurse died in the east wing before the fire trucks could even make it up that gravel road. They condemned the land in ninety-nine. Nobody has been out there since."

"No," I said, shaking my head rapidly. "No, I was just there. There was a woman in the lobby, she gave me the box, I scanned it on my tablet—"

I reached for my company tablet, which I had carried in under my arm, and tapped the trip log. The screen was completely blank between 2:15 AM and 3:55 AM. According to the GPS tracker, my car had never left Route 9.

Greg pointed a shaking finger at the front of the silver cooler. "And even if it were a real pickup, I couldn't take it. Look at the tamper seal."

I looked down. The red plastic security tag threaded through the steel latch was snapped clean in half.

Greg backed away from the counter, reaching for the wall phone. "Take that thing out of here, Marcus. Seriously. You look like hell. Go home, get some sleep, and I will tell dispatch you went home sick."

Heart pounding in my throat, I grabbed the cooler, hurried back down the concrete ramp into the empty underground parking garage, and set the box on the hood of my Subaru directly beneath a humming fluorescent light fixture.

I had to know. My hands were shaking so violently I could barely unclip the rusted metal latches. Taking a deep, ragged breath, I threw the heavy lid open.

There were no blood vials inside. No surgical trays. No dry ice.

The interior of the cooler was packed halfway to the rim with freezing, pitch-black river mud and wet pine needles. Resting neatly on top of the mud was a soaked, crumpled white cotton hospital gown, and beside it, a brittle, yellowed plastic patient wristband.

With trembling fingers, I picked up the wristband and held it under the fluorescent light. Printed across the faded plastic in block black letters was a name.

*VANCE, MARCUS.*

And beneath it, in the admission date field: *10/14/1998.*

Nausea surged up my throat, hot and sour. I dropped the wristband onto the hood, stumbled back to the driver's side door, and climbed inside. Reaching up behind the rearview mirror, I popped the micro-SD card out of my personal windshield dashcam and slid it into the media slot on the side of my work tablet.

My fingers were so numb I had to tap the screen three times to open the video folder. I scrolled down to the file timestamped *02:34 AM* and hit play.

The grainy infrared footage filled the screen. Through the windshield, my Subaru turned off the highway onto Old Mill Road, bouncing through the fog until the headlights illuminated the end of the clearing.

There was no glass vestibule. There was no buzzing amber bulb.

Standing at the edge of the weed-choked lot were the collapsed, blackened ruins of a burned-out cinderblock building, its charred roof beams open to the night sky.

On the video, the driver's door of my car swung open. I stepped out into the beam of my own headlights. I walked calmly to the back of the Subaru, popped the hatch, and pulled an old, dented silver cooler out of my own trunk. Without hesitating, I walked straight into the pitch-black ruins of the burned building.

For twenty-two minutes, the camera recorded nothing but empty, fog-shrouded pine trees swaying in the dark.

At 02:56 AM, I walked back out of the ruins carrying the cooler. My posture on the video was rigid, unnatural, my head tilted slightly to the left. I opened the passenger door, placed the cooler onto the seat, and climbed behind the wheel.

Before putting the car into gear, the version of me on the screen turned his head slowly and stared directly into the dashcam lens. In the pale green night-vision glare, my eyes were wide and completely unblinking, thick black smudges smeared beneath them like dried mascara. My pale lips stretched into a thin, joyless smile, and through the cabin microphone, my own voice whispered softly into the quiet car:

*"Keep driving. He still thinks he is awake."*

I dropped the tablet onto the floorboard, gasping for air as my chest tightened. Slowly, terrified of what I would see, I raised my eyes to the rearview mirror.

Sitting calmly in the backseat between my strapped-in coolers, the woman in the wet white hospital gown was staring straight back at me. And in the reflection of the mirror, her face was gone, replaced entirely by my own.
