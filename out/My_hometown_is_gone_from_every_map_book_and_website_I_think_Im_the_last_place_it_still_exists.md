So this is going to sound weird, but I can't find anything about Clayton, Arizona on the web. Like, the town doesn't even show up on Google Maps or Apple Maps or even Waze. If you don't know where or what Clayton is, it's a small town in the north of Arizona, about 30 minutes west of Navajo Nation. It's not a big town, pretty small actually, with I think no more than maybe 2,000 people. Old copper mining town first settled back in the 1800s; most people eventually left for Phoenix or Flagstaff after the mines dried up in the 30s. I know all this because I did a report on them for my Arizona History class junior year. I got a B+. My teacher wrote "Great primary source work!!" across the top in red pen, and I was kind of proud of that, because that man did not hand out exclamation points.

Clayton High doesn't show up anywhere either. Neither does my history teacher. I checked. I've checked a lot of things this week.

Okay. Let me back up.

I grew up in Clayton. I lived there from the day I was born until I left for college five years ago, and I've lived down in the Valley ever since. My mom still lives there, in a little stucco house a couple blocks off Main with a carport she painted turquoise one summer because she said the town needed some color. There's an old blue Ford pickup under it that's been there as long as I can remember. My dad's, I always figured. He moved to Phoenix when I was little.

Mom and I talk every Sunday night. That's our thing. She tells me who's sick and who's pregnant and who got arrested, and I pretend I've been eating vegetables.

About six weeks ago she told me some mining company out of Canada had been poking around the old Ruth Ann mine on the north end of town. Copper prices are insane right now, apparently, so all these old claims are suddenly worth something again. There were trucks on Main Street. Surveyors at the diner every morning. She said it was the most excitement Clayton had seen since the twins down the street set the football field on fire. I said that was great, jobs and everything, and I meant it.

Two weeks after that, she told me they'd drilled through the concrete into Number Six.

I asked what Number Six was. She said, "The deep one. The one they closed." Then she didn't say anything for so long I thought the call had dropped. Then she said, "It's been real quiet up here since, baby. The birds stopped."

I asked what she meant. She laughed and said she was being silly, she was getting old, and had I been eating vegetables.

Last Sunday she didn't call.

That has never happened. Not once in five years. I called her at nine, then ten, then eleven. Monday morning I got the recording: *the number you have reached is not in service.* So I figured I'd call the diner on Main, because she's eaten breakfast there basically every day of my life. I didn't have the number saved, so I googled it.

That's where this actually starts. Because there's no diner. There's no Clayton.

I want to be clear about how hard I looked, because I know the first comment is going to be "you spelled it wrong."

Google gives me Clayton, New Mexico. Clayton, Missouri. Clayton County, Georgia. Nothing in Arizona. Wikipedia has a list of every populated place in the county, and Clayton isn't on it. The county website doesn't list a Clayton school district or a Clayton fire department. I tried the state archives. I tried the old newspaper databases through my college library, which is how I found out my login still works. Nothing. Not one mention of the Ruth Ann mine, either, and that thing had a stamp mill you could see from the highway.

Then I started looking for people.

My grandma died when I was nine. She's buried in Clayton Cemetery under a pink granite headstone with a hummingbird carved on it. I know that because I've helped my mom pull weeds off that grave every Memorial Day of my life. One of those grave-finder websites has her. Same name, same dates, same hummingbird.

Buried in Flagstaff.

My best friend Marisol had an Instagram she'd been posting to since seventh grade. It's gone. Not private. Gone, like the username was never taken. Every photo she tagged me in is gone off my profile. Our high school group chat is still in my phone, but every message in it is from me. Five years of me talking to nobody, and the weird part is it doesn't even read weird. I'm just saying stuff. Like I was talking to myself the whole time.

I called every Clayton number in my contacts. Most were disconnected. One went to a very nice man in Ohio who has had that number for nine years and has never been to Arizona.

So on Saturday I drove up there.

If you've never driven it: you head north out of Flagstaff toward the Navajo Nation, and about forty minutes up there's a turnoff on the left for the county road. It runs west across the plateau, all juniper and cinder and red dirt, 41 miles across to the highway on the other side. Clayton sits right about in the middle, at mile 22.

I know that because when I was a kid, my mom made a game out of counting mile markers on the way home from Flagstaff. She'd say "What's the magic number?" and I'd yell "TWENTY-TWO!" and she'd honk the horn when we passed it. Every single time. Even when I was seventeen and begged her to stop.

The turnoff is still there. The sign just has a county road number on it now, like it apparently always has. There's no little green sign underneath that says CLAYTON 22.

I drove it anyway. Mile 1, mile 2. Sagebrush, a cattle guard, the dry wash where Marisol and I used to... where we used to go. Mile 12, mile 13. At mile 19 you're supposed to come over a rise and see the water tower, with the old stamp mill up on the hill behind it.

I came over the rise at mile 19 and saw more road.

Mile 20. Mile 21. Mile 22, and I slowed to a crawl and stared at that little green marker like it was going to explain itself. Juniper on both sides. Mile 23. Mile 24.

At mile 27 I hit the stop sign at the highway.

The road is 27 miles long now. I turned around and drove it back to make sure. Twenty-seven. The markers go 1 through 27 with no gaps and nothing skipped. There's no spot where the asphalt changes color or the fence posts stop lining up. It's like somebody cut fourteen miles out of the middle of Arizona and the two ends just healed together. Like skin closing over a splinter.

I went back to mile 22, pulled onto the shoulder, and got out.

This is the part I keep coming back to, and I don't know how to describe it so it makes sense. It was quiet. Not normal desert quiet, where there's still wind and bugs and your engine ticking. My engine *was* ticking. I could see the juniper moving in the wind. But the second I stepped off the shoulder into the brush, it all just went away. My boots weren't making any noise on the cinders. I could feel them crunching. I couldn't hear it.

I yelled for my mom. I yelled it as loud as I have ever yelled anything.

It didn't echo. It didn't even feel like it left me. It was like screaming into a pillow, except the pillow was the whole sky.

I got back in my car and locked the doors, like that would do anything, and opened my camera roll, because I needed to see her face.

I have a picture from last Christmas. My mom and me in her kitchen in matching reindeer sweaters, her arm around my waist, both of us cracking up because the timer went off before she was ready.

It's still on my phone. It's me in the reindeer sweater, standing in the desert at night, blown out white by the flash. Juniper and cinders, and nothing behind me but black. My arm is curved out around empty air at exactly the height of my mom's shoulders. I'm laughing at somebody.

There's no gap. That's what I can't get past. It's not like she's been cut out. It's a perfectly normal photo of a person standing alone in the desert at night, hugging nothing, laughing at nothing. And I don't remember ever standing there. I remember her kitchen. I remember the timer.

My graduation photo is me in a cap and gown in an empty field. My prom photo is me all dressed up with my hand held out, holding on to nobody.

I drove home without stopping. I don't really remember the drive.

Here's where it gets worse.

My roommate Devin was still up when I got home, and I told him everything. Probably too much, probably too fast. He listened, he really did. He's a good guy. He asked good questions. Then about ten minutes in he squinted at me and said, "Sorry, where'd you say you grew up?"

I told him. He nodded.

Five minutes later he asked again.

I wrote CLAYTON, ARIZONA on a sticky note in big block letters and stuck it on his laptop. He looked at it, looked at me, and said "Okay" in this careful voice, the one you use with somebody who's having an episode. The next morning I found the sticky note in the kitchen trash. He doesn't remember throwing it out.

So it isn't just that the town is gone. People can't *hold on* to it. It slides right off them.

And then I found out it isn't just the town.

I keep my important papers in a folder in my closet. My high school diploma is in there. It's from some online charter school I've never heard of. According to my transcript, I was homeschooled through it for all four years, which I guess explains why there's no Clayton High. My financial aid forms list no parent information. My emergency contact at work is a blank field.

The world isn't leaving holes. It's filling them in. It's coming up with reasons for everything to make sense without Clayton in it. Grandma's in Flagstaff. I was homeschooled. My Christmas picture is just a weird picture of me in the desert.

My birth certificate was still right, though. Born at the hospital in Flagstaff (Clayton never had one), and my mom's name typed right there in the box. Her real name, spelled right. I sat on my closet floor holding that piece of paper and cried, because it was the first thing all week that was still true.

The next morning the box for mother's name was empty.

Not whited out. Not scratched off. Just a box nobody ever typed anything into. Which is impossible. You can't get a birth certificate like that. I think that's what scares me most. It's stopped bothering to make sense.

I need to tell you about Mr. K.

For that Arizona History report, my teacher wanted a primary source. So I went down to the senior center, which used to be the old company store, and asked if anybody there remembered the mines. They pointed me to an old man everybody called Mr. K, because his last name had about four more consonants than anybody wanted to deal with. He was ninety-three. He'd been eight years old in 1934, the year the Ruth Ann shut down, and his father had worked Number Six.

I recorded the whole interview on my phone. That recording is gone. I checked. My report is still in my Google Drive, believe it or not, same title, same date. It's three pages about copper mining in Jerome. It's not even good.

So this is from memory. I'm getting it as close as I can.

Mr. K said Number Six was the Ruth Ann's deepest shaft, down around 2,100 feet. In the spring of 1934, a crew blasted through a face down there and found a hole.

"Not a cave," he said. "Caves got walls. Caves got a floor."

His father told him the men shined their lamps into it and the light just went in. It didn't land on anything. Not rock, not dust hanging in the air. It went in and didn't come back. They threw a rock and never heard it hit. A man on the crew named Ray tied a rope around his waist and walked in maybe ten feet while the others held the line.

They pulled him out after about a minute. He walked out on his own two feet. He was fine.

And nobody knew who he was.

"Pop said looking at him was like looking at a word in a language you don't speak," Mr. K told me. "You know it means something. You just can't get at it."

Ray's wife didn't know him. His kids didn't know him. He went around town for a few days knocking on doors, and people were polite to him the way you're polite to a stranger. Then one day he wasn't around anymore, and nobody wondered where he'd gone.

The only reason Mr. K's father remembered him at all was a little tally book. Every morning before a shift, he wrote his crew's names down in it. He'd look at Ray's name on that page and he could almost hold on to him. Almost.

The company poured forty feet of concrete down Number Six that summer. They told everybody the ore had played out.

"Copper didn't run out, honey," Mr. K said. "We stopped going down."

I asked him what he thought was down there. I was sixteen, and I thought this was a fun spooky bit for my report.

He thought about it for a long time. Then he said, "It isn't dark down there. Dark is something. That's *before*."

He said concrete doesn't stop it. It just slows it down. He said that's where everybody went, all those families who "moved to Phoenix" after the mines closed. "You ever get a letter from one of them? A Christmas card? Anybody in this town ever have a Phoenix address for a single one of them?" He said he'd been asking for eighty years.

"People don't leave Clayton," he said. "They just stop having been here."

Then he said the thing I've thought about every hour since Saturday.

"It doesn't take you. It takes that you were ever here. And it follows the remembering. That's how it finds things."

When I got up to leave, he held on to my hand and asked if my daddy was still around. I said no sir, he moved to Phoenix when I was little.

Mr. K didn't say anything. He just looked at me for a long time and patted my hand.

At the door he told me one more thing. "If they ever open up Six again, you don't come back here. You don't even think about it. You let us go."

I didn't get it. I was sixteen. I said thank you, went home, and wrote in my report that Mr. K "recalled that the mines closed due to declining ore quality."

Great primary source work!!

I've been trying to remember my father's face for three days.

I can't. I can't remember his name. I can't remember his voice, or one single thing he ever said to me. And here's the part that makes me want to throw up: I don't think I ever could. I just never noticed. "He moved to Phoenix" sat in my head where a whole person should have been, and I never once poked at it.

That blue Ford under the turquoise carport has been there my whole life. I never saw anybody drive it.

On Tuesday night I called my mom's number again. I don't know why. I'd called it twenty times by then and gotten the recording every time.

This time it connected.

No ringing. No recording. No static, either. It was *that* quiet. The mile 22 quiet. The kind that isn't the absence of sound so much as something else sitting where the sound should be.

And then, very far away, I heard my mom say my name.

Not yelling. Not scared. Just saying it the normal way, the way she'd say it when I walked in the door. But from very, very far down.

I said, "Mom?"

And the quiet on the line leaned in.

That's the only way I can put it. It got closer. Like it had been busy with something else, and it heard me, and it turned around.

I threw my phone across the room. There's a crack across the screen now. I haven't touched it since, except to write this.

Things are going faster now.

My manager called me "Jordan" twice yesterday. That's not my name. When I corrected him, he apologized, and then he did it again an hour later. This morning Devin asked me how long I'd been living here, kind of embarrassed, like he knew he should know. Some of my own memories are getting thin, like a shirt that's been washed too many times. I had to check my notebook just now to remember Mr. K's real last name. It took me a full minute to find the page.

I've been writing everything down. I'm on my third notebook. Mr. K's father wrote his crew down every morning, and paper held on to Ray for a while. So that's what this is. This post is paper. As much as I can get down, in as many places as I can put it, in front of as many people as I can find.

Here is what I remember.

Clayton, Arizona. Founded 1879. Population around 2,000. Twenty-two miles down the county road. Copper town. The Ruth Ann mine up on the north hill, and the water tower you could see from mile 19.

My mom, who painted her carport turquoise because the town needed some color, and who honked at mile 22 every single time.

Mr. K, ninety-three, who tried to warn me.

My history teacher, who didn't hand out exclamation points.

Marisol, my best friend from kindergarten through graduation, who wanted to be a large-animal vet and could burp the entire alphabet.

Big Lou at the diner, who gave free pie to anybody who'd just been dumped.

The librarian who let me check out twelve books at a time. Pastor Gene. The twins who set the football field on fire.

Ray, who walked ten feet into the dark in 1934.

My dad, whose name I don't know.

They were real. It was real. All of it was real.

There's a girl's name in the list up there. It's further up in the post, too. I wrote "my best friend from kindergarten through graduation" next to it.

I don't know who that is.

And there's a word all over this post. It's in the very first sentence. It's in almost every paragraph. Seven letters, starts with a C. I know it's the name. I know it's the most important word I've ever written. But every time I get to it, my eyes slide right off, like trying to read in a dream.

You can still read it, can't you?

Please put it in the comments. Say it out loud. Write it down somewhere. I think as long as somebody's still holding on to it, it isn't finished.

But Mr. K said it follows the remembering.

I'm sorry. I think I just showed it where all of you are.
