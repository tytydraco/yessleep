Let me start by telling you a truth you can’t say to most yuppies without pushback: Not all animals are created equal. I should know; I peel them off the side of the road for a living.

It’s safe to say some animals make a bigger mess than others. No one is calling me to scrape up a squirrel, and we generally let a raccoon decay out there as well.

But if you’re unlucky enough to hit a deer or a black bear and you survive, chances are you’ll see me out there with the EMTs. I’ll be the guy with the flatbed truck, shovel, and the power washer making the side of the road stain-free again.

They call it “Animal Control,” but it’s really just cleanup.

And I’ve seen it all, both dead and alive. None of it prepared me for the monsters. I guess nothing could have, which is why I am so confused about how I got here.

Why was I chosen?

How am I surviving all of this?

Let me start from the beginning.

I was there the day everything changed, 100 years ago… or was it just 24 hours?

It started on a sunny morning. My day began in the front yard of a righteous prick in yuppieville Connecticut, where money has bought these people everything their hearts desire, and they have no idea how to take care of most of it.

Jack Miller, the owner of this vast property, stood between the two giant oak trees at the foot of his half-mile-long driveway. These were old-growth forest, the only ones he didn’t cut down in order to install a stupid fountain and hedgerow out here.

Jack called me frantic, and I had answered.

“You’ve got Gus; animals are no fuss…”

I sat next to Jack in his Quad and took the long ride back to his mansion. He didn’t want my F-250 tearing up his newly paved driveway. Jack took me through the glorious house, modeled after some French castle—I wasn’t listening—and into a storm cellar.

The place reminded me of that wine storage place in that '40s Hitchcock movie my Dad and I watched a few weeks ago. You know the one. In the movie, there was like uranium hidden there.

Anyway, as we strutted past expensive bottles of wine, I heard the loud meows.

Apparently, there was a cat that was stuck between a few loose bricks.

Jack was indicative of a lot of the people who had moved to the New Haven area. He was in charge of a data center they were clearing forest space to put in.

My name was on a dozen petitions to stop him and his company, but I also needed to cover my rent and didn’t want to see his cat get hurt, so I was here to help.

As long as those checks came straight from his personal account and not Bionetic Industries, I didn’t think that made me a hypocrite.

His animals needed help, too.

The life of an Animal Control guy is not a glamorous one, even in the expensive ZIP codes, where all your clients are Wall Street bros and trophy wives who have no idea what to do with all the animals that came with the farms they decided to retreat to once they cashed out.

So I spend a lot of time advising them. And believe it or not, I have sifted through pig shit more than once looking for lost wedding bands and a TAG Heuer watch.

But at least you could charge them more. I had a ton of student loans to pay off. And the F-250 was a splurge, considering I lived in a studio apartment.

That had more to do with my divorce. You don’t want to hear about that now; suffice it to say, it got me out of New York City and out there to the country.

Somewhere I could finally be alone… well, and close to my aging father.

I followed Jack deeper through his maze of worldly goods.

The loose bricks where the feline had gotten stuck were behind one of those old gun closets, the kind you’d see in rural English hunting mansions. It was full of every kind of gun you know about, and a few only insane collectors do.

Being in the country, I was immune to most Second Amendment Stans, being that I also was strapped pretty much everywhere I went out here, with a tame Glock-17 I had for efficiency.

The times I’d used the pistol, I was putting something out of its misery.

Today, I hoped, would not be one of those days.

And I was right; it would be unlike anything I had ever experienced.

The Miller house was indicative of that, but down here, in front of this armory, I was more in shock than in awe; this was a cabinet filled with strategic urban warfare.

I even saw what I thought were Claymore mines.

Jack saw me eyeing them and smirked.

“Oh, they’re real. I know a few weapons contractors. My company has a lucrative deal with them and the government. They hooked me up.”

“What do you use them for?” I asked slowly, hoping this wasn’t some sort of elaborate blood sport I had been lured into participating in.

“I dunno. I just thought it would be cool to have them. Like in Metal Gear Solid.”

Jack’s wealth and ignorance were enticing to me, even as he spoke fluent douchebag in this place. All I could do was nod back with enthusiasm.

Someday, maybe I’d have enough money to be as big of a tool. No, that was just my ex talking.

If I knew then what I know now, I’d have handed him one of the mines and pulled the safety pin. But back then, I nodded along with his bullshit.

I was not going to start any shit; I just wanted to be on my way.

Jack just shrugged. “Guns are just kinda cool. I guess I got addicted after my wife told me no more Pokémon cards. There are so many different kinds, and each has its own experience.”

Goddamn rich people, I thought.

We shifted the cabinet a few inches so I could reach the meows. The cat was indeed back there, but was not stuck in the bricks. She was sitting with a sweet litter of kittens.

I took them out of the wall one by one, and then the scared mother cat, who hissed and scratched, but calmed as soon as I put her in a loose wine box with her kids.

“Shit… you want them?”

I wanted to tell him off, but I just nodded and told him it would cost.

Jack reached for his wallet without hesitation.

I loaded the box with a couple of golf towels I took off a few sets of clubs in the corner. As I looked around, this guy had random collections of all the most expensive things in the world, and none of them made him satisfied. I should find comfort in that, but all I got was jealous.

And a little pissed, because he didn’t want to collect the cats!

I had found homes for three of the ten before I made it back into town, and arranged for all of them to be picked up at a local retirement center. The old people there were going to bottle-feed the kittens first. And that place could use a mother cat to chase away the mice.

That’s when the call came in from my Dad. He told me I had to get to his place right away, and his voice assured me there was something going on I had to see for myself.

My Dad was the most boring guy I had ever met. I had never heard him so worked up, not even when my Mom died.

So I pressed the gas on the truck and burned half a tank speeding to his homestead.

# THE HAMSTER BALL

Dad lived in the middle of nowhere, just below Sleeping Giant State Park. He bought a place and moved from D.C., where I grew up, when he retired.

I never knew how to answer when people asked me what he did for a living. He was one of those guys who went to an office and never said much more than his boss was an asshole and they were making him work late that night.

It had something to do with computers, the environment, defense contractors—that’s all I had picked up in the thirty-five years I’d been on this planet.

His passions were our pets, which he filled our house with after Mom died, and which I spent all my spare time taking care of when he was stuck long hours at work.

You could say Animal Control chose me from a very young age.

I parked my truck next to another in the driveway.

That’s why I was so shocked to pull down his long driveway and see Leslie waiting for us. And damn, of course she looked really good in her green uniform and broad-brimmed hat.

She was exactly the kind of woman you’d want to meet at the end of the world.

My history with women was actually a lot like my history with animals. I enjoyed the ones with claws and teeth, even though I always wound up getting scratched and bitten.

Leslie’s line of work fit the bill there. She was a park ranger and worked part-time for the game warden. She told me the only dangerous animals in Connecticut were rich people with new hunting rifles, and that made me laugh so hard I was immediately smitten.

I ran into her a lot in my line of work, but post-divorce and living in a 600-square-foot apartment, I haven’t had the confidence to ask her out.

Even worse, I made the mistake of talking to my dad about that last week during a very sad birthday dinner he bought me. He told me he had a solution for that, and now, standing next to her in his front yard… I was worried he called her here to put the pressure on me.

The sky had darkened on my drive, like we were in store for one of those springtime showers.

As thunder crackled, we both took refuge in my Dad’s open garage.

“I knocked,” Leslie said. “Your Dad told me he was getting a big surprise ready and to wait for you out here. Got any idea what he has in store?”

I tried to respond eloquently, but all I managed was, “Your breath is really nice.”

She laughed at me openly, and I turned fuchsia this time.

Leslie dug in her pocket and took out a pack of Juicy Fruit.

“The flavor doesn’t last long, but it's my favorite.”

I tucked a piece in my jowls and felt my dry mouth dissipate. It was nice being close to her and watching the falling rain. Even the crackle of lightning felt metaphorical between us. This could have been the start of a romantic moment, but that’s when Dad’s voice cut through the air.

“It’s time.”

We turned to see my father look from us up to the sky, where clouds were circling ominously.

“It’s time you get inside the hamster balls,” Dad said, like it wasn’t the most insane thing we’d heard in our lives. He beckoned for us to enter the home.

“Sorry—the what?!” Leslie and I responded in unison.

We followed Dad into the house with all our stuff. He didn’t help us carry anything; he was too agitated. Rain pitter-pattered on the roof, and the thunder was getting louder. We could barely hear ourselves think, let alone listen to my Dad talking.

“I know you two haven’t worked out yet, but you need to get out of your own way. You can’t do this alone… trust me, I’ve tried.”

He kept going on and on about life and needing someone, the kind of rambling that made Leslie and me share a look, and made me begin to feel guilty that I had not been there enough for him.

“I’m here now, let’s focus on that and try to…”

“I invited you both because I want you to be here tomorrow, too.”

“You’re freaking me out, Dad.”

That’s when I felt all the words leave my mouth and the air leave my lungs.

Instead of seeing his rickety old couch, stained coffee table, and ottomans, the whole room had been gutted. The hardwood floors were ripped up and replaced with endless coils of wire leading to various generators around the room.

The floral wallpaper was gone, ripped to shreds, and so was the insulation and the drywall.

Instead, the electrical system was exposed on the walls, and sparks were flying.

In the middle of the room were two clear balls… just big enough for a moderately sized human to stand inside. They had chairs in their center and compartments on the floor, like footlockers.

“Get in.”

These words came out direct and forceful. It was highly unusual for the old coot.

We assumed my father was kidding, but his face said otherwise. In fact, he’d gone very pale, and his eyes were watering, as if he was about to cry.

“I don’t have time to explain, but if you both get in and pack up, I’ll try before they’re here.”

Leslie’s face showed she was as concerned as I was. But we both were having trouble formulating words.

“I’m sorry, I think we should all just take a moment to talk this out.”

Leave it to Leslie to logic through this. I would think she’d know the men in this family are just impulsive doers by now.

Dad tapped his foot, indicating there was no time for that.

“I know you think I’m crazy. I am crazy. But right now, as your father and our friend, I’m doing my best to save your lives. Where you take this from here, well, I wish I could be there to see it, but I know you’ll do well.”

“Okay, Dad, I think we should make you like a cup of tea or something. And also maybe get you away from all this electrical gear because I think it’s affecting your brain.”

Another thunder strike rattled the windows, but this one didn’t go away. It just kept shaking, like we were in the middle of an earthquake. Every time we thought it would subside, the whole world would shake again.

“August, I am your father. I have never asked anything of you. But right now, I’m telling you and this lovely woman to get in the goddamn hamster ball so I can see you both live.”

Neither of us moved until he snapped—“NOW!”

If I had known these would be my last words to him, I would have said something more meaningful. I would have told him I loved him.

I would have told him that every sacrifice he made for me as a kid, all the freedom he trusted me to have, and all the grace he gave me when I screwed up meant the world to me.

That they were a warm blanket that covered me as I mourned my mother, and that I wish I could have found a way to repay him for that.

Instead, all I mustered was “ALRIGHT! CHILL OUT!”

Leslie and I each walked to our respective balls.

“You don’t have to do this,” I told her.

“I know,” she said. “But I’m kind of scared of whatever is about to happen.”

And with that, Leslie entered the sheer casing that seemed to call to both pure senses of curiosity and adventure—and also the chance to step aside from someone you just showed all your cards to, and to be alone.

They had pentagonal entrances on the underside that we had to stoop to climb inside.

We threw our packs into the footlockers below the chairs and then strapped into the pilot chairs as Dad went around the room, turning on all the generators.

The earth was shaking more frequently now, and between the thunder and the generators, you couldn’t hear a damn thing.

Leslie looked at me through her translucent ball, and I looked back at her and shrugged. I touched the exterior, and it felt like very thick glass.

That’s when Dad’s voice hit us through a small speaker in the hamster balls.

“Don’t bother to talk back; this microphone only goes one way.”

He was wearing a headset and carried a thick metal stopper. Before I could protest, he pressed it into the opening of my ball, and it sucked shut.

Then he quickly did the same to Leslie’s ball.

Even if we wanted to get out to yell at him, the chair buckles pulled us tightly.

We both realized we had no control over what was happening.

“My work for the government—well, this is what I was in charge of all those years. I wasn’t sure what they were going to use it for, but I guess they knew this was coming. Of course they knew it was coming, what am I saying? The answers were in front of me. You can’t have one population growing as fast as ours without natural predation. You taught me that.”

There was a gust of what felt like wind, and the roof of the house ripped off. As I looked up into the sky, I saw black clouds… and something flying in them.

“They’re here.”

Rain splashed down on the hamster balls.

And the roar of an animal I had never heard before pierced through our ears. Its bellow broke the glass windows and announced an imminent threat.

Dad looked past the balls toward something we couldn’t see, then he tapped some comfort onto the balls’ thick exterior.

“Special polymer. Unbreakable. Dissolves with the right mixture of liquid chemicals in about 100 years.”

Leslie and I were not having fun with this scientific invention at that moment. And I don’t think he was either. He looked forlorn, resigned to something about to go down.

“The world is going to need you more than you know. When you get out, find each other.”

Dad backed away from the balls, toward the garage.

“It’ll work out. I know it. Just… have a nice nap.”

“The hell it will. Either get in here or get back here. Let us out!”

I was losing my cool, both scared of whatever made him afraid and overcome by the immediate feeling that I had absolutely no idea what was about to happen, but knew it would change me forever.

My Dad didn’t budge; he just blew me a kiss.

“You’re going to need each other.”

I wanted to ask for more, but a giant, scaly foot descended into the home and kicked my hamster ball through the remaining walls and into the sky.

# KAIJU WORLD CUP

I’m sure you want to hear about these formidable beasts, but indulge me for a moment. I played all the available sports growing up, and soccer was easily my worst. I liked anything where you could run into someone at full speed, and they frown on that in European football.

Banging into people and walls is sort of how I got out my mom’s death in a healthier fashion than some of the other choices that are available to the youth of America.

So in between my journeys out in the wilderness and school, Dad made sure I tried anything full-contact to get my energy out and to stay out of trouble.

Especially after Mom died and no one was home to look out for me.

My reward for being good and not getting into trouble? Well, aside from complete autonomy, I got to watch Godzilla movies with him at night. We’d curl up on the couch, open some sodas and chips—I was a salt and vinegar guy—and bask in the destruction.

The pizza would arrive halfway through, and we’d pause to talk to each other and recount the descriptions we saw on screen. I vividly remember teaching my Dad about how Godzilla was actually an apex predator we created through our recklessness.

He just smiled and let me rant.

To this day, I think about that when I see parents ignore their kids. My Dad embracing me as a weird little kid was one of the reasons I’m alive… for now.

But the point is, my Dad loved anything with “Kaiju” in it. You know, that fancy Japanese word for big freaking monsters that crash around cities and wreak havoc on the landscape.

That’s why I wish I could have seen his face when we saw the first one. But at that moment, I was like forty feet in the air, sailing like a soccer ball, and I only saw its face because I literally broke the low cloud surface before I fell backward.

And it was gnarly and full of teeth.

The beasts bellowed with an echo like a bullhorn.

Every bit of movement felt strained; they were not smooth but herky-jerky. Even the ones flapping in the sky felt like they were adjusting to life here.

Like the Tin Man in need of an oil can.

But I was about to see them shake the rust off in real time.

I landed hard, but the hamster ball stayed intact, and the seat's shocks provided some cushion against the impact.

Too bad my piece of Juicy Fruit was forced out of my mouth and hit the inside of the hamster ball. It stuck there in a gob of my own spit, but I had no time to wipe it off.

As I gazed out of the contraption, I saw several other enormous animals strutting across the landscape. They had to be a hundred feet, maybe two hundred.

These were different breeds than whatever kicked me.

In fact, they all looked like different animals. Some more mammalian, others reptilian, all kind of herding together, not attacking one another but scavenging for food on the ground.

Sniping and snarfing any person they could get their forked tongue or claw around.

They varied in size, from ones that were as big as a house, to ones whose heads careened up into the clouds.

Each had its own deafening voice. Their screeches and screams were so vile they would send piss down your pant leg.

The only thing that kept me from shitting myself was that my brain was going haywire. These seemed to defy most of what I knew about animals on this planet…

And that’s when I realized there was a strong possibility they weren’t from here.

Or maybe they were, and we had not seen them in a very long time.

And then I puked, because the jostling and rotation of the ball had my head actually spinning, too. The ball was gyroscoped, and it kept me upright, but I had never had a good stomach for roller coasters, and this felt like the ride from hell.

As I got my bearings, I saw Leslie’s ball speeding past me. The sides of her ball were all scratched, like something with big nails had tried to get in.

We both bumped into cars trying to escape, and pinballed until we came to a stop in someone’s front yard.

She took a second to look over, then we both looked back at my Dad’s house, which had been flattened. We saw a long, pointed tail drag over it, demolishing every window and wall.

By the time it disappeared, you couldn’t even tell there was a house there.

We sat for a moment and tried to catch our breath.

She was mumbling words to me, but I couldn’t hear anything. I could see fear in her eyes, so all I could muster was… “It’ll be okay,” which I had to mouth to her twice for her to get it.

She shook her head, “No.”

Even through the thick polymer, I understood her. And I sighed in agreement.

We took the landscape in. From this vantage point, we could see billows of smoke in the distance, and knew this was not isolated to our corner of the world.

This was everywhere, and it was really bad.

On the wooded side, tall trees were tossed into the air like they were nothing. Scars emerged in the forest; our attackers left muddy footprints the size of football fields.

The beasts had spread out over the vista, but there were more flying in the sky. They flapped leathery wings and swooped down like fighter jets, skimming the ground and gyrating away from debris.

I could see them feasting both near and far. They tossed red masses into the air or eviscerated them on the ground. They looked like they were in the buffet line.

My brain was trying to catch up with my eyes to understand what was going on, and that’s when I understood… they were eating people.

One by one, I saw them leap from tree cover, swoop down, and snatch and claw.

This was classic predation behavior, like a lion munching on an antelope. But this wasn’t the Serengeti.

It was fucking Connecticut.

Our hamster balls had stopped rolling in the front yard of some random rich couple. They were outside in their Lululemon active gear, gawking at the destruction.

They had a bunch of horses in their front yard. They were much smarter and had taken off, leaping over their fences and heading toward the woods.

If they were as smart as them, they would have followed.

But modern humans lack the foresight of wild animals.

Instead, they looked from the sky at us, then ran to the balls.

One of those giant things spotted them and swooped from the clouds. Its snout pressed the woman against my ball, wrapped its tongue around her, and then swallowed her whole.

I rocked back and forth as the man charged the beast, but he was snatched up by the claw on one of the giants and lifted into the clouds.

His screams died out as the animal flapped far away; the initial wind from its wings blew us back down the country lane, rolling at a leisurely pace.

We rolled past cars retreating and even a few people in golf carts.

A few of the flying beasts spotted us and landed atop the house to get a better look. They swooped back and forth, taking out stragglers until just our hamster balls remained in the grass.

The roof of the house these winged monsters rested on cracked and cracked under their weight.

As the attic and brick walls caved in, they jumped down and stomped across the yard, long tongues flicking at Leslie’s ball first. Our roll was too slow, and they caught up quickly.

She was screaming so loudly I could hear it through the cacophony of noise surrounding us.

One of the monsters bit down on her ball with its alligator snout.

Its teeth slid off the glass, which I had come to believe had to be some sort of indestructible polymer. The kaiju quickly gave up, but not before its tail smashed into my ball and sent me rolling down the road.

The last I saw of Leslie, a massive foot had pushed her ball deep into the mud of that front yard and rolled her into what used to be a vast koi pond.

I supposed she would be safe under the surface there, but I had no idea how we would get out of these things and how much air we’d have left, too.

My ball bounced off a John Deere someone had left behind. As I grasped around, trying to loosen the straps holding me in, I felt another rumble.

My ball skidded into a smashed Volkswagen and rested.

Perhaps the biggest of these monsters I’d seen was now walking toward me. It had feet the size of 18-wheelers, and its body drooped in long, thick fur.

You could call it sort of a woolly mammoth, but it was leaner, and its eyeballs bulged on the sides of its head and rotated quickly, assessing the situation as it lumbered.

My ball had rolled many miles from where I started. And somehow, I was back where my day began, just down the road from the Miller residence, staring at the two old oaks that remained.

For just a beat, I centered myself and took a deep breath.

That’s when a paw crunched down on one of the oak trees I so loved, but it amazingly missed the one I was now under.

The beast seemed to groan with every step.

As it passed over me, I could see some people clinging onto the fur, as well as long strands of debris caught in its hair. They were dropping and hitting the ground, likely not surviving those falls. I closed my eyes and tried to pivot as bodies fell near me and didn’t get back up.

As soon as the thumping ceased, I opened my eyes and assessed the damage.

The rain was creating incredibly sloppy conditions, and my ball had slid into a part of the destroyed road into a drainage pipe that ran underneath the other giant oak.

And these traipsing monsters left pond-sized footprints that were quickly filling with rain. I saw flash floods of debris and corpses crossing the fields, all headed toward me in this new ditch.

The water and debris hit me with a thud and lifted me and spun me around again until my ball boinked off the stone garden wall.

I was now in another ditch, and mud and grime swirled around me, covering the ball.

Just a few hours ago, I was out here helping a horse. Now I watched as animals I had never seen before marched through the field, toward the town.

They destroyed shopping centers and car parks as they went, knocking into apartment buildings and spraying fire and brimstone when necessary.

I saw a birdlike monster with a bear’s head swoop from the air, pick up a fire truck, and drop it on a church before plucking its inhabitants out and swallowing them whole.

The visceral acceptance of leaving the top of the food chain made me feel clammy.

I caught my breath and tried to wipe the condensation from inside the ball.

There was no sign of anyone out there, and no sign of Leslie. I looked the two hundred or so yards up the lane where I left her ball, but could see nothing.

I banged on the glass inside, but to my surprise, it started to turn grey. It was tinting from the inside. At first I thought this was my doing, but even the bottom I couldn’t reach was changing.

That’s when I heard the hissing. There was some sort of pink liquid filling the ball from tubes hidden in the spirals of the ball.

I looked down, but there were no leaks. This liquid was coming from inside the apparatus and rising; now it was mid-thigh, covering everything in front of me.

The interior of the ball became a computer screen with words that read: “Protective lining released” and then “Cryofreezing initiated.”

“You gotta be shitting me.”

The exterior of the ball was now rock-like and impenetrable. As the pink goop covered my face and neck, I gagged and swallowed some, but soon found I was able to breathe even though I was now completely submerged.

The goo was thick and cold. I could see it freezing solid from the bottom upward. I felt a wave of calm wash over me as the ball's exterior grew opaque, and a deep sleep whisked me away.

My last thoughts were for the world I was leaving behind, and a fear of what I would wake up to see. But nothing would have prepared me for the changes that would occur over the next 100 years. I’d just have to see it to believe it.

And even then, it would take me a while to understand...
