When I was a little girl, during the summer months when school was out, I would spend the week with my grandmother out in the countryside. In hindsight her house wasn’t particularly large, but having grown up in an apartment it seemed endless. And every corner of her house screamed of the vestigial decadence that had all but vanished years prior to my birth.

Grandmother was not wealthy. The child of poor farmers and married to an army man, even my parents had more than her. But still, there was something opulent about her house. The way the sun hit the tick lace curtains and glittered of the glass crystalware. Grandfather’s handmade heavy wooden furniture covered in her embroidered tablecloths.

Everything in our apartment was corkboard and plastic, synthetic and mass produced. Made to be used then discarded. Designed for the garbage heap, containing disposal procedures imprinted onto it. Everything in Grandmother’s house felt like a work of art, something designed to outlive its owner. Something permeant, immortal.

I used to love exploring her house, rooting around in closets and digging through drawers to find all manner of little things, the products of decades of life in this single space. grandfather’s old pins, mother’s old toys, even great grandmother’s recipe clippings. Whenever I found something, I would bring it to her, and she would recount the story tied to it while I lied on the living room floor and sucked on lemon candies.

Possibly the only thing better than rummaging through the odd corners of her house were the lemon candies she kept in a bowl by the fireplace. They were wrapped in yellow and white wrappers with twists at both ends and when you pulled at the end of both twists the candy would spin and come open. The taste was simple, but sweet like fresh lemonade. I liked them, ultimately, because they reminded me of summer with grandmother.

When someone dies, we fill them up with all sorts of chemicals so that they won’t rot. Long after they’re forgotten, they lie perfectly preserved beneath the earth. Life, ironically, does not provide the same mercy. The living can deteriorate in ways we spare the dead. Grandmother’s body failed bit by bit until she could barely walk. Her mind lost moment after moment until she couldn’t tell me from mother. It took her slipping just recently for her to move out of that house and into an assisted home. Now she’s surrounded by plastic and corkboard too. Designed for finite use, imprinted with the instructions for its own unmaking. A ticking clock until an expiry date.

One day after I finished spending time with her, I stopped by her house. It had been weeks since anyone had been inside. My parents hired realtors, they’ll sell the place soon. I let myself in with the key I’d had on my ring since middle school and walked around the house, letting my memories of the place wash over me. It was just like she left it, but without her. Like a faithful old steed staunchly awaiting its departed master. But she isn’t coming back, and the steed is due for the glue factory. They’ll have an open house and sell anything they can for pennies and anything they can’t they’ll send to the landfill. Her beautiful, hand embroidered tablecloths rotting beside and endless, ever-expanding mass of gimcrack garbage.

Like an emperor surveilling prisoners of war I picked out a few to be spared the slaughter. One of mother’s old dolls. Grandfather’s framed sketch of a waterbird. Great-grandmother’s cookbook in a cursive I can’t even read. As I complete my circle, I end up in front of the little glass bowl of lemon candies. I put my pardoned mementoes down and took a single candy from the bowl. With a practiced, ingrained motion I pulled the wrapper open. Pavalovain conditioning kicks in and there is a bit of joy, a moment of childish excitement as all those candies from all those years before surge back. I place it in my mouth.

For a brief flash, everything feels right. I can feel her beside me, sitting on the couch, telling me a story. The taste is there, but its not alone. As my tongue flicks over the candy there is the strange feeling of perforation. moment of confusion and then a sharp stinging pain. I spit it out and it bounces on the carpet, but the writhing inside my mouth won’t fade. I rake my tongue with my fingers as I watch the little hole-riddled candy start to squirm as ants pour out.

Ants are a small thing, easy to overlook through tear-filled eyes. But the moment you notice one, suddenly you can notice hundreds. You focus shifts, allowing your vision to process things you had overlooked before. Suddenly I could see the lines moving up the table, the countless little black forms squirming out of the bowl. Then down and to my shoes, my socks, the squirming black forms crawling up my leg. I screamed trying desperately to shake them off, but agitated they only began to bite, rooting themselves into my flesh.

Sobbing with panic I watched as more and more poured forth from countless places, from cracks in the walls, from the spaces between the floorboards, from the gaps between the drawers in the cabinets. All the little places I used to hide, to search, all the little treasures I used to cherish, a nest for little squirming forms. The mementos, even the mementos were covered in them. But I could not leave them behind. I scooped them up in my arms and ran out of the house towards my car. They squirmed up my arms, my chest, my neck, my face.

I made it to my car and placed the final, cherished reminders of my childhood in the back. Collapsing into the front seat I began to desperately pluck ant after ant off my body. They had festered into my clothes so I tore them off and threw them out. As if satisfied with my sacrifice though perhaps just lacking any further places to hide, I managed to pull most of them from my flesh.

The entire drive home was dotted by the feeling of remnants crawling their way out of my hair. When I finally made it home, I collapsed into the shower, scrubbing myself until I was confident I was rid of them. In the days since I still sometimes find them in my car or wake up to their feeling crawling across my flesh. More often now it’s just the feeling, the feeling of little legs moving across my body but no matter how much I search or scratch of squish I can’t find the source.

When grandmother dies and we bury her, the ants won’t get her. She’ll be preserved perfectly forever in her little box, filled up with chemicals. The artifacts of her life, the things that defined her, they’ll all go to the ants in her stead. But when I die? I don’t have anything to take. Nothing but mass-produced garbage designed to be reduced into refuse. In the end, when I am dead and gone, they’ll get nothing from me.
