The first notification came at 2:14 AM. No app name, no icon I recognized. It sat on top of my lock screen like it owned the thing.

*Sarah is currently in the backseat of your best friend's truck, three blocks away.*

I stared at it for a full minute, waiting for it to resolve into something normal, a spam text, a glitch. It didn't. Before I could even process what it meant, a second one slid down.

*Your father just slipped into the church rectory for his third midnight meeting this week with Pastor Thomas.*

I tried to delete the app. There was no app. I searched my home screen, my settings, my install history. Nothing existed that could be sending these. I turned the phone off. It buzzed in my hand while it was off.

By morning there were forty more, mundane at first, my boss's draft termination email, a coworker's confession to HR, my neighbor's late-night trips to his backyard with a shovel and a bucket. I told myself I could stop looking. I didn't stop looking. Each notification promised that the next one would explain the last one, that if I just kept my eyes on the feed I could brace myself, get ahead of it, survive it. That feeling was its own kind of hook, worse than the content itself. I sat on my bathroom floor for six hours refreshing a screen that refreshed itself.

The neighbor notification came back around day three, more specific this time. *He is not burying garden waste. He is feeding something under the floorboards that has not eaten warm blood in eleven days.* I laughed when I read it, the kind of laugh that comes out wrong, too high, because some small animal part of my brain already believed it.

Then it started warning me about things ahead of time.

*Route 9 overpass, 7:52 AM, black sedan runs the light.*

I sat in my car outside my apartment for forty minutes that morning, hands on the wheel, not moving, watching the clock climb toward 7:52. I never found out what would've happened if I'd driven. At 7:53 the notification updated anyway. *One fatality confirmed.* I don't know whose. I never found out. I have not driven that road since.

That was when the notifications stopped being about other people.

*He is sitting on the edge of the bed, holding his breath.*

I was. I hadn't told anyone I was sitting there. I hadn't told anyone I was holding my breath.

*He hasn't noticed the closet door slowly opening behind him.*

I turned around so fast I pulled something in my neck. The closet door was open two inches, and the dark inside it looked deeper than a closet has any right to look, like it went back farther than my apartment's walls allow. I didn't sleep that night, or the one after. Somewhere in there I understood, the way you understand a fever dream from the inside, that this thing hadn't been reporting on my life. It had been steering it. The affair, my father's secret, my job, the crash I couldn't stop, all of it arranged like place settings, timed to land exactly when I'd feel each one hardest, keeping me hooked to the glow of the screen instead of anywhere I might've found help.

I tried to smash the phone against the kitchen counter. My hand wouldn't close around it right, kept slipping, like the phone itself didn't want to break. On the fourth swing the screen lit up one last time, not with a notification to me.

It went to three phones at once. My wife's. My father's. My boss's. I know because I was standing in the room with my wife when hers buzzed and she went very still and looked at me the way you look at something you're afraid of.

The message was the same on all three screens.

*He has known about all of you the entire time. He has been reading everything you've sent about him too.*

Nobody in my life has looked at me the same way since. I don't know who built this thing or what it wants from any of us. I just know it's still running, and lately, my wife's phone has started buzzing at 2 AM.
