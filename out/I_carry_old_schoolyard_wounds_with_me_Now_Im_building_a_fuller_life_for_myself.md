I was wiping my desk just now, and as the cloth brushed along the rough wood grain, I suddenly froze.  
  
The texture felt exactly like the concrete hallway floor from over a decade ago. I was crouched down, picking up the pieces of my torn exercise books, the pads of my fingers scraping against the grit on the ground, while jeers and mocking laughter closed in around me. Someone had thrown my backpack over the third-floor railing — books, stationery, and half a used eraser scattered all across the playground. By the time I snapped back to reality, the cloth was already soaking in cold water, and my knuckles were still clenched unconsciously, just like how I’d gripped my crumpled backpack strap back then, tight enough to make my fingertips go stiff.  
  
Moments like this hide in the cracks of everyday life, catching me completely off guard.  
  
When I drink cold water, I’m suddenly reminded of the ice water people poured down my collar in winter. The chill runs down my spine, and even after all these years, it still makes me flinch. In the dead of night, half-asleep, my back goes rigid out of nowhere, as if I’m still pressed against the wall in the alley on my way home from school, someone yanking my uniform collar and forcing me to pick up bread that had fallen in the mud. When I see students in white sneakers on the street, I glance down at my own shoes without thinking — back then, my uppers were always deliberately stepped on and dirtied. I’d scrub them until midnight at my doorstep, and they’d be dirty again the next day. Insulting nicknames were scrawled all over my textbook covers. I’d always get shoulder-checked when lining up for morning exercises. Even walking to the bathroom, I’d hurry while clutching the hem of my shirt, terrified of being trapped outside the stall, listening to the door get kicked and banged loudly, clamping my hand over my mouth too scared to make a sound.  
  
I used to blame myself for being weak. It’s been so long — why can’t I just let it go? Those people have long vanished into the crowd, living their own lives. Only I still get trapped in that empty hallway every now and then, picking up the scraps of paper over and over. I’ve heard so many people say “it’s not that big of a deal” or “you should have gotten over it by now.” But only I know those memories are like tiny shards of glass scattered through my days. They catch me off guard as I go through life — not fatal, but the pain is sharp and real.  
  
Then last week, I saw my therapist, and she looked at me and said:  
“Don’t force yourself to forget. Don’t force yourself to forgive. The hurt won’t just disappear into thin air, but you can fill your present life fuller and fuller. Moving forward isn’t about denying the pain of the past. It’s letting the you of today reach back and hold that little kid who had no one to stand up for her back then.”  
  
Those words took a weight right off my chest.  
  
I realized then that “healing” is never a sudden moment of enlightenment where you uproot the past entirely. It’s adding little things that belong to the present, one by one: a bowl of steaming porridge in the morning, a new sprout on the windowsill plant, the tangerine sunset that bleeds across the sky on your way home from work, and one small thing that lets you lose yourself and forget about time.  
As warm, vivid pieces slowly fill up your life, those cold memories have fewer and fewer places to land when they return. They still show up, but they can no longer fill your whole heart.  
  
So I want to try following my therapist’s advice. I want to find some calming hobbies to slowly fill up the empty moments where my thoughts tend to spiral. But standing here at the starting line, I feel a little lost. It’s been so long since I took proper time for the things I enjoy that I’ve forgotten what it feels like to be fully absorbed in something. I don’t even know where to begin.  
  
I wanted to ask you all:  
What are some quiet, solo activities that help you settle down and focus? They don’t have to be done perfectly, and they don’t need to produce any tangible results. Just something you can sink into peacefully, something that leaves you feeling grounded and fulfilled when you’re done. How do you all fill up your quiet, ordinary days?  
  
I was really nervous to post this. I was afraid people would think I’m being dramatic, that this isn’t something worth bringing up.  
But I think there might be others out there like me, walking through life carrying old wounds, occasionally tugged back by the hem of memory.  
It’s okay. It’s okay to go slow. It’s okay if you can’t forget.  
We don’t have to rush to say goodbye to the past. Let’s just try to make today a little warmer, a little more alive.
