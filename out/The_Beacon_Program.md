Entry 001: Universe\_0/ArturoGonzalez/EchoGateDynamics

My name is Artie. I’m starting The Beacon Program so that the unfortunate few may warn their fellow living beings of what’s to come. It’s the least I could do for the part I played in causing all of this. Perhaps I’m getting ahead of myself, so I’ll start at the beginning.

I used to work for a research facility called EchoGate Dynamics in the 1980s. When you think of the 80s, you might think of arcades, VHS stores, classic slasher films and synth music. It was also the time that gave way to the birth of the modern internet and MTV. When I think of the 80s, I think of EchoGate Dynamics and the Double-Dip recession. The only reason I decided to work at that godforsaken place.

It was March 22nd, 1982 and I was living in Maine at the time. I’ll never forget that date because it was the same day that Iron Maiden released their 3rd album. I was laid off at K-Mart that morning because the franchise owner realized our location wasn’t profitable enough for his liking anymore and would be closing the store within a month. I was overqualified anyway, with my doctorate in theoretical physics. I kept telling myself that, but the truth was, I loved that job. There was nothing to stress over, my coworkers were nice to me, and the pizza place that was adjoined to the store had the best slice in town.

“I’m sorry, Artie.” I remember Mr. Goff’s face didn’t match his tone. The store manager tried to come off as genuinely hurt, but his tone just gave off nothing but boredom. “We haven’t hit the designated sales numbers and can no longer employ any of you much longer. Since your department is the one that we’re shutting down first, you and Randy will be the first to go. Thank you so much for all of your help. You can go home.”

I wished he had told me all of this over the phone instead of waiting for me to show up after I had already spent money on the bus fare to get there. Hell, even letting me work one more shift would have been nice, but he couldn’t spend another penny on me, I guess. I was even planning on spending some of my cash on that new Iron Maiden cassette tape for my walkman. Now, every cent felt precious, almost heavier in my pocket. I decided not to spend my change on another bus ride back and began walking home instead, my nose prickling and making my eyes water a bit. With no other way to pay my rent for my shitty studio apartment or buy more food for Ember, my goofy orange cat, I slumped down the streets, face down and hands in my pockets, where I began rubbing the $12.50 subconsciously. I thought about where to spend it as my decision would be critical.

I got home, my legs sore from the three mile walk and four flights of stairs I had just completed. I checked my mail cubby before entering my apartment and retrieved a stack of envelopes and flyers, bringing the pile to my door as I fetched my keys. I opened up and walked inside, closing the door behind me and setting everything on the table. In my daze, I hadn't realized Ember was being unusually quiet. When the thought finally hit me, I found him under the table, looking at me with wide, terrified eyes, sitting on a puddle of his own urine. He turned his gaze from me to the landline, puffed himself up, and ran under my bed. Then the phone rang.

I should've listened to what my gut was feeling at that moment. I should've listened to Ember. Somehow, my cat sensed that something horrifying had set things in motion to happen the way they did, but instead, I stupidly answered the phone.

“Hello, Mr. Gonzalez. This is Darren Moore with EchoGate Dynamics. It is our understanding that you have recently started looking for a new line of work. How would you like to put that doctorate of yours to good use?” The voice on the other line was dull and gravelly. How did they know, I remember asking myself.

“Y-Yes.” I was stunned. I remember thinking that maybe my luck wasn’t so bad after all. How little I knew.

“Do you have time for a quick, over-the-phone interview?” Darren asked me.

“Yes, sir.” I replied. I looked over to my cat, trembling under the bed as I said this.

“Pay no mind to your cat. Be professional.” He said firmly. This made me check the window to see if someone was looking inside. Nothing but the street below with cars passing by. I blinked hard and shook my head, bringing myself back to the phone call. ‘Be professional’ his command echoed in my brain. He continued.

“Now, to start us off, could you provide more information about your doctorate in theoretical physics, Mr. Gonzalez?”

“Of course, um... My studies had me focused on high energy physics and quantum field theory. I graduated two years ago, but keep the information fresh in my mind by rereading the textbooks I bought back in college.”

“Great. Now, from what we can see, Mr. Gonzalez, you have no experience actually working in the field. During your studies, did you ever intern at any companies relative to your degree?”

“No.” I said regretfully, thinking this would make them rethink their consideration for me. The truth was that I could’ve done more while studying, but I didn’t think it would be necessary. I thought my work would speak for itself.

“Great. We prefer it that way, in all honesty. It’s easier for thinking minds to focus solely on the task at hand if they’re not thinking of how their studies had been applied in the past.” Mr. Moore sounded like he had set something down. I heard a clink.

“Okay.” I felt a ringing sensation in my right ear while I held the phone to my left.

“Final question. Do you know what a wormhole is, Mr. Gonzalez?”

“Are you talking about an Einstein-Rosen Bridge?” I asked.

“So, you’re familiar with the concept?” He answered my question with a question.

“Yes.” I answered.

“Great. That’s all we need to know. Do you have paper and a pencil on you?”

“Yes.” I replied one more time.

“Write this address down. 2072 Jay West Avenue. We will arrange for a driver to come pick you up tonight. If you give them this address, they’ll know where to go. Be outside of your apartment complex by 8 PM.” The phone clicked.

Was that it? No background check, no second interview and no second thoughts? At the time, I didn’t know how they got all of my information or what kind of work they wanted me for, but I was happy to be employed again. I helped my cat feel better in any way I could and he eventually came out of his hiding spot. I gave him a bath, he ate for a while and then curled up on my bed. I pet him and patiently waited for that evening. I began to wonder why the interview was so brief and why they asked specifically about wormholes, but any theories I came up with made me sound like a lunatic.

I remember getting dressed in a button up with a tie and my only pair of black slacks. I wore my black polished shoes and even snipped a bit of my hair to make it less shaggy and look more professional while I waited for 8 to roll by. I prepared a briefcase with my notepad, a few pens and pencils, my birth certificate and social security card in case they needed it. I stuffed my wallet with all the loose bills I had in my possession in case I needed it for anything. By 7:40 I was already waiting in front of my building. Exactly at 8, my car showed up. I had been expecting a yellow cab, but a black, two door Crown Vic pulled up to where I stood. It flashed its lights so I walked up to it and tapped the window. The driver inside, a bald, middle aged man stared straight forward. No reaction to my knock. After a bit of hesitation I opened the door.

“EchoGate Dynamics?” I asked. The man nodded in response, his eyes never refraining from the road ahead. I climbed in and as soon as I closed the door, he took his foot off the brake, gently pushed his foot on the gas pedal and drove. The ride was 40 minutes, all in silence. I was too shy to try to drum up any conversation, and I think that even if I tried, he wouldn’t have responded. How I longed for my walkman then.

“Arrived.” His voice eventually jostled me from my thoughts. We were pulling up to a huge, brutalist commercial building, 25 minutes away from the city. This building looked out of place in the middle of the woods. The red, neon sign lit up the packed parking lot in an eerie fashion. The image of a polygonal spiral preceded the words EchoGate Dynamics. The chauffeur drove me up to the door and I asked how much. He just shook his head and pointed at the building. I mustered a thank you before closing the door. He eased the car out of the parking lot and away from view. Looking back on it now, I regret ever setting foot in the place to begin with. Hindsight is always 20/20, huh?

I realize I’ve been reminiscing instead of providing useful information on what all of this is about, so I’ll make a long story short. I ended up working at this company for the longest seven years of my life. Our mysterious benefactor pumped a virtually unlimited amount of money for us to continue our research into parallel universes. I didn’t believe in any of that, it’s theoretical physics, after all, but I figured as long as Daddy Warbucks kept the money coming, I’d write up and solve all of the equations and build all the gadgets he wanted.

There were 518 total people assigned to EchoGate, though most only worked a couple of months out of the year. Quite a few of my colleagues were from all over the globe. I got to talking to a lot of them and even turned some of them into my friends along the way. We’d hang out after work at bars or go to Colby’s house where we played billiards until the sun came back up. We’d vent to each other about the job and sometimes even help each other out with whatever we were working on at the time. Talk hardly ever deviated from work, since it seemed it was mostly what we all had in common, but work always gave us something to talk about.

Seven years had passed, and it felt like my hard work had been paying off. I had a brand new car and bought my own home. Things were pretty repetitive during all that time, working from sun up to sundown 5 days out of the week, coming home to my cat and spending some quality time with him or hanging with my work friends over and over, until one fateful night. One of the tests we were running had opened a rift in space time. It was microscopic and stable. This may not sound like much to a lot of you, but to us, it changed everything. Of course, almost nothing solid could pass through a wormhole so small, but with the help of some of the devices we were accredited with building, we could transmit radiowaves. If we thought we were stacked with work then, we definitely would be now.

I know how hard it can be to think about the concept of a wormhole, but this one we created was even more revolutionary. We had done the impossible and opened the smallest pinhole one could imagine, that also pierced the same exact point in time and space in all other universes. So, to better help you understand, imagine a bubble in a pot of boiling water. It originated from the water itself, where it had heated up and gained energy in little pockets. Now, imagine every bubble that gets created in this pot is a new universe born into existence. Are you starting to get the picture? Let’s take it one step further and have you envision not just a pot of water, but an ocean extending for all of infinity as far as the eye can see. We inconceivably found a way to connect every single bubble across all of existence.

In the same moment it opened, our sensors picked up readings beyond our comprehension. I was in charge of building the device that handled communications between realities and also the dampeners we used to reduce the background noise as well as sort and filter the information from each universe to catalog. I remember the cheers, tears and overall excitement in the room as we realized we had completed this. I also remember being one of only two researchers in the room to be visibly shaken by the horror of realizing the gravity of the situation. Mellie Owens, the one responsible for spearheading the code for the complicated computer we used, was also distraught. She began to cry. Unlike the others, her cries weren’t of joy.

Everything escalated so quickly after that. I went home that night to reflect on the possible consequences of my contributions. Man shouldn’t play with affairs he has no business interfering with. That small tear in the cosmos opened up communications for all of infinity, sure, but it brought something back with it that I couldn’t wrap my mind around. I thought it was paranoia at the time, but I knew in my bones something felt different. Like we had opened a gateway for cosmic beings from warped realities beyond our comprehension to enter the infinite universes that used to be off limits for them. I don’t know how, but I knew that to be a fact even then. Six shots of Scottish whisky were the only reason I was able to sleep that night.

Over the next week, we successfully received messages that we could understand from over 10,000 realities responding to our call, coming in from parallel universes where not much was different. We received signals from over 13 million other universes that we didn’t have the tools to decipher yet, though we had a team dedicated to that. There were a surprising number of universes in which the residents spoke English just like us. One was in Farsi, another in what we believed to be a different version of ancient Sumerian. One that struck me with particular fascination was Universe 7292, which communicated in morse code. Our morse code. No deciphering needed.

At first, I almost let it go though the dampener, as I could only hear short and long beeps, but once I picked up the pattern, I began to quickly translate it onto paper.

“Hello, Universe 0, My name is Charles. I study at Werner Lake University in the country of Mercia and I picked up your signal.”

Charles repeated this message multiple times until we sent a reply to his universe directly the next day. Colby was the one assigned to maintain communication with him, but since I oversaw the logs, I got to peek at the chatter as I pleased, as long as my responsibilities were fulfilled. It turned out that in Charles’s reality, something had made it so their timeline was almost a century behind ours. We had estimated it to be around 1895 there.

Despite being behind in time, their technology was nearly comparable to ours. They lacked in certain departments as far as machinery went, but their medicine, morality, and science was far more advanced. Somehow, we found out that in his reality, the burning of the Library of Alexandra had never come to be. This set them ahead for their time in comparison to our universe and it made me jealous. If I had been the one assigned to communicate with him, I’d ask more questions regarding the library, but instead, Colby thought it more important to ask Charles about his personal life, like his favorite food and what his degree was in (It was risotto and he was working on his Bachelor of Science degree, by the way).

Over time, we found out that Mercia was a country that didn’t exist in our reality, but it sat in the middle of their version of England. Charles had tried to get us to communicate with someone higher up than him, but everyone he tried to talk to would dismiss him as crazy or thought he was playing some sort of prank. This made him our only contact for Universe 7292. He asked more questions than he answered, which was frustrating, but overall, he was a good kid. Bright, too. The only reason we had reached him was because he was experimenting with radiowave technology and managed to pick up this signal. I thought of him as naive for believing our explanation so quickly, though I was grateful to him regardless for being so open with the bits of information he provided.

On day 6 post-initial contact, we noticed he had ceased communications. We left the line open in case he ever got back to us, but we never heard from him again. His last message to us said something about a possible, upcoming solar flare, so we figured it may have knocked out whatever communication array he had built for himself.

The next day, we started receiving a lot of strange messages from a surprising amount of universes we had well-established comms with. Some screamed at us about people being hunted down by giant land squids, others said that people were being replaced by uncanny, cheaply made clones. Two realities informed us that their moon exploded and that the end of the world as they knew it was inevitable. Both of those universes begged us to remember them. A feeling of unease swept across the main floor of the facility that night, but our supervisor reminded us that this was just part of the job and that we should’ve expected things like this.

None of that sat right with me. I felt like there was a pattern somehow, but despite my degree, my mind wasn’t advanced enough to catch onto whatever the pattern was. I took some notes to try to work it out at home, clocked out at the end of my shift, and drove to my abode with my briefcase in the passenger seat.

My brain felt no better when I was home. I stared at the data I had written down until my head hurt, trying desperately to see the bigger picture, but I couldn’t see anything. Perhaps there was no correlation after all, I remember telling myself. I had trouble relaxing, so I finished my bottle of Scottish Whisky and eventually lulled into a deep sleep.

My phone rang around noon the next day, waking me up from my stupor. I stumbled to my feet, eyes still closed and crusted over and picked up the phone with my voice slushing out a meek “Hello?”

“Hello, Mr. Gonzalez.” It was the man that had interviewed me, Darren Moore. “I wanted to congratulate you and thank you for your hard work.” The sour taste in my mouth made me want to gag, but I composed myself. “You can expect quite the considerable bonus on your next paycheck.” Now that I had thought about it, I’d never met Mr. Darren. The only time we had ever spoken was during that concise interview when I had first started. My right ear began to ring again like it did 7 years prior as I held the receiver to my left. There was a soft, staticky crinkling sound in the background of the call that I almost didn’t notice. 

“Thank you, Mr. Moore.” I replied simply.

“I’d like to see you in person, Mr. Gonzalez. You and the rest of the crew. I’ll be at the facility at 11 PM and I’d love for you to be there.” The static grew louder for a split second and I could hear a heavy, percussive thrum in the background while a stranger’s voice cut in.

“Don’t go!” The high-pitched shrilling static resumed as soon as the voice finished talking and as soon as it started, it ended as well. The phone line went back to being quieter.

“Can I expect you to be there?” Darren asked me, though it felt like a command somehow. I hesitated for a moment. It didn’t seem like he heard the stranger’s voice. Another static sound.

“Tell him you’ll be there.” The voice almost seemed like a whisper this time, the other sound still roaring in the background. Some more static and the line went quiet again.

“I’ll be there, sir.” It took everything in me not to let my voice waver.

“Great. See you tonight, Artie. Say hello to your cat for me.” He laughed to himself after he said this. The way he said my name set off red flags all over my body. My teeth felt like they were being grated, my skin was covered in goosebumps, and it felt like my pupils dilated. The line clicked and I hung up the phone. I looked at the clock on my wall to check the time and realized I was supposed to be at work in an hour. I hurriedly started scrambling to get ready, when I noticed the state of my cat. Mouth foaming, eyes shut tight, seizing on the floor.

“Ember? EMBER!” I ran over to him and tried to see if he swallowed anything, but his mouth was clenched down hard.

I threw my shoes on and rushed with him still seizing in my arms to the parking garage. Everything else was a blur. I called out of work using the vet office’s phone while I figured my situation out. The vet said she couldn’t do anything for my best friend who ended up passing away in that office. She solemnly offered me a pamphlet for a place nearby that did pet cremations for cheap. They gave me Ember back along with a clay pawprint in a bag. A fucking grocery store bag.

By the time I left the clinic, it was already dark out. I got home, wrought with depression and confusion. Something in me refused to accept the idea that my ten year old cat had died. I sobbed alone on my couch that night, silently whispering to myself that it should’ve been me. I blamed myself, even though I didn’t know what caused my cat to seize. I was lost in my sadness for several minutes, when a thought interrupted my crying.

“It wasn’t my fault...” I told myself out loud. My voice was hoarse and I was all sniffly. “It was Darren.”

My cat had been so healthy since the day I found him in the alleyway by my old apartment complex. He was skinny and flearidden, but always had so much spirit. He was chatty and quirky and healthy as a bull every time I took him in for a check-up. The only two times he had ever acted strange were the times Darren had called me. Who or what was he, really? Why did he cause my cat such fear? Why did he kill him? I gritted my teeth. What could I even do? I’d never met him before in my life so I didn’t even know what he looked like. Then I saw red. The rage I felt in that moment overcame me as I grabbed a large, heavy wrench I kept in the storage closet and checked the clock again. 10:44 PM. If I drove now, I’d be at EchoGate by 11:20. I was sure he’d still be there.

I couldn’t bring myself to leave Ember at home. I felt like if I wished hard enough and my presence was still close to him, somehow, he’d magically come back. The thunderstorm that night lit up the rainy roads in flashes that night. My thoughts became dark and clouded like the sky, occasionally lit with lightning strikes of questions regarding my sanity. Was I really about to beat a man to death out of the grief I felt over my cat? Did I really think this through? What if this was all just coincidence? There’s no way a man could cause something like this, could he?

“Not a man. An entity.” I said out loud. I interrupted the sounds of the rapid patter of the pouring rain, the squeaks of my windshield wipers, the aggressive hum of my speeding engine and the occasional boom from the thunder. Usually I had a tape playing or the radio blasting, but this time I was focused. My hands gripped the steering wheel tighter. I was in the woods now, no street lights to guide my way. My yellow-tinted halogen headlights were the only thing letting me see what was ahead, aside from the intermittent flashes. At one point, lightning struck and something caught the corner of my eye.

A humanoid figure among the trees to my left. I blinked hard and wiped my tears to remove some of the blurriness. I kept glancing at the rear-view mirror to see if it was still there during the next bolt of lightning. It wasn’t. I shook my head in aggravation and smacked myself to snap out of it. Shortly after, I came upon a sharp turn in the road and now I knew I was 10 minutes away. This made my heart begin to pound harder than before. Whether it was the anticipation or the scare, I wasn’t sure, but looking back I think it was both. I bit some skin on my lip so hard it began to bleed. I wanted all of this to be over. I stepped on the gas harder, getting my money’s worth on the black XJS I had just bought.

I made it to the turnoff that led into the parking lot sooner than I thought, probably due to the speed I was going at. I followed the road like I had a thousand times before until I couldn’t anymore. Not because there was something obstructing the way, but because the road just ended. Right where the security booth should be, stood a giant Douglas Fir. A tree not found in Maine. I looked as far as my headlights could reach and saw nothing but more of the same trees. No parking lot, no facility, just a forest that shouldn’t be here.

“What? No!” I grabbed the wrench on the floor where my feet were and looked at the black bag that held my beloved cat before stepping out of the car. I jogged a few feet forward, looking for any trace of what I knew should be right there in front of me. I couldn’t go far, since my headlights barely made a dent in that darkness, but a sudden flash lit up the area like it was daytime. Among the trees, a figure stood. Not even the lightning could penetrate the shadow of its silhouette. The thunder cracked and echoed through the downpour. From the flash of light, I had seen that the figure stood 10 feet tall at least and was only about 40 feet ahead of me. My grip on the wrench weakened and my car’s headlights flickered. Another flash and another boom. I saw more figures dotting the line where the trees were more dense. Then three lightning strikes in quick succession of each other made the figures seem like they were in a terrifying stop-motion movie. They were walking toward me.

The following explosive sound of the thunder shook me out of my trance and I dropped the wrench, gunning it to my car. If you remember, I hadn’t turned on the stereo, but now it was blaring static at max volume. My ears begged for me to turn it off, though the obvious priority was to get the hell out of there. I slammed that car into reverse and forced my tires to move. I was certain that if the figures wouldn’t kill me, my heart surely would. It felt like it was wanting to break its way out of my ribcage.

My car skidded for a moment before tearing down the road in the direction I came from. A million thoughts passed my mind but I couldn’t think with the static blaring in my head. I reached for the volume knob when a voice silenced the static for me.

“Don’t go!” It was that stranger’s voice exactly how they had said it on the phone. The static returned for a while as I began to connect some dots. Then it died down again, where the stranger continued their dialogue from before. “Tell him you’ll go.” The static came back, louder than ever and I shut the radio off with a trembling hand. My mind sped faster than my car did now, but all my thoughts suddenly stopped when I heard a soft crinkling sound like on the phone call. I couldn’t hear it over the radio’s noise before, but now I heard it clear as day. The crinkling was coming from my passenger seat.

I snuck a glance over at the seat to see Ember slowly poking his head out and sniffing the air. I slammed on the brakes. This was too much. Between trying to put distance between myself and the figures, driving in dangerous conditions, and now seeing my cat come back from the dead, my brain shut down for a moment as I was paralyzed.

I stared at him and he blinked at me slowly. His purrs were loud as he bunted me with his head. His way of asking me to pet him. I choked on the lump in my throat while my stomach did flips and I began sobbing louder than I ever had while fighting the urge to throw up from the emotions. I held him tightly and he rubbed his head on my cheek as if to wipe my tears. My best friend had returned to me.

He suddenly jumped out of my arms and into the backseat and hissed at the car’s back window. I looked to see what he was upset about and saw the figures just standing and staring from far away. I understood what he was trying to tell me and I floored it, speeding out of the woods and back toward the city. When I got home, I packed everything of value into some luggage and took off to some Motel in Connecticut. I thought my home wouldn’t be safe anymore. Later that week, I received my last ever direct deposit. 209.1 million dollars.

I went back home briefly after letting things cool off a few weeks later to try and see if I had left anything else I needed, but the key didn’t turn the lock. I peered inside from one of the blinds and the house was empty. Carpets vacuumed, walls repainted white, and even the stains on some of the countertops were removed. I stopped at Colby’s house but it was empty too. I rang his phone, but the operator said that the call couldn’t be completed as dialed.

After that, I tried going to my old friend Randy’s house, but he swore up and down that he didn’t know me. I stopped trying to explain myself after he threatened to call the police. Any investigations I tried to conduct regarding any of my coworkers’ whereabouts came up empty. It’s like they never existed at all. I was about to give up, when I came across a picture of Mellie Owens in the town paper. She had donated a huge amount of money into cancer research. She wasn’t smiling in the photo. 

It’s 2026 now. Mellie and I live in a modest farmhouse in New Mexico. We got married a few years after meeting up again. Ember is still with us with no signs of aging. The cat is as spry as he was when I first found him. We joke that he’s possessed by some benevolent spirit because he seems to keep the wraiths away (that’s what we call those figures), but part of me thinks that might not be a joke. Every few months, Mellie and I come across a missing persons case that catches our attention. They stop being searched for because no one can seem to remember them. We still don’t know what these things are exactly, but we’re getting close, I think.

In the meantime, Mellie and I have thought long and hard about what we should do with our funds. We’ve spent a lot of time investing it in various ventures so that we wouldn’t have to worry about running out of money anytime in the future. I came up with the idea to start The Beacon Program to reconnect with that wormhole in space and do something good with it. We put our minds together and rebuilt some of the technology we used to keep the realities warned about what we’re each dealing with in the event that some of these entities decide to jump universes.

So, if you happen to find yourself dealing with Wraiths like us in Universe 0, or notice people have completely disappeared and no one remembers them, post your experience here. Get in contact with us and we’ll help you figure out a solution. Otherwise, if there’s anything else you may be facing, educate the rest of the realities. Together, we will make it out of this.

The next post you see here may be an update from me, or perhaps someone else with access to this connection that will provide information regarding whatever entity they are dealing with. Until then, stay safe and be good to one another. We will make it through this.
