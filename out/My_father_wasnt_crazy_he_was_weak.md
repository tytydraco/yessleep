I was a curious child. My mother, Fiona, on the other hand, was rather ordinary. My father, Frederick George Sr.—whose name I unfortunately inherit—was far from ordinary. The lying, cheating, drunk, abrasive cunt.

On my Fourth birthday, a cold Sunday lunchtime. A steaming roast chicken sat on the table, presenting its posterior for chomping. The veggies were mushy and wet, the gravy watery and flavourless. But my favourite, Yorkshire puddings, were plump and soft, with just the right amount of crunch to satisfy my itchy gums. Losing all of your baby teeth at the same time is rather strange, I suppose, but as we've established, I am a curious child.

The grinding sound of Mummy and Daddy shouting hurt my ears. I didn't mind it too much, though, because my mouth was full of those perfect Yorkshire puddings.

I could hear the TV again when Daddy stormed away and Mummy's shouting turned into sobbing. I was perplexed, I was mostly wondering as to why when Daddy came back he was pointing a long metal pipe at Mummy.

“Bang!” the metal pipe exclaimed loudly.

Smoke poofed out of the end of the pipe. Mummy stopped crying, and then Daddy started! It was all rather confusing to me, but my Yorkshire puddings were far too plump and crunchy for me to worry about all that nonsense.

Daddy started playing with Mummy's red paint, trying to wipe it all over the floor with his shirt. He must've gotten bored pretty quickly though because he decided it was his turn with the bang pipe. Daddy turned to me, deep crimson filling the whites of his eyes, he mumbled something incoherent to me, then he put his own red paint all over the walls and ceiling with another “bang!”.

It grew very quiet, very quickly. Sitting there in the silence, a creeping thought came to me—an epiphany, an awakening in my tiny little mind. If Mummy and Daddy weren't going to eat their food, then I could have two more of the most plump Yorkshire puddings in the world. And, of course, they had the perfect amount of crunch.

My university days are finally over. My girlfriend and roommate, Polly Organson, is a rather ordinary girl. Turning 21 in just two days, I have made plans for Polly to meet my parents. My adopted parents, of course, considering the bloody murder-suicide that occurred on my fourth birthday.

For some reason, that day doesn't affect me as much as you'd think. Curious, as was foretold.

The tea with my adoptive parents—good, boring people who smelled of laundry detergent and mild anxiety—had passed with predictable grace. Polly had smiled, eaten her cucumber sandwiches, and proved herself thoroughly, aggressively ordinary.

But as we left, my adoptive mother pressed a heavy, dust-caked shoebox into my hands. “We found it in Frederick Senior’s lockbox after the… the incident,” she whispered, unable to look me in the eye. “We thought you should have it when you came of age. We never opened it.”

Now, back in our cramped university flat, Polly was asleep in the bedroom. I sat at the kitchen table beneath the harsh, buzzing hum of a cheap fluorescent bulb.

I cut the brittle tape on the box with a paring knife. Inside lay a stack of water-damaged betting slips, a broken silver watch, and a thick, leather-bound ledger. The leather was warped, stiffened by some dark, dried substance along the spine, and it smelled faintly of low tide—of rotting kelp and stagnant, salt-heavy mud.

I opened the ledger.

The first few dozen pages were exactly what I expected: the frantic, illegible scrawl of a violent alcoholic. Bar tabs, names of men Frederick Sr. owed money to, and venomous rants about my mother, Fiona. But halfway through the book, the handwriting changed. The aggressive, jagged strokes smoothed out into a terrifyingly precise, microscopic script.

I leaned closer, the light catching the ink. It wasn't black. It was a deep, nauseating shade of greenish-purple that seemed to catch the light incorrectly, as if the ink itself sat slightly above the paper.

March 14th. The geometry of the bedroom is wrong. I measured the corners today with Fiona’s sewing tape. Three corners are ninety degrees. The fourth, above the wardrobe, is eighty-seven. Yet the wardrobe sits flush. It bends when I look away. I can hear the grinding behind the drywall. Not mice. It sounds like a great jaw. A jaw chewing on dry stone.

I felt a strange, familiar prickle at the back of my neck. Grinding. I remembered the sound of my parents shouting on my fourth birthday. I had thought it was just their voices.

I flipped the page. The entries grew denser, losing all sense of dates or structure. Paragraphs broke away into geometric sketches—spirals that didn't seem to have a central point, and multi-angled stars that made my eyes water if I stared at them for more than a few seconds.

Then, my eyes caught a passage underlined so heavily the pen had torn through the parchment.

It does not speak in words. It speaks in textures. It wants the world to be crisp. It wants the marrow dry. It showed me the deep places where the green water sleeps beneath the stars, and the pressure there is beautiful. It told me the lineage must be harvested before the skin softens. Fiona won't let me do it. She says the boy is teething. She doesn't understand. His teeth aren't falling out. They are being recalled.

I stopped breathing.

My hand instinctively went to my jaw. I remembered my fourth birthday. I remembered the strange, empty feeling in my gums, the wet mushiness of the vegetables, and the profound, exquisite satisfaction of the crunch in those Yorkshire puddings. I had thought losing all my baby teeth at once was a medical anomaly.

I looked back down at the microscopic script. At the very bottom of the page, written over and over again until the paper was a shredded mass of ink, was a phrase in a language that made my tongue feel thick and useless just trying to shape it silently in my mind:

Ph'nglui mglw'nafh Cthulhu R'lyeh wgah'nagl fhtagn.

Beside the text was a rough charcoal sketch. It was a depiction of a towering, gelatinous entity rising from an abyssal sea, its face a cluster of feelers. But Frederick Sr. hadn't drawn it with terror. The charcoal strokes were reverent.

I didn't feel horror. I didn't feel the sickening panic that had driven my biological father to paint the living room ceiling with his own brains. Instead, a profound, icy clarity washed over me. The world suddenly felt a little less blurry. The ordinary, mundane fog of my university life—of Polly’s soft, wet sighs from the bedroom—felt incredibly distant.

I ran my finger over the microscopic, impossible ink.

"So," I whispered into the empty kitchen, a small, genuine smile creeping onto my face. "You weren't mad after all, Dad".

From the bedroom, Polly stirred, calling out sleepily, "Fred? Honey, is everything okay in there?"

I looked toward the bedroom door. For the first time, I didn't see my girlfriend. I saw a temporary fixture. A soft, fragile, ordinary thing, waiting to be cleared away from the table.

"Everything is perfect, darling," I called back, my voice smooth and entirely devoid of warmth. "Go back to sleep."
