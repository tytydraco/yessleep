[**\[Part 1\]**](https://www.reddit.com/r/nosleep/s/VLriozD8jn)

I came from a small town. It was one of those places where, even if you didn’t know everybody’s name, you’d recognize their face. It never seemed to grow or shrink, no matter how long you lived there, except on rare occasions. The older folks died off slowly, while the younger ones came from families who’d been there for generations.  Because of this, even though I didn’t know every first name, last names almost always left an impression. If you are from a small town, you’d understand the importance of being a Prescott or maybe a Henderson, a Williams or maybe even a Cooley. None of those names were mine, but in school you always knew whose dad owned the car dealership and whose mom ran the café. Not every piece of news about a family was so prestigious, though. Every town also had its Hughes, Longs, Kippses and Harrises. The drunks and addicts, the families who owed everybody money, the ones the town joked were so “po’” they couldn’t afford the “r.”

Social politics mattered most in places like that. A Prescott couldn’t be seen chatting it up with a Kipps, and a Henderson couldn’t invite a Long to one of their mixers. Nobody ever said those rules out loud, but even my thirteen-year-old brain understood them, thanks to something my uncle often repeated: “Birds of a feather flock together.” I felt them hardest at ten, when I went from being raised by my uncle, a prosperous, educated man and the favorite teacher of most of the town, to being raised by my father, the man the town still called a known addict and a deadbeat. By blood I belonged to one side and by upbringing the other, and both kept their distance.

In a small town, people often had little to do between festivities, and while we waited for the homecoming and fall festivals to come into season, the sport that held everyone’s attention was gossip. Gossip fed into the ego and made one feel larger than they were. Years later, when I moved away, I discovered that the big shots of the town I grew up in would have been nothing in places like Houston, Buffalo, Santa Monica, and more. I grew half convinced that these people were aware of that fact, but craved the power and control that the environment gave them. Small towns made people feel large. Small towns gave people the control they desired. Through the art of gossip they learned to control the narrative. They learned how to steer conversation away from any problems in their own lives and magnify the simple and unfortunate events of another. If every small town was a flock, mine was a wake of vultures.

As we pulled into the parking lot, I could see the natatorium taped off. Officer Mendez’s cruiser was parked out front, and he was talking with Sheriff Morrison.

“Good morning, Hank. I’m glad you were able to make it into class today. How’s everyone holding up? Is your dad doing well?” Mrs. Trout cast her line for something juicy. The stale-coffee stench was bad, but the question was worse. She might as well have asked me if my dad had fallen off the wagon. I hated her for reminding me of the question I’d often asked myself.

“My uncle killed himself seventy yards away. I can see the natatorium from my seat. How are you doing?” I’d hoped  to shut her up, but she persisted.

“He was a good man, your uncle. Tyler and I had dated in high school, you know.”

This was something not even the all-knowing God of creation knew.

“I bet you did.” I rolled my eyes, threw my backpack on the back of my chair and laid my head down.

“Hank?” The emotional hag was smiling at the sight of my misery.

“Ma’am?” I picked my head up and pushed the hair out of my eyes.

“Your flash drive for summer reading. I never received it from yesterday.” She held out her hand, waiting. The bell hadn’t even rung, yet here she was, wrestling a reaction out of me. I figured I’d have to pay up sooner or later, so there was no time like the present.

“I don’t have it.” I rested my hand beneath my chin.

“Did you forget it at home?” She raised an eyebrow over the rim of her glasses.

“No, ma’am.” I stared at her, unwavering.

“Well, son. Where is it?” She crossed her arms over her sagging overweight chest.

“Where does a shadow lay its head to sleep?” I asked. Tyler taught me that riddle when I was nine. He loved testing how I thought, and I’d picked up his habit of answering a question with a riddle.

“Hank, I don’t have time for this. Do you have the flash drive or not?” She bounced her open hand, waiting to receive what I knew was nothing. My classmates filtered in, drawn to the tension between Mrs. Trout and me like rubberneckers at a wreck.

“I bet he didn’t do it!” Jenny squealed. “My daddy says the only thing your family ever does is—”

I stood up, pounding my hands against my desk. “Is what, Jenny?”

Her large doe eyes grew glossy as her soft lips trembled. Jenny was a mean girl, but I had a crush on her since the first day of second grade. Her mother owned the local bakery. “Never mind… I am sorry to hear about your uncle. My family is planning on making a basket for your family and Mrs. Sarah.”

*Oh boy, for the price of my uncle’s death I get to have dry scones and an overly tart danish.*

“How thoughtful of you, Jenny!” Mrs. Trout actually clapped her hands together.

Dustin began to laugh from the back of class, his sharp face contorted in amusement. “Wow, his uncle kills himself and you’re going to torture him more with dry scones and sour danishes?” Given a little more time, I probably would have laughed at this.

“Dustin!” Mrs. Trout pursed her lips.

“I’m just saying, I don’t think anyone wants to eat that sh—” He was cut off by the bell.

“Okay, class!” Mrs. Trout began, cutting off the obscenity that Dustin was about to drop. “Thank you all for completing your summer reading projects. We just have one last presentation to get through today before we go over this semester’s syllabus. Hank, come on up.” She knew I didn’t have it, but she wanted to humiliate me further.

“No. I already told you I don’t have it.” Normally my filter was in place, but that day I didn’t care who I smarted off to.

“Did you do the assignment, Hank?” The whole class had their eyes turned towards me. I could accept defeat or double down.

“Did you brush your teeth, Mrs. Trout? I keep catching a foul smell when you talk and I’m not sure where it’s coming from.” The class roared with laughter as her face went red.

“Hallway, now!” she bellowed.

I got up and grabbed my bag before heading out the door. Jenny looked at me, appalled. Dustin smirked and gave me a thumbs-up.

Mrs. Trout followed me out and pulled the door shut. The bravado wilted and the tears came. I looked up at her through the blur.

“Hank, you cannot be acting out like this in my classroom. I simply will not tolerate it.”

I looked up at her, unable to see the expression on her face. The tears had swallowed my focus. “I’m hurting, Mrs. Trout. It’s barely been a day and I miss him. I just want him back.”

The next thing she did surprised both of us. She hugged me. “I’m sorry he’s gone, Hank. I truly am. If you need some time out here to sit and reflect, that’s okay. I know you probably didn’t complete your project, but if you want to bring me your files by Friday, I don’t see the harm in giving you a fair grade. I won’t take off any points. Is that okay?”

I nodded my head.

“Good. Now, take as much time as you need. I know you two were close. If Officer Mendez asks, which I don’t think he will, just tell him you needed some time alone and that I gave it to you. We are on your side, Hank.”

“Thank you.” And I meant it.

The door clicked shut behind her, and the classroom shrank to a murmur, then to nothing, as if someone had turned a dial.

The hallway was quiet. I could hear the water fountain’s compressor cycling, the slow tick of the clock above the trophy case, and somewhere far off, footsteps that never seemed to come any closer. Officer Mendez, I told myself. The air carried a thin, sweet chemical smell, and it took me a moment to place it: chlorine. The natatorium was seventy yards and two sets of doors away, but I would have known that smell anywhere. It was the smell of Tyler’s hands.

I’d had a joke for every adult in that building. I didn’t have one for the hall.

I slid down the lockers until I was sitting on the cold tile, set my backpack in my lap, and laid my head across the top. Overhead, a fluorescent tube buzzed and stuttered, throwing quick shadows across the floor. I watched the light go in and out of existence, too tired to look away. My eyelids sank as if I were wading through Jell-O, and each blink lasted a little longer than the last. The flicker quickened. The dark stayed longer. At the edges of my vision the shadows crept inward, patient as rising water, until they had swallowed everything but a thin, shaking slit of light.

My heart began to race, and my skin prickled. Somewhere down the hall, a door opened. My eyes snapped open to black. Not dusk, not shadow: black. The windows, which had held a flat gray morning a moment ago, showed nothing at all, no parking lot, no sky, no natatorium, as though the world outside had been unplugged. The little window in the classroom door was dark, too.

Surely I didn’t sleep that long, I thought. If I had, someone would have woken me. Anyone.

I shut my eyes, and when I opened them, the gray daylight was back, ordinary and dull, and a single fluorescent tube twitched overhead like a dying insect. Somewhere behind me, a door closed. It wasn’t slammed. It was shut gently, the way you’d close a door on a sleeping child.

The clock ticked, each second slower than the last. I didn’t close my eyes. I don’t remember blinking. But the light slipped away from me again.

This time I wasn’t alone.

I couldn’t move. I couldn’t blink. At the edge of my vision, where the lockers met the floor, stood a man. He was too tall, his head bent beneath the ceiling tiles, and the dark rolled off his shoulders in ribbons that drifted toward me, feeling the air like tendrils. I began to tremble. I wanted to scream, but all I could do was shake. I felt him before I understood him: hatred, cool hatred. Rage burned out. This was patient. It had waited a long time and was in no hurry. He looked at me like a possession he’d just purchased, eager to use it.

The lights came back. The hallway was empty. I was alone, gasping, still unable to move.

I blinked, and—

He was leaning over me. His face was lost beneath time, and where a face should have been there was only a smile, wide and wet and made entirely of absence. His head tilted slowly. He said nothing. He didn’t need to. When he shifted, noise was disturbed, not absent, but placed underwater.

Then I heard Tyler. He was screaming, and threaded through the screaming, like a second voice, was a laugh in Tyler’s voice that had never been his.

Where does a shadow lay its head to sleep? I had never solved Tyler’s riddle. Now I knew: Nowhere and everywhere all at once. The man bent lower.

I willed myself to scream, and—

**Gone.**

The scream tore out of me a second too late, ragged and raw, as if it had been waiting its turn. The classroom door banged open. Mrs. Trout stood there, wide-eyed, with the whole class craning over their desks behind her. Sound flooded back into the hall: chairs scraping, someone’s nervous laugh, the world pretending it had never stopped.

“Hank! Are you okay?”

I began to sob and shook my head. “No. No, I’m not okay. I’ll never be okay.”

Right behind my ear, a voice with no tongue spoke through its teeth. *This is all your fault.* Nobody else turned their head.

“I want to go home, please. C-can I c-call my d-dad?” I was starting to sound like Greg.

“Of course. I’ll let the office know you’re on your way.”

Somewhere across town, my father was working the overtime I’d sneered at that morning. I was about to pull him off it, and I didn’t care.

I walked down the hall. I didn’t want to pass the window, but I did. Seventy yards away, the natatorium sat behind its yellow tape, every window dark.

“Why did you do it, Tyler?” I asked no one.

No one answered. But somewhere behind me, soft and dry, something rattled and cooed.
