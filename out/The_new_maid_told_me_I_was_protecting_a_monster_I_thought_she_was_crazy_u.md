The ceiling fan turned overhead, its low, rhythmic hum filling the silence of the flat.

I woke up to the sound of the doorbell. Two sharp, impatient rings. I sat up, rubbed the sleep from my eyes, and walked out into the hallway. When I peeked through the brass peephole, I saw our new maid. Her head was tilted at a bizarre angle, her face pressed unnervingly close to the lens. She was smiling—a wide, static grin.

I unlocked the door. "You’re early."

She didn't answer immediately. She just kept that empty smile fixed on me as she stepped inside. As she passed the glass cabinet in the hall, I caught her reflection. For a split second, her reflection didn't walk past. It stayed completely still, turning to look right back at me. When I spun around, she was just walking normally into the kitchen. I shook my head, brushing it off as grogginess.

My mother stepped out, handing her a broom. "Start with the living room today."

The maid nodded without a word. I poured a glass of water, sat on the sofa, and started scrolling through my phone.

A few minutes later, the sound of sweeping stopped. I looked up. The maid was standing dead in the center of the room. She was facing a blank concrete wall, standing completely motionless.

"Everything okay?" I asked.

She turned her head slowly. Her face remained blank for a second before snapping into a polite smile. "Yes. Just cleaning."

She resumed sweeping. I frowned, taking my glass into the kitchen. "Maa, she’s acting weird. She was just staring at the wall."

"She’s just quiet, Ravi," my mother said without looking up. "Don't overthink it."

When I walked back into the living room, I froze.

Sitting on the far end of the sofa was a child—a boy, no older than seven, wearing oversized, faded clothes. Hanging from his neck on a thin string was a small, antique brass key.

The maid stopped sweeping. She turned her entire body toward the kid, her eyes locking onto him with a cold, unblinking focus. She took a slow, deliberate step toward him.

The boy panicked. He bolted off the sofa, running straight toward me. Instinctively, I stepped between the maid and the child.

"Hey! Stop," I said, holding a hand up. "What are you doing?"

The maid stopped, her eyes burning past my shoulder. "Move."

"Why? What’s your problem?"

Behind me, the boy grabbed the hem of my shirt, trembling violently. "Please don't let her take me," he whispered, his voice dry and raspy.

I looked down at the terrified kid, then back at the maid. "Hey, kid, where are your parents?"

"They're gone," the boy muttered.

The maid took another step forward. "You don't know what you're protecting."

"Then tell me!" I snapped. "Why are you cornering a child?"

"That isn't a child."

I let out a sharp, nervous laugh. "What the hell does that mean? He's a kid!"

The boy broke into silent, desperate tears, clutching me tighter. To me, the situation was simple: an unhinged woman was threatening a helpless child.

"Back off," I said firmly. "Go finish the kitchen. Now."

The maid stared at me for three long seconds. Then, slowly, she turned and walked away.

I knelt beside the boy. "Hey, it’s okay. Nobody's touching you. What did she mean earlier?"

The boy looked up, wide-eyed. "She wants the key. She wants to open the dark room."

Before I could ask what that meant, he crawled onto a small floor mattress beside the sofa, curling into a ball. I sat beside him, keeping a reassuring hand on his shoulder. "I'm right here."

By late afternoon, heavy gray shadows stretched across the floor. The boy fell asleep, holding fast to my sleeve. My own eyelids grew heavy, and I drifted off.

When I opened my eyes, the room was bathed in a sickly gray light. The ceiling fan had stopped completely. The boy was awake beside me, hyperventilating in total silence.

Then came a sound from the back hallway. Scrape. Metal dragging against ceramic tile. Scrape.

The boy began to cry softly, hiding behind me. I tried to sit up, but my body wouldn't move. Sleep paralysis. I was pinned to the floor, trapped inside my own useless frame.

A shadow shifted. The maid stepped out of the dark, holding her bag. She walked silently toward the mattress and stood directly over us, staring down at the boy.

Panicking, I threw all my strength into my right arm. My fingers brushed the heavy glass water bottle on the side table. I shoved it with everything I had.

It hit the floor and shattered.

The sharp crack broke the paralysis. I gasped, air rushing into my lungs as I bolted upright. The boy was cowering under the blanket, weeping hysterically.

I scrambled to my feet and ran into the kitchen. "Maa!"

I burst into the room and stopped dead. My mother and sister were standing by the counter, completely motionless. Mid-motion. My mother held a spice container suspended in air; my sister stared frozen at her phone. Neither was breathing.

"Maa?" I grabbed my mother's shoulder and shook her hard. "Maa, wake up!"

My mother blinked rapidly, gasping for air. Beside her, my sister jolted back to life.

"Why are you grabbing her like that?" my sister complained.

"You were frozen!" I yelled. "The kid—he’s terrified! The maid is doing something!"

My mother frowned. "What kid?"

I froze. "The boy. On the mattress."

"Ravi... what boy? There is no boy here."

I backed out of the kitchen and ran into the living room. The mattress was empty.

Down the hall, the master bedroom door was wide open. The wall safe had been ripped apart. Stacks of cash and gold jewelry were strewn across the floor—completely ignored.

The maid was on her knees in front of the safe, clawing frantically through papers. Her fingernails were split and bleeding. She pulled out a small velvet box, shook it, emptied it, and dropped it with a look of pure rage and grief.

"What are you looking for?!" I demanded.

She stood up slowly, turning to face me. Her voice was a low rasp. "Where is it? The boy took the key to the dark room."

She stepped closer, her eyes wide and glassy. "Seven years ago, my son went missing from this building. Everyone said he died. But he didn't leave. Something took him. And today, I smelled him on you. I saw him hiding behind you."

Cold dread pooled in my stomach. "That kid isn't your son."

"No. He isn't." She leaned in, her breath cold against my skin. "He is the thing that took my son. And you protected him."

My blood ran cold. Memory flashed back to me stepping between them, thinking I was the hero.

Behind her, the balcony door didn't open—it simply dissolved into absolute, pitch-black void. The maid took a step backward into the darkness and vanished.

My eyes snapped open.

I bolted upright in bed, gasping for air, sweat pouring down my neck. Bright morning light filled my room. The ceiling fan turned normally overhead.

"A dream..." I whispered, wiping my face. "Just a nightmare."

I walked into the living room. Everything was normal. My mother was reading the newspaper; my sister was drinking coffee. No mattress. No torn-open safe.

"Maa?"

"Morning!" she said cheerfully. "Go wash your face, I’ll make tea."

I turned toward the kitchen, but my foot kicked something on the floor.

Clink.

Lying on the ground was the heavy glass water bottle from my bedside table.

And right beside it lay a small, antique brass key on a string.

My hands trembled as I knelt and picked it up.

Behind me, a small voice whispered from the dark shadow beneath the dining table.

"Don't give it back to her."

I turned slowly. Crouching beneath the table, holding tightly to my mother's chair leg, was the boy. He stared at me with a dark, wide smile. My mother kept reading her paper, completely oblivious to him sitting inches from her feet.

Ding-dong.

The front doorbell rang sharp and clear.

My mother folded her paper. "Oh, that must be the new maid."

"Maa, wait! DON'T OPEN IT!"

She pulled the door open anyway.

The maid stood outside. She looked straight past my mother, her gaze locking onto the brass key in my hand—and then down at the boy crouching at my feet.

Her smile vanished, replaced by an expression of cold, terrifying resolve.

"I told you," she whispered. "You shouldn't have opened the door."
