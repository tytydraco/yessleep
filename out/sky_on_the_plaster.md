If only I could hear my mother call me “beloved,” “sweetheart,” “sunshine”… For all fifteen years, she never addressed me that way. She always found words a bit more pleasing, like “blockhead,” “pretzel,” or just “kid.” I’m starting to forget my own name myself. I suppose it was easier for her to call me “kid” — though even then, she’d hardly have used my name.

She cooked terribly. Breakfast was always an egg drowning in oil, lunch was pasta, and dinner consisted of ready-made meals that you could just pop into the microwave. I washed the dishes most of the time. Though calling it “most of the time” is a stretch... I just washed the dishes. Burnt eggs, oil, and stuck pasta — nothing escaped me unwashed. Mom constantly says nothing good will ever come of me. But I think she’s wrong: I would make an exceptional dishwasher.

The plates are washed, the dishes put away. I don’t idle away my time all that often, but sometimes it happens. I stare up at the ceiling, where I painted a sky: a bright sun, fluffy clouds. I close my eyes and imagine that the sewage water trickling down the pipe in the corner of my room is waves crashing against the shore.

— Son...

Did I imagine that?

— Son, please don’t leave the dishes by the sink. Be a good boy.

Mom’s voice drifted from the kitchen. In an instant, my immersion in idleness was shattered.

— Mom?

— What, sweetie?

— Is everything alright with you?

— Everything is just fine, Glebushka, why do you ask?

What did she just call me? Something is definitely wrong with her. Or something is wrong with me. The voice drifts from the kitchen along with the sounds of a knife chopping something.

— Mom, are you cooking?

— I want to chop some greens. Aunt Sveta suggested a recipe for celery soup.

Celery... soup? I slowly got off the couch and shuffled toward the kitchen.

— Ah... I knew it...

There was no one in the kitchen. No mom, no knife, no celery.

— Well, what is it, Glebushka?

Her soft, gentle voice came from behind me. I didn’t turn around.

— Mom?..

She took me by the hand. I felt her foul breath and the clatter of her rotten teeth right by my right ear:

— Wait for dinner, bunny. Go to your room.

Her hand smoothly turned me away from the kitchen, and dragging my feet, I shuffled back to my room, averting my eyes as much as I could.

I sat down on the couch. The curtained windows creaked in the wind. I looked up at the sky again: the cracked blue of the plaster was illuminated by a single old, dim lightbulb. How many days have passed since the moment I painted this sky? I don’t know if it resembles what’s outside the window anymore. I hear birds — which means they are still flying, which means they still have somewhere to fly. And what about me? Will I be able to fly like before?

I remember Dad swinging me on the swings. They lifted me so high that it felt like I could touch the sun with my hand. And then I would come back down. Was it worth it — growing up?

— Little son, come eat, and don't forget to wash your hands!

I stood up. Waddling from foot to foot, I made it to the bathroom. I put my hands under the hot water. Hands... thick fingers with overgrown nails, dirt gathered underneath. Palms all covered in wrinkles and folds. I looked at my reflection: the bloated body of a forty-year-old man, looking like a swollen drowned corpse. On my greasy, balding head sat something resembling a face. Once upon a time, it was one, but now there is only a patchy beard and eyes as empty as a dried-up well.

I washed my hands with soap and slowly walked to the set table, where she was sitting. My mother.

— Sit down, my good boy.

I sat down. On the table stood a glass and a bottle of liquor. A celery soup steamed in a bowl before me.

— Mom...

— Enjoy your meal, little treasure.

I began to shovel down the hot celery mush. Tears welled up in my eyes — whether from the taste or from the pain, I couldn't tell.

— Oh, what a handsome man you've grown into! I look at you and I just can't get enough. Your dad would be proud of you, you've become a real man.

— Mom...

— I love you, son.

— Forgive me...

I managed to utter those final words before plunging my head face-first into the bowl. And now I am drowning in this soup, and finally, I am warm. Left on the table is an empty bottle of vodka, my lifeless body, and a full plate of sleeping pills.

They won't find me and Mom right away. Though I knew: it would be a very long time before our relatives bothered to search for Mom's corpse, missing from the graveyard.
