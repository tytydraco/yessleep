Closed Eyes

I was born with my eyes closed. That's what Mama always told me.

"Some babies just take longer to open them," she'd whisper, her fingers moving carefully around my eyelids, cool and steady, like she was tucking in something fragile. "Yours are just taking their time, baby. Don't rush it."

It's been eight years since I was born. Eight years of darkness, and Mama's voice, and the smell of this house.

The smell is the first thing I remember noticing, back when I was small enough to still ask questions about everything. Wet wood. Mildew. Something sweetish and rotten underneath it, like fruit left too long in a bowl no one empties. Mama says old houses smell like that. She says it's just age.

The floor creaks in the same six places every time I cross my room — I've counted them, mapped them with my feet the way sighted children map a room with their eyes. The walls make sounds at night. Long, low groans, like something enormous shifting its weight, settling further into the ground. Mama says it's just the house breathing.

I used to believe everything Mama said. I still want to.

Every night, before she turns off the lamp, I ask her the same question.

"Mama? Are my eyes open or closed right now?"

And every night, in that same practiced, gentle voice — a voice I've started to notice never wavers, never once in eight years — she says:

"Closed, baby. Still closed. That's how we keep you safe."

Safe. She's never explained what from.

There are things about Mama's hands I try not to think about.

They're steady in a particular way. Not a mother's clumsy, loving steadiness — a practiced steadiness, the kind you'd need for careful, repetitive work. Every morning she runs her fingertips along my eyelids, pressing gently at the corners, checking something. Some mornings she makes a small sound in her throat, almost disappointed, and reaches for a little tin box she keeps on a high shelf. I hear it open. I hear a thin, cold thread pulled taut. I feel a pinch, quick and precise, at the edge of my eye, and then it's over, and she kisses my forehead, and it's like nothing happened at all.

I asked her once — I was six — what was in the tin.

"Medicine," she said. "To help you heal."

"Heal from what?"

Her hand found my wrist in the dark, gripping harder than I'd ever felt her grip anything. "You don't need to remember," she said. "That's my job. To remember for you."

I didn't understand what she meant. I still don't, not really. But something about the way she said remember for you has sat wrong in my chest for two years now, like a stone I swallowed and never digested.

Sometimes, late at night, I hear her talking to herself in the kitchen. Not on the phone — I checked, once, feeling along the wall for the phone cord and finding nothing, no wire, no receiver, nothing at all. Just her voice, alone, murmuring the same words over and over, too quiet to make out. It isn't the voice of a woman talking to her daughter. It's the voice of someone rehearsing an apology to a person who cannot hear her.

I've never doubted that Mama loves me. I want to be clear about that, because after tonight, I don't know what I'll be allowed to believe anymore.

Tonight, she tucked me in the way she always does. Blanket smoothed to my knees. A kiss, dry and quick, on my forehead. Her weight lifting off the mattress.

"Mama?" I asked. "Are my eyes open or closed?"

"Closed, baby." Already moving. Already leaving. "Still closed."

The door clicked shut. Her footsteps faded down the hallway, past the place where the floorboard always groans, and further, until the house swallowed the sound of her entirely.

That's when the itch started. Small, at first. Deep in the corner of my left eye, where the thread always pinches tightest. I pressed my palm against it and told myself, the way I always do, don't, don't, don't.

But something felt different tonight. Looser. Like a knot that had finally, after eight years, begun to give.

I scratched.

It didn't just loosen. It tore — a small, wet unraveling, like a seam splitting down old fabric — and something thin and cold slid out from beneath my eyelid and dropped against my cheek.

And then there was light.

Except it wasn't the light I'd imagined, all those nights lying awake wondering what seeing might feel like. There was no warm glow, no golden window light I'd built up in my mind from the way Mama described sunny days. What poured into my eye was gray and thin and sick-looking, filtering down through a ceiling that wasn't a ceiling at all — it was open in places, broken through, strung with something dark and hanging that I would later understand were roots, or wires, or rot, I still don't know which.

I turned my head, and through the gap where a wall should have been, I saw the rest of the house for the first time.

There was no wallpaper. No warm bathroom rug, no lamp with its soft yellow shade. The room was a shell, gutted and gray, walls peeling back to black wood beneath, water stains spreading like continents across a ceiling that sagged low enough to touch.

And in the corner — the corner I had slept beside every night of my life without once knowing it was there — stood a row of cribs.

Not one. Not two. Six, maybe seven, pushed together along the rotted wall, rusted bars furred with dust, blankets long gone to gray rags inside them. Some were empty. Some were not, and I made myself stop looking before I understood what I was seeing in the ones that weren't.

I was not the first child in this room.

I don't know how many came before me. I don't know what happened to make room for me here. But I understood, all at once and completely, that Mama's gentle hands and her tin box and her careful thread had done this before. Many times before. And that every night she'd whispered closed, baby, still closed, she had been speaking a sentence she'd worn smooth from repeating it to other children in other cribs, in this same rotted room, long before me.

Down the hall, I hear it — the floorboard. The one that always groans, six steps from my door.

She heard me. She's coming back.

I know I should close my eyes. I know that's what she needs from me, what her hands and her thread and her careful, gentle lies have all been protecting.

But I don't close them.

The door opens.

Mama stands in the frame, but she isn't Mama the way I know her. Her left eye is swollen shut, the skin around it a deep, wrong purple-black. Her neck is a map of bruises, some old and yellowing, some fresh enough to still look wet. Her arms hang at her sides, trembling, like she's holding herself together through sheer will and nothing else.

Behind her, filling the doorway, a shape moves that is not a shape I have words for — too tall, too still, its face catching the gray light in a way that makes it look wrong at every angle, like something built out of a person rather than born as one.

A knife is buried in the side of Mama's neck, just below her jaw. Not deep. Just enough that a thin, steady line of red is tracking down into her collar, and she isn't screaming, isn't even flinching, as though she stopped being able to feel it a long, long time ago.

Her one good eye finds mine across the room.

"Oh, baby," she whispers, and her voice is wet in a way it has never been before. "You weren't supposed to see this part."

The shape behind her tilts its head.

And the door swings shut.
