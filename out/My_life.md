I'm seventy-three now. My hands shake too much to hold a pen properly anymore, so I'm typing this instead, slow, one finger at a time. I was never a famous detective. I worked small cases in a small town my whole career — stolen cars, bar fights, the occasional break-in. Nothing that made headlines. I want you to know that before I tell you the rest, because what happened to me wasn't something a hardened big-city cop walked through. It was something that broke an ordinary man who thought he understood his own life completely.

The case started nineteen years ago. Children were going missing from our town, one every few months, always between the ages of six and nine. The ones we found were never found alive. Every single one had the same thing done to them before they died — a slow, deliberate kind of cruelty I still can't write down without my hands shaking too badly to type. The town went quiet in the worst way. Parents stopped letting kids walk to school. Every stranger became a suspect.

I was assigned to the case with my partner, a younger officer named Desmond — sharp, a little too eager, the kind of cop who took every unsolved case home with him. Desmond noticed the pattern first. Every child taken had, at some point in the weeks before, visited the same small church on the edge of town, where an odd, reclusive groundskeeper everyone called Father Elias kept to himself.

Elias became our first real suspect. He had a record from decades earlier, unrelated but ugly enough to look bad on paper. We brought him in twice. Both times his alibi held. Both times I watched Desmond grow more convinced we were staring right at the killer and just couldn't prove it yet.

Something about Elias never sat right with me as the killer, though I couldn't explain why at the time. I understand exactly why now.

There was a third person who mattered to this case, though I didn't realize it until near the end. A young mother named Corinne ran a small daycare two streets from my house. Every missing child, we eventually discovered, had spent time at that daycare within a year of disappearing. Corinne wasn't a suspect. She was, without knowing it, a thread that could have unraveled everything a full year earlier — because during an interview, almost in passing, she mentioned that a woman from the neighborhood used to volunteer there sometimes, helping out on days they were short-staffed.

I didn't think anything of it at the time. I wish I had.

The case ran cold for almost a year after that. Then, four months ago, another child went missing. This time it was a classmate of my own daughter Ruth — a boy named Toby, who lived two streets over from us. For the first time in the entire case, we found something new at the scene: a single strand of hair caught on the fence behind Toby's house, long, gray-blonde, unmistakably a woman's.

I remember standing there with the evidence bag in my hand, running through every woman connected to the case, and I remember my mind refusing, physically refusing, to finish a thought that was trying to form. I told myself it belonged to a neighbor. A jogger. Anyone at all.

It matched my wife.

I didn't confront her right away. I told myself I needed more before I could even consider it. So I did what thirty years of training had taught me to do. I watched. Quietly. The way I'd watch any suspect.

I noticed things I'd spent two decades not noticing. The way she always volunteered to pick Ruth up alone on the exact days another child had last been seen alive. A locked drawer in the garage she'd always told me was just old nursing supplies. The nights she said she couldn't sleep and went for long walks alone, coming back an hour or two later than she said she'd be gone.

Three weeks ago, I followed her.

She walked to the old groundskeeper's shed behind Elias's church — a building we had searched twice ourselves and found completely empty both times. I watched her let herself in with a key. I watched her come back out forty minutes later, calm, unhurried, wiping her hands on a cloth before folding it neatly into her coat pocket.

I went in after she left.

I won't describe what I found in there. I've been a police officer for thirty years and I have never, not once, had to leave a scene and be sick in the grass before I could make myself go back in and finish looking. Everything the case had been missing was there. Photographs. A kind of ledger, dates written in careful, familiar handwriting I'd seen on grocery lists and birthday cards for twenty years. And on the last page, dated two days from when I found it, was a single name.

Ruth.

Our daughter. Her own daughter. Next.

I need to back up now, because none of what happens next will make sense unless you understand who Marlene was to me, and it's only now, writing this out, that I understand how completely I never actually knew her at all.

I met Marlene when I was twenty-six, my first year on the force. She was a nurse at the hospital where I got stitched up after a bad domestic call. She was, by every appearance, the gentlest person I had ever met. She drove an hour out of her way to bring soup to an elderly patient with no family left to visit him. She cried during sad commercials. She once spent an entire Saturday nursing a bird back to health that had flown into our window, feeding it with an eyedropper every two hours through the night.

We married two years later. For twenty years, my marriage was the one part of my life that never gave me a single reason to doubt it.

I think about that bird sometimes now, and I wonder if she kept it alive out of love, or because she liked deciding exactly when something small and helpless got to stop suffering.

I confronted her the night I found that ledger. I'm not going to pretend I handled it the way I was trained to handle a suspect, because in that moment she wasn't a suspect to me. She was still my wife, sitting across the kitchen table where we'd eaten ten thousand dinners together, looking at me with the same gentle eyes she'd had the night she saved that bird, and she said the words I don't think I will ever stop hearing for the rest of my life.

"I always finished with the ones I loved the most. It's the only way I know how to keep them from ever leaving me first."

I asked her about Ruth. She just smiled, small and sad, like I was the one failing to understand something obvious.

"She was always going to be the last one," she said. "The most important one is always saved for last."

I want to tell you Ruth is safe now. I want to end this the way a real detective closes a case file, everything accounted for, everything resolved.

I can't.

Marlene was arrested that same night. I sat in the back of the patrol car with her, my own wife, in handcuffs, and I couldn't look at her the entire drive. She never once stopped smiling.

Three days later, during transport to a psychiatric evaluation, the van she was in was involved in a collision. When they opened the doors, her restraints were empty. No body. No blood. Nothing but the cloth she always carried, folded neatly on the seat where she'd been sitting, like she'd simply stood up and walked out through a locked door that never opened.

Ruth has been staying with my sister since that night. I check the locks on every door in that house myself, every single evening, twice.

Two nights ago, Ruth called me, crying, saying she found something on her pillow when she got home from school.

It was a small, folded cloth. Clean. Carefully folded. Exactly like the one Marlene always carried in her coat pocket.

I haven't slept since. I keep thinking about what she said to me that last night at the kitchen table  that the most important one is always saved for last  and I don't know anymore if that case is finished, or if I simply haven't found out yet how it really ends.
