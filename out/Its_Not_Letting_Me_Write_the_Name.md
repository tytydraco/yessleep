Before you dive in, do yourself a favor. If you ever find yourself wandering the streets of New York, and someone whispers about a restaurant named ████████, just walk away. Wait a minute. The name was censored automatically. It's not letting me write the name. Hell.

 

Trust me, I’ve already tried to write that name out, three times now, but it keeps getting censored. I’m going to tell you everything I remember. Promise me this though. If you ever piece together the name of that restaurant, don’t go looking for it. Seriously.

 

It all started on a Sunday night. I remember the air, cold enough to sting your cheeks, but I didn’t want to go home, and neither did Ahana. We drifted through streets we barely recognized, half-lost, half-killing-time, hoping the city would give us something. Anything, worth remembering. And that’s when we saw it. I’d swear it hadn’t been there before. Heavy black door, fat gold handle, yellow glow from inside. Through the glass I saw tables jammed with people. A new restaurant? Or had it just slipped past us all this time?

 

I nudged Ahana, “Let’s check it out.” She kind of shrugged, didn’t really care, but followed any way.

 

We walked in, right into this odd hush. Maybe twenty people, maybe more. Hard to tell, because they all acted the same. Bored, fidgety and annoyed. All the plates, perfectly clean and unused. Nobody had food. Nobody even had water. The only thing moving in there were the phones. Lit-up faces, scrolling, waiting. No menus, just the flicker of annoyance growing across the room. The waiters and staff all stood together with a warm smile.

 

Five minutes went by. A big guy at a table near us stood up, shoved his chair back so hard I thought it would break.

 

“This is ridiculous,” he growled, then left in a huff. Then a couple more, annoyed, shuffled after him. But for every person who left, two more slipped in, almost excited. Weird, right? It's like the room was breathing, letting people out, sucking more in.

 

People started getting louder, complaining and shouting at the staff for service. The low buzz turned hostile.

 

Finally, the chef walked out from the kitchen and stood before us. I mean, he towered over everyone. A big guy with a huge white hat. He looked calm, almost like he was enjoying all the fuss. Right away, someone barked out, “What’s going on here?”

 

“We’ve been waiting twenty minutes!” someone else yelled.

 

“You haven’t even taken our orders!”

 

He just smiled, hands folded, silent for a beat. Then he said, smooth as anything: “We don’t serve until the restaurant is full.”

 

People snapped. Threats, laughter, confusion. Everyone just shouting over each other, trying to get noticed. He let them rant for a second, then raised his hands. “Calm down,” he said, but nobody did.

 

And then he dropped it. “I haven’t told you the best part.” His eyes glinted, that smile never going away. “If you eat here. After you waited, with the restaurant full. You get free food here, for the rest of your life.”

 

The room went still. Someone let out a short, ugly laugh. “You’re joking, right?”

 

“Completely serious.”

 

That was all it took. Suddenly, everyone wanted in. Out came the phones again, calling friends, sending frantic texts, yelling for family to join. The crowd swelled. Chairs filled up, people sitting wherever they could, shoulder-to-shoulder. The whole thing felt feverish. Like a contest nobody wanted to lose.

 

The chef took another long look, smiled wider. “Good,” he said, clapping his hands. “Now we’ll begin.” People actually cheered. The tension broke, just a little. But then he raised a finger, and the room snapped back to waiting.

 

“One last thing.” His voice carried across the clamor. “Anyone who leaves. Well, don’t worry. I will still serve you.”

 

Nervous laughter bounced off the walls, but the mood soured fast. A few muttered about leaving. One man stood. “I’m not waiting around for this nonsense.” He walked out. Others followed, but most stayed, glued to their seats by hope, or maybe just stubbornness.

 

The chef watched them go with a look I didn’t like. Like he already knew what they'd choose. He stepped into the kitchen and the staff who were standing together followed him inside in a single line. A heavy silence filled in.

 

A few seconds ticked by. Then servers pushed out platters, one after another. The smell was phenomenal. People started grabbing plates before the servers even finished handing them out. For the first time, the energy shifted. Hungry, eager, but nervous, too. Everyone was stealing glances at everyone else, trying to read the room.

 

I hadn’t gotten my food yet when this old woman appeared at my elbow. Crumpled dress, shaking hands, voice barely there. “Excuse me. Is it true the food is free?”

 

I nodded. “That’s what they say.”

 

She looked desperate, hungry. “I haven’t eaten since yesterday. Would you mind if I took your seat?” Her voice cracked, not begging, just hope barely holding itself together.

 

I looked at Ahana. She didn’t even notice, still glued to her phone and then back at the old woman. I stood up, let her sit, and she slid in like she’d been waiting her whole life.

 

“Thank you,” she whispered, more to herself than to me.

 

I walked out. Found a hot-dog cart outside because, honestly, after that circus, normal food sounded good. I got a hot dog, sat across the street, and watched through the glass. The whole thing felt like some viral stunt, a pop-up restaurant playing mind games for social media. That bit with the death threat. Just marketing, right? Who really takes that stuff seriously?

 

My hot dog was nothing special, but I was starving. I watched the windows. Inside, everyone had finally started eating. But I realized something. Nobody was talking. It was just eerie silence.

 

Ten minutes, then twenty, passed. I kept looking for Ahana. Scanned the faces. Nothing. Then a sharp, twisted laugh echoed out from inside. It didn’t sound like a joke, though. It was too jagged, forced. And then another, and another. It started spreading, bubbling. Everybody in there started laughing. All at once, mouths wide, eyes fixed, teeth on display.

 

If you’ve ever heard laughter that sounds like screaming, you’ll know what I mean. I felt my nerves spark like a frayed wire.

 

I walked up to the glass, pressed my face close. That’s where I saw it. All these people, standing, necks twisted toward me. Their mouths shouldn’t have been able to stretch that wide, not without tearing. White teeth, red-stained lips, blood dripping down chins. Their grins slashed across their faces, overexposed, monstrous. One by one, they closed in on the glass, pressing up, eyes locked on me. I scanned the crowd again, heart thumping, “Ahana?” My voice sounded weak even to myself.

 

In the back, I caught a glimpse. Someone shielding their face, hiding, maybe Ahana, or maybe some new nightmare variation. For a second, I wondered if she’d eaten or if she was still herself. I couldn’t tell. Panic rose in my throat, sour and sharp.

 

As I backed away, something tapped the window. Gentle, then sharper.

 

I looked. The old woman was right there, her smile soft, almost sweet, completely normal. She lifted her hand, holding a plate with just a few scraps, and pointed at me, then at the food.

 

I blinked. Her lips twisted. They started to stretch. Slow at first, then way, way too wide. Her face split open into the same awful smile. Blown open, ragged, stained red.

 

Behind her, faces pressed closer, greedy mouths opening, bloodied lips twitching into permanent, grotesque grins. Twisted laughter swelled, and in the middle, I could hear Ahana's voice.

 

That’s when every survival bone in my body kicked in. I ran. I didn't care. I put as much distance between me and that restaurant as I could, zigzagging blocks, lungs burning, legs jelly.

 

I made it home somehow, slammed my door, crashed to the floor, hands shaking. I focused on anything real. My breath, the textures of the rug, heart pounding like a drum.

 

When I could move again, I realized I was still clutching the hot-dog wrapper. Blood speckled the paper. My thumbprints. Fresh, red. I raised my hand to my lips; it came away sticky.

 

A shudder. I tried to recall. When did that happen? Then it hit me. The guy selling hot dogs. White chef’s coat. Tall hat. Calm, steady eyes. Just for a split second, I hadn't recognized him.

 

I stumbled to the bathroom, turned on the harsh light. My reflection looked like me. Blank, pale, but me.

 

I forced a smile.

 

And my mouth began to open.

 

And kept opening.
