The events of that night have stayed with me, the utter strangeness of them keeping the memories fresh, when most everything else has faded to time. 

I don’t remember where I had been going, but I do recall that I had been driving for a while when the thing ran out into the road. Maybe I should have been better prepared; every few miles there was a sign warning about deer crossing. But it was late, and the empty road had been one long featureless stretch for over an hour at that point, and I guess I got complacent.

I’d only glanced down for a second to fiddle with the knob for the radio, far enough away from both the town I was leaving and the town I was headed towards that most of the frequencies were little more than static.

I’d seen it out of the corner of my eye, some dark mammalian thing leaping out of the night too fast for me to avoid. Not that I didn’t try, slamming the brakes, twisting the wheel, blind panic making me stupid. Whatever it was hit the front of my car hard.

When I finally skidded to a stop, I sat there in the dark, hands gripping the wheel at ten and two, my heart pounding. The radio was a sibilant hiss pockmarked with bursts of almost identifiable sound. I unglued one hand from the wheel long enough to cut the engine and it ceased, leaving me in the quiet dark, pierced only by the yellow slice of headlights and the pinging of the engine as it cooled.

One of those stupid yellow signs shone back at me like some modern coat of arms. A deer rampant. The sign was flecked with gore.

I reached across to the glove box, flipping it open and grabbing the flashlight inside. I wanted to see what it was I had hit, if it was okay, if it was going to live.

Not a deer, I didn’t think. It hadn’t looked like a deer.

I unbuckled my seat belt and pushed the door open, the car’s interior light coming on. The smell is what hit me first, something sulfuric and egg-like, strong enough to make my eyes water.

I covered my face with my shirt and with my other hand shone the flashlight around the driver’s side, though it illuminated little more than what I could already see. 

The air was very still. And then I heard the moan.

I’d almost calmed down at that point, but the sound had my heart leaping again in an instant. It sounded like a woman. Had I hit a woman?

It didn’t matter at that moment that whatever I had seen had been too big to be human, or had been on all fours. If I’d hit a person– I didn't want to finish that thought.

I staggered to my feet, adrenaline pumping through me and making my legs shake. I followed the moaning around the back of the car where the tail lights cast the road and shoulder in red shadows.

Something lay crumpled in the grass on the side of the road like a black tarp. It didn't move.

I let the beam of my flashlight trace over its shape, my mind finally making sense of what I was looking at. It was some kind of great black dog, larger than any I had ever seen before. 

I think it was paralyzed. Only its muzzle moved, showing flashes of teeth as it let out another long, too human sounding moan. One eye watched me, showing the white as it rolled in the socket. My flashlight caught something yellow at its neck. A metal tag.

I squeezed my eyes shut for a moment, but it was worse somehow, to turn away from its suffering. I pulled an old blanket out of the trunk of my car and laid it over the poor beast, sitting cross legged in the grass by its side. I made myself stroke its neck, and I remember how warm it was, how soft its fur.

It didn’t suffer long.

I reached for the tag when it breathed its last, knowing I had to reach out to the owner. But engraved on the front of the yellow diamond was my own name, first and last. I turned it over. There was no phone number, but the day’s date was pressed into the metal. 

I didn't know what to make of it at the time. I have some theories now.

Decades have passed since, and though I should be old, I haven’t aged a day. Whatever I did, I seem to have placed myself outside the flow of time. It’s okay for now; I’m grateful for the extra time. But I don’t know what will happen to me in a hundred years. In a thousand. 

I suppose I’ll find out.
