​A couple of years ago, I was browsing through a quiet weekend flea market in the old quarter of my city when I noticed an elderly man selling tarnished silverware and rusted tools off a dusty blanket. Tucked behind a stack of copper plates was a heavy, six-sided brass oil lamp. Unlike the cheap tourist souvenirs you see everywhere, this one had intricate geometric grids and tiny, backwards numbers scratched into the metal around its base.

​Curiosity got the better of me, and I bought it for almost nothing. The old man didn't even count the bills I handed him; he just wrapped the lamp in newspaper, shoved it into my hands, and packed up his blanket before I had even walked ten feet away.

​When I got back to my apartment and tried to clean the brass, I noticed the narrow spout where the wick sat had been plugged shut with hardened red wax, and a thin iron nail was driven straight through the lid so it couldn't be lifted. Thinking a previous owner had just modified it to keep oil from spilling, I grabbed a pair of pliers, yanked the rusted nail out, and scraped away the wax.

​Inside, there was no oil. Instead, the belly of the lamp was stuffed tightly with dry, brittle black hair wrapped around a small piece of charred animal bone. A wave of stale, metallic air hit my nose, smelling faintly of burnt cloves and copper.

​Disgusted, I dumped the contents into a trash bag, washed the lamp in the sink, and set it on my bookshelf before going to bed.

​That night, I woke up at 3:10 AM because my bedroom was freezing. Not just winter-cold, but the kind of deep, damp chill you feel inside an underground cave.

​Across the room, the hallway light was on—even though I always turn every light off before sleeping—and a dark silhouette was visible through the frosted glass panel of my closed bedroom door. It was standing completely motionless right outside, pressed flat against the glass. It was far too tall to be a normal person, its shoulders hunched awkwardly near the top of the doorframe.

​Then, the brass handle of my door began to rattle. It wasn't a forceful jiggle, like a burglar trying to break in. It was a slow, rhythmic tapping of fingernails against the metal—*tick, tick, tick*—followed by the sound of something dragging a heavy foot across the hallway tiles.

​I stayed frozen under my sheets, staring at the glass, waiting for the shadow to move. After twenty minutes, a dry, raspy voice spoke from the other side of the door. It didn't use words at first; it just mimicked the exact sound of my own voice clearing my throat from earlier that evening, followed by the metallic *clink* of pliers hitting a table.

​It repeated those two sounds over and over in a loop until the first pale light of sunrise hit my window. The second the room brightened, the shadow vanished from the frosted glass, and the hallway light clicked off by itself.

​I thought I was losing my mind from lack of sleep, so the next evening, I put the brass lamp inside a shoebox and locked it in my hallway closet.

​That turned out to be a mistake.

​Around 2:30 AM the second night, I didn't hear tapping on the door. I woke up to the sound of my closet door creaking open in the hallway, followed by the unmistakable *scritch-scratch* of an iron nail being dragged across my bedroom wall—not outside in the hall, but inside the room with me, moving slowly along the plaster toward the head of my bed.

​When I bolted upright and switched on my bedside lamp, the room was empty. Except there, etched deep into the white paint right above my headboard, was the exact same grid of backwards numbers that was carved into the base of the brass lamp. And sitting neatly on the edge of my nightstand was the rusted iron nail and the clump of black hair I had thrown into the outside dumpster the day before.

​I didn't wait for sunrise. I grabbed a pair of oven mitts, shoved the hair, the bone, and the nail back inside the lamp, melted an entire candle over the spout until it was overflowing with wax, and drove six hours south before dawn. I buried that thing four feet deep in the middle of an empty salt flat miles away from any road, and I have never bought anything secondhand since.
