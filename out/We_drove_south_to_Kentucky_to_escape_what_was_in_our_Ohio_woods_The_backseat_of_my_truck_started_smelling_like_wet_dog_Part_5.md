We left Cleveland around seven o’clock that evening, pushing hard down I-71 toward Cincinnati before cutting south into Kentucky.

I drove with the cab heater cranked low and the radio turned off, my eyes bouncing between the yellow center line and the side mirrors. Sarah sat in the passenger seat with her arms wrapped tight around her waist, staring straight ahead into the dark. In the backseat, Wyatt was curled up under a heavy quilt, quiet for once. Usually an eight year old boy on a five hundred mile road trip can't sit still for twenty minutes, but he hadn't asked a single question since we left the driveway.

Around midnight, somewhere past Columbus on a stretch of highway where the trees crowd right up to the guardrail, I caught a shadow moving fast in the ditch beside the right lane

My grip tightened on the steering wheel. I pushed the speedometer up to 75, then 80, watching the dark shape keep pace effortlessly through the brush and weeds. It didn't run like a deer or a coyote. It loped in long, heavy, rhythmic bounds, upright enough that its broad shoulders cleared the tall weeds

"Mark?" Sarah’s voice was barely a whisper. "Why are you going so fast?"

"Nothing," I said, keeping my voice steady so Wyatt wouldn't hear the panic in it. "Just trying to make good time before we hit the hills."

We didn't stop for gas until we were well past Cincinnati and deep into Northern Kentucky, pulling off at a bright, well lit truck stop near Florence around two in the morning.

I kept my hand on the butt of my shotgun as I walked around to pump fuel, scanning the dark tree line behind the semi trucks and watching the security cameras buzz overhead. Nothing came out of the woods. For a second, I let myself breathe. Maybe five hundred miles really was enough. Maybe they didn't care enough to chase us out of state.

When I got back into the cab, Sarah looked over her shoulder into the backseat. She frowned, wrinkling her nose.

"Do you smell that?" she asked softly.

I sniffed the air. Beneath the faint scent of gasoline and cold coffee from the truck stop, there was something else. Heavy, musky, and foul like smell almost like wet wool, rotting leaves, and copper.

The exact smell of the mud in our flowerbed back in our now abandoned farmhouse.

I turned around in my seat and shone my flashlight toward the back. Wyatt was still under his quilt, breathing slow and even. Nothing was back there. Nothing could fit behind the front seats without us seeing it.

Except when I pulled the flashlight beam back toward the rear window, I saw a thin smudge of dark, greasy dirt right on the edge of the back glass and a faint, wet print pressed into the dust on the cover of the truck bed.

It hadn't followed us in the woods. It had somehow hitched a ride.

We didn't sleep another wink the rest of the way down to her parents' place outside Lexington. We pulled into their gravel driveway just as the sky was turning a pale, bruised purple before dawn.

Her dad, a retired ironworker named Bill who keeps a shotgun of his own by the back door, met us on the porch with a mug of coffee and a sharp look in his eyes. He took one look at my crumpled Chevy hood, the plywood scraps still stuck to a nail in the truck bed, and the exhaustion etched into Sarah’s face, and didn't ask a single stupid question.

"Get them inside," Bill said quietly, resting a heavy hand on my shoulder. "I'll help you unload."

We’ve been here for six hours now. The house sits on thirty acres of rolling pasture and thick ridge timber, a lot hillier than the flat cornfields back home, with a long gravel drive winding up from the county road.

It’s quiet here right now. Too quiet.

Bill just walked back from the edge of the back pasture a few minutes ago. He didn't look scared ironworkers don't scare easy but his jaw was tight when he stepped onto the porch.

"Found some big tracks down by the creek bed," he told me, leaning a rifle against the doorframe. "Bigger than any coyote or stray dog around here. And they weren't walking on four legs."

Sun’s going down in a couple of hours. This time, I’m not alone with a shotgun and I’m done running.
