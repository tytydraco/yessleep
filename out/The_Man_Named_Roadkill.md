The first time I heard about Roadkill, I thought it was an internet urban legend.  
You know the kind. Some guy on a forum claims his cousin saw something in the woods, somebody else claims their uncle knows a cop who investigated it, and eventually the whole thing becomes another story people tell each other at two in the morning.  
I never believed in any of that stuff.  
I still don’t know if I believe in Roadkill.  
But I don’t drive down country roads anymore.  
And if I ever hit an animal, I don’t stop to check what happened to it.  
I know how that sounds.  
Let me explain.  
I used to work night shifts at a warehouse outside a small town in Tennessee. Nothing exciting. Ten-hour shifts, bad coffee, and a forty-minute drive home through roads that barely had streetlights.  
Most nights, I’d get off around 2:30 a.m.  
There was a stretch of road about fifteen minutes from my house that locals called Dead Man’s Curve. It wasn’t actually that dangerous, at least not if you knew how to drive it. It was just a long bend surrounded by trees, with a shallow ditch running alongside the shoulder.  
There were always dead animals out there.  
Possums. Raccoons. The occasional deer.  
You got used to seeing them.  
One Thursday night in October, I hit a possum.  
I didn’t even have time to react. It ran out from the grass, straight into my headlights, and disappeared beneath the front of my car.  
Thump.  
I checked my rearview mirror.  
I could see something lying in the road behind me.  
I felt bad, obviously. But what was I supposed to do? Get out in the middle of the road at two in the morning to check on a possum?  
I kept driving.  
About five minutes later, I noticed a pair of headlights behind me.  
Nothing unusual about that. It was a public road, after all. But these headlights were strange.  
They stayed the same distance away no matter what I did.  
I sped up.  
They sped up.  
I slowed down.  
They slowed down.  
When I turned onto my road, they continued straight.  
I remember thinking that was weird, but I didn’t give it much thought after that.  
Not until the following night.  
I was scrolling through Facebook during my break when I saw a post shared by somebody from my hometown.  
It was a picture of a raccoon lying dead on a road.  
The caption read:  
“If you hit an animal on Old Mill Road last night, you might want to go back and check on it.”  
The comments were full of jokes.  
Somebody said the raccoon had a family to feed.  
Somebody else said the driver should be arrested for leaving the scene of an accident.  
Then I saw a comment from an account with no profile picture.  
It said:  
“He saw you leave.”  
That was it.  
Four words.  
I don’t know why they bothered me so much. People post creepy nonsense on the internet every day.  
I checked the account.  
It had been created three years earlier, but there were no posts, no friends, and no other comments.  
I clicked away.  
A few minutes later, I checked again.  
The comment was gone.  
I wish I’d forgotten about it then.  
I really do.  
Two nights later, I was driving home from work when I saw a man standing on the shoulder of Dead Man’s Curve.  
At least, I thought it was a man.  
He was tall. Ridiculously tall. The kind of tall that makes you question your own sense of proportion.  
He stood perfectly still beside the ditch, facing the trees.  
He was thin, too. Not just skinny, but lanky in a way that made his arms look too long for his body. They hung beside him almost to his knees.  
He wore dark clothes.  
I couldn’t make out his face.  
My headlights passed over him for maybe two seconds.  
Then I was past him.  
I checked my rearview mirror.  
He was standing in the middle of the road.  
I slammed on the brakes.  
There was nothing behind me.  
No man.  
No headlights.  
Nothing but empty asphalt and trees.  
I sat there for a solid minute, trying to convince myself I’d seen somebody walking along the shoulder and that my tired brain had exaggerated it.  
Then I noticed something in the ditch.  
A dead possum.  
The same one I’d hit two nights earlier.  
I knew it was the same one because of the white patch of fur on its back. I’d seen it clearly in my headlights when I’d passed that night.  
Except now it was lying several feet away from where I’d originally seen it.  
And there were footprints in the dirt beside it.  
Bare footprints.  
They started at the animal.  
They went toward the road.  
And they stopped at the edge of the asphalt.  
There were no footprints on the other side.  
I drove home.  
I didn’t stop again.  
The next few nights were normal.  
I almost convinced myself I’d imagined the whole thing.  
Then, on Monday, I found a dead bird on my front porch.  
I live alone. My porch is covered, and there’s no tree directly above it.  
The bird was lying on its side, its wings tucked against its body.  
There was nothing visibly wrong with it.  
I assumed a cat had brought it there, although I’d never seen a cat around my house.  
I picked it up with a plastic bag and threw it away.  
When I came back inside, I noticed something scratched into the paint on my front door.  
It was small and shallow, like somebody had used a nail.  
Three words.  
YOU LEFT IT.  
I called the police.  
They took a report, looked around the property, and told me there wasn’t much they could do. No footprints. No signs of forced entry. No cameras on my house.  
One of the officers asked if I’d had any problems with neighbors.  
I told him no.  
I didn’t mention the possum.  
I don’t know why. Maybe I was embarrassed.  
Maybe some part of me was beginning to suspect that whatever was happening had something to do with that animal.  
That night, I slept with every light in the house on.  
The following Wednesday, I finally told my coworker about everything.  
His name was Mike. He’d lived in the area his entire life, and he’d heard just about every local legend there was.  
I expected him to laugh at me.  
He didn’t.  
He asked me exactly where I’d hit the possum.  
I told him.  
He went quiet.  
Then he asked me whether I’d seen a tall man on the shoulder of the road.  
I felt my stomach drop.  
“How do you know about that?”  
Mike looked around the break room before answering.  
“My dad used to tell me about him when I was a kid.”  
He called it an old story. Older than the internet. Older than most of the houses around here.  
According to Mike, there had always been stories about a man who appeared on rural roads after somebody hit an animal and drove away.  
Nobody agreed on what he looked like.  
Some people said he had the face of a dead animal.  
Others said he had no face at all.  
But everyone who claimed to have seen him described the same thing.  
He was tall.  
He was thin.  
And his arms were too long.  
“People call him Roadkill,” Mike said.  
I laughed.  
Not because I thought it was funny. I just didn’t know what else to do.  
Mike didn’t laugh with me.  
He told me his father had known a man who swore Roadkill had followed him home after he’d hit a dog.  
The man started finding dead animals outside his house.  
First, a bird.  
Then a rabbit.  
Then a stray cat.  
Every time, there was a message.  
Every time, the messages got shorter.  
Until one morning, the man found a dead deer on his front lawn.  
There was no message that time.  
Just a single muddy footprint on the deer’s back.  
Mike said the man disappeared three days later.  
His car was found abandoned on a country road.  
The driver’s door was open.  
There was blood on the steering wheel, but no sign of a struggle.  
The police never figured out what happened to him.  
“That’s an old story,” Mike said. “Probably not even true.”  
I asked him what Roadkill supposedly did to the people he followed.  
Mike stared at his coffee.  
“I don’t know,” he said. “Nobody who knows has ever come back to tell it.”  
I changed my route home after that.  
It added twenty minutes to my commute, but I didn’t care.  
For a while, I thought that was the end of it.  
Then I started noticing the animals.  
A dead squirrel outside my workplace.  
A dead rabbit beside the road near the grocery store.  
A bird lying on the sidewalk outside my apartment.  
I know what you’re thinking.  
Animals die. That’s what they do.  
But these animals weren’t being hit by cars.  
At least, I didn’t see any evidence that they were.  
They were just lying there.  
And every single one of them was facing the same direction.  
Toward me.  
I stopped going outside unless I had to.  
I started checking my windows before bed.  
I bought a security camera and mounted it above my front door.  
For three nights, nothing happened.  
On the fourth night, my camera detected movement at 3:17 a.m.  
I watched the footage the following morning.  
At first, there was nothing.  
Then, at the far edge of the frame, a figure emerged from the darkness.  
It walked toward my house.  
Slowly.  
Its legs moved normally enough, but its arms swung far below its hips.  
It was wearing what looked like a dark jacket.  
Its head was tilted to one side.  
When it reached my porch, it stopped.  
It stood there for almost four minutes.  
Then it raised its head and looked directly into the camera.  
The footage was grainy, but I could make out something hanging from its right hand.  
A thin, curved piece of metal.  
Like a fishing hook.  
Except much larger.  
The figure reached up with its other hand and touched the camera.  
The screen went black.  
When the footage resumed, it was gone.  
I checked the porch.  
There was nothing there.  
No dead animals.  
No messages.  
Nothing.  
For the first time in days, I felt relieved.  
Then I noticed the timestamp on the recording.  
The camera had detected movement at 3:17 a.m.  
But the footage showed the figure standing on my porch at 3:17 a.m. on Thursday.  
The night before.  
I’d checked the camera on Wednesday morning.  
I’d watched the footage.  
I’d checked the porch.  
And I’d seen nothing.  
I don’t know how that was possible.  
I still don’t.  
I’m writing this because I don’t know what else to do.  
Last night, I found a dead dog outside my house.  
It was a brown dog with a red collar. I’d seen it around the neighborhood before. It belonged to an older couple who lived a few streets away.  
I didn’t touch it.  
I called the police.  
While I was waiting, I noticed a small piece of paper tucked beneath the dog’s collar.  
It had my name on it.  
Not my first name.  
My full name.  
Written in handwriting I didn’t recognize.  
Beneath it was a single sentence.  
“You had your chance.”  
I went inside and locked the door.  
I called Mike, but he didn’t answer.  
I called the police again and asked whether they’d found anything.  
They hadn’t.  
I checked the security camera.  
The footage from the previous night had disappeared.  
All of it.  
Every recording.  
Except for one file.  
It was a video from 3:17 a.m.  
The timestamp was correct.  
The footage showed my front porch.  
The door.  
The dead dog.  
Then the figure appeared.  
It didn’t walk out of the darkness this time.  
It was already standing there.  
Facing my front door.  
Its head was tilted to one side.  
Its arms hung almost to its knees.  
And in its right hand was that curved piece of metal.  
A fishing hook.  
The figure raised its left hand and knocked on my door.  
Three times.  
I heard those knocks while I watched the footage.  
Not through the camera.  
From upstairs.  
Three slow knocks.  
I froze.  
The video continued playing.  
The figure leaned forward until its face was inches from the camera.  
I still couldn’t make out its features.  
But I could see its mouth.  
It was smiling.  
Then it lifted the hook and dragged the point down the front door.  
The video ended.  
I heard another sound from downstairs.  
A slow scraping noise.  
Like metal being dragged across wood.  
I haven’t moved from my bedroom.  
I can hear something walking around downstairs.  
Every few minutes, it stops.  
Then I hear three knocks.  
First, from the front door.  
Then, from the kitchen window.  
Now, from the bedroom door.  
I don’t know if it can get in.  
I don’t know what it wants.  
I don’t know if it actually has anything to do with the possum.  
Maybe Mike was right. Maybe Roadkill is just a story people tell.  
Maybe I’ve gone crazy.  
But there’s something I haven’t told you.  
When I first hit that possum, I didn’t just drive away.  
I checked my rearview mirror.  
I saw it lying in the road.  
And I saw something else.  
A second shape, standing over it.  
I thought it was another animal.  
I thought it was a dog.  
But it stood up on two legs.  
And it watched me leave.  
I just didn’t want to admit that part.  
I thought if I ignored it, it would go away.  
The footsteps have stopped now.  
I can hear breathing on the other side of the door.  
And something is scratching at the wood.  
Slowly.  
Carefully.  
Like it’s writing something.  
I’m going to post this and call the police one more time.  
If I don’t update this, please don’t come looking for me.  
And if you ever hit an animal on a country road at night, please—  
Please don’t just drive away.  
Because I think I’ve finally figured out what Roadkill does.  
I don’t think he kills people because they hit animals.  
I think he kills them because they leave them behind.  
And I think he makes sure they understand what it feels like to be the thing left on the road.  
The scratching has stopped.  
I can hear the doorknob turning.  
Oh, God.  
There’s a piece of paper sliding underneath the door.  
It says—  
“Now you know how it feels.”
