There’s few things more entertaining than reading about other people’s problems. One of those few things: reading someone else’s solutions to them.

I’d always enjoyed reading advice columns ever since I was a little girl reading my mom’s magazines. As an adult, I would have the Internet as well to peruse my favourite weekly advice givers and their followers’ various issues. Of all these advice columns, my favourite was easily Dear Prudence.

Prudie would have the most rational, witty, level-headed solution to a problem that could be reached. I also loved the kindness in all her answers. She didn’t always sugarcoat what she said, but her typed responses were always considerate of the various parties’ feelings and perspectives. Her musing language choice was gentle not to offend and her responses were at least a few paragraphs long. Most importantly, she was always polite—manners were huge to Prudence.

The great thing about advice columns is you can always learn something, even if the advice isn’t for you. 

People around the country—and world—would write in to ask Prudie about various social situations where etiquette and goodness had been potentially breached. Stuff like cold in-laws, thoughtless neighbours or ungrateful friends. Or bad coworkers. 

See, the place where I’d most often read the advice column was at work. It was my escape from the mundane stresses of my job working in a mid-level office for a manufacturing company. And, fittingly enough, one of those stresses driving me to read the column became my shy coworker Allen. Allen was a friendly guy to work with. But I found myself always jumping in to help him when he wasn’t able to do something—and would watch him receive all the credit from my oblivious boss Dave, without saying a word to correct it.

He was so otherwise sweet, however, that I didn’t know how to address it with him—or if I should at all. Other people tended to like him—our doddering middle-aged accounting manager Margaret practically loved him. I felt I couldn’t just call him out. Honestly, I had no idea how to resolve the situation and could use some sage advice of my own. Then, while reading the column one day for escapism, it occurred to me to put these two sides of my life together.

For the first time, I wrote in to the Dear Prudence column myself, sending it off to the attached email address for submissions. It read:

*“Dear Prudence,*

*I’ve been working at my current company for several years now and I’ve never felt a lot of respect from my boss. He might be sexist but I don’t know. I felt like my efforts have warranted a promotion and some recognition for some time, but nothing has come of it. I’m worried a big reason for this is my coworker.* 

*We get along great and he’s a lovely person—but he’s just so incapable. I’m always jumping in to help him with something, whether it’s finishing a report or formatting a presentation or calculating the right sales figures. When the time comes for him to receive praise, my biased boss assumes it’s his work—and my colleague doesn’t correct him!*

*It drives me mad but I feel like I can’t say anything because he didn’t ask me for help in the first place. Plus I don’t want my boss to think I’m petty (even if I am). Help! I don’t think my coworker is doing it intentionally—I don’t want to hurt his feelings. But I’d also like to finally receive a drop of credit for my work.*

*What should I do?*

*Signed,*  
*Office Pushover to the Rescue”*

Despite reading the weekly column so much over the years, it hadn’t once occurred to me to write in myself. I guess because I can normally handle situations and it’s not in my nature to speak up—which is why I was having my workplace dilemma in the first place.

Truth be told, I didn’t expect my story to get a response as there was a huge readership for the column, all who commented frequently on every post and were probably all submitting their own dramas. The comment section was one of my favourite things about the column as well. Everyone always had something to build off of Prudence’s advice. Sometimes you would even find wiser, more levelheaded advice from a commenter. Occasionally, I commented myself, but often just lurked.

But even if Prudence only responded to me privately in an email, her guiding hand would be a huge help.

Imagine my surprise one day at work when I opened my computer—after having helped Allen make an Excel spreadsheet—and saw my own letter on the Dear Prudence website. It was the very first letter on the page and the headline was about it.

*“My Coworker Is Stealing All My Credit and I’m Losing All My Courage to Stop Him!”*

It was a typical Prudie column title and yet it was about my story this time. I was giddy to see it.

*“Dear Office Pushover to the Rescue,*

*Accomplished American businessman P. Belcher once said ‘Nothing will kill a great employee faster than watching you tolerate a bad one.’ This occurrence appears to be in action already in your office, where a devoted and promising employee such as yourself is being stunted by an incompetent and deceptive one.*

*You write with concern for your coworker’s feelings, but unfortunately, it does not appear that he holds the same courtesy for yours. How else would an employee allow someone to be deprived of credit, someone caring enough to aid them in the first place? You may view your small-minded boss as the culprit for your stagnance.*

*But unaware bosses are everywhere and will remain so. However, a colleague who is willing to capitalise off of your minimisation and look the other way reveals something far more intolerable. Far from a reason to excuse his behaviour, your coworker’s niceness is part of his game. He is not only a dunce, but a leech. For this there is only one solution.”*

I felt slightly confused by what I was reading. Prudence wasn’t normally this harsh—even with people in stories who were far worse. She must *really* be moved by my situation, I thought. Then I read the next line of her advice.

*“I am afraid you are going to have to kill your coworker.”*

My eyes froze reading the screen. Did Prudence really just write…to murder my coworker? Surely that was a misprint. I read on to quell my concern and my horror only grew.

*“In order to eliminate a parasite such as him, you will need to act with stealth and planning.  I recommend you strike when he least expects it, possibly after hours in your workplace. Ensuring an alibi and lack of a digital papertrail is also worth noting if you choose to do it here. Using a weapon of strangulation will help with the prevention of physical evidence. I myself am rather fond of the poetry of “choking” the life from someone who has stifled your career.*

*He may beg for his life, but remember that this is all he does. That is one more favour you shan’t grant him. Once his existence and body have been disposed of in a remote location, your path to promotion and success will have been cleared. Just remember to be your usual empathetic, professional self when the police interview you. If you don’t want to spend the rest of your life as a pushover, you will need to push through your hesitation.* 

*The office is “kill or be killed”, one way or another. Please let me know how things work out, dear.*

*Love,*  
*Prudie”*

Dumbfounded, I couldn’t believe what I had just read. I was sure that I had to have clicked onto a troll website by accident. Some kind of sick parody of Dear Prudence. But then, how had they gotten my letter? And why was the URL for the column correct?

My next assumption was that the Dear Prudence website must have been hacked. I thought that for a few seconds until I scrolled down and read the comment section.

*“Great advice Prudie! Bad coworkers really do need to be taken down a peg or two. LW1, if you’re reading this, get some better colleagues (after you’re done murdering this one)!”*

*“Second the idea of murdering the coworker at the office—but would recommend a faster method of execution, such a blunt force trauma. This will reduce the chances of being spotted.”*

*“For Letter Writer 3, I think you should give your ex another chance, it sounds like a total miscommunication. For Letter Writer 1, I think you should use acid to dissolve the body instead.”*

Absolutely no one was addressing the elephant in the room of Prudie’s unhinged, bloodthirsty letter, acting like it was a perfectly normal thing to suggest a reader do. Even worse, people were joining in and giving sick advice of their own. It was like some parallel online hell to the sane, grounded column I’d read daily. 

Shouldn’t these comments be getting removed for breaking rules? Shouldn’t Prudie’s whole response? What was happening at Slate?

Over the following weeks, I tried my best to forget about that column—and the entire Dear Prudence column in general. Nothing was being said about this strange phenomena anywhere online. And no one else that I knew in my life read the column, so there was no one to discuss it with.

Forgetting the insane advice became difficult however, as the trend began to spread to Prudie’s other letter responses as well.

Before long, Prudence was recommending that people poison their in-laws for being standoffish with them, burn their neighbours alive in fires for being too loud, drown their friends on camping trips for being flaky. The most eccentric response I saw from her was Prudie telling someone to shoot crossbow bolts into their colleagues’ backs for taking their parking space—something about the *poetic irony* of stabbing them in the backs. Evil Prudie loved that just as much as Sensible Prudie had.

The comments grew more and more vicious, descending into rabid, sadistic calls for violence and aggression in retaliation. All the “advice” was now to commit murder, torture, kidnapping—and then how to cover it up and evade capture. It was like an 18th century angry mob in the form of a discussion forum.

Worst of all was how inescapable it was for me. Each week, Prudie would post an update post for my letter—the one that had started it all. All she would share was that I hadn’t taken her advice—which she somehow omnisciently knew.

*“Office Pushover to the Rescue still has not taken my advice and dealt with her coworker. Pity.”*

The comments would then proceed to direct anger and indignation my way. Even though I’d written in anonymously, every one of them felt like they were pointed at me. The column showed up in my email every week. I felt like I had to read it.

Slowly but surely, I began to have a breakdown and lose my grasp on reality from exposure to this toxicity. The last straw came when Allen—oblivious to this war raging online over his continued survival—took credit for my work again. I’d corrected a huge error on a major company agreement he was about to send. He’d thanked me, yet mentioned none of it to our superior when he was being lauded for it. I sat in that briefing room, overlooked, and saw the advice column and comments flash through my mind.

Finally, I decided to follow Prudie’s advice. I decided to kill Allen.

That evening, while Allen was working late in the office by himself, I snuck back into the office building—after creating a false exit timestamp and roommate alibi, like Prudie had suggested. I’d fashioned a garrote out of rope using the commenters’ instructions and ambushed him at his cubicle using their techniques. Whipping the rope around his neck, I channelled all my pent up rage and frustration into the act of choking him.

He struggled and released the tightness on his neck just enough to beg for his life.

“Please…I’m…sorry…Lindy…I’ll…give…credit…I…swear!” he sputtered between gasps, clawing at the garrote while I held him from behind.

For a moment, I hesitated. I felt the urge to help him again. This wasn’t the kind of person I was.

But then the words of Prudence came back to the forefront of my thoughts. I needed to push through these feelings of hesitation to get anywhere in my life. The office was a kill or be killed place—he’d already killed my career opportunities, why not kill him? Why did I ask for advice if I wasn’t going to follow it?

Reinvigorated, I finished what I started.

By the time Allen’s lifeless body slumped over from suffocation, I was in a frenzy of elation. I had really done it. I’d made Prudence proud.

That’s when I felt an arrow bolt pierce my back.

A second later, another one plunged into Allen’s corpse. Falling to my knees, I turned around in time to see that the shooter was Margaret from accounting. She was standing clutching a crossbow. Across the room, I could see her computer still open, the Dear Prudence column on the screen. She was a reader of it—and a writer to it.

“That’s the last time either of you fuckers will take my parking spot!” she screamed before turning and charging out of the building.

Oh yeah, I *had* been doing that the past few months.

After Margaret left, I realised that I didn’t have much time left. Bleeding out steadily, I crawled my way to my computer and began to type. In little under an hour, I wrote up the story of everything that happened. The long awaited update to share with this deranged version of Prudence, about how following her advice went. Soon I’ll hit “post”.

I had to confess these actions, not to sate Prudie’s bellicose readers, but to make sure the world knows the truth. Before I die from blood loss, I want to make it abundantly clear that it was me, not Margaret, who killed Allen.

I’m not having a coworker take credit for any of my work again.
